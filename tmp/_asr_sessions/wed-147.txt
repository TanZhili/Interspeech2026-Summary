ID: 1312
TITLE: Convolutional Dynamic Rotary Positional Encoding
PRESENTER: Euijin Hong
ABSTRACT:
Rotary Position Embedding (RoPE) is a strong alternative to relative positional encoding in modern automatic speech recognition (ASR) encoder models. However, its discrete, uniformly-spaced chronological index is misaligned with the continuous, semantic nature of speech features. We propose Convolutional Dynamic RoPE (CD-RoPE), a dynamic positional encoding complementing Branchformer's attention path by warping the rotary temporal index conditioned on local acoustic context via a lightweight depthwise-separable convolution. Additive position modulation preserves RoPE's harmonic structure, enabling continuous input-dependent shifts. CD-RoPE achieves consistent WER improvements over RelPos on Branchformer across all LibriSpeech test sets with 2.2M fewer parameters. Targeted robustness evaluation on Speech Robust Bench (SRB) confirms that CD-RoPE's advantage is strongest under temporal perturbations, validating the temporal warping mechanism.

ID: 2070
TITLE: Diffusion Language Models for Speech Recognition
PRESENTER: Davyd Naveriani
ABSTRACT:
Diffusion language models have recently emerged as a leading alternative to standard language models, due to their ability for bidirectional attention and parallel text generation. In this work, we explore variants for their use in speech recognition. Specifically, we introduce a comprehensive guide to incorporating masked diffusion language models (MDLM) and uniform-state diffusion models (USDMs) for rescoring ASR hypotheses. Additionally, we designed a new joint-decoding method that combines CTC and USDM by integrating the framewise probability distributions derived from CTC with the labelwise probability distributions computed by USDM at each decoding step, thereby generating new candidates that combine strong language knowledge from USDM and acoustic information from CTC. Our findings reveal that USDM, as well as MDLM, can significantly improve the accuracy of recognised text. We publish all our code and recipes.

ID: 2676
TITLE: Do speech foundation models really learn words?
PRESENTER: Robin Huo
ABSTRACT:
Self-supervised speech foundation models are now used in a wide array of downstream applications, including traditional speech recognition and as the basis for tokens in speech-aware language models. Attempts to understand their usefulness have largely focused on probing their representations' ability to discriminate phonemes and words. However, discriminative ability for words need not imply specialized representation of words per se. Good discrimination of words may be explained by good encoding of word form (phonemes) rather than form-independent word representations encoding identity or syntactic/semantic properties. By partialling out phoneme information using residualization, we show that, in later layers, HuBERT and wav2vec 2.0 do in general learn representations which encode words with reasonable fidelity independently of local phonetic content. We show that this simple approach to disentanglement can enhance higher-order linguistic information in word discovery tasks.

ID: 2439
TITLE: Readability Does Not Predict Speech Recognition Errors: Contrasting Human and Machine Perception.
PRESENTER: Baptiste Ramonda
ABSTRACT:
This study challenges the historical link between the readability of a text and its intelligibility by automatic speech recognition (ASR) systems. While textual complexity has a significant impact on human comprehension, its effect on modern end-to-end architectures remains unclear. We present a controlled experimental pipeline using the CLEAR corpus, speech synthesis, and acoustic degradations (babble noise, reverberation). Our results demonstrate a near-perfect decoupling between readability, measured by the Global Readability Index, and intelligibility, measured by the Word Error Rate. This orthogonality persists under different acoustic degradations and is validated on natural speech. The absence of a &quot;cognitive load&quot; on the model during the transcription process confirms that it is now completely independent of textual complexity.

ID: 905
TITLE: Probing Linguistic Information in Speech Embeddings: A Diagnostic Analysis across Acoustic and Structural Domains
PRESENTER: Simon Gonzalez
ABSTRACT:
Speech embeddings have become a central component of modern automatic speech recognition and speech-language models; however, their linguistic interpretability is still under active research. This study adopts a diagnostic approach to examine the extent to which speech embeddings encode linguistic features. We analyse linear associations between pretrained acoustic embeddings and a set of core acoustic, phonetic, and linguistic structure features. The results show that embeddings encode acoustic and phonetic properties, particularly temporal dynamics, spectral structure, and voice-quality related features, while higher-level linguistic structure is represented more weakly. Lexical complexity shows a measurable association, whereas morphological and syntactic complexity show limited linear accessibility. These findings suggest that embeddings capture linguistic information in a distributed and gradient manner, with strongest sensitivity to properties closely tied to signal realisation.

TITLE: I Am No One: Style-Aware Paraphrasing for Text Anonymization
PRESENTER: Ahmed Sohair Khan
ABSTRACT:
Authorship attribution models can re-identify users from seemingly anonymized text by exploiting stable stylistic fingerprints, even after explicit identifiers are removed, posing a growing privacy risk for text publishing and analytics. This risk extends to speech-derived text such as ASR transcripts of meetings and call-center conversations, where stylometric leakage can persist even after acoustic anonymization. Differential privacy-based anonymization often severely degrades text quality and utility. We propose a style-aware, prompt-driven anonymization approach that uses pretrained large language models to construct compact stylistic profiles from minimal samples and rewrite text to suppress identifiable style markers while preserving meaning. Across blog and review datasets, our approach reduces authorship attribution F1 by 60-70% while maintaining content quality and readability, substantially outperforming DP-based and non-DP baselines.
