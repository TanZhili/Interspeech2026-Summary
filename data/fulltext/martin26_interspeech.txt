Abstract
Sleep deprivation and its consequences are a major public and
personal health issue. In this study, we draw on the SOMVOICE
corpus to estimate sleep deprivation and its symptoms (sleepi-
ness, fatigue, performance loss) using speech samples. We pri-
oritised interpretability and frugality, using simple yet robust
and validated acoustic features (eGeMAPS and Snack) and clas-
sifiers (SVM, Random Forest, Gradient Boost). We evaluated
the sex- and age-related biases of our classifier, and reported our
energy consumption and carbon footprint. Using SHAP values,
we investigated the relative importance of each feature to the
classification, allowing comparison with previous studies and
driving the field towards mechanistic approaches. Finally, us-
ing a symptom network and performing cross-task evaluation,
we investigated whether the classifiers had generalised the task
they had been trained on, or a more general, less specific con-
struct.
Index Terms : Sleep deprivation, speech biomarkers, inter-
pretability, frugality
1. Introduction
1.1. Context
Sleepiness is a significant public health issue associated with
higher risks of disability and mortality [1, 2], with a high preva-
lence in the general population, with up to one in three people
affected [3]. In this context, clinicians need tools to regularly
measure patients’ sleepiness levels in natural settings, such as
at home, without requiring dedicated tasks. Moreover, sleepi-
ness is a major risk factor for accidents at work [4, 5] and driv-
ing accidents [6, 7]. V oice and speech recordings are promising
candidates for this purpose: they can be easily collected using
smartphones, recorded passively, and have already been linked
to multiple health conditions, including sleepiness [8].
1.2. Previous works
As soon as 1997, Harrison et al. [9] observed changes in percep-
tual scores of “appropriate intonation” and “volume” in 9 sub-
jects undergoing a 36h-sleep deprivation protocol. Since then,
automatic approaches have obtained promising classification
performances, e.g. 86.5% accuracy obtained by Nwe et al. [10]
using cascaded spectrogram filters on dialogues from 12 speak-
ers placed under a 36h-sleep deprivation protocol. McGlinchey
et al. [11] observed decreases in pitch, bark energy in some

(max. 2h of sleep). Icht et al. [12] compared the speech (sus-
tained phonation, words, and sentences) of 47 healthy partici-
pants and found the harmonic-noise ratio (HNR) to be signifi-
cantly higher after 24h of sleep deprivation than after a normal
night. Finally, Thoret et al. [13] obtained a balanced accuracy
of 77% combining generic auditory representation and SVM,
on a corpus of 22 women performing reading tasks during three
days of partial sleep deprivation.
Interestingly, most of the existing literature has focused
more on the estimation of its adverse effects than on sleep
deprivation itself. The detection or estimation of subjective
sleepiness, as reported by the participants using the Karolinska
Sleepiness Scale [14], has been the focus of two Interspeech
challenges respectively in 2011 [15, 16] and 2019 [17]. The
datasets from these challenges have since then been used to de-
velop classification or regression pipelines (e.g. [18, 19, 20]),
reaching a maximum balanced accuracy of 77.6% on a subset
of the Sleepy Language Corpus [21] and Spearman correlation
ofρ = 0.387 with the ground truth on the SLEEP corpus [22].
Alongside sleepiness, part of the literature focuses on other
consequences of sleep deprivation, including fatigue and cogni-
tive performance. Greeley et al. [23] found that fatigue signifi-
cantly alters Mel-frequency cepstral coefficients (MFCCs) and
formant frequencies of specific phones, enabling correlations
between vocal changes and reaction times. De Vasconcelos et
al. [24] reported lower elocution and articulation rates, more
silent and filled pauses, and the emergence of vocal fry under fa-
tigue. Baykaner et al. [25] showed that speech features predict
cognitive performance within a 5–12% error margin, notably
through longer pauses and reduced formant variation. Using a
multimodal approach, Heaton et al. [26] achieved a correlation
of r = 0.68, linking fatigue to increased vocal creak and re-
duced speech motor coordination (articulator velocity). Finally,
Riad et al. [27] employed deep learning models, obtaining an
AUC of 0.68 for fatigue detection, highlighting perturbations in
prosody, articulation, and fluency.
1.3. Objectives
We aim to investigate the feasibility of automatically detecting
sleep deprivation and its adverse outcomes (sleepiness, fatigue
and performance loss) using only interpretable acoustic features
and simple classifiers, while avoiding sex- and age-related bi-
ases and controlling our energy consumption.
While the most recent approaches in speech processing for
health focus on using (or training) foundation models to extract

(e.g. sleepiness). These simple pipelines also allow investiga-

performances: biased models should be avoided since they can
amplify systemic inequalities and epistemic injustices [33].
On the other hand, we aimed at reducing the energy con-
sumption and carbon footprint of training our models: while
training deep-learning models would require important compu-
tational resources – for a low generalisation ability regarding
the small amount of data we have, simpler models (e.g. SVM
or tree-based models) can achieve similar performances [34, 35]
while having a significantly lower energy consumption[36].
2. SOMVOICE dataset
This study relies on the SOMVOICE dataset [37]. This cor-
pus contains the recordings of 28 participants (16 women) un-
dergoing a Multiple Sleep Latency Test after a normal night at
the sleep lab, or after a Total Sleep Deprivation protocol (ran-
domised order). Contrary to previous corpora, participants in-
cluded in the SOMVOICE dataset have been screened to ensure
they do not present any symptom of sleep disorder but also to
ensure they have sufficient reading proficiency. The criteria and
the other aspects of the corpus are extensively described in [37].

Among the multiple clinical information available in the
corpus, we focus on the sleepiness-related ones (Table 1). First,
each recording is labelled by thesleep deprivation status of the
speaker (normal night vs. total sleep deprivation). For each con-
dition, participants underwent a Multiple Sleep Latency Test
(MSLT) [38]. Every two hours between 9 a.m. and 7 p.m.,
they were placed in a dark room and instructed to sleep. Sleep
latency was measured using EEG as the time from test onset
to the first 30-s epoch of any sleep stage. Following clinical
criteria for hypersomnolence [39, p.161], latencies were bina-
rized as lower or higher than 8 min. Subjective sleepiness was
assessed before each recording using the Karolinska Sleepi-
ness Scale (KSS) [14], a single-item Likert-like scale ranging
from 1 (not sleepy) to 10 (falling asleep), with a threshold at 5.
Participants also completed a Visual Analog Scale for Fatigue
(V AS-F)[40], consisting of a 100-mm line; values above 50
mm were considered high fatigue. Finally, between nap oppor-
tunities, participants performed a Psychomotor Vigilance Test
(PVT) using PC-PVT2.0 [41]. Reaction times were recorded
over 10 min, from which we extracted the median speed and the
Response Time Divergence (RTD) [42].
2.2. Audio recordings and preprocessing
Before each iteration of the MSLT, the participants were seated
at a desk and asked to read aloud a text of approximately
150 words, without dialogues, from Le Petit Chose (Alphonse
Daudet). Participants read twelve texts in the same order, in-
dependently from their sleep-deprivation status. The dataset
comprises a total of 336 recordings (28 speakers× 6 texts× 2
conditions) with an average duration of 60.6s (sd: 7.9s), which
we sliced into speech segments. In order to preserve speech
prosody, we took care of not cutting the recordings in the mid-
dle of speech segments. To do so, we applied a V oice Activ-
ity Detection system (rVAD [43]) and iteratively aggregated the
segments (speech and silence) until a duration of at least 20
seconds was reached, which has been previously shown to be
the minimal duration of audio for features to converge [44, 45].
This results in a total of 818 segments with an average duration
of 24.3s (cf. Table 2).

3. Method
3.1. Features
For each speech segment, we extracted the 88 eGeMAPS fea-
tures [46]. Originally introduced to perform emotional analysis,
a growing literature has demonstrated their interest for health-
related tasks, and more specifically regarding sleepiness [13].
In order to compare with the eGeMAPS computed with the
opensmile toolbox [47], we extracted a smaller set of similar
features using the toolbox snack [48]. These 27 features have
originally been validated on social affect classification [49] but
have also been used successfully to estimate subjective sleepi-
ness [21]. We also tested their early fusion (designed as both)
3.2. Classification pipelines and evaluation
We compared three pipelines: a Support Vector Classifier
(SVC), a Random Forest (RF) and a HistGradientBoostingClas-
sifier (GB). The details of the hyperparameters search grids are
available in our code online 1. The classification pipeline was
implemented using scikit-learn (1.8.0)[50].
We rigorously evaluated our pipelines using a nested strat-
ified group K-fold approach: for each iteration of an external
10-fold, we performed internal 5-folds to obtain the best hyper-
parameters. The split procedure was stratified so that each fold
(both external and internal) contained the same ratio of positive
and negative samples; but also the same ratio of women/men
and younger/older participants. Finally, we prevented data leak-
age by assigning all the segments of a given speaker to a unique

performed majority voting to compute recording-scale perfor-

better computational stability, all the reported metrics are com-

3.3. Models comparison
To evaluate the statistical differences between the three feature
sets, we labelled each recording as ‘correctly classified’ or ‘in-
correctly classified’ and performed McNemar tests. A signif-
icant test (p < 0.05) means that the differences between two
lists of labels are not attributable to random – and thus that one
model significantly outperforms the other one.
3.4. Bias evaluation
To evaluate the sex- and age-related biases of our models
we took the correctness labels from model comparisons and
computed a logistic regression ( statsmodels package, v
0.14.6 [51]): Misclassified∼ age× sex. Effects are
expressed as odds ratios (ORs), which indicate how the odds of
being misclassified change with a one-year increase in age. The
interaction term (age× sex) tests whether the effect of age dif-
fers between women and men; a significant interaction indicates
that age influences misclassification differently across sexes.
Significant effects indicate that classification performance sys-
tematically varies with age and/or sex, i.e. model biases.
3.5. Features importance
In order to provide the same explanation for tree-based models
as for Gaussian-based SVM classifiers, we computed, for each
task, the median SHAP coefficients [52] ( shap Python pack-
age (0.50.2)) of each classifier across the external folds (Fig-
ure 1). In order to compare different classifiers, SHAP coeffi-
cients were scaled (maxabs scale) before taking the median.
3.6. Energy consumption and carbon footprint
Finally, we estimated the carbon footprint and the energy
consumed by the training and evaluation/explanation of our
pipelines using the Python package codecarbon (3.2.2)[53].
While the exact calculation of these metrics is a difficult
task [54] and a field of research by itself, these metrics provide
an order of magnitude of this consumption – which may then be
compared with billion-parameters deep-learning models.

4.2. Features contribution
Some tendencies are common to multiple symptoms. For in-
stance, all the tasks are associated with a lower average energy,
similarly to what has been observed by Icht et al. [12]. We
also observe lower H1A1mean for KSS, Fatigue, PVT-speed
and PVT-RTD, as well as higher H1A3 mean for Sleep depriva-
tion, MSLT and PVT-RTD. These metrics are related to phona-
tory quality [55] and can help differentiate between modal and
breathy vowels. Similarly, we observe higher CPPMean – i.e.
better voice quality [56] – for KSS, Fatigue and PVT-Speed,
which could be a marker of an alteration of the articulatory coor-
dination, notably impacted by fatigue [26]. Finally, the classifi-
cation of sleep deprivation is associated with a higher H1Mean,
but a lower value for PVT-speed and PVT-RTD classification.

specific for some tasks, such as a higher F1 mean for MSLT
(not previously observed [57]), a better harmonicity (lower
H1H2mean) for KSS (not previously measured [21]), a higher
B2mean for Fatigue (in line with formants alteration observed
in [23]), a less breathy voice (higher HNR05) for PVT-Speed
(in line with [12]) and a higher F0mean for PVT-RTD (not ob-

4.3. Energy consumption and carbon footprint
The experiments were run using the distributed computational
resource Grid5000 ( grvingt2 nodes) [58]. The experiments on
the three sets of features (classification + SHAP values compu-
tation) consumed a total of 0.450 kWh, representing 8.55 g of
CO2 (19 g CO 2eq/kWh on 7 February 3). Moreover, these ex-
periments did not consume any water for cooling. These metrics
only report the cost of using these devices, but did not take into
account the ecological cost of their fabrication.
5. What did classifiers generalise?
Our experimental results raise the question of the specificity of
the generalised concept. Martin et al. [57] previously showed
that classifiers trained on specific symptoms may instead gen-
eralise broader, correlated syndromes. In line with this, we in-
vestigate whether our models learnt the intended tasks or, con-
versely, captured more general and less specific constructs re-
lated to sleepiness and fatigue.
In order to quantify and visualise the relationship between
our different classification targets, we computed and drew a
symptom network [59, 60]: nodes correspond to the symptoms,
while the edges correspond to the ℓ1-regularised pairwise cor-
relation coefficients between the variables. We computed the
graph using the MGM package (1.4)[61] and visualised the re-
sulting graph in Figure2 using the qgraph R package (1.9.8).

Classifiers trained to estimate Sleep deprivation, MSLT
(physiological sleepiness) and KSS (subjective sleepiness) have
generalised quite specifically the concept they were trained on,
despite the high correlations between Deprivation and MSLT,
and between Deprivation and Fatigue. Conversely, classifiers
for performance metrics (PVT) are almost interchangeable:
when trained on PVT-Speed, they obtain good performances on
estimating PVT-RTD (UAR>80%), and vice versa. This result
is consistent with their definition (they both measure metrics re-
lated to reaction speed), which translates into a high correlation
between the two variables (r = 0.57 in Figure 2). Finally, there
is a confusion between Fatigue and KSS (subjective sleepiness),
the classifier trained on detecting Fatigue achieving even higher
performances than the classifier specifically trained to do so
(UAR=69.1% vs. 66.2%). This confusion between subjective
ratings of sleepiness and fatigue is not surprising, since the con-
fusion between the two terms in general language is well-known
in sleep medicine [62, 63].
6. Conclusion
In this article, we automatically estimated symptoms linked to
sleep deprivation, in a frugal and interpretable way. In addition,
we reported the sex- and age-biases of our models, and investi-
gated the level of clinical specificity of the constructs learnt by
the models. In line with the constraints of this article about in-
terpretability and frugality, we aim to pursue this work towards
the joint estimation of these symptoms by employing Bayesian
inference, combining the symptom networks with the estimates
obtained by machine learning classifiers [64].
