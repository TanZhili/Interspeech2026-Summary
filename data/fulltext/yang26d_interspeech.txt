Abstract
Korean exhibits substantial regional variation, comprising
five major dialects in addition to the standard Seoul dialect.
These dialects convey distinctive prosodic patterns and reflect
important aspects of cultural and speaker identities. However,
most Text-to-Speech (TTS) systems are primarily designed for
standard language, limiting their ability to represent dialectal
characteristics, particularly in low-resource dialect settings. Al-
though face-based speech synthesis provides a viable alterna-
tive when reference audio is scarce or noisy, modeling dialec-
tal prosody within this paradigm remains largely unexplored.
To address this gap, we propose K-DIALECT, a multimodal
face-based TTS framework for synthesizing low-resource Ko-
rean dialects. The model integrates a face encoder, a dialect-
conditioned pitch predictor, and a FiLM-based fusion module.
Experiments on six Korean dialects demonstrate improvements
in dialectal fluency, naturalness, and modality alignment.

1. Introduction
Recent advances in neural text-to-speech (TTS) synthesis have
brought synthetic speech closer to human-level naturalness [1].
However, most systems are trained on standard language cor-
pora, limiting their ability to capture prosodic variations in di-
alects and regional accents [2]. Dialect is not merely a periph-
eral variant of language but an important carrier of speaker iden-
tity and cultural diversity [3]. TTS systems that fail to represent
dialectal characteristics often produce speech that sounds un-
natural or homogenized [4].
Meanwhile, dialectal speech resources are scarce and
unevenly distributed, making approaches that rely on large
amounts of reference audio impractical [5, 6]. These chal-
lenges are particularly severe in low-resource dialect settings.
Such limitations motivate methods that explicitly model dialec-
tal prosody while decoupling it from the speaker identity. In this
study, we focus on Korean, a low-resource language with five
major regional dialects, Gangwon, Gyeongsang, Jeolla, Jeju,
and Chungcheong, in addition to the Seoul dialect [7, 8].
Previous TTS methods mainly rely on reference speech or a
designated speaker to guide the synthesis [9, 10, 11]. These ap-
proaches benefit from leveraging the prosodic information em-
bedded in the reference signals [12, 13]. However, they are
limited in low-resource dialect scenarios, where clean reference
speech is scarce [6]. Moreover, artifacts present in the reference
audio are directly transferred to the generated output [14, 15]. In

able.1 Our contributions are summarized as follows:
• We propose a framework that jointly controls speaker identity
and dialectal prosody without reference speech.
• Our approach is designed for low-resource dialects, requiring
only a face image and a dialect identifier at inference.
• We explicitly model dialectal rhythm and intonation by pre-
dicting a frame-levelf 0 trajectory.
2. Method
2.1. Pitch Predictor
We introduce a dialect-conditioned pitch predictor (Figure 1b)
that directly generates a fundamental frequency (f 0) trajectory.
The predictor is combined with a Feature-wise Linear Modu-
lation (FiLM) mechanism [22] to integrate pitch and stylistic
information. It receives a dialect ID as input, which is first
mapped to an embedding vector and then processed by an MLP
consisting of two hidden layers with non-linear activations. The
model outputs a sequence of relative pitch offsets in semitones
(∆fsemi) aligned with the length of the phoneme sequence.
During training, the ground-truth semitone offset is deter-
mined by the difference between thelogf 0 contour of the ref-
erence audio and a speaker-specific baselinef 0 estimated from
the speaker embedding. The pitch predictor is optimized using
an MSE loss between the predicted and ground-truth trajecto-
ries. This allows the model to learn characteristic dialectal pitch
patterns without requiring reference speech at inference time.
To fuse prosodic and stylistic information, we adopt FiLM
conditioning. Given a latent representationx, FiLM applies
feature-wise affine transformations conditioned on the predicted
pitch embedding, enabling explicit control over dialectal intona-
tion patterns while preserving the speaker’s identity.

2.2. Face Encoder
Speaker identity, which anchors both timbre and habitual speak-
ing style in synthesized speech, is derived from a static face im-
age. We adopt a dual-branch face encoder architecture consist-
ing of two complementary components, as shown in Figure 1c.
The first branch is based on ArcFace [23] which is trained to
capture identity-preserving facial features. The second branch
is based on CLIP [24] which captures a broader stylistic and
visual context. Each branch produces a 512-dimensional em-
bedding, and the two embeddings are concatenated to form a
1024-dimensional visual representation. This representation is
passed through a fusion network composed of two hidden lay-
ers with Layer Normalization and GELU activations and then
ℓ2-normalized to stabilize the embedding space.
To align the visual representation with the acoustic counter-
parts, the face encoder is optimized using four objectives. Con-
trastive loss enforces consistency between image and audio em-
beddings. The MSE loss aligns the predicted style embeddings.
Distillation loss encourages the speaker embedding to match a
pretrained teacher encoder. Finally, an orthogonality constraint
disentangles the speaker identity and stylistic subspaces.
2.3. Training Objective
The full system is trained using a multitask objective that jointly
optimizes acoustic modeling, multimodal alignment, and pitch
prediction. The text encoder and GPT-based decoder are opti-
mized with cross-entropy losses for phoneme-to-text alignment
(Ltxt) and mel-spectrogram prediction (Lmel). The face encoder
is trained with contrastive (L contrast), MSE (L mse), distillation
(Ldistill), and orthogonality (L ortho) losses as described in Sec-
tion 2.2. The pitch predictor is trained using an MSE loss on
semitone trajectories (L pitch). The overall training objective is
defined as the weighted sum of all loss terms:
L=λ txtLtxt +λ melLmel +λ conLcontrast +λ mseLmse
+λ distLdistill +λ orthoLortho +λ pitchLpitch.(1)
The weighting coefficientλbalances the contributions of acous-
tic modeling, multimodal alignment, and pitch supervision.
2.4. Inference
During inference, the system receives a text sequence, face im-
age, and target dialect ID. The text input is processed by aDi-
alect Translatorbased on a T5 [25] architecture. The trans-
lator is pre-trained on a large-scale 42 GB Korean corpus and
fine-tuned on a parallel corpus consisting of standard Korean
and six regional dialects. This module converts sentences be-
tween standard Korean and regional dialects, ensuring consis-
tency in lexical choices and sentence-final endings. As a result,
the model can handle diverse text inputs while preserving the
semantic content and adapting them to the target dialect.
From the facial image, the face encoder extracts a speaker
embedding, while the pitch predictor uses the target dialect ID
to generate a pitch trajectory, which is subsequently converted
into pitch embeddings. The synthesizer takes the phoneme se-
quence of the dialect-translated text and applies FiLM-based
modulation to the hidden states using both speaker and pitch
embeddings. Finally, the acoustic model generates either a mel-
spectrogram or discrete audio tokens for speech synthesis.
3. Experiment
3.1. Dataset
We utilize the Korean Dialect Dataset from AI Hub [26]. This
dataset is a comprehensive collection spanning six distinct Ko-
rean dialects: Seoul, Gangwon, Gyeongsang, Jeolla, Jeju, and
Chungcheong dialects. It includes audio data from 2,749 unique
speakers, with a total length of approximately 1,511.55 minutes.
The dataset is composed of high-quality audio files recorded at
a sampling rate of 22.05kHz and corresponding transcribed text.
Notably, only the Seoul dialect data includes facial images and
information on mouth shape changes. Accordingly, the face en-
coder is trained exclusively on the Seoul subset, whereas the
other subsets are used to train the other modules.
In our training split, the Seoul subset contains 1,245 utter-
ances and 244.17 minutes of speech, while each of the other
five dialect subsets contains 3,000 utterances. The durations of
the Gangwon, Gyeongsang, Jeolla, Jeju, and Chungcheong sub-
sets are 243.11, 241.28, 258.85, 230.92, and 293.22 minutes,
respectively. Thus, although the number of utterances differs
between Seoul and the other dialects, the total speech duration
is relatively balanced across dialects.
3.2. Implementation Details
Our model is trained using a single NVIDIA RTX A6000 Ada
49GB GPU with a batch size of 10. To prevent overfitting, we
randomly sample different face images of the same speaker and
paired them with identical utterances for every training step.
The model is optimized using AdamW with a learning rate of
5×10 −6 for a total of 1.2 million steps. For performance com-

3.3. Evaluation Protocols
3.3.1. Objective Evaluation
For objective evaluation, we use 1,015 utterances from 29 Seoul
test speakers, corresponding to 83.70 minutes of speech. Since
paired face images are available only for the Seoul subset, Seoul
face images are used as the visual condition. Each utterance
is synthesized under six dialect conditions by changing the in-
put dialect text and dialect identifier, resulting in 6,090 syn-
thesized samples in total. The general speech quality is eval-
uated using three standard objective metrics. The speaker en-
coder cosine similarity (SECS) measures the cosine similarity
between speaker embeddings extracted from synthesized and
reference speech, where higher values indicate better preserva-
tion of speaker identity. The word error rate (WER) evaluates
intelligibility by comparing the recognized text from the syn-
thesized speech with the input transcription, with lower values
indicating clearer pronunciation. Mel cepstral distortion (MCD)
assesses spectral fidelity by measuring the distance between the
mel-cepstral coefficients of synthesized and reference audio,
where lower values correspond to higher acoustic similarity.
For dialectal prosody, frame-level pitch error metrics alone
are often insufficient to capture its unique characteristics. Com-
monly used measures, such asF 0-root mean squared error
(RMSE), are sensitive to temporal misalignment and tend to
overlook perceptually important phenomena, including over-
smoothed or flattened pitch contours [11]. As a result, speech
may exhibit a low numerical pitch error while failing to con-
vey the distinctive intonational properties of regional dialects.
This issue is particularly pronounced in Korean, where dialec-
tal identity is primarily expressed through sentence-final bound-
ary tones and dynamic pitch variation rather than absolute pitch
values [28, 29]. Accordingly, we adopt pitch analysis metrics
that incorporate contour and structural information for dialec-
tal prosody evaluation. In addition, frame-level pitch accu-
racy is assessed in a complementary manner usingF 0-dynamic
time warping (DTW)-RMSE, which accounts for the temporal
alignment between pitch trajectories. Specifically, the global
pitch contour similarity is evaluated using the global shape dis-
tance (G-SHAPE). Phrase-final intonation characteristics are
analyzed using the phrase-final scalar distance (F-SCALAR),
which reflects pitch level differences at sentence boundaries,
and the phrase-final shape distance (F-SHAPE), which captures
differences in boundary tone contour shapes. Temporal pitch
dynamics are further evaluated using the modulation distance
(MOD), which quantifies the degree of pitch variation over time
and reflects the extent of pitch over-smoothing.

and three systems. Participants are presented with synthesized

3.4.2. Dialect-wise Prosody Analysis
Table 2 presents the dialect-wise prosody evaluation results
for the six Korean dialects. The proposed model gener-
ally achieves lower errors (F 0-DTW-RMSE) than the base-
line across all dialects, indicating an overall improvement in
frame-level pitch alignment. Consistent improvements are ob-
served with contour- and structure-based metrics. The proposed
model records lower G-SHAPE values than the baseline across
all dialects, demonstrating a stable reproduction of the global
pitch contour structure. Improvements are also observed in the
phrase-final prosody metrics. The F-SCALAR value is gener-
ally reduced, and the F-SHAPE value shows lower values across
dialects, indicating accurate modeling of sentence-final intona-
tion contours. In addition, the MOD value is consistently re-
duced across all dialects, showing that the proposed model bet-
ter preserves temporal pitch variation and avoids excessive pitch
flattening. Overall, the proposed model shows improved perfor-
mance over the baseline in both frame-level pitch accuracy and
contour- and structure-based prosody metrics, with more pro-
nounced and consistent gains observed in metrics that capture
the global contour structure and phrase-final intonation patterns.

3.6. Efficiency of Face Encoder
Figure 3 illustrates how the proposed face encoder disentangles
the speaker identity and speaking style from the facial image.
The identity encoder (ENC ID ) primarily captures speaker-
specific traits, such as facial structure, whereas the style encoder
(ENC style) focuses on expression-related cues that correlate
with prosody. This separation enables the model to generate
speech that preserves the speaker’s identity and reflects the ex-
pressive style, which is crucial in a multimodal setting where
both natural prosody and speaker similarity must be maintained.
4. Conclusion
This study introduces K-DIALECT, a multimodal TTS frame-
work that extends face-based speech synthesis to low-resource
environments of Korean dialects. We integrate a dual-path
face encoder for disentangling the speaker identity and style,
a dialect-conditioned pitch predictor for prosody modeling, and
a FiLM-based fusion module. We demonstrate that our sys-
tem achieves higher dialectal fluency and naturalness than a
voice-based baseline while maintaining strong speaker similar-
ity across six Korean dialects, highlighting its effectiveness. Al-
though this study represents the first attempt to expand face-
based TTS into dialectal-speech synthesis, future work can ex-
plore modeling intra-dialect variation.
