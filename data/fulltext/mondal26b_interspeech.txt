Abstract
Dakhini, a contact variety spoken in the Deccan region of In-
dia, remains severely underrepresented in curated speech re-
sources despite widespread conversational use. This work
presents a data-centric study aimed at informing systematic
Dakhini speech corpus development. Using telephonic con-
versational recordings, we examine factors relevant to cor-
pus development, including context-driven code-switching and
speaker variation across gender and educational backgrounds.
Phonological reductions, auxiliary omission, and participial
variation are observed in spontaneous speech, while formal
contexts trigger shifts toward standardized Hindi/Urdu forms.
These patterns highlight the importance of speaker stratifica-
tion and dialect-aware annotation when curating low-resource
conversational datasets. Our findings provide guidelines for
developing Dakhini speech corpora and lay groundwork for
dialect-sensitive ASR and conversational modeling in under
documented language varieties.

1. Introduction
Speech data collection for well-resourced languages has ben-
efited from decades of established methodology, standardised
protocols, and large speaker pools. However, when the target
variety is a rare or endangered dialect - particularly one that ex-
ists primarily as a spoken, community-bound phenomenon with
no formal written standard - the data curation pipeline faces a
qualitatively different class of challenges. This paper exam-
ines these challenges through the lens of Dakhini (Hyderabadi
Hindi), a dialect spoken in the Deccan region of southern India
that blends Persian, Old Urdu (Dehlavi), Kannada, Marathi, and
Telugu. We argue that robust data collection for such varieties
demands thorough structural and sociolinguistic understanding
of the dialect before any recording session begins.
Dakhini presents a compelling case study precisely because
of its complex sociolinguistic standing. It is not codified in for-
mal educational systems, has no widely accepted written form
in modern use, and its speakers exist along a continuum rang-
ing from full Dakhini-dominant speech to varying degrees of
code-switching with standard Hindi/Urdu. A prior survey of
60 speakers demonstrated that speaker behaviour shifts mea-
surably between informal and formal settings, with women in
particular showing a stronger tendency to shift toward standard
Hindi/Urdu with Dakhini features, and educated speakers of
both genders doing the same [1]. This is not an incidental ob-
servation: it is a fundamental data collection risk. If a speaker

perceives a recording session as formal or evaluative, they may
unconsciously suppress the very dialect features the researcher
intends to capture. This phenomenon, well-documented in soci-
olinguistics as the Observer’s Effect [2], is significantly ampli-
fied in the case of non-prestige dialects. Speakers of Dakhini,
like speakers of many regional dialects without official recog-
nition, have internalised the association between their dialect
and informality, intimacy, or lower social register. Faced with a
microphone, a researcher, or an institutional framing, they may
perform a more standard variety rather than producing naturalis-
tic dialect speech [3]. The result is a dataset that is acoustically
clean but linguistically inauthentic, systematically missing the
phonological and morphosyntactic features that define the di-
alect.
What distinguishes this problem from general data quality
concerns is that the drift cannot be identified or corrected with-
out prior structural knowledge. For a collector unfamiliar with
Dakhini phonology, the reduction of long vowels (e.g., /aad-
mii/→/admii/), consonant assimilation patterns (e.g., /itnaa/
→/ittaa/), the characteristic non-use of the auxiliaryhai, or the
substitution ofmere seformujh semight go unnoticed or might
even be treated as speaker error. In reality, these are diagnos-
tic features. Their presence or absence is precisely what dis-
tinguishes genuine Dakhini from a Dakhini-inflected standard
Hindi. A structural and phonological checklist, derived from
careful prior linguistic analysis, is therefore not merely helpful
for data collection, it is the primary quality control mechanism.
The preliminary structural investigation covered Dakhini’s
phonology (consonant assimilation, vowel-length reduction),
morphology (noun and adjective classes), pronoun system, and
verbal constructions, documenting systematic divergences from
standard Urdu. These findings directly informed participant se-
lection, elicitation design, and the annotation rule sets described
in this paper. This paper makes three contributions: (i) articu-
lating the specific sociolinguistic and structural challenges of
collecting speech data from a non-prestige dialect community;
(ii) proposing a principled dialect-aware data collection and an-
notation methodology grounded in prior structural analysis; and
(iii) describing elicitation and recording design strategies that
maximise authentic dialect production rather than monitored
speech.

3. Motivation
Despite its historical depth and sociolinguistic richness, spon-
taneous spoken Dakhini remains underexplored, with most re-
search focusing on its historical evolution, literary tradition, or
structural description in controlled contexts [5]. These stud-
ies affirm Dakhini’s distinctiveness but overlook its function
in natural, contemporary speech, especially in mediated set-
tings like telephonic communication. The dialect’s structural
closeness to standard Urdu often leads to its marginalisation as
a regional variant, complicating the identification of authentic
usage and risking the inclusion of code-switched or standard-
ised forms in spontaneous speech data. Moreover, earlier work
rarely integrates sociolinguistic variables such as gender, edu-
cation, and context sensitivity into methodological design. Ad-
dressing these gaps, the present study develops an empirically
grounded, context-sensitive checklist for participant selection,
conversational prompting, and real-time dialect identification,
ensuring corpus authenticity while contributing to the preserva-
tion and documentation of Dakhini as a living, evolving linguis-
tic heritage.
4. Conversational Data Collection: Design,
Ethics, and Speaker Recruitment
Conversational speech data were collected through telephonic
interactions as illustrated in Figure 1 between pairs of partici-
pants who were already familiar with each other, ensuring eco-
logical validity and minimising the social distance that typi-
cally triggers code-switching. Each pair was composed of one
male and one female speaker. This design served a dual pur-
pose: it reflects naturalistic interactional contexts in Dakhini-
speaking communities, and the acoustic contrast between male
and female voices significantly reduces speaker confusion dur-
ing automated diarization, improving segmentation reliability
on spontaneous conversational data.
To further reduce the Observer’s Effect, an asymmetric
awareness protocol was adopted. Within each pair, only one
participant, referred to as the anchor speaker, was informed
of the recording setup prior to the conversation. The anchor
speaker was briefed to lead the conversation naturally, main-
tain turn-taking, and steer discussion toward a pre-agreed topic
menu when needed, without revealing the recording context to
their interlocutor. This approach follows the established soci-
olinguistic practice of covert recording with retrospective con-
sent, a methodology grounded in Labov’s foundational work
[2], which demonstrated that prior awareness of recording sys-
tematically alters speaker behaviour in ways that undermine di-
alectal authenticity.
The corpus comprises recordings from 160 speakers from
Hyderabad, India, including approximately 10% from early-
settler families. Participants range in age from 17 to 50 years,
with a gender distribution of 70% male and 30% female.
The cohort spans diverse educational and socioeconomic back-
grounds, with approximately 20% having higher educational
attainment and 20% belonging to upper socioeconomic strata,
while the majority represent middle- and lower-income groups.
Speakers are multilingual, with varying proficiency in Dakhini,
Urdu, Telugu, and English. Each recording session lasted ap-
proximately 20 minutes, from which about 5 minutes of repre-
sentative conversational speech were selected for inclusion in
the corpus.

Following each conversation, the uninformed participant
was fully debriefed and the research intent was explained
clearly, that the goal was to build a linguistically rigorous cor-
pus of Dakhini to support its recognition in academic research
and language technology, and that their contribution as a native
speaker was foundational to this work. Informed consent was
then sought explicitly before any data was retained. Record-
ings were accepted into the corpus only upon explicit consent;
sessions where consent was withheld were discarded in their
entirety with no data retained. Participants were also informed
of their right to withdraw their data at any stage. Post-consent,
diarization was applied to retain only the audio of the target
speaker, as these segments represent the most unmonitored di-
alect production. The anchor speaker’s audio was excluded
from the primary corpus.
Topic control was introduced to address a practical chal-
lenge encountered in pilot recordings: fully unstructured con-
versation, while naturalistic, produced sessions dense with in-
terjections, tangential corrections, and topic drift that compli-
cated processing and reduced usable dialectal content. Rather
than scripting interactions, anchor speakers were provided a
loose menu of culturally resonant, casual topics such as a re-
cently released film, a new restaurant opening, celebrity news,
or a recipe exchange such as preparing biryani. This guided but
informal design preserved spontaneity while keeping sessions
tractable. Speaker recruitment required careful and transparent
communication. Dakhini speakers, particularly those with for-
mal education, often carry an internalised stigma around dialect

5. Challenges in Data Collection and
Community Engagement
One of the earliest challenges in this project was the identifi-
cation of authentic Dakhini speakers, who are not uniformly
distributed across Southern India, but concentrated in specific
pockets of the older neighbourhoods, where intergenerational
dialect transmission has remained relatively intact. Conven-
tional open recruitment strategies systematically missed this
population, attracting speakers whose speech showed substan-
tial convergence toward standard varieties, making hyperlocal
community-embedded outreach through trusted neighbourhood
intermediaries essential [6]. Effective recruitment ultimately re-
quired working through existing social networks and local con-
tacts within specific neighbourhoods to identify speakers whose
dialect use was consistent and naturalistic across both formal
and informal contexts [7].
Even once identified, recruiting speakers was far from
straightforward. The contemporary data market has gener-
ated well-founded wariness in many communities around how
recordings would be used, stored, and accessed. Scepticism
layered onto the internalised stigma that many Dakhini speak-
ers already carry toward their own dialect [1]. This made in-
dividual solicitation largely ineffective and, more problemati-
cally, tended to produce participants who agreed to record but
then monitored their speech heavily, defeating the purpose of
the collection entirely. Rather than individual solicitation, the
recruitment strategy was restructured around community infor-
mation sessions, where small groups of prospective participants
engaged in open dialogue with the research team about the
project’s linguistic objectives and methodology. These sessions
reframed participation as collective contribution, an opportunity
for speakers to play an active role in bringing their dialect into
formal linguistic scholarship and language technology, with the
message that without their voices, Dakhini would remain invisi-
ble to the tools and systems that increasingly mediate daily life.
6. Database Preparation Methodology
6.1. Recording Modality: Prioritising Linguistic Authentic-
ity Over Acoustic Control
Telephonic interaction was chosen over studio recording as a
deliberate methodological decision. Studio environments in-
troduce the formal, evaluative context that most reliably trig-
gers dialect suppression in speakers of non-prestige varieties.
The telephone is a channel already associated in speakers’ daily
lives with casual, intimate exchange - and for the uninformed
participant, the interaction was indistinguishable from an ordi-
nary call, eliminating any behavioural cue that could prompt
self-monitoring. The resulting trade-off in acoustic quality was
conscious and considered: reduced signal fidelity was accepted
in exchange for linguistic authenticity, which for dialect docu-
mentation is the more consequential variable.
Unlike face-to-face interaction, telephonic exchanges lack
visual cues and often encourage more spontaneous, everyday
language use. This property was intentionally leveraged. The
preliminary study thus also functioned as a methodological re-
hearsal, helping define appropriate conversation themes, iden-
tify dialect-sensitive lexical triggers, anticipate shifts toward
Urdu, and establish criteria for evaluating authenticity in real-
time data.
6.2. Diarization
The conversational recordings were processed through a struc-
tured speech diarization (pyannote speaker-diarization-3.1 [8])
pipeline consisting of three principal stages: (i) extraction of
speaker embeddings to capture speaker-specific acoustic char-
acteristics (ECAPA-TDNN embeddings [9] trained on V ox-
Celeb [10] dataset), and (ii) clustering-based segmentation to
assign speaker labels to contiguous speech segments. The di-
arized output, in the form of Rich Transcription Time Marked
(RTTM) files, was subsequently reviewed for segmentation ac-
curacy. During the chunking stage, a gradual amplitude ramp-
up and ramp-down was applied at the boundaries of each seg-
ment so that abrupt cuts do not introduce audible artefacts in the
concatenated speech. Segments corresponding to each speaker
were then concatenated to generate individual speaker-level au-
dio files. This dual representation - retaining the original con-
versational structure while producing speaker-specific compila-
tions - facilitates both interactional analysis and speaker-level
linguistic investigation.
Given the spontaneous and dialect-rich nature of the data,
particular attention was paid to diarization robustness. Natural
conversations often include overlapping speech, backchannels,
and rapid turn exchanges, which challenge automated segmen-
tation systems. A manual verification stage was therefore incor-
porated to correct speaker misattributions and boundary incon-
sistencies. Corrected outputs were archived to support iterative
refinement of the diarization models, thereby improving system
performance on dialectal conversational speech over time. The
mixed-gender pair design also contributed to improved diariza-
tion accuracy.
6.3. Annotation Framework: Semi-Automated Workflows
with Dialect-Aware Rule Sets
A central objective of the project is to streamline the annotation
process through semi-automated workflows while maintaining
high levels of linguistic reliability. Since most available ASR
systems are trained on standard Hindi or Urdu, their outputs sys-
tematically misrecognise or normalise Dakhini-specific forms,
transcribing, for instance, the Dakhini /ittaa/ as the standard /it-
naa/, or missing dropped auxiliaries entirely. In our work, we
used IndicConformer Hindi ASR 1. Rather than treating these
outputs as errors, the annotation pipeline, as shown in Figure 2,
was designed to treat ASR-to-standard mismatches as positive
dialect signals: divergences between the ASR transcript and the
audio are flagged as candidate sites for dialect-specific anno-
tation rather than simple transcription errors. Drawing directly
from the structural analysis of Dakhini, the following rule-based
detection modules were implemented as listed in Table 1. The
following abbreviations were adopted for the tags:

Rather than applying uniform human review across all seg-
ments, the pipeline uses tag density as a triage signal. Segments
receiving multiple overlapping tags, simultaneously flagged for,
say, PA, MAUX, LD: are prioritised for detailed human review,
as they represent the highest concentration of dialectal activity
and carry the greatest risk of compounded ASR error. Con-
versely, segments with no dialect flags are passed through with
lighter review, allocating annotator effort where it is most con-
sequential. Annotators were trained using standardised guide-
lines derived directly from the structural analysis, with worked
examples for each tag category. Inter-annotator agreement [11]
was measured on a held-out validation set, with disagreements
used iteratively to refine rule boundaries and update annotator
guidelines.
To ensure methodological rigour, systematic quality con-
trol procedures were incorporated into the annotation pipeline.
Inter-annotator agreement metrics were employed to measure
consistency, particularly for dialect classification and code-
switching identification. Periodic blind re-annotation of ran-
domly selected samples was conducted to detect annotation
drift and maintain standardisation over time. Automated out-
puts were benchmarked against manually curated reference seg-
ments to quantify transcription and tagging accuracy. The
modular design of the pipeline enables iterative improvement:
corrections introduced during human validation are fed back
into the automated models, gradually enhancing performance
through supervised refinement. Metadata tagging, including
participant background, conversational topic, and recording
conditions were systematically maintained to support sociolin-
guistic analysis and facilitate reproducibility.
