Abstract
Fine-grained automatic dysarthric speech assessment can assist
clinicians in providing targeted treatment. Yet, prior work of-
ten defaults to extracting features from a single self-supervised
layer, potentially discarding relevant information. In this work,
we first adapt the Wav2Vec2-Large encoder to dysarthric speech
via unsupervised domain adaptation, then perform a layer-wise
analysis to study layer preferences across 25 Darley–Aronson–
Brown perceptual dimensions using the Speech Accessibility
Project corpus. Our results show that the optimal layer varies
across dimensions. We therefore fuse all layers via learnable
scalar mixing to leverage complementary cues across layers,
surpassing single-layer baselines. The learned weights reveal
dimension-specific preferences aligned with speech-subsystem
groupings, suggesting that matching representation depth to
each dimension’s acoustic–linguistic nature could be important
for multi-dimensional dysarthric speech assessment.
Index Terms: Dysarthria assessment, Wav2Vec2, scalar mix-
ing, DAB perceptual dimensions
1. Introduction
Dysarthria is a motor speech disorder resulting from various
neurological conditions. It can affect people across all age
groups and create significant social and communication barri-
ers [1]. Its manifestations vary widely depending on the un-
derlying lesion site and severity. Due to high inter- and intra-
speaker variability, therapy cannot rely on a one-size-fits-all ap-
proach [2]. A comprehensive, individualized assessment is es-
sential for Speech-Language Pathologists (SLPs) to deliver tar-
geted interventions and improve treatment outcomes.
Auditory-Perceptual Analysis (APA) is widely regarded as
the clinical standard for assessing dysarthria. SLPs use clin-
ical frameworks such as the Darley–Aronson–Brown (DAB)
system to evaluate a speaker’s condition [3]. This process re-
quires SLPs to listen to the same speech sample multiple times,
rating different deviant speech dimensions. It is inherently sub-
jective, time-consuming, and expertise-dependent. Hence, au-
tomated speech assessment tools that can provide objective,
detailed speech profiles grounded in granular clinical frame-
works are critical. Such tools could assist SLPs in diagnosis
and progress tracking, enabling more effective clinical manage-
ment [4]. However, research on automated assessment of per-
ceptual dimensions defined by comprehensive clinical frame-
works such as DAB remains scarce.
Although machine learning (ML) shows promise for auto-
mated dysarthria assessment, progress is constrained by data
scarcity and a lack of fine-grained clinical annotations. Early
public datasets such as UA-Speech [5] and TORGO [6] were
relatively small (∼10 hours) and primarily designed for Auto-
matic Speech Recognition (ASR). Critically, they provide only
coarse, speaker-level intelligibility and severity labels rather
than dimension-level perceptual ratings grounded in clinical
frameworks. Consequently, prior work has largely focused on
isolated outcomes such as intelligibility [7, 8], dysfluency [9],
and dysarthria detection [10], leaving comprehensive speech
profiling underexplored. Moreover, relying on global labels can
overlook intra-speaker variability, introducing label noise.
There are ongoing efforts to produce larger corpora with
granular clinical annotations. For instance, [11] collected a
dataset (N=102) with expert ratings on GRBAS voice-quality
dimensions, and [12] studied four perceptual dimensions of
DAB using data from 87 speakers across four dysarthria sub-
types. However, both datasets remain private. More recently,
the Speech Accessibility Project (SAP) released over 400 hours
of dysarthric speech from 959 speakers across five common
etiologies, making it the largest publicly available dataset to
date [13]. SAP provides annotations for 45 DAB perceptual
dimensions, with∼3% of the data rated. A recent pilot study
leveraged this labeled subset to predict seven voice-quality fea-
tures [14], but this covers only a fraction of the DAB frame-
work, leaving most dimensions open for modeling.
In automatic speech assessment, ML approaches com-
monly use frozen self-supervised learning (SSL) encoders (e.g.,
Wav2Vec2 [15]) as feature extractors, typically using the last
encoder layer. However, the optimal layer is task-dependent.
A layer-wise analysis of Wav2Vec2 for dysarthria reported
that early layers outperform the final layer for binary detec-
tion, while later layers suit severity classification [16]. Prob-
ing studies further show that acoustic and phonetic information
in Wav2Vec2 is distributed non-uniformly across layers. Lower
layers retain more low-level acoustic cues, while upper-mid lay-
ers encode phonetic structure [17, 18, 19]. Since DAB dimen-
sions reflect heterogeneous attributes linked to distinct speech
subsystems [3], we hypothesize that optimal layers will differ
across dimensions. Yet, existing work targeting subsets of DAB
dimensions has defaulted to using the last layer [14], potentially
discarding information relevant to specific speech dimensions.
In this work, we investigate the utterance-level assessment
of 25 DAB perceptual dimensions (see Section 2) using the SAP
dataset. We adopt a two-stage framework. In Stage 1, we ap-
ply Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA) based unsupervised domain
adaptation (UDA) to Wav2Vec2 using the unlabeled portion of
SAP to reduce the domain mismatch between typical and patho-
logical speech [20, 21]. In Stage 2, we freeze the adapted en-
coder and use the labeled subset to train assessment models.

Critically, we conduct layer-wise probing across all 25 encoder
layers to examine whether different perceptual dimensions ben-
efit from different layers. Building on these findings, we employ
learnable scalar mixing [22] to derive soft layer combinations,
enabling the model to leverage information distributed across
multiple layers. Our contributions are threefold:
• LoRA-based unsupervised domain adaptation improves
Wav2Vec2 representation quality for dysarthria assessment.
• To our knowledge, we provide the first systematic layer-wise
probing of Wav2Vec2 across 25 DAB dimensions, showing
that optimal layers vary by dimension.
• We show that learnable scalar mixing outperforms single-
layer selection and the learned weights reveal dimension-
specific layer preferences aligned with clinical speech sub-
systems and prior SSL interpretability findings.
2. Dataset
We utilize the Speech Accessibility Project (SAP) [13], the
largest publicly available dysarthric speech corpus of Ameri-
can English to date. We use its latest release (version 2025-11-
02), which contains 368,155 utterances (over 400 hours) from
959 speakers across five primary etiologies: Parkinson’s disease
(39.4% of samples, 354 speakers), ALS (26.8%, 271 speakers),
Cerebral Palsy (18.7%, 178 speakers), Down Syndrome (9.3%,
96 speakers), and Stroke (5.9%, 60 speakers). All official train-
ing, development, and test sets are speaker-independent. Given
the test set is reserved for challenge evaluation, we establish a
reproducible partitioning benchmark described in Section 2.2.
2.1. Clinical Annotations & Dimension Selection
SAP provides ratings from trained SLPs for 45 DAB speech
dimensions using a 1–7 integer scale (1 = typical, 7 = severe
deviation). Of these, 11,168 samples (3%) from 435 speakers
are rated, while the remaining 356,987 samples (97%) are un-
rated. Annotation coverage varies by etiology due to phased
recruitment. Dimensions associated with early-recruited etiolo-
gies (e.g., Parkinson’s disease) have higher coverage, whereas
later-added ones (e.g., Stroke) are underrepresented. We there-
fore exclude 20 dimensions with fewer than 1,000 rated sam-
ples. The label distribution of the remaining 25 is shown in
Figure 1. Notably, ratings are highly imbalanced and skewed to-
ward typical and mild values; even the dimension with the high-
est mean severity (Naturalness) is only moderate (µ= 3.6).
2.2. Data Partitioning
For Stage 1, we use unrated samples from the original train-
ing set for UDA and unrated dev samples for evaluation. For
Stage 2, since the official SAP test set is withheld for challenge
evaluation, and prior work [14] did not release partition details,
we repurpose the labeled Dev set as our Test set and split the
labeled training set into Train and Dev subsets.
To ensure a comparable severity distribution between Train
and Dev subsets, we implemented a speaker-disjoint stratifi-
cation strategy 1. We constructed a severity profile for each
speaker, which comprises the mean, standard deviation of
pooled ratings, and counts for mild/moderate/severe rating
bins (thresholds 2.5 and 4.5). Speakers are clustered via
k-means (k= 5) on these profiles and then proportion-
ally allocated to Train and Dev subsets. The resulting splits
contain 8,637/1,029/1,392 utterances from 346/36/53 speakers

3. Methodology
Experiments were conducted on three NVIDIA GeForce RTX
3090 GPUs using PyTorch [23] and HuggingFace Transform-
ers [24]. Audio samples were resampled to 16 kHz and peak-
normalized. Labels were scaled from the 1–7 ordinal scale to
the range[0,1]at pre-processing. Stage 1 UDA required ap-
proximately 58 hours. Each Stage 2 linear probing experiment
was completed in approximately 2 hours, and scalar mixing
training took approximately 3 hours. Model performance was
evaluated using Mean Absolute Error (MAE), which measures
the average prediction deviation on the rescaled label range, and
Spearman’s rank correlation (ρ), which assesses rank agreement
between predictions and ground truth. All reported metrics in-
clude 95% bootstrap confidence intervals with 1,000 iterations.
3.1. Stage 1: Unsupervised Feature Adaptation
For UDA we adopt Wav2Vec2-Large [15] (317M parame-
ters, 1024-dim hidden states, 24 transformer layers), is a self-
supervised model trained via contrastive learning with masked
prediction. It was selected for its strong performance on speech
assessment tasks [16, 25] and well-documented layer-wise anal-
yses [18]. However, it was pre-trained on typical speakers, cre-
ating an acoustic mismatch with dysarthric speech. To bridge
this domain gap while preserving learned representations, Low-
Rank Adaptation (LoRA) [21] was applied. Our pilot experi-
ments showed that full fine-tuning degraded performance con-
siderably, further motivating this parameter-efficient approach.
A LoRA adapter (rankr= 32, swept over{8,16,32,64}
on dev;α= 64, i.e.α/r= 2; dropout 0.05) was applied
to the query, key, value, output, and feed-forward layers of
all 24 transformer layers, introducing∼9.4M trainable param-
eters while keeping the 317M base frozen. We trained with
the standard Wav2Vec2 contrastive masked-prediction objective
(p= 0.065, span 10) on 315,102 unlabeled utterances, using
learning rate3×10 −4, weight decay 0.1, effective batch size
64, and linear decay over 12,000 steps.

3.2. Stage 2: Supervised DAB Dimension Classification
All Stage 2 experiments were conducted with the same hyperpa-
rameters for fair comparison. AdamW optimizer [26] was used
with learning rate5×10 −4, weight decay0.01, effective batch
size 32 (via gradient accumulation), maximum 15 epochs, and
dropoutp= 0.1. Consistent Rank Logits (CORAL) loss [27]
was employed to respect the ordinal structure of DAB ratings.
3.2.1. Layer-Wise Probing
The adapted encoder was frozen as a feature extractor. For each
encoder layerl∈ {0,1,...,24}(layer 0: CNN feature ex-
tractor output; layers 1–24: transformer outputs), an indepen-
dent linear probe was trained to predict all 25 dimensions. Hid-
den statesH (l)∈R T×1024 were aggregated via masked mean
pooling, followed by dropout (p= 0.1) and dimension-specific
CORAL heads (Linear 1024→6). To evaluate domain adapta-
tion effectiveness, two encoder configurations were compared:
(1) frozen Wav2Vec2-Large without adaptation, and (2) frozen
Wav2Vec2-Large with frozen LoRA adapters from Stage 1.

5. Conclusion
In this study, we investigated the automatic assessment of 25
DAB perceptual dimensions. With a lack of clinical annota-
tions, our LoRA-based UDA on unlabeled SAP improved the
quality of the Wav2Vec2 representation for dysarthria assess-
ment. Our layer-wise probing analysis on the adapted encoder
showed that optimal laye
