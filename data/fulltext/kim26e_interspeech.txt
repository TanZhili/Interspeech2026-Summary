Abstract
Partially spoofed audio localization aims to detect short manip-
ulated spans within an utterance. Most prior work formulates
the task as binary frame authenticity or boundary detection,
which overlooks how neighboring frames evolve across manip-
ulations. In this paper, we argue that directional inter-frame
changes provide a more reliable localization cue, and propose
a transition-aware framework that classifies Real to Fake and
Fake to Real transitions. Our method combines a multi-scale
GRU backbone for local continuity and long-range flow with a
multi-head objective: a frame head for authenticity, a transition
head for adjacent-frame transitions, and a refinement head that
fuses both signals for temporally coherent predictions. Exper-
iments on the PartialSpoof Dataset and the PartialEdit-E1/E2
show state-of-the-art performance, demonstrating the effective-
ness of temporal-order modeling for partially spoofed audio lo-
calization at a challenging 20 ms resolution.
Index Terms: partially spoofed audio, spoof localization, audio
boundary detection, transition-aware localization
1. Introduction
Recent advances in text-to-speech (TTS) [1] and voice conver-
sion (VC) [2] make it increasingly difficult to distinguish syn-
thetic speech from genuine human speech. Such highly realis-
tic synthetic audio poses serious social and economic threats to
voice security systems [3], and has made speech deepfake de-
tection a critical research topic [4]. Specifically, recent attacks
take the form of partial audio spoofing, in which only specific
segments of a genuine utterance are synthesized or replaced.
By modifying fine-grained units, such as words, characters, or
even phonemes, an attacker can precisely alter the semantic
content of a sentence, deceiving both machine-based systems
and human listeners [5]. This calls for specialized detection
approaches capable of identifying subtle and fine-grained ma-
nipulations within speech signals.
Beyond simply identifying whether an audio clip con-
tains spoofed regions, the Partially Spoofed Audio Localization
(PSAL) task seeks to locate the specific segments that have been
manipulated, a fine-grained deepfake analysis problem that has
recently received increasing attention [6]. Several partial audio
spoofing datasets have been developed for this purpose. One of
the most widely used benchmarks for PSAL is the PartialSpoof
Dataset (PSD) [7, 8], which is based on ASVspoof 2019 [9, 10].
Recent datasets have moved toward more realistic and diverse
editing scenarios, as exemplified by the PartialEdit dataset [11].

In contrast to PSD, which contains only a single spoofed region
per utterance, PartialEdit contains multiple manipulated seg-
ments within a single utterance, making the localization prob-
lem more temporally complex. They also differ in how spoofed
regions are constructed: PSD relies on randomly selected ma-
nipulation segments, whereas PartialEdit uses an LLM to iden-
tify word-level manipulation spans.
Existing approaches to localizing partially spoofed audio
can be divided into two categories. The first category focuses on
frame-level inconsistency [12, 13, 14, 15]. These methods as-
sume that fake frames exhibit statistical irregularities compared
to real frames, and models are trained to separate the frames
based on these differences. However, such supervision is of-
ten limited for partially spoofed audio, because frame-level dif-
ferences are influenced not only by manipulation artifacts but
also by intrinsic variability in speech. A single binary loss
may be insufficient to capture the subtle temporal patterns that
distinguish manipulated regions from naturally varying gen-
uine speech. The second category relies on boundary-aware
strategies that explicitly exploit manipulation boundary infor-
mation [16, 17, 18]. However, detection becomes challenging
when an utterance contains multiple manipulated spans or when
the boundary cues are intentionally smoothed or hidden.
Despite the advances, prior efforts typically formulate the
frame authenticity (or boundary) prediction as per-frame (or
per-boundary) classification tasks. This leaves little room for
explicitly modeling the temporal dependencies between the
neighboring frames. Accordingly, as illustrated in Figure 1, we
hypothesize that jointly modeling pairs of adjacent frames, thus
capturing their temporal transitions, allows the model to better

• We propose a multi-head learning objective that supervises

temporally coherent localization to realize transition-aware

• Extensive experiments show that our method achieves
state-of-the-art localization performance across three PSAL

2. Related Works
For PSAL, most existing countermeasures (CMs) relied on
frame-level authenticity as the primary cue for detection [6, 19].
These systems estimated whether each frame was bona fide
or spoofed, and then inferred manipulated regions by thresh-
olding the scores and grouping contiguous high-score frames.
Early works typically used low-dimensional acoustic features,
such as MFCC, LFCC, and CQCC, as inputs and combined
them with classifiers, including LCNN, ResNet, SENet, and
LSTM, to infer manipulated regions [20, 21]. For instance,
[22] proposed an LFCC-based SELCNN–BiLSTM multitask
architecture that strengthened representation learning with a
CNN front-end while complementing temporal context with
a BiLSTM module. Building on this line, [23] introduced a
self-attention–enhanced SELCNN–BiLSTM model that inserts
a self-attention block between SELCNN and BiLSTM to re-
fine segment representations, whereas [19] presented a CRNN-
based framework that jointly captures local acoustic patterns
and longer-range temporal information.
As pretrained speech representations became widely
adopted, front-end embeddings, such as Wav2Vec2 [24] and
WavLM [25], were increasingly combined with ResNet-,
LCNN-, or ResBlock-style backbones [16, 26, 27]. This combi-
nation became one of the dominant approaches in recent PSAL
systems. The Wav2Vec2–BiLSTM system [28], for instance,
performed localization by clustering manipulated frames in the
embedding space. Meanwhile, the multi-granularity fusion
framework [29] strengthened localization decisions by integrat-
ing an utterance-level subsystem with a segment-level subsys-
tem.
Beyond frame-level authenticity, several methods were pro-
posed to explicitly emphasize discrepancies between manip-
ulated and non-manipulated regions in the embedding space.
TDL [12] enhanced localization cues by introducing an embed-
ding similarity module that encouraged separability between
bona fide and spoofed frames. GNCL [13] adopted a dual-
branch architecture with a CNN branch and a graph-based
branch and used a consistency loss between them to highlight
manipulation-related discrepancies for localization. AGO [14]
mitigated domain shift through a gradient reversal layer and
adopted an alternating update strategy to jointly exploit region-
level and boundary-level information for PSAL. PET [15] ex-
ploited pairwise self-consistency by extracting high-frequency
components with EWT, constructing frame-wise similarity ma-
trices, and training an LCNN to regress these self-consistency
maps. Although these inconsistency-driven approaches allevi-
ated some limitations of authenticity-based CMs, they still pri-
marily relied on separating manipulated and non-manipulated
frames in the embedding space. However, frame-level variation
is shaped not only by manipulation artifacts but also by intrinsic
acoustic, phonetic, and prosodic variability in speech. As a re-
sult, emphasizing embedding discrepancy alone may be insuffi-
cient to capture the subtle temporal patterns that distinguish ma-
nipulated regions from naturally varying bona fide speech [30].
In addition to authenticity- and inconsistency-based mod-
eling, there was a line of work that explicitly focused on the
boundaries of spoofed segments. Such boundary-aware ap-
proaches aimed to capture stitching artifacts at the start and
end points of spliced regions to reduce the reliance on global
frame-level authenticity, and empirical analyses showed that
CMs trained on partially spoofed audio tended to concentrate
their evidence near these transition regions [31]. A multi-
task, dual-head architecture with a shared backbone was in-
troduced in [16], where frame-level spoofing classification and
boundary detection were jointly optimized, enabling localiza-
tion by detecting transition points while predicting spoofed
frames. BAM [17] enhanced partial spoof localization with a
boundary-aware attention mechanism consisting of a bound-
ary enhancement module to aggregate intra- and inter-frame
cues for more discriminative boundary features and a bound-
ary frame-wise attention module to gate frame-to-frame inter-
actions guided by predicted boundaries. CFPRF [18] progres-
sively refined the start and end timestamps of forged segments
through a coarse-to-fine proposal refinement procedure. How-
ever, most boundary-driven methods ultimately treated bound-
aries as point-wise binary labels and supervised CMs to clas-
sify individual frames or short segments. This formulation did
not directly encode the manner in which representations should
change across the boundary region. In contrast, we explicitly
model adjacent frame transitions (Real→Fake, Fake→Real, or
None), encouraging the model to learn boundary cues from the
evolution of representations across neighboring frames.
3. Proposed Method
In this section, we describe the proposed framework for the
PSAL. Figure 2 shows the overall framework. First, we em-
ploy a pretrained self-supervised learning (SSL) model to ex-
tract speech features at a 20 ms frame resolution. These speech
features are fed into MS-GRUs to effectively capture local tem-
poral continuity across adjacent frames. The resulting hidden
states are fed into the frame, transition, and refinement heads to
learn the frame-level authenticity and predict whether each pair
of adjacent frames corresponds to the manipulation boundary.

3.1. Pretrained Self-Supervised Front-End
We adopt a pretrained self-supervised speech model,
Wav2Vec2-based XLS-R-300M [32], as the front-end to
process raw speech. As the SSL front-end provides repre-
sentations learned from large-scale unlabeled speech data, it
encodes richer acoustic and speaking patterns than traditional
LFCC- or MFCC-based preprocessing. We extract frame-level
features at a 20 ms resolution using the SSL front-end model.
The extracted self-supervised speech features are denoted as
h= (h 1,h 2,...,h T )∈R T×D , whereTandDdenote the
number of frames and feature dimensions, respectively.
3.2. Multi-Scale GRU
The proposed MS-GRU is designed to capture both the local-
ized variations induced by partial manipulations and the global
temporal dynamics. To this end, the input sequence is processed
through two pathways: (i) a 1D convolutional branch that ob-
serves the signal at multiple temporal scales and (ii) a bidirec-
tional GRU (Bi-GRU) branch that models the utterance-level
sequential context. The resulting representations are then dy-
namically fused at each frame using the GEGLU gating mech-
anism [33]. Before feeding the frame embeddings into the MS-
GRU, we apply a linear projection to the frame embeddings to
map them into the model dimensiondmodel. All subsequent tem-
poral modeling is performed in an identical dimensional space.

4.1. Experimental Setting
4.1.1. Datasets

duct experiments on three partial audio deepfake datasets: Par-
tialSpoof Dataset (PSD) [7, 8], PartialEdit-E1, and PartialEdit-
