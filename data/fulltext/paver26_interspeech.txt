Acoustic correlates of voice quality settings: variation within and between 
individual speakers 
Alice Paver and Kirsty McDougall 
University of Cambridge 
aegp2|kem37@cam.ac.uk 
 
Abstract 
Since perceptual assessments of v oice quality (VQ) are 
sometimes criticised as subjective, there is a tendency for 
acoustic measures to be treated as objective correlates of 
articulatory settings. Most acoustic studies generalise across 
speakers, despite VQ being highly speaker-specific; apparent 
effects may therefore reflect inter-speaker variation. Acoustic 
correlates of supralaryngeal VQ also remain underexplored. 
This study models group-level effects and speaker × quality 
interactions across multiple measures (CPP, spectral tilt, HNR, 
long-term formants) for diverse laryngeal and supralaryngeal 
settings. 
Group results showed increased spectral tilt measures for both 
breathy and nasal voice,  as well as increased formant 
frequencies for breathy VQ. However, significant variation was 
found for individual speakers . These findings challenge 
assumptions concerning acoustic correlates of VQ and have 
implications for forensic phonetics and related fields. 
Index Terms: voice quality, acoustic phonetics, forensic 
phonetics, individual variation  
1. Introduction 
1.1. Voice quality 
Voice quality (VQ), also described as ‘the characteristic 
auditory colouring of an individual speaker’s voice’ [1], refers 
to the habitual articulatory settings adopted by a given speaker. 
Typically, VQ settings are described in relation to a 
hypothetical ‘modal’ or ‘neutral’ voice, with frictionless 
periodic regular voicing and a neutral and uniform cross-
sectional area of the vocal tract [1, 2, 3, 4]. Settings are then 
defined as deviations from this neutral vocal tract configuration. 
Some examples include breathy or creaky phonation, 
palatalised or fronted tongue position, rounded lips or 
raised/lowered larynx. Perceptual assessment schemes such as 
the Vocal Profile Analysis (VPA) protocol [1, 2, 5] are widely 
used, particularly in the UK. These schemes are used by experts 
to assess multiple possible vocal settings by rating the degree to 
which the speaker deviates from a hypothetical neutral on a 
scale from 1-3 [6]. Due to the large number of possible settings, 
this type of analysis can be time-consuming, particularly when 
multiple recordings are involved, as with forensic speaker 
comparison cases. The subjectivity of such perceptual rating 
schemes has motivated interest in acoustic measures often 
assumed to be more objective. Phonetic research has thus often 
operationalised VQ settings through acoustic measures.  
1.2. Acoustic correlates of VQ 
To date, studies report a range of acoustic measures typically 
extracted for VQ analysis. Firstly, there are measures of spectral 
tilt (H1-A1*, H1-A2*, H1-A3*, H1-H2*, H2-H4*), which 
indicate the distribution of energy across the frequency range 
[7, 8, 9]. The first three of these (H1-A1/A2/A3*) compare the 
amplitude of the first harmonic with the amplitude of the 
spectral peak closest to the first, second and third formants 
respectively. These measures therefore reflect overall spectral 
balance between low-frequency harmonic energy and higher-
frequency energy shaped by the vocal tract. H1-H2* reflects the 
amplitude difference between the first and second harmonics 
and is commonly interpreted as a local measure of spectral tilt 
in the low frequency region. Correspondingly, H2-H4* reflects 
the amplitude difference between the second and fourth 
harmonics, measuring spectral tilt across a slightly higher and 
broader frequency region than H1-H2*. Spectral tilt measures 
are frequently used to explore breathy voice, as a steeper 
degradation of energy in the spectrum, i.e. a greater spectral tilt 
value, is assumed to reflect the more gradual glottal closure and 
greater airflow through the glottis it induces [3, 4]. Studies have 
also noted the acoustic similarity between breathy and nasal VQ 
as evidenced in increased spectral tilt values [10, 11]. 
Secondly, studies exploring VQ often measure noise-
related variables such as cepstral peak prominence (CPP) and 
harmonics-to-noise ratio (HNR; measured across frequency 
bandwidths of: 0-500Hz, HNR05; 0-1500Hz, HNR15; 0-
2500Hz, HNR25; and 0-3500Hz, HNR35, respectively). CPP 
quantifies the amplitude of the dominant cepstral peak relative 
to the surrounding cepstral energy. Strongly periodic signals, 
producing harmonics which are clearly defined and regularly 
spaced, typically have a higher CPP value, while aperiodic 
signals, such as those induced by breathy, creaky, or noisy 
phonation, tend to have a lower CPP value. Similarly, HNR is 
a measure of the ratio of harmonic (periodic) energy to noise 
(aperiodic) energy; a less clear and periodic signal with more 
noise, such as with breathy or creaky VQ, normally produces 
lower HNR values than modal voice [3].  
Some studies have noted a lack of consistency and 
transparency in the methods used to extract acoustic measures 
of VQ [9, 12]. In fact, due to the number of subjective decisions 
that must be made during the acoustic measurement process 
(e.g., software or algorithm used, maximum frequency in Hz, 
number of formants), acoustic measures are arguably no t 
always more objective than any other method involving human 
decision making. In addition, the lack of baseline values for 
what constitutes a typical ‘modal’ value for any of these 
acoustic measures makes it difficult to judge what constitutes 
normal or abnormal values to a meaningful degree. 
Furthermore, previous research has often been conducted only 
on steady-state vowels, limiting the applicability of findings to 
continuous speech [9, 12]. 
Most research has focussed on correlates of laryngeal and 
phonatory settings such as breathy or creaky voice; a glance at 
the VPA scheme illustrates the wealth of articulatory settings 
for which reliable acoustic correlates have not yet been 
established. Seminal work in [4] explored the acoustic 
correlates of VQ settings for two speakers and discussed how 
long-term spectral measures, often characteristic of laryngeal 
VQ settings, vary little when supralaryngeal VQ is modified. 
This work identified that formant frequency values can instead 
be good discriminators of supralaryngeal VQ. A recent paper 
investigating acoustic correlates of controlled voice quality 
modifications in 15 Czech speakers was one of the first to 
explore supralaryngeal as well as laryngeal settings across 
multiple speakers [13]. When compared with habitual voice, 
both lip-rounding and pharyngealization yielded higher F2 
frequency, while palatalisation showed lower F2 frequency, and 
open jaw correlated with higher F1 frequency. Changes in 
formant values relative to the neutral baseline are therefore

1.3. Speaker-specificity of VQ in forensic phonetics 
Thanks to the great number of possible articulatory setting 
combinations, as well as the degrees to which they may deviate 
from ‘neutral’, VQ is highly speaker-specific. VQ analysis has 
multiple applications, such as in sociophonetics [14, 15], speech 
pathology [16], and forensic phonetics [17] for which, in 
particular, this speaker-specificity is pertinent [6, 17]. Experts 
are typically tasked with characterising speakers appearing in 
multiple audio samples, and compare the samples to assess the 
likelihood that they were produced by the same speaker. Such 
experts must consider both between - and within-speaker 
variation in VQ. In conducting VQ analysis, forensic 
phoneticians, as well as other experts, may use acoustic 
measures to help characterise a speaker’s voice [18]. 
Most research on acoustic correlates of voice quality 
implicitly assumes that specific measures increase or decrease 
in systematic ways under particular VQ settings, often relative 
to a hypothetical ‘modal’ voice. However, in reality this 
‘modal’ voice is not an objective and consistent acoustic 
reference point across all speakers but instead is a collection of 
speaker-specific habitual configurations. In the present study, 
the alternative term ‘default’ voice is therefore used to describe 
a speaker’s baseline habitual VQ.  
Although studies have reported acoustic correlates of 
laryngeal settings [7, 8, 9], these correlates are typically 
calculated across speakers. Consequently, apparent VQ effects 
may partly reflect inter -speaker differences [19, 20], as 
observed in more forensically-motivated research [7, 13, 15, 
21]. Indeed, despite some findings at the group level which 
were in line with previous research, the authors of [13] noted 
the inconsistency of acoustic shifts between individual 
speakers. Further research into the reliability of acoustic 
correlates of VQ is therefore sorely needed. Furthermore, few 
studies have investigated variation at the individual level, i.e. 
acoustic correlates of VQ when the same speaker adopts 
different VQ settings in different recordings. This type of 
research gives vital insight into within-speaker variation, a key 
concern in forensic phonetics for which analysts are frequently 
required to compare recordings of allegedly the same speaker 
made on different occasions. 
1.4. Research questions 
This study addresses two central research questions: 1) Is there 
evidence for reliable acoustic correlates of some supralaryngeal 
VQ settings? 2) Are acoustic correlates of VQ robust across 
speakers, or are they speaker-contingent? By using stimuli with 
a range of VQ guises produced by the same speakers, in a 
similar way to [13] but with speakers of British English, this 
study attempts to isolate VQ-related acoustic change at the 
group level from broader speaker-specific variation. 
2. Methods 
2.1. Materials 
Recordings were taken from the Person-Specific Automatic 
Speaker Recognition (PASR) dataset [22], featuring recordings 
of native British English (BE)-speaking phoneticians. After 
evaluating the speakers in the dataset, a subset of four male 
speakers (PASR nos. 1, 3, 4, 6) was selected for analysis here 
owing to inherent differences in male and female voices when 
it comes to acoustic correlates of VQ [23]. Two additional male 
speakers were rejected; the first had a salient regional accent 
very different from the other speakers, and the second did not 
produce a sufficient audible perceptual change in some key VQ 
settings. Speakers had been recorded reading the Rainbow 
passage in multiple guises, including various VQ settings, over 
three separate sessions, with three repetitions of each setting in 
each session. Six of the VQ guises (comprising a range of 
laryngeal and supralaryngeal settings) were chosen: default 
(DEF), breathy (BRT), fronted tongue body (FTB), nasal 
(NAS), denasal (DEN), and lowered larynx (LLX). This 
provided 54 recordings per speaker (n=216), each around 30s 
in duration. 
2.2. Acoustic analysis 
In preparation for acoustic analysis, transcripts of the audio 
samples were force-aligned and transcribed at the level of the 
phoneme using the Montreal Forced Aligner [24]. Acoustic 
measures were extracted from vocalic segments only using 
VoiceSauce [25] in MATLAB [26]. A pre-test established that 
optimal settings for long-term formant (LTF) frequency 
measurements were 5kHz max, 5 formants, using Praat’s 
formant tracking algorithm. F0-based measures (i.e. harmonic 
frequency measures) were also extracted using Praat, with a 
minimum of 40Hz and a maximum of 200Hz  [23]. The 
following measures were extracted for analysis: CPP, H1*-
A1*, H1-A2*, H1-A3*, H1*-H2*, H2-H4*, HNR05, HNR15, 
HNR25, HNR35, and LTF frequencies F1, F2, F3 and F4.  
2.3. Statistical analysis 
All statistical analysis was performed in RStudio [27]. To 
prepare for data analysis, average values for each speaker ´ 
setting ´ segment were calculated across sessions and 
repetitions. Values were also z-score normalised prior to fitting 
the model to allow acoustic measures with different 
measurement scales (e.g. LTF in Hz, HNR in dB), to be 
compared with one another. A linear mixed-effects regression 
model was fitted to the data using the lme4 package [28], with 
a three-way interaction between VQ setting, acoustic measure, 
and speaker, and a random intercept of segment. Estimated 
marginal means were subsequently calculated for pairwise 
comparison analysis using the package emmeans [29]. Data 
visualisation was conducted using the package ggplot2 [30]. 
Due to the nature of the research question, analysis of the results 
focuses on comparing default voice with non-modal VQ 
settings (i.e. the other VQ settings selected for this experiment).  
3. Results 
3.1. Group results 
Figure 1 shows boxplots illustrating the distribution of values 
for each VQ setting, separated into panels for each acoustic 
measure extracted. Each box in the boxplots represents values 
across all four speakers. At the group level, the statistical 
analysis revealed a significant interaction effect between 
measure and VQ setting (F(65, 9359) = 24.7, p < .001). A 
number of acoustic measures were significantly correlated with 
non-modal VQ settings, including several spectral tilt measures 
and some long-term formant frequencies. As expected, BRT 
resulted in significantly higher spectral tilt values than DEF [7, 
8, 23]. However, H2-H4* was lower in BRT than DEF VQ. 
NAS VQ was associated with significantly higher values than 
DEF VQ for all spectral tilt measures. Noise-related measures 
(CPP, HNR) were all significantly lower for BRT than DEF. 
Some new correlations appeared for supralaryngeal VQ 
settings. DEN resulted in lower values for H1-A1* and H2-
H4*. In addition, LLX VQ was also associated with 
significantly lower HNR05 values. Finally in terms of LTF 
values, BRT VQ raised values for all of F1-F4, while nasal 
voice had higher F2-F4. F4 was in fact raised for all non-modal 
VQ settings, except FTB.

4. Discussion 
The findings from the present study demonstrate that while 
several acoustic measures may differentiate VQ settings at the 
group level, these effects are not uniformly realised across all 
speakers in the group. Additionally, some measures were found 
to be indicators of VQ modifications only for individual 
speakers. This tension between group-level reliability and 
individual-level variability has important implications for both 
phonetic theory and forensic speech science.  
At both group and individual levels, some robust trends 
were observed. Breathy and nasal VQ were associated with an 
increase in spectral tilt measures extracted in this study. These 
findings are in line with previous research. The present study 
also found some correlations between acoustic measures and 
VQ settings at the group level which have not been observed 
previously. Denasal VQ is the direct articulatory antithesis of 
nasal VQ; correspondingly, the present study showed a trend 
for DEN VQ in the opposite direction to NAS VQ in H1-A1* 
and H2-H4*. With such a small number of speakers available 
for this study, it is not possible to speculate as to the reliability 
of these measures for measuring denasality; future research 
should aim to explore the generalisability of these findings.  
A further surprising group finding was that breathy voice 
elicited higher LTF frequency values for F2, F3 and F4. This 
was unexpected, as BRT is laryngeally defined and LTF values 
are expected to reflect supralaryngeal settings. Firstly, this 
increase could be due to artefacts of acoustic measurement. The 
accuracy of LPC-based formant tracking methods may be  
negatively impacted by a lack of clearer formant structure 
induced by reduced relative amplitude of higher frequencies as 
well as increased noisy energy in the signal. BRT VQ may also 
result in a change in f0, which could affect estimation of 
formant frequencies. Secondly, formant shifts may reflect co-
occurring supralaryngeal adjustments which speakers adopt

when producing BRT VQ. If speakers tend to adopt a slightly

compensatory articulatory behaviour, as with nasalisation of 
front vowels [31], perhaps to maintain intensity or clarity, this 
may explain a shift in LTF values. 
This explanation highlights a complication with acoustic 
analysis of VQ concerning its multi-componential nature. 
Labels such as “breathy” or “ nasal” may be analytically 
separable, but in natural speech they may co-occur or partially 
overlap. Acoustic measures, in turn, are sensitive to multiple 
articulatory and phonatory influences. For example, the present 
group-level results indicate that both breathy and nasal VQ are 
associated with higher H1–A1*, while denasal VQ elicits lower 
H1-A1*. If a speaker’s habitual voice involved both BRT and 
DEN settings, the net acoustic outcome would depend on the 
relative magnitude of these competing influences . This 
illustrates that acoustic measures do not map transparently onto 
single articulatory adjustments. The absence of an expected 
acoustic change therefore does not necessarily imply the 
absence of a change in VQ.  For forensic analysis, this 
consideration of multidimensionality is particularly important. 
Interpretation must be multidimensional and speaker-relative 
rather than based on isolated variables. 
 Relational descriptions of the type relied upon in acoustic 
analyses of VQ presuppose the existence of a stable reference 
point against which deviation can be measured. In practice, this 
is usually taken to be ‘modal’ voice, yet this is not an objective 
acoustic standard but instead speaker-specific habitual 
configuration. This creates a methodological and interpretive 
problem. If two speakers produce nasal VQ, one may exhibit 
higher H1–A1* values than the other due to anatomical 
differences, habitual glottal configuration, or articulatory 
setting. Without independent knowledge of each speaker’s 
baseline, it is impossible to determine whether a given H1–A1* 
value represents “breathiness”, a habitual characteristic, or 
simply inter-speaker variation. In other words, as these results 
show, acoustic measures do not encode absolute articulatory 
states. They index positions within a speaker-specific acoustic 
space. The present findings, particularly the significant speaker 
× quality interactions, provide empirical support for this 
baseline problem. They demonstrate that acoustic correlates of 
VQ are not invariant shifts along fixed dimensions, but speaker-
contingent adjustments relative to an individual’s baseline.  
The lack of consistency in how acoustic shifts apply across 
speakers when adopting VQ settings may not just reflect a 
difference in habitual VQ (i.e. DEF). Speakers may employ 
varying articulatory techniques to achieve a similar perceptual 
target, resulting in different acoustic shifts. Figure 2 illustrates 
that some speakers show idiosyncrasies in the acoustic profiles 
associated with VQ changes; speaker 6 shows upward shifts in 
F4 for all non-modal VQs, while speaker 4 has raised F3 and 
F4 in LLX. Speaker 3 is the only one not to show any 
relationship between LTF values and supralaryngeal VQ 
settings, while speaker 4 is the only one for whom HNR05 is 
associated with supralaryngeal VQ. Additionally, some group 
findings appear to be driven largely by a single speaker; for 
example, the increase in F2 and F4 associated with NAS, which 
was only significant for speaker 6. Meanwhile, speaker 1 is the 
only one with raised F1 in BRT, and speaker 3 is alone in having 
raised HNR in BRT. The speaker-specificity of acoustic 
correlates is particularly relevant for forensic phoneticians 
conducting VQ analysis. In the case of a speaker deliberately 
manipulating their VQ for the purposes of voice disguise, 
caution must be taken when conducting acoustic analysis.
