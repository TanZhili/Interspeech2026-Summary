Leveraging Discriminative Capabilities of Self-Supervised Neural Audio
Fingerprinting for Efficient Speech Data Annotation
Kemal Altwlkany ID 1,2, Elmedin Selmanovic ID 2
1 Infobip
2 Faculty of Science, University of Sarajevo, Bosnia and Herzegovina

Abstract
Self-supervised neural audio fingerprinting methods have
achieved great results in music retrieval systems. We show
how such methods can be applied to efficiently organize speech
data annotation tasks. First, they can be used to remove du-
plicates, which we successfully apply to industry-specific data,
resulting in halving the number of data samples that need to
be annotated. Second, we show that despite being trained only
on music, the embeddings produced by neural fingerprinting
methods are better at capturing acoustical properties of speech
than speech-specific models, such as WavLM. We leverage this
property of the embeddings and show how to subsample large
datasets while preserving dataset diversity and avoiding biases
towards overrepresented data points, using a technique known
as farthest point sampling. This allows annotators to focus their
already limited and valuable time on diverse data samples while
avoiding duplicate and similar samples.
Index Terms: speech, data annotation, audio fingerprinting,
self-supervised learning, speech embeddings
1. Introduction
Data quality and quantity are crucial for the success of modern
AI models, but as neural architectures advance, so does their
hunger for more data. Studies show that 96% of enterprises
encounter data challenges, including data quality and labeling in
AI projects, with data scientists spending twice as much time on
data loading, cleaning, and visualization than on model training,
selection, and deployment [1].
A 2020 paper claimed that 95% of industry-deployed ma-
chine learning models were trained on supervised data [2],
but despite this, companies reported difficulties in the early
stages of data collection and preprocessing [3]. Crowdsourc-
ing emerged as an inexpensive and scalable way of increasing
the amount of data, but has its own limitations: the data is often
lacking in terms of quality, or it may not be shared with external
annotators due to privacy and legal constraints [2, 4, 5].
Data challenges are not limited to a certain modality and are
common topics in text [6, 7], image [8], and speech [9] process-
ing. Resolving issues regarding data privacy and data quality
have inspired frameworks such as confident learning [10] and
federated learning [11], as well as data annotation software such
as EvalAI [12] or Label Studio [13] which solve commonly en-
countered issues such as serving and storing the data, compar-
ing data, multi-user access, etc.
1.1. Industry specialized speech datasets
Despite the improvements, challenges remain, primarily in in-
dustry applications. Industries often require data that is specific
for their domain of expertise, which is not easily available on-
line.
Regarding speech data specifically, most public data is ori-
ented towards automatic speech recognition. Such data is not al-
ways applicable to other speech tasks, e.g. in telephony, where
speech is the primary modality of data, many tasks require data
that can only be obtained by recording phone calls, for example
detecting voicemails and answering machines [14, 15, 16] or
detecting spoofed and fraudulent calls [17, 18, 19, 20, 21, 22].
This is obviously a very sensitive topic from a privacy perspec-
tive: prior to recording, user consent must be obtained. Fur-
thermore, user data may not be shared with external annotators,
which heavily impacts the scale at which data can be annotated.
Subject to local laws and regulations, data may sometimes be
kept only for short periods of time, which means that the en-
tire data processing and model training must be completed in a
short time span, before the data is permanently deleted. Sim-
ilarly, some regulations prohibit using the data of one user to
train models that would be facilitated for other users.
1.2. Paper contributions
We focus on cases where organizations are required to collect
and annotate their own data. This implies that the data cannot be
crowdsourced, is not sufficiently available online, and annotator
time is expensive and of limited availability. We contribute by
proposing two methods that use audio fingerprinting to make
the process of speech data annotation more efficient.
The first issue we address is optimizing annotator time and
avoiding redundant annotation. We show that audio fingerprint-
ing can be used to remove duplicate entries from datasets, ensur-
ing that a single annotator will not spend time labeling identical
data. As we show from a real industry dataset, duplicate data
can account for even 50% of the dataset. This technique can be
applied to datasets where duplicated data may naturally occur,
such as voicemails which are often shared across users of the
same telecommunication service provider.
Second, we wish to optimize data diversity and representa-
tiveness. If an annotator can only annotate a small percentage
of the data, e.g. 1000 samples from a dataset of50 000samples,
how should those 1000 samples be selected? We show that self-
supervised neural audio fingerprinting models excel in captur-
ing the acoustical properties of speech, producing embeddings
which cluster around similar audio, better than speech-specific
models, such as WavLM [23]. By strategically sampling the
data from the embedding space produced by audio fingerprint-
ing methods, we can maximize data diversity, meaning that an-
notators with limited time availability will be shown a more di-
verse set of samples, compared to what they would have been
shown by randomly sampling directly from the data.

2. Neural Audio Fingerprinting
Audio fingerprinting methods specialize in identifying audio
even in cases of severe audio distortions, such as background
noise, reverb, non-linear playback rate, and other. Due to their
high applicability and commercial success, these models were
optimized for music retrieval and music identification applica-
tions [24, 25, 26, 27]. Traditionally, methods relied on design-
ing features which provide a good balance between robustness
to audio distortions and sensitivity between different audio files.
In the past decade, state-of-the-art results have been
achieved using self-supervised neural audio fingerprinting tech-
niques, that are trained using a contrastive learning framework
[28]. These neural models rely on convolutional [29, 30], graph
[31], transformer [32], or conformer [33] encoders to produce
sufficiently unique and robust embeddings for audio files.
We use the pre-trained conformer (PTC) model [33], as it
is a novel approach that achieves good results in robustness to
both noise and temporal distortions. The model is lightweight
(26.2M parameters), has the lowest inference latency of all ap-
proaches with comparative accuracy, and provides a practical
advantage as it is easy to use and comes with a checkpoint
model.
3. Removing duplicates
Through collaboration with our industry partner Infobip, we ob-
tained a dataset of50 000voicemails, which we further refer
to as the voicemails dataset. These audio files are proprietary
and are used for voicemail and answering machine detection, a
commonly encountered problem in telephony [14, 15, 16]. The
data is very sensitive from a privacy perspective, it has been
anonymized, with metadata completely omitted. In practice,
the data has very restricted access, with only a few employees
being granted permission to label/annotate the data, with prior
user consent. Given these conditions, data annotation cannot
be scaled easily, and since data scientists with granted access
are often senior and reputable employees, using their time as
annotators is very expensive.

3.1. Voicemails
Our first suggestion is using audio fingerprinting to identify and
remove duplicate entries. Given the nature of the dataset, this
is a common occurrence: subscribers of the same telecommu-
nication provider are likely to use the same voicemail service,
so repeated entries are common. Using PTC [33], we identified
that nearly half of the recordings (24 983) are duplicates of at
least one other recording. This is illustrated in Figure 1, where
we plot the cumulative number of occurrences of the top 1000
files. The most common file is repeated 562 times in the dataset,
whereas the top 5 files are repeated a total of 1693 times, indi-
cating that the data follows a power-law (Pareto-like) distribu-
tion.
By simply excluding the duplicates, we obtain a substan-
tially lower dataset, as the annotators can now focus on25 017
recordings, instead of50 000, without losing any information at
all.
3.2. Robocalls
We repeated the process on a publicly available dataset of robo-
calls [34]. The dataset contains 1432 robocalls, which were col-
lected and manually annotated by the US Federal Trade Com-
mission. PTC revealed that only 504 of these calls are true
uniques, while 928 of them are near-duplicates (64.8%). This
is expected, as robocalls contain very slight alterations and are
automated calls, with perpetrators commonly using voice syn-
thesis techniques to generate fraudulent content [20, 21, 22].
3.3. Performance on a synthetic, labeled dataset
The issue with the voicemails and robocalls datasets is that we
have no way of assessing the accuracy of the deduplication pro-
cess without annotating all the files (and additionally burdening
the annotators to identify duplicates). We therefore construct a
simple, synthetic duplicated dataset to verify our approach.
We use the VCTK speech corpus [35] and sample data
from it using the same power-law distribution observed from
the voicemails dataset: we select a random file from the VCTK
corpus and createN i replicas of it, whereN i is the number of
replicas of thei-th file from the voicemails dataset. In total,
we create11 420replicas using the first1000files and add an
additional 1000 unique files. In summary, we have a total of
12 420files of which2000are unique. Note that the replicas
are not identical copies, we slightly augment them with back-
ground noise and transcode them using a speech codec to verify
the robustness of the process, and to better simulate the realistic
setting of the voicemails dataset.
Our results indicate that the fingerprinting process works
well: it identified 2158 files as being unique, which is 7.9%
more than the true number of duplicates (2000). Furthermore,
PTC identified that the top 5 files were repeated a total of 1613
times, which is 4.73% less than the actual number of top 5 repli-
cas (1693). Good results are to no surprise since PTC is trained
to identify the same audio, even in non-ideal conditions.

4. Capturing acoustical properties
In the previous section, we showed that removing duplicates can
substantially reduce the number of data instances that need to be
annotated. Despite a two-fold reduction, annotating25 017data
samples is still a cumbersome task. Furthermore, our improve-
ment is only applicable to data sources that naturally contain
repeated entries, such as voicemails or robocalls.
Imagine that an annotator can only annotate1000samples
from a collection of50 000files. How do we select which files
the annotator should process? For example, the most occurring
file in the voicemail dataset is repeated 562 times, compared to
a non-duplicated file that appears just 1 time, which means ran-
dom subsampling would favour the repeated file. This process
can be modeled as sampling from a hypergeometric distribu-
tion with a total population sizeN= 50 000, a sample size
n= 1000, and the number of times a file occurs isK= 562
for the repeated file andK= 1for the unique file. Thus, the
expected number of occurrencesE[X 562]andE[X 1]for these
files within a 1000-sized sample is:
E[X] =n· K
N, n= 1000(1)
which yields:
E[X562] = 11.24(2)
E[X1] = 0.02(3)
This implies that on average, the most repeated file will
comprise 11.24 out of 1000 files in the subsample, while any
particular unique file is≈500 times less likely to be included
in the subsample at all. From an annotator’s perspective, this is
troubling as random subsamples would contain several copies
of highly repeated files, while not containing any examples of
rarely occurring files.
Using a t-SNE plot [36], we can visualize the differences in
how this affects the acoustic diversity of the 1000-sized sample.
If we compute the embeddings using PTC [33] of audio that
is randomly sampled from the dataset, the embeddings cluster
in dense regions, since the dataset contains files which are re-
peated. This is shown in Figure 2a. Even though such a sub-
sample proportionally resembles the original dataset, we would
ideally like to have a more diverse subsample, so that the anno-
tator can see as many distinct samples as possible.
4.1. Strategic sampling
We suggest applying a strategic sampling technique. First,
we compute the embeddings of each file using PTC [33].
Now, instead of drawing a random subsample from the original
dataset, we sample by maximizing the differences in the embed-
dings space using a technique from computer vision known as
farthest-point sampling [37]. The idea is to start from a random
data point in the embedding space and select the next embed-
ding point by maximizing its distance from the rest of the set.
This results in well-distributed data over the embedding space,
as seen in Figure 2b. The data points are spread out over the em-
bedding space, and are not clustered around highly represented
samples.
4.2. Does diversity in the embedding space correlate to
acoustically diverse datasets?
In the preceding subsection we managed to obtain diverse and
spatially distant embeddings, but does this translate to more di-
verse audio in the subsample? More specifically, are the embed-
dings generated by PTC [33] capable of capturing the acoustical
properties of audio? If we show that the embeddings really do
capture the acoustical properties of speech, then diversifying the
subsample in the embedding space will correlate to diversifying
the audio subsample in terms of acoustical properties, regard-
less of whether the dataset contains duplicates or not.
We focus on three properties which impact the acoustical
characteristics of speech: the gender of the speaker, the accent
of the speaker, and the unique voice of the speaker. To showcase
this, we once more use the publicly available VCTK speech cor-
pus [35]. We extract embeddings from the VCTK corpus using
PTC [33], and as a baseline, we use a state-of-the-art speech
embedding model, WavLM [23].
t-SNE plots of the embeddings produced by PTC and
WavLM are shown in Figure 3. Clearly, the embeddings pro-
duced by PTC are better separated and seem to distinguish bet-
ter between the characteristics of the speaker’s voice. This is
especially prominent for the speaker and gender images, as the
embeddings form clusters per gender and per speaker. However,
visualizations are not sufficient, so we quantify the results.

4.3.1. Gender
The embeddings produced by both PTC [33] and WavLM [23]
encode the information about speaker gender well, which is sup-
ported by the accuracy, precision, recall, and F1-score being
above 99% for both models. We performed McNemar’s test to
assess differences in classification errors between the two mod-
els. The test showed a significant difference between the per-
formance of the models (χ2(1) = 21, p=.000082). An odds
ratio of 2.67 suggest that PTC is 2.67 times more likely to be
correct in cases when the models disagree. The associated ef-
fect size is medium to borderline large (Cohen’sg= 0.2273).
Although the absolute margins are small (both models at 99%+
accuracy), PTC has a statistically significant strong advantage
and provides better results in all categories.
4.3.2. Accent
Regarding accent, the situation is not unanimous. WavLM has a
higher average accuracy, but PTC has better average precision,
recall, and F1-score. McNemar’s test is statistically significant
(χ2(1) = 1258, p≪1e −10) with a small effect size (Cohen’s
g= 0.0556). An odds ratio of 0.8 suggests that, in cases of
disagreement, WavLM is 1.25 times more likely to be correct.
4.3.3. Speaker
PTC achieves significantly higher values of accuracy, precision,
recall, and F1-score compared to WavLM. This is supported by
a statistically significant McNemar’s test (χ 2(1) = 176, p≪
1e−10) and a strong effect size (Cohen’sg= 0.3639). An
odds ratio of 6.35 suggests that in cases of disagreement, PTC
is more than 6 times as likely to be correct. Overall, even though
the embeddings produced by WavLM capture information about
the speaker’s voice, the embeddings produced by PTC encode
significantly more speaker information.
4.3.4. Discussion
We argue that the obtained results make sense: WavLM excels
at automatic speech recognition, where the goal is to accurately
transcribe the audio, regardless of the speaker’s voice. Con-
versely, PTC is trained with the goal of uniquely identifying au-
dio, specifically music. The acoustical properties are thus more
important and better represented in the embeddings.
5. Conclusion
We provided two practical approaches how audio fingerprint-
ing techniques, trained to uniquely discern between music, can
be used to optimize the expensive task of speech data annota-
tion. The first approach is applicable to any dataset in which
duplicate content may naturally occur. We identified duplicate
content on a real industry dataset from telephony, resulting in
50% less data samples, without losing any information. Similar
results were obtained on the publicly available VCTK dataset.
Our second approach is more broad and can be applied
regardless of the dataset: we showed that employing farthest
point sampling in the embedding space of neural fingerprints
can ensure data diversity when creating small subsamples of
the original dataset. The results were confirmed on the VCTK
dataset, where we showed that embeddings generated by PTC, a
self-supervised neural audio fingerprinting method, are better at
preserving acoustical properties of speech than speech-specific
models such as WavLM. This technique enables us to maximize
annotator time, ensuring that the annotator will see a more di-
verse set of samples, even when analyzing a small subsample of
the original dataset.
For future work we plan to extensively test our approach
on more data and different speech tasks, as well as take into
consideration other neural fingerprinting approaches.
