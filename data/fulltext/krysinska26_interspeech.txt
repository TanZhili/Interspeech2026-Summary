Abstract
Connectionist Temporal Classification (CTC) is widely used for
phoneme recognition due to its alignment-free training objec-
tive, but it often produces highly peaky posterior distributions
dominated by the blank token. This is particularly challenging
during early training, when phonemes compete against strong
blank token dominance and unstable alignment dynamics.
We propose Transitional Objective Learning (TOL), a
curriculum-learning framework that mitigates this issue by dy-
namically transitioning between training objectives of increas-
ing granularity. Instead of optimizing a fine-grained phoneme
objective throughout training, TOL begins with coarse-grained
phonetic objectives that provide stable learning signals and
gradually shifts toward full phoneme-level supervision.
Experiments on English, French, and Polish show that TOL
consistently reduces Phoneme Error Rate (PER), accelerates
convergence, and improves training stability compared to con-
ventional CTC fine-tuning.
Index Terms: CTC, phoneme recognition, curriculum learning,
hierarchical objectives
1. Introduction
CTC [1] has become a foundational approach in
sequence-to-sequence problems, particularly in Automatic
Speech Recognition (ASR). A key advantage of CTC is that
it removes the requirement for frame-level annotations by
marginalizing over latent alignments during training. As a
result, models can be trained on datasets that lack alignment
annotations, which is particularly beneficial for tasks such as
phoneme recognition, where annotation is time-consuming and
requires specialized expertise. Despite these advantages, CTC
has one major downside – highly peaky posterior distributions,
in which the blank symbol dominates the majority of time
frames while non-blank labels are emitted sparsely [2, 3, 4]. To
allow the model to align input and output sequences of differing
lengths, we need to allow the model to output ”nothing”, at
certain time steps. Thus, CTC extends the vocabulary with a
special blank tokenϵ[5, 6]. However, during the initial stages
of training, the model tends to predict only the blank token.
This behavior arises because in CTC the blank token is present
between each character, so the model is more likely to assign a
higher posterior probability to the blank token than any other
because that will decrease the loss the most. This leads to
an early local minimum where predicting blanks exclusively
results in the best loss decrease and flat loss landscape. As a
consequence, gradient magnitudes remain small, the model
receives weak training signals for actual phoneme distinctions
during these initial stages, and learning meaningful label
alignments is delayed [2]. This phenomenon is sometimes
referred to as the ”suppression phase” [3]. The weak training
signal in this phase lasts for a significant time of training with
very little improvement in loss and no improvement in model
output. As training progresses, the posterior probability for
blank token is close to one, the model enters the ”peaking
phase” [3], where it starts to assign higher probabilities to
actual target labels at specific time steps, resulting in the
characteristic ”peaky” behavior of CTC models. This transition
is crucial for the model to learn accurate alignments and make
meaningful predictions [4]. A natural way to address the
optimization difficulties of ”suppression phase” is to reconsider
how learning signals are presented to the model over time and
what can be done to diminish blank token dominance.
In this work, we introduce Transitional Objective Learning
(TOL), a novel Curriculum Learning framework that exploits
dynamic transitions between multiple training objectives of in-
creasing granularity. TOL employs a set of distinct loss ob-
jectives, each capturing different acoustic features of phonemes
(e.g. voicing, spectrum of the burst, formant transitions). Train-
ing begins with coarse-grained objectives that help eliminate
the blank token dominance in the early behavior of CTC train-
ing and provide more stable learning signals. As training pro-
gresses, the emphasis gradually shifts toward finer-grained ob-
jectives, ultimately converging on full phoneme-level recogni-
tion.
2. Curriculum Learning
Curriculum Learning (CL) offers a principled framework for
structuring the learning process by exposing the model to sim-
pler concepts before progressively introducing more complex
ones [7]. This concept was formally introduced and motivated
by Bengio et al. [8], who drew a compelling analogy to the
pedagogical principles observed in human and animal learning,
where structured curricula significantly facilitate knowledge ac-
quisition. The hypothesis is that initiating the training process
with simpler concepts and progressively increasing the diffi-
culty could act as a continuation method in optimization, poten-
tially guiding the learning process towards better local minima
and improving convergence speed [9, 10].
3. Transitional Objective Learning
Inspired by CL methods applications in speech [11, 12, 13],
we present Transitional Objective Learning. TOL utilizes a set
of distinct loss functions concurrently during training. Each
of these loss functions is designed to capture a learning objec-
tive at a different level of abstraction. Phonemes can be classi-
fied into broad groups characterized with different acoustic fea-
tures (e.g., distinguishing periodic from aperiodic sounds [14],

The key novelty of TOL lies in the dynamic transition be-

tially, loss functions corresponding to more general or simpler
aspects of the task (representing, for instance, broad acoustic
categories) dominate the learning signal, encouraging the model
to first capture foundational patterns and weaken the blank to-
ken dominance. As training progresses, the influence of these
initial objectives gradually diminishes, while the contribution
from loss functions representing the more specific, fine-grained
target task (such as individual phonemes) steadily increases and
eventually takes precedence.

4. Related work
Hierarchical multi-task learning approaches were proposed to
explicitly exploit the structure of speech. Sanabria and Metze
[16] introduced a hierarchical multitask learning framework in
which multiple CTC losses at various granularities (from char-
acters up to word-level units) encourage the model to form use-
ful intermediate representations of speech. The architecture of
the model consists of a shared encoder and auxiliary compo-
nents providing task-specific CTC loss functions. The shared
encoder learns simultaneously multiple tasks by forming repre-
sentations useful for recognizing character, subword and final
word labels. This approach experimentally proves the impor-
tance of using the hierarchy of speech characteristics to guide
learning. The idea of hierarchical multitask learning is further
extended by Lee and Watanabe [17], who proposed interme-
diate loss regularization to relax the conditional independence
assumption of CTC by conditioning later predictions on inter-
mediate ones. Such methods provide a natural way to gradually
guide the model from learning basic acoustic distinctions to-
ward more nuanced phoneme-level classifications.
In a related vein, Goyal et al. [18] proposed a hierarchical
class-based curriculum loss for multi-label classification, inte-
grating hierarchical constraints directly into the loss function.
This approach assigns non-uniform weights to labels based on
their position within the hierarchy. It enforces a constraint such
that the loss of a child node must exceed that of its correspond-
ing parent node, effectively realizing a dynamic class-based
curriculum learning strategy. When the parent’s loss surpasses
the child’s, the model prioritizes minimizing the parent’s loss.
In other words, this class-based CL loss forces the model to
first correctly recognize general concepts (vowel, consonant, si-
lence), and only after learning these concepts the loss steers the
model in the direction of more fine-grained labels.
In contrast to prior hierarchical multitask methods that use
multiple auxiliary CTC losses to learn intermediate represen-
tations simultaneously [17, 16, 19], or methods that incorpo-
rate hierarchical constraints into the loss [18], TOL explic-
itly schedules the learning process via weighted loss functions
that transition the model’s focus from an easier coarse-grained
task to a more challenging fine-grained task. Unlike previous
works [16, 19], TOL does not require any architecture changes,
thus it can be applied to pretrained models too.
5. Experiments
5.1. Datasets
We evaluate the proposed framework on three phoneme recog-
nition datasets: TIMIT 1 [20], LnNor 2 [21, 22], and Vi-
bravox3 [23]. Each dataset presents different phonetic inven-
tories, speaking conditions, and phoneme annotation qualities,
allowing us to assess the robustness and generalizability of our
approach.
5.2. Baseline model
We conduct our experiments using the wav2vec 2.0 architec-
ture [24], a self-supervised model that learns rich speech rep-
resentations from raw audio via large-scale pretraining on un-
labeled data. wav2vec 2.0 is widely adopted in ASR due to
its strong performance after CTC-based fine-tuning with lim-
ited labeled data [25]. Our choice is motivated by practi-
cal considerations: training acoustic models from scratch is
computationally expensive, whereas pretrained models are the
de-facto standard in real-world systems. Evaluating TOL on
such a model therefore reflects realistic deployment scenarios
and highlights its practical relevance. For each dataset, we use
a language-matched pretrained wav2vec 2.0 model as the base
acoustic model:

TOL helps to shorten the blank-token-dominant phase by

dominated regime earlier, leading to faster convergence and
more reliable performance gains.
6.3. Optimization stability
To assess the impact of TOL on optimization dynamics, we an-
alyze two key indicators of training stability: gradient norm
and gradient variance. These metrics provide insights into how
smoothly the model traverses the loss landscape. The norm of
the gradient vector across all model parameters quantifies the
magnitude of the update signal at a given training step. A large
norm indicates a steep slope in the loss landscape suggesting
large parameter updates (potentially causing instability or ”ex-
ploding gradients”), whereas a small norm signifies a flatter
region, which might indicate convergence is near or that the
optimization is suffering from ”vanishing gradients”. Gradient
variance reflects the consistency of the gradient direction esti-
mated from different subsets of the training data. High variance
implies noisy or conflicting update directions between batches,
potentially hindering convergence, while low variance suggests
a more stable and consistent optimization path.
Figures 4 and 5 illustrate that both the gradient norm and
gradient variance are consistently lower in the TOL training
regime compared to standard training. This suggests that the
gradual transition of loss objectives in TOL contributes to a
more tempered and stable optimization process. The stability
likely contributes to the improved convergence characteristics
and performance observed in our experiments. In particular, the
gradient variance is an order of magnitude smaller when using
TOL.

7. Conclusions
This paper introduced TOL, a curriculum-learning framework
that improves CTC phoneme recognition model training by dy-
namically transitioning between loss functions of increasing
granularity. TOL enables models to learn coarse-grained acous-
tic patterns before refining fine-grained phoneme representa-
tions through explicitly scheduled objectives. TOL consistently
improved training efficiency and final performance in English,
Polish, and French. The observed reductions in PER, faster
convergence, and increased training stability stem from TOL’s
ability to mitigate early-stage optimization challenges in CTC
training – blank-token dominance. By prioritizing broad pho-
netic classes early and gradually shifting focus toward detailed
phoneme classification, TOL facilitates smoother and more ef-
fective representation learning. The experiments in this work
are limited to a sigmoid transition schedule and a set of four
auxiliary objectives, tuned on a single dataset. Exploring al-
ternative scheduling strategies, a richer set of acoustic-phonetic
objectives, and broader tuning across datasets may further en-
hance the effectiveness of TOL and is left for future work.
