Abstract
V owel sequences such as /ia/ can be produced and categorized
either as a monosyllabic diphthong /ja/ or as a heterosyllabic
hiatus /i.a/. In order to study whether lexical stress and posi-
tion within word facilitate or block glide formation, we ana-
lyzed the duration and formant dynamics of /ia/ in large cor-
pora of Italian and Romanian. In line with their respective
phonological categorization of /ia/, Italian speakers produced
shorter and slightly shallower formant trajectories than Roma-
nian speakers, who generally tended to produce more hiatus-
like formants. In Italian, gliding seemed to be blocked when
the vowel sequence was stressed, while gliding was preferred
in Romanian only for unstressed medial /ia/. At the same time,
there was a lot of overlap between the two languages, indicating
that the diphthong-hiatus contrast may be gradient both across
and within languages. Thus, this study provides the basis for
future investigations of the perceptual categorization of /ia/.
Index Terms: Italian, Romanian, diphthong-hiatus contrast,
functional analysis
1. Introduction
In this study, we investigate the acoustic realization of the vowel
sequence /ia/ in Italian and Romanian. In the Romance lan-
guages, sequences of a high front vowel followed by a non-high
vowel are phonologically categorized either as a hiatus /i.V/ or
as a diphthong /jV/ (where V = /a,5, e,E, o,O/). Importantly, this
distinction has an impact on the syllabification of words, e.g.pi-
anocan either be syllabified as /pi.a.no/ or /pja.no/. While most
Romance languages including Italian classify the overwhelm-
ing majority of vowel sequences as diphthongs, others such
as Portuguese and Romanian have a preference for hiatus se-
quences [1]. However, both cross-linguistically and within lan-
guages that have a diphthong-hiatus contrast, it has been found
that there is a bias in speech production towards diphthongs
[1, 2, 3, 4]. This bias is related to factors that are known to lead
to a greater overlap of articulatory gestures [5], e.g. prosodic
or phonological factors and speech styles that limit the duration
of the vowel sequence. Our investigation focuses on the effect
of lexical stress and position within the word (initial vs. me-
dial) on the vowel sequence’s duration and formant dynamics.
Despite the close historical relation between Italian and Roma-
nian, their distinct phonological classification of /ia/ makes for
an interesting starting point in examining the language-specific
ways in which these two linguistic factors facilitate or block
glide formation when the vowel sequence is realized in speech
production.

1.1. Italian
In Italian, sequences such as /ia/ are typically diphthongs, i.e.
they constitute the syllable nucleus [6, 7, 8, 9]. According to
phonological analyses, there are only very few exceptions, such
as when /i/ is stressed [10], after a liquid [6], or across a mor-
pheme boundary [6, 9]. However, the distinction between hia-
tus and diphthong bears almost no functional load. Hiatus is
already rare in Italian, but it additionally tends to collapse in
rapid or connected speech, in the speech of younger speakers,
and in function words [7, 9, 11]. According to [11], the further
the vowel sequence is located from the stressed syllable in the
word, the more likely it is to be produced with a palatal glide
instead of a full high front vowel. Lexical stress also affects the
duration of vowels in Italian in the expected way, i.e. they are
longer when stressed [7]. In an acoustic investigation, [8] found
the same expected pattern for the sequence /ia/: in stressed po-
sition, the sequence was approximately 150 ms long, whereas it
was only 72 ms long in unstressed position. Studying isolated
word productions of only one professional speaker, [6] found
that the difference between diphthong and hiatus was expressed
in terms of duration, even when the sequence was unstressed,
e.g. the vowel sequence inpianale/pja."na.le/ (engl. platform)
was 96 ms long, while it was 148 ms long insciatore/Si.a."to.re/
(engl. skier). We are not aware of any study that has investi-
gated the formant dynamics of these sequences in Italian.
1.2. Romanian
In Romanian, by contrast, vowel sequences of rising sonority
are preferably categorized as hiatus, especially in stressed or
word-initial position and across morpheme boundaries [12, 13].
That is, glide formation in hiatus sequences is blocked when the
phonological circumstances allow for longer vowel durations.
Diphthongs only consistently appear when they have been de-
rived from the so-called ‘breaking’ of the Late Latin stressed
mid vowels /E/ and /O/ or from the process of /l/ palatalization.
In an articulatory-acoustic study, [14] found that the hiatus was
around 200 ms long with an F2 onset at 2220 Hz whereas the
diphthong was ca. 150 ms long with an F2 onset at 2100 Hz.
Together with the lower vowel-to-vowel lag in /ja/ vs. /i.a/ iden-
tified in the articulatory part of that study, these differences in-
dicate that the two elements of a diphthong are more blended
than those of a hiatus [15]. The less tightly controlled articula-
tion of the hiatus elements, however, also means that there can
be more articulatory and hence acoustic variation in the produc-
tion of /i.a/ compared to /ja/ [5]. For instance, [1] found that
when the second element of /ia, io, ie/ was stressed, the entire
sequence was about 200 ms long, while it was less than 175 ms
in pre-tonic and pre-pre-tonic position. In word-initial position,

1.3. Research Questions and Aim
Examining two languages that differ in their general phonolog-
ical categorization of /ia/ sequences will help to broaden our
understanding of the diphthong-hiatus contrast and its manifes-
tations in speech. Previous studies have shown that the phono-
logical contrast between diphthongs and hiatus sequences does
manifest acoustically [1, 2, 6, 8, 12, 13, 14, 15, 16], though
the contrast is likely a gradient one in speech production. That
said, if Italian speakers produce these sequences overwhelm-
ingly as diphthongs, we would expect shorter durations and
transitory formant trajectories especially in the first half of the
sequence, as well as shallower transitions between the two el-
ements [6, 8, 16, 17]. Romanian speakers, on the other hand,
should produce longer formant trajectories with steeper tran-
sitions if there is indeed a preference for hiatus sequences in
Romanian [13, 14, 15]. However, since both categories exist in
both Italian and Romanian, we are also interested in the circum-
stances that may block or facilitate gliding. That is, in which
language-specific ways are the duration and formant dynamics
of /ia/ affected by lexical stress and position within the word?
Given previous studies [1, 11], we expect unstressed vowel se-
quences in both languages to be shorter and potentially have
flatter formants than stressed ones. An initiality effect, how-
ever, is only expected for Romanian [1] (there are no prior stud-
ies about the initiality effect in Italian).
2. Data
The data used in this study consist of radio and TV show record-
ings which were originally collected to train an ASR system and
forced-aligner [18]. A total of 168 hours of acoustic record-
ings (57k unique words) were available for Italian [19, 20], and
300 hours (48k unique words) were available for Romanian
[21]. The corpora consist of a mix of read and spontaneous
speech, i.e. broadcast news and interviews. No information
about the speakers was available (see section 3.1). The acous-
tic data had been run through the aforementioned ASR system
and forced-aligner [22, 23] which resulted in a highly accurate
segmentation into words and canonical phonemes [24]. From
the segmentation we extracted all words (plus/minus one word,
if available) that contained a sequence of /i/ or /j/ followed by
/a/ or /5/. The canonical transcription of the first element as /i/
or /j/ was not an indication of its actual realization. In total, we
extracted 24,371 tokens of Italian /ia/ and 19,972 tokens of Ro-
manian /ia/. We annotated lexical stress for all word types that
contained our extracted tokens by applying stress rules auto-
matically wherever possible, relying partially on the Romanian
Lexical Data Bases [25]. All annotations were checked manu-
ally. In the process we removed 160 tokens of /ia/ for Italian
and 30 for Romanian which either had a stressed first element
or were in post-tonic position. The remaining tokens either had
a stressed second element or appeared before the stressed sylla-
ble. In addition, the vowel sequence tokens were labelled auto-
matically for position within the word, i.e. as initial when /ia/
was preceded by a maximum of two consonants, and as medial
otherwise.
Using thewrassp::forestalgorithm (version 1.0.5,
[26]) in R with default settings, we estimated the first two for-
mants between the start and end of the vowel sequences. Given
the amount of data, a manual check of the formants trajectories
was not feasible. Instead, we opted for two clean-up proce-
dures. First, we excluded all tokens for which more than 25%
of the extracted F1 and/or F2 values were zero (i.e. not measur-
able). This affected 530 Italian and 2047 Romanian tokens of
/ia/. Second, we automatically cleaned up the formant trajecto-
ries by replacing F1 and F2 values outsideQ 1 −1.5·IQR and
Q3 + 1.5·IQR with the following or preceding value, whereQ1
andQ 3 are the first and third quartile, respectively, and IQR is
the interquartile range calculated asQ 1 −Q 3. This means that
F1 and F2 data points that would appear as outliers in a boxplot
were replaced by the more reasonable values at the following or
preceding time point. This procedure affected 31% of the Ital-
ian tokens and 53% of the Romanian tokens. Carefully com-
paring the original and cleaned up F1 and F2 for a few dozen
tokens per language, we checked that this procedure improved
the quality of the trajectories by reducing measuring errors and
did not lead to unintended changes or biases. Subsequently,
following the approach by [16], the formants were transformed
from Hertz into Bark using the packagephonR(version 1.0.7,
[27]), thus representing the formants along an auditory scale. In
order to normalize for gender-related effects, the mean of each
trajectory was subtracted from it, which centers each formant
around zero. Lastly, F1 and F2 were linearly time-normalized
and smoothed using B-splines, which was a prerequisite for the
functional analysis that will be explained below.

3. Analysis
For this study, we broadly followed the analytical approach pre-
sented by [16] who investigated the diphthong-hiatus contrast in
Spanish by analyzing the duration of the vowel sequence sepa-
rately from its formant dynamics. In contrast to [16], we con-
verted duration from milliseconds to log normalized units, and
we did not apply landmark registration in the functional analy-
sis of the formants.

3.1. Duration
First, we analyzed the effect of language, lexical stress, and
initiality on the log normalized duration of /ia/ by means of a
linear mixed effects regression, using thelmerTestpackage
(version 3.2.0, [28]) in R. Apart from the three fixed factors,
we also considered every possible two- and three-way inter-
action between them. Typically, we would have included ran-
dom effects for word type and speaker to account for variabil-
ity related to the drawn sample. While we did include a ran-
dom intercept by word type, the corpora did not provide unique
speaker IDs for us to use in the random effects structure of
the LMER. However, the long audio files were not just divided
into utterances, but also into shorter so-called segments which
had unique IDs. These segments could still contain the speech
of multiple speakers, but it was the best option to account for
variability related to individual speakers, recording time, and
recording situation / speech type (news, studio or street reports,
interviews). Thus, we also included a random intercept by seg-
ment ID. The model was automatically pruned using thestep
function, but all terms were maintained. Post-hoc comparisons
were run using estimated marginal means as provided by the
emmeanspackage (version 2.0.1, [29]).

4.2. Formant Dynamics
Figure 3 shows the mean estimated formants, reconstructed us-
ing PC1 and PC3 for each combination of language×stress
×initiality. Three main patterns can be observed from the for-
mant dynamics. First, the stressed sequences are much steeper
than the unstressed ones in Italian (regardless of initiality). That
is, when /a/ is stressed, both vocalic segments are more acous-
tically peripheral than when the entire sequence is unstressed.
F2 also flattens towards the end of the normalized timescale for
stressed, but not for unstressed /ia/, indicating a steady state
when /a/ is stressed. Second, in Romanian, the unstressed me-
dial sequences stand out from the other trajectories because they
have a much flatter transition between the two elements of the
sequence, especially in F2. The other trajectories, including for
unstressed initial /ia/, show more extreme formant values at the
start and end, steep transitions in between, and a steady state in
the final third of the F2 trajectory. The third main pattern visi-
ble from Figure 3 is that all Romanian sequences have steeper
formant trajectories than their Italian counterparts. The largest
difference can be observed for unstressed initial /ia/ which pat-
terns with unstressed medial /ia/ in Italian (i.e. flat formants)
and with stressed /ia/ in Romanian (i.e. steep formants).
5. Discussion
In summary, the analysis of the duration and formant dynam-
ics of /ia/ has shown that the realization of the vowel sequence
is conditioned by both stress and initiality in language-specific
ways. In Italian, lexical stress is the main factor determining
the acoustics of /ia/: when /a/ is stressed, Italian speakers pro-
duce the sequence with a longer duration, more peripheral vo-
calic elements and steeper transitions between them than when
the entire sequence is unstressed. Even though Italian vowel
sequences of rising sonority are generally classified as diph-
thongs, it remains to be tested whether they are perceived as
such even when they are stressed, given their hiatus-like acous-
tic configuration. In Romanian, on the other hand, unstressed
medial /ia/ had the shortest and flattest formant trajectories. If
shorter and flatter formants are an indication of more blended
articulatory gestures [1, 5, 14], it may be the case that gliding is
preferred in unstressed medial position. So far, it has only been
shown that Romanian listeners are very consistent and accu-
rate in distinguishing hiatus sequences from etymological diph-
thongs [1], but future perceptual studies should additionally ac-
count for lexical stress and position within word when present-
ing vowel sequence stimuli [32]. Initiality played only a minor
role in the production of the vowel sequence, but medial /ia/
was shorter than initial /ia/ when the sequence was unstressed
in both languages. Further factors such as phonetic context, the
presence of morpheme boundaries, and sociodemographic char-
acteristics of the speakers [6, 9, 11, 12, 13] will be assessed in a
follow-up study.
