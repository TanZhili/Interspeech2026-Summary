Abstract
Measuring articulatory movement traditionally requires expen-
sive laboratory equipment, constraining scale and clinical trans-
lation. We analytically validated a markerless pipeline using
MediaPipe’s Face Mesh model to capture 3D articulator move-
ment trajectories from a single standard RGB camera. Per-
participant calibration transformed model outputs into millime-
ters, enabling direct benchmarking against optical motion cap-
ture. Accuracy was quantified with 3D positional error, and
movement fidelity was evaluated relative to each landmark’s ex-
cursion and speed at 30 and 15 fps under matched filtering. The
system achieved millimeter-scale tracking accuracy (mean 3D
uRMSE 2.03 ± 0.59 mm), low normalized displacement error
(6.63 ± 1.28%), robust depth recovery, and no loss of utility at
15 fps—establishing single-camera 3D tracking as a scalable,
analytically viable technology.
Index Terms: 3D face tracking, speech kinematics, data col-
lection validation, MediaPipe face mesh
1. Introduction
Articulatory measurements have been used for decades to track
motor development, document disease progression, and assess
treatment outcomes [1–6]. Direct kinematic measurement cap-
tures subtle, mm-scale movement patterns with enough sensi-
tivity to detect motor changes that precede declines in speech
intelligibility and facial movement in neurodegenerative and
neuropsychiatric populations [4, 7–13]. Historically, measuring
speech movements has required laboratory equipment– elec-
tromagnetic articulography (EMA), marker-based optical mo-
tion capture, ultrasound– which offers high precision and has
generated foundational insights [14–16], but is costly, space-
intensive, and requires travel to a laboratory. This excludes
precisely the populations for whom sensitive kinematic moni-
toring may offer the greatest value: individuals with advanced
disease or limited access to academic medical centers. [17].
Here, we validate a fully markerless, single-camera pipeline
for 3D articulator kinematic analysis using standard laptop
video. Using MediaPipe Face Mesh [18, 19], we extract 3D

2. Related Work
Recent advances in computer vision allow detailed facial-
landmark tracking from standard RGB video, and camera-based
methods have already identified clinically relevant speech-
motor markers in Parkinson’s disease and ALS [4, 5, 8, 9, 20].
However, prior validations have relied on multi-camera arrays
or dedicated depth-sensing hardware [21, 22], or have validated
only derived summary feature (e.g. movement rate, peak ampli-
tude, movement, syllable duration) without confirming that the
underlying trajectories are accurate [23–25]. Trajectory-level
fidelity matters for applications that characterize movement co-
ordination, track change over time, or compute velocity and ac-
celeration. A further limitation is that model outputs are typi-
cally expressed in arbitrary or normalized units, making them
difficult to interpret physically or compare across studies [26].
To our knowledge, no study has demonstrated millimeter-scale,
depth-resolved 3D speech kinematics from a single RGB cam-
era in physically interpretable units while benchmarking full
trajectories against optical motion capture. Depth is the central
challenge: from a single RGB camera, scale and front-to-back
motion must be inferred from the image alone, yet articulatory
movements are inherently 3D, and collapsing them to 2D dis-
cards critical biomechanical information. Interpreting error also
requires accounting for movement scale, since absolute mil-
limeter error is uninformative without magnitude of the move-
ment being tracked. By establishing depth-resolved 3D speech
kinematics from a single RGB camera in physically meaning-
ful units and evaluating error relative to movement magnitude
and frame rate, this study moves video-based tracking toward a
practical, scalable tool for research and clinical use.

3. Methods
3.1. Participants and Tasks
Four adult laboratory staff (ages 26-58; 2 F, 2 M) with no history
of motor speech disorders participated. All recordings were ob-
tained in a controlled laboratory environment under consistent
lighting. Participants completed three tasks spanning a range
of movement amplitudes and temporal demands: (1) a diado-
chokinetic (DDK) task consisting of rapid syllable repetitions
(e.g., /pa/, /ta/, /ka/), eliciting small-amplitude movements; (2)
a sentence repetition task (SRT; “Buy Bobby a puppy.”), elic-
iting natural connected speech; and (3) a maximal movement
(MMM) task requiring exaggerated lip and jaw excursions to
capture large-amplitude motion. Together, these tasks provided
a robust sample (n=116 segments), capturing variability in both
range of motion and movement timing.
3.2. Recording Systems
Video was recorded using the webcam of a MacBook Pro at
30 fps. A 15 fps dataset was generated by temporal down-
sampling to evaluate robustness across sampling rates. Videos
were processed using MediaPipe Face Mesh (MFM, v0.10.21)
[18], yielding 3D landmark coordinates in model-defined space
(Fig. 1). Reference trajectories were recorded simultaneously
using an optical motion capture system (Cortex 10, Motion
Analysis Corp.) at 120 fps with eight infrared cameras (Eagle,
Motion Analysis Corp,). Reflective markers were placed on the
center lower lip, center upper lip, bilateral lip corners, bilateral
jaw, and head reference landmarks corresponding to selected
MFM vertices (Fig. 1). CTX trajectories were low-pass filtered
and downsampled to 30 and 15 fps for comparison with video-
derived trajectories.

3.5. Filtering and Derivatives
Full-length trajectories were low-pass filtered prior to segmen-
tation and derivative computation. For frame-rate comparisons,
both 30 and 15 fps datasets were filtered using a matched 7.125
Hz cutoff (below the 7.5 Hz Nyquist limit of 15 fps) to ensure
equivalent bandwidth. Identical zero-phase filters were applied
across systems and conditions. Temporal alignment between
systems was achieved by applying a constant offset to CTX
timestamps based on visible movement onset. Segment bound-
aries were defined on the MFM timeline and applied identically
across frame-rate conditions. From filtered, head-stabilized po-
sition traces, displacement was defined relative to segment on-
set, velocity was computed as the first temporal derivative of
displacement.

4. Results
4.1. Positional tracking accuracy
Positional accuracy was quantified using uRMSE in millimeters
(Fig. 2). Across tasks, mean 3D uRMSE was 2.03 ± 0.59 mm
at 30 fps and 2.03 ± 0.78 mm at 15 fps under matched band-
width conditions (low-pass filter: 7.125 Hz). Breaking down

Together, these findings confirm millimeter-scale positional ac-

4.2. Movement fidelity
Displacement accuracy was assessed using error normalized
to each landmark’s 95th percentile range of motion (Fig. 4).
Across tasks, mean normalized displacement error was 6.63 ±
1.28% at 30 fps and 8.96 ± 4.40% at 15 fps. Because this mea-
sure accounts for how large movements are, these results indi-
cate that trajectory amplitude is well-preserved relative to each
landmark’s movement range, and that 15 fps does not inflate
displacement error within the speech-relevant frequency band.

5. Discussion and Conclusions
Optical motion capture remains the gold standard for speech
kinematics, but its cost and infrastructure requirements limit
scale and access. Prior depth-sensor approaches reported spa-
tial errors of approximately 2 mm RMSE relative to optical
motion capture [22]. The present findings are consistent with
this benchmark, but unlike earlier depth-based systems, the
current pipeline runs on conventional video using an actively
maintained model. Remote studies involving consumer-grade

These possibilities are especially significant for special popula-

Several findings warrant caution in interpreting the accuracy
results. While lateralxaxis tracking error was low, dynamic
agreement was consistently the weakest across landmarks and
tasks. This likely reflects the relatively small magnitude of lat-
eral movements during speech, which places them closer to the
noise floor rather than a fundamental limitation of monocular
perspective geometry. Normalized speed error (∼14%) was ap-
proximately double the normalized displacement error (∼7%),
indicating that velocity estimates are more susceptible to noise
than position estimates and may constrain applications requir-
ing precise kinematic derivatives. More broadly, these findings
should be interpreted within the scope of the present validation.
Recordings were obtained under controlled laboratory lighting,
real-time streaming and video compression were not evaluated,
and the sample was limited to healthy adults performing a con-
strained set of speech tasks. Error magnitudes may increase
with greater head movement, variable lighting, compression ar-
tifacts, or unstable network conditions, all of which are common
in naturalistic or remote recording environments. Validation
in larger, more diverse, pediatric, and clinical populations will
be necessary to establish generalizability and support broader
translational claims. Finally, the per-participant mm calibration
introduced here will require further standardization and evalua-
tion outside the laboratory to support large-scale deployment.
In summary, MediaPipe Face Mesh recovered mm-scale 3D ar-
ticulatory kinematics from a single RGB camera, matched op-
tical motion capture accuracy, and showed no degradation at 15
fps. Single-camera 3D tracking substantially lowers technical
barriers to quantitative speech kinematics and provides a scal-
able foundation for clinically viable articulatory measurement.
