Phonetic evidence for contrastive voicing in Nakanamanga coronal plosives

1 Australian National University, Australia

Abstract
For Nakanamanga, an understudied Oceanic language of Vanu-
atu, it has long been debated whether there is one or two coro-
nal plosive phonemes. This uncertainty has affected historical
reconstructions and the understanding of language relationships
in the region. However, previous descriptive work has been lim-
ited in scope. Recent phonological analyses of Nakanamanga
varieties argue for two contrastive phonemes, with voicing as
part of the distinction, but acoustic evidence has not been ex-
amined. Based on data for the Tongoa variety, proposed to have
a voiceless dental /t ”/ and a voiced prenasalised postalveolar /nd/,
this study investigates their phonetic realisation, VOT, and clo-
sure duration. Results show that /t ”/ is consistently voiceless,
with positive VOT and shorter closure, while / nd/ is reliably
voiced, prenasalised, with negative VOT and longer closure;
the nasal-to-oral closure ratio is 1.88:1. These findings provide
clear acoustic support for a /t ”-nd/ contrast in Nakanamanga.

1. Introduction
Nakanamanga (ISO 639-3: llp; Glottolog: nort2836), also re-
ferred to as Nguna, Ngunese, or North Efate, is a Southern
Oceanic language spoken in central Vanuatu, with an estimated
10,000 speakers [1]. Previous research on Nakanamanga has
included comparative lexical studies [2], a sketch grammar [3],
and a recent dictionary [4]. Phonological investigation, how-
ever, has been limited, and the only existing phonetic study
is preliminary [5]. One unresolved issue is whether Nakana-
manga has one or two coronal plosive phonemes [3, 4]. Recent
phonological analyses suggest that two contrastive coronal plo-
sives are present. In the Tongoa variety, these are reported as a
voiceless dental plosive /t ”/ and a voiced prenasalised postalveo-
lar plosive /nd/ [6]. The present study investigates whether this
proposed voicing contrast is supported by phonetic evidence.
1.1. Phonetic correlates of phonemic voicing
V oice onset time (VOT), the interval between the release of a
plosive constriction and the onset of periodicity in the follow-
ing voiced segment, is a well established acoustic correlate of
plosive voicing. Crosslinguistic studies [7, 8, 9] have identified
general ranges for VOT: long-lag VOT is positive and typically
greater than 30 ms, short-lag VOT is positive but smaller, and
negative VOT indicates prevoicing. VOT is commonly used to
investigate plosive voicing contrasts [10, p. 852]. Languages
with two-way plosive voicing contrasts can be broadly cate-
gorised into two types [9]. In “aspirating” languages, both

phonologically ‘voiced’ plosive exhibiting short-lag VOT and
the phonologically ‘voiceless’ plosive exhibiting long-lag VOT
(e.g., English [11] and German [12]). In “true voicing” lan-
guages, one category is phonetically voiceless (positive VOT)
and the other fully voiced (negative VOT), as observed in Rus-
sian [13] and Turkish [14]. VOT and closure duration are some-
times inversely related, with longer VOT associated with shorter
closure durations [15].
1.2. Prenasalised stops
Prenasalised segments are reported in approximately 12% of the
world’s languages [16]. A prenasalised stop consists of a nasal
and a stop segment, where the nasal is almost always voiced
(except occasionally word-initially), and the plosive component
may be voiced or voiceless. In most languages, prenasalised
stops are fully voiced across both nasal and oral portions [17,
p. 256]. The duration of prenasalised stops is dominated by
the nasal closure, with the oral closure typically brief. For ex-
ample, the nasal-to-oral ratio ranges from approximately 75%
in Erromangan to 95% in Manado [18]. Prenasalised stops
also exhibit areal patterns, being common in Austronesian and
Papuan languages, as well as in certain South American fami-
lies [17]. Within the Oceanic subbranch of Austronesian, pre-
nasalised stops are typically voiced and may occur at multiple
places of articulation, generally mirroring the distribution of
plain stops or nasals. Well documented examples include Fijian
in the Polynesian subbranch [19] and, in Vanuatu, Tamambo
[20]. An exception to this symmetry is Erromangan, where
voiceless prenasalised stops are limited to specific places of ar-
ticulation, while a prenasalised voiced alveolar stop co-occurs
with a broader series of voiceless stops and nasals [21].
1.3. Plosive voicing in central Vanuatu
Historical reconstruction suggests that Proto North and Cen-
tral Vanuatu included prenasalised voiced plosives in con-
trast to voiceless plosives, conventionally represented with the
graphemes of the voiced plosives. Progressive devoicing has
been proposed in this region [22, p. 10], corresponding to the
descriptions of central Vanuatu languages, where plosive voic-
ing varies across neighbouring languages. In Namakura, there
is a distinction between voiceless and voiced (prenasalised) plo-
sives across four places of articulation (/p, mb, t, nd, k, Ng, >kp,
>Nm >kp/) [23], whereas Lelepa and Nafsan have only one single
voiceless series (/p, t, k, >kp/) [24, 25].
Nakanamanga is geographically and genetically situated
between these languages. Previous work has been inconclusive
regarding the number of coronal plosives and their phonetic re-
alisation. The sketch grammar proposed a single coronal plo-

2. Research questions
Following the recently revised phonological description of the
Tongoa variety of Nakanamanga [6], this study investigates
whether acoustic phonetic evidence supports the proposed anal-
ysis of coronal plosives, asking: (i) Do acoustic measures sup-
port /t ”/ and /nd/ as distinct phonemes? (ii) Is there phonetic
evidence confirming that /nd/ is realised with prenasalisation?
3. Methods
3.1. Participants
Eight native speakers of Nakanamanga from Tongoa Island par-
ticipated in this study (five women, three men; aged 21–56).
Although all participants are originally from Tongoa, some cur-
rently live in Port Vila. As typical in Vanuatu, all participants
are multilingual [28], speaking Nakanamanga alongside Bis-
lama and either English or French to varying extents. They also
have exposure to neighbouring languages, including Namakura
and other languages of Efate, through typological proximity,
language contact, and social factors such as intermarriage.
3.2. Materials
Based on extensive piloting using the Nakanamanga dictionary
[4], a wordlist of 12 disyllabic CV .CV items was compiled. The
orthography generally does not distinguish /t ”/ from /nd/, so care-
ful lexical selection was necessary. In the present study, the tar-
get plosives occurred in the onset of the second syllable, i.e., in
word-medial position. Althought the contrast is not restricted
to medial position, the distribution of available tokens is shaped
by morphology: nouns often carry ana-prefix, while verbs may
undergo consonant mutation conditioned by tense-aspect-mood
categories, limiting the availability of suitable word-initial to-
kens. Nevertheless, rare minimal pairs in initial position are
attested, such as /t ”oa/ ‘chicken’ vs /ndoa/ ‘Polynesian triller’,
supporting the relevance of the contrast beyond the word-medial
position targeted in the present wordlist.
The preceding vowel in the first syllable was consistently
/5/, either short or long. This was not an experimental manip-
ulation but was dictated by limited lexical availability. The de-
sign aimed to include six words per plosive, with three featur-
ing a short /5/ and three featuring a long /5:/. The twelve lexical
items in the wordlist are shown in Table 2. To avoid order ef-
fects, words were elicited in pseudo-random order, ensuring an
even distribution of plosive categories throughout the list.

3.3. Data collection
Speech recordings were made in relatively quiet indoor settings
on Tongoa Island and in Port Vila, with a Zoom H6 portable
recorder and a RØDE NT3 cardioid microphone (96 kHz, 24-
bit). To avoid orthographic influence, stimuli were presented as
pictures, with Bislama prompts provided when needed. Each
participant produced five consecutive repetitions of each word
within the carrier phrase: /n5f5s5:n5w5i5... epei n5k5n5m5:N5/
“This word . . . is Nakanamanga.” All recordings have been
archived with PARADISEC [29].
Variability in syllable count arose due to vowel reduction
and verbal morphology (e.g., subject prefixes or transitive suf-
fixes) [6]. Most productions retained the intended disyllabic
shape (81%), while trisyllabic forms accounted for 18% and
quadrisyllabic forms 1%. No monosyllabic tokens were found.
Analyses focused on the target plosive in the onset of the second
syllable of the stem. Although the wordlist aimed for balanced
representation across the two coronal plosives, lexical limita-
tions resulted in uneven distribution.
3.4. Data processing
All audio recordings were downsampled to 44.1 kHz, 16-bit for
acoustic analysis. Target plosives were manually segmented
in Praat [30] at multiple levels: word, syllable, phoneme, and
phone. V oice onset time (VOT) was measured from the release
burst to the onset of voicing. Closure duration was segmented
from the start of the stop closure to its release, and for /nd/, from
the start of the nasal closure to the onset of the oral release.
Nasal versus oral closure intervals were distinguished using in-
tensity cues, with the midpoint of the intensity drop marking the
boundary between nasal and oral segments. Figure 1 illustrates
example segmentations, including /N>m5:t ”a/ ‘snake’ and /n5:ndi/
‘banana’. After excluding tokens affected by noise, the dataset
included 443 tokens (Table 3).

3.5. Database and statistical analysis
An EMU Speech Database was made from the downsampled
recordings (44.1 kHz, 16-bit) together with their Praat .TextGrid
annotations. Measures of voice onset time (VOT) and closure
duration were extracted using the EMU Speech Database Man-
agement System [31] and theemuRpackage [32] within R [33].
To examine the effects of plosive type, preceding vowel length,
and syllable count on these measures, linear mixed-effects mod-
els were fitted using thelmerTestpackage [34], with random
intercepts included for both speaker and lexical item.
4. Results
4.1. V oice Onset Time
All tokens of /t ”/ were phonetically realised as voiceless plosives,
whereas all /nd/ tokens were realised as prenasalised voiced plo-
sives. Some /t ”/ tokens exhibited multiple release bursts; for con-
sistency, the VOT boundary was measured from the onset of the
first release. For / nd/, the oral closure showed stronger voic-
ing than /t ”/, consistent with its status as a voiced plosive. Any
slight carry-over voicing from the nasal portion is noted but is
not treated as a confound in the present analysis.
Figure 2 and Table 4 present mean V oice Onset Time (VOT)
and standard deviations for the two phonemes. The voiceless /t ”/
exhibits general positive VOT values (mean = 29 ms, SD = 14),
indicating a lag between release and voicing onset. In contrast,
the voiced prenasalised / nd/ consistently shows negative VOT
(mean =−98 ms, SD = 24), reflecting substantial prevoicing.
The larger standard deviation for /nd/ indicates greater variabil-
ity in prevoicing, which is realised across both the nasal and
oral closure phases (Figure 1).

5. Discussion and Conclusion
The present study investigated the phonetic realisation of coro-
nal plosives in the Tongoa variety of Nakanamanga, address-
ing two primary research questions: (i) whether /t ”/ and / nd/
are distinct phonemes, and (ii) whether / nd/ is realized with
prenasalisation. Acoustic analyses provide clear affirmative
answers. VOT measurements show a substantial difference:
voiceless /t ”/ exhibits positive VOT (mean = 29 ms), whereas
voiced prenasalised / nd/ consistently displays substantial pre-
voicing (mean =−98 ms). Closure duration further supports
this distinction, with / nd/ having a significantly longer mean
closure (98 ms) than /t ”/ (80 ms). Analyses of the nasal and oral
components of /nd/ show that the nasal portion accounts for ap-
proximately 65% of total closure duration. Linear mixed-effects
models confirm that the effects of Phoneme on both VOT and
closure duration are significant, and these contrasts are consis-
tent across speakers and lexical items. Collectively, these find-
ings provide robust acoustic evidence that /t ”/ and /nd/ are distinct
phonemes, with /nd/ reliably prenasalised.
From a typological perspective for acoustic measures, /t ”/
aligns with a short-lag voiceless plosive [8, 9, 10], while / nd/
fits the profile of a fully voiced prenasalised stop [17, 18, 21].
The observed nasal portion of 65% in Nakanamanga is slightly
lower than reported in other languages (lowest approximately
75%) [18], likely due to differences in measurement: in this
paper, segmentation was based on acoustic intensity, whereas
previous work used articulatory airflow measures [18]. Future
work incorporating nasal–oral airflow data for Nakanamanga
could provide more directly comparable measures.
Considering the languages of central Vanuatu, the Tongoa
variety of Nakanamanga occupies an intermediate position. Na-
makura exhibits a full contrast between a voiced (prenasalised)
plosive series and a voiceless series across all places of articula-
tion [23], whereas Lelepa and Nafsan have only a single voice-
less plosive series [24, 25]. In the Tongoa variety of Nakana-
manga, voiceless plosives occur at bilabial, velar, and labial-
velar places of articulation (/p, k, >kp/), yet a voicing contrast
is observed in the coronal space, including a voiceless dental
/t ”/ and a voiced prenasalised postalveolarnd/. This pattern may
reflect its contact with Namakura. Future research should exam-
ine the Nguna variety of Nakanamanga to determine whether its
coronal plosives align more closely with Lelepa and Nafsan in
terms of phonetic realisation. A more detailed understanding
of coronal plosives, voicing contrasts, and phonetic realisations
in Nakanamanga will contribute to historical reconstructions
and inform our understanding of contact, dialect chain and hu-
man migration history in this region. Interestingly, the presence
of one single prenasalised coronal plosive in Nakanamanga re-
sembles the inventory of Erromangan. Both languages deviate
from the more common Oceanic pattern, in which voiceless and
voiced (prenasalised) plosives typically occur at the same places
of articulation [18, 21, 17]. Another interesting future direction
is to investigate place-of-articulation asymmetries. In Tongoa,
/t ”/ is dental, whereas /nd/ is postalveolar. Systematic examina-
tion of formant patterns could illuminate acoustic correlates of
PoA in prenasalised voiced stops and determine whether similar
patterns exist in the Nguna variety or other Oceanic languages
with voiced prenasalised series contrasting with voiceless stops
at different articulatory locations [21, 19].
In conclusion, this study provides the first acoustic evi-
dence supporting a contrastive voicing distinction among coro-
nal plosives in Nakanamanga. The findings demonstrate that
/t ”/ is consistently voiceless, while /nd/ is reliably voiced and
prenasalised, with a predominant nasal closure. These results
contribute to a more detailed phonetic and phonological under-
standing of central Vanuatu languages and underscore the value
of integrating acoustic data with phonological analyses in un-
derstudied Oceanic languages.
