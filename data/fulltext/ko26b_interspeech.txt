Abstract
Automatic speech recognition (ASR) is expected to handle
speaker and environmental variability, yet deployed systems
encounter continual distribution shifts as new speakers arrive.
Continual speaker adaptation personalizes one model along a
speaker stream without revisiting past data, but sequential up-
dates can cause catastrophic forgetting. Low-rank adaptation
(LoRA) offers a parameter efficient mechanism, and orthogonal
low-rank adaptation (O-LoRA) reduces interference by separat-
ing update subspaces. However, enforcing task agnostic separa-
tion can suppress transfer among acoustically similar speakers.
We introduce similarity conditioned signed orthogonal LoRA
(SCOLoRA), which leverages speaker embedding similarity to
dynamically balance subspace alignment and orthogonal sepa-
ration via signed coefficients. Across ASR benchmarks, SCOL-
oRA consistently improves new speaker adaptation and reduces
forgetting over LoRA-based continual learning baselines.
Index Terms: continual learning, speaker adaptation, low-rank
adaptation, personalization
1. Introduction
Deep neural network (DNN) based automatic speech recogni-
tion (ASR) has advanced rapidly, and neural acoustic models
now form a core component of modern ASR systems [1]. Nev-
ertheless, deployed systems encounter distribution shifts caused
by speaker identity, channel characteristics, background noise,
and speaking style. Among these factors, speaker variability re-
mains a major source of acoustic mismatch, motivating speaker
adaptation and personalization methods [2, 3]. Speaker adapta-
tion reduces speaker dependent mismatch by updating or con-
ditioning a model with limited speaker specific data [4].
Classical adaptation approaches include maximum a pos-
teriori estimation and maximum likelihood linear regression in
hidden Markov model based ASR frameworks [2, 3]. In neural
ASR, speaker adaptation has been studied through lightweight
conditioning signals and parameter efficient mechanisms that
modulate internal representations [5,6]. However, practical ser-
vices increasingly operate in streaming settings, where speakers
arrive sequentially and per speaker data are limited and time lo-
cal. In such settings, retraining on all historical data is often
infeasible due to storage, privacy, and computational cost, mo-
tivating continual ASR [7–9]. A key challenge is catastrophic
forgetting, where learning new speakers degrades performance
on earlier ones [10–12]. In speaker streams, relatedness varies
across speakers; thus, uniformly isolating speaker specific up-
dates can block transfer among acoustically similar speakers,
motivating similarity-aware control of sharing and separation.

Recent progress in parameter efficient fine-tuning (PEFT)
offers a practical toolbox for personalization under resource
constraints. Adapters, BitFit, and low-rank adaptation (LoRA)
update only a small parameter set while largely freezing the
backbone, enabling efficient deployment and storage of per-
sonalized updates [13–15]. This paradigm also suits continual
learning: a frozen backbone with lightweight task modules low-
ers training and storage costs and is used in parameter efficient
continual learning frameworks [16–18]. In ASR, LoRA has re-
cently been explored for speaker personalization, highlighting
its practical appeal for speaker specific adaptation [19]. How-
ever, sequential low-rank updates can still suffer speaker inter-
ference when fixed low-rank capacity must serve a long stream.
A representative strategy for reducing interference in PEFT-
based continual learning is to explicitly separate task specific
update subspaces. Orthogonal low-rank adaptation (O-LoRA)
extends this geometric principle to LoRA by encouraging or-
thogonality between low-rank update subspaces across tasks
[20]. While task agnostic orthogonality is effective for suppress-
ing interference, it does not account for task relatedness. In con-
tinual speaker adaptation, speaker similarity can be estimated
using speaker embeddings [21, 22], and leveraging information
from acoustically similar speakers has been shown to be ben-
eficial for adaptation [23]. These observations suggest that a
similarity-aware mechanism that modulates sharing versus sep-
aration is a better fit for speaker streams than a uniform, task
agnostic separation rule.
We propose similarity conditioned signed orthogonal LoRA
(SCOLoRA), a LoRA-based continual speaker adaptation
method that conditions the degree and direction of subspace in-
teraction on speaker similarity. SCOLoRA uses speaker embed-
ding similarity to determine when low-rank updates should be
encouraged to align and when they should be pushed toward
stronger separation. Although multiple similarity back ends are
possible, we adopt cosine similarity for its simplicity, robust-
ness with length normalized embeddings, and low overhead in
streaming settings [21, 22, 24]. In addition, SCOLoRA intro-
duces lightweight learnable components that calibrate the simi-
larity to coefficient mapping as the speaker stream evolves. The
main contributions of this study are as follows:
• Continual speaker adaptation is formulated as a practical
rehearsal-free continual learning problem.
• We introduce SCOLoRA, which conditions signed orthogo-
nal constraints on speaker similarity to enable transfer across
similar speakers while mitigating interference across dissim-
ilar speakers.
• We propose norm fixed LoRA bases with lightweight calibra-
tion, addressing the limits of task agnostic subspace separa-
tion in LoRA-based continual learning.

3. Method
We introduce SCOLoRA, which addresses a key limitation of
O-LoRA: it enforces orthogonality uniformly across speakers,
which can block transfer when speakers are acoustically simi-
lar. We therefore make speaker relatedness an explicit control
signal by extracting speaker embeddingse t with a speaker en-
coder and computing cosine similaritiesS i,t between the cur-
rent speakerSpk t and past speakersSpk i. A similarity con-
ditioned signed controller convertsS i,t intoλ(S i,t), which
weights the subspace regularizer between the current LoRA ba-
sisAt and past basesAi. A negative coefficient encourages sub-
space alignment for similar speakers, whereas a positive coeffi-
cient enforces orthogonal separation for dissimilar speakers.

4. Experiments
4.1. Datasets
We evaluated continual speaker adaptation on TEDLIUM2 [30],
TEDLIUM3 [31], and CHiME3 [32]. Each speaker corresponds
to one index in the speaker stream, and we used a 9:1 dev split
of the available adaptation data per speaker.
TEDLIUM2 (TED2).TEDLIUM2 contains 207 hours of TED
talk speech, with official dev and test sets of approximately 1.6
and 2.6 hours, respectively. We constructed an 8 speaker stream
and reported per speaker WER on the test set.
TEDLIUM3 (TED3).For TEDLIUM3, the duration of speech
(hours) for the speaker adaptation partition is 346.17, 3.73, and
3.76 for train, dev and test sets. We constructed an 8 speaker
stream and reported WER on both dev and test sets.
CHiME3.CHiME3 is a noisy speech benchmark collected in
real environments. We built a 4 speaker stream and reported per
speaker test WER and the average WER.
4.2. Experimental Setup
We used Whisper small [33] as the ASR model and imple-
mented all experiments in ESPnet [34] on one NVIDIA RTX
4090 GPU. We froze the backbone and trained only LoRA
adapters; SCOLoRA additionally optimized a lightweight sim-
ilarity to coefficient mapping with negligible parameter over-
head. LoRA was applied to attention projection modules with
rankr= 4,α lora = 8, and dropout 0.05; we used the warmu-
pLR scheduler with 1,500 warmup updates to a peak learning
rate of5×10 −4. Adaptation for all speakers started from the
same previously fine-tuned checkpoint, shared across all meth-
ods. At each speaker indext, we attached a newly initialized
LoRA branch and trained it onDt, selecting the best checkpoint
on the speaker specific dev split. We then merged the learned
update into the backbone weights for evaluation.

5. Results
5.1. Continual speaker adaptation results
Table 1 shows TEDLIUM3 sequential speaker adaptation re-
sults. SCOLoRA achieved the lowest average WER on both
splits: 4.11% on test and 4.41% on dev. On the test split,
SCOLoRA improved over SeqLoRA, EWC [11], L2P [27], O-
LoRA [20], InfLoRA [28], and GainLoRA [29], which achieved
4.35%, 4.34%, 4.27%, 4.33%, 4.32%, and 4.25%, correspond-
ing to absolute reductions of 0.24 WER relative to SeqLoRA
and 0.22 WER relative to O-LoRA. The gains were consistent
across speakers on test, suggesting that SCOLoRA reduced in-
terference as the speaker stream progresses. This advantage held
on the dev split. Beyond accuracy, SCOLoRA attained the low-
est forgetting score in Table 1, reaching 0.05. This improved
over EWC at 0.06, SeqLoRA at 0.10, L2P at 0.17, O-LoRA
at 0.19, InfLoRA at 0.11, and GainLoRA at 0.09. Table 3 re-
ports cross corpus continual speaker adaptation results in terms
of WER, where SCOLoRA consistently improved performance
on TEDLIUM2, TEDLIUM3, and CHiME3. Overall, SCOL-
oRA improves recognition while strengthening retention under
rehearsal-free continual adaptation.
5.2. Ablation studies
Table 2 examined the contribution of each component under two
similarity thresholds, 0.20 and 0.25. A positive only similarity
conditioned variant reached an average WER of 4.23% under
both thresholds. Introducing signed weighting yielded a clear
improvement, reaching 4.15% under the 0.20 threshold. Mak-
ing the slopeαlearnable slightly improved robustness under
the 0.25 threshold, reaching 4.18% compared with 4.19%. The
full model, combining signed weighting, learnableα, and theλ
router, performed best, reaching 4.11% under the 0.20 threshold
and 4.12% under the 0.25 threshold. Notably, the full SCOL-
oRA showed minimal sensitivity to the threshold choice.

6. Conclusion
This study proposed SCOLoRA, a rehearsal-free continual
speaker adaptation framework for end-to-end ASR that con-
ditions signed subspace regularization on speaker embedding
similarity. By enabling subspace alignment for similar speakers
and orthogonal separation for dissimilar speakers under a norm
fixed LoRA parameterization, SCOLoRA improves training sta-
bility and mitigates interference. Experiments on TEDLIUM2,
TEDLIUM3, and CHiME3 demonstrated consistent WER re-
ductions, including improved average performance and reduced
forgetting on TEDLIUM3.
