Alignment-Aware Continued Pre-training for Multilingual Speech
Representation Learning

Abstract
Large multilingual self-supervised learning (SSL) models pro-
vide strong acoustic representations; they are typically adapted
to ASR through direct supervised fine-tuning. This paradigm
defers acoustic–symbol alignment entirely to the fine-tuning
stage, which may limit cross-lingual generalization under het-
erogeneous scripts and long-tailed language distributions. We
propose an alignment-aware continued pre-training framework
on top of multilingual SSL foundation models.Instead of direct
fine-tuning, we introduce an intermediate stage that jointly op-
timizes SSL and CTC objectives to impose textual alignment
constraints on representation learning. We analyze the ASR
performance of different modeling units introduced in the align-
ment and introduce a language-aware dual-codebook quantizer
to mitigate multilingual competition. Experiments demonstrate
consistent gains over direct fine-tuning and continual SSL adap-
tation, with improved cross-lingual transfer under unified tex-
tual spaces.
Index Terms: multilingual ASR, self-supervised learning, ro-
manization
1. Introduction
Self-supervised learning (SSL) has enabled multilingual speech
foundation models trained on hundreds of languages [1, 2, 3, 4,
5]. They provide acoustically robust representations that gen-
eralize across phonetic systems and domains [6]. In standard
practice, these models are adapted to downstream ASR tasks via
supervised fine-tuning. However, SSL objectives primarily en-
courage acoustic invariance and masked prediction consistency,
rather than explicit alignment between continuous speech rep-
resentations and the corresponding textual symbolic space. The
misalignment between the optimization objectives of SSL and
ASR results in suboptimal performance for the final fine-tuned
model [7], and this performance degradation can be further ex-
acerbated in multilingual settings.
In particular, differences in scripts, phoneme inventories,
and orthographic conventions define distinct projection spaces
from speech to textual symbols [8]. In such a heterogeneous
setting, directly fine-tuning a SSL foundation model may not
fully exploit pretrained representations, especially for cross-
lingual transfer and unseen languages [9]. This motivates a key
question:how should multilingual foundation SSL models be
adapted for ASR beyond simple fine-tuning?
We address this question with an alignment-aware contin-
ued pre-training framework. Instead of directly performing su-
pervised fine-tuning, we insert an intermediate stage between
foundation pre-training and ASR finetuning, where SSL and

allocation of discrete capacity in the quantization module. Ac-
cordingly, we analyze modeling units as structural alignment
constraints and propose a language-aware dual-codebook de-

Experimental results on the FLEURS dataset show that the pro-
posed method achieves up to a 15% relative reduction in WER
compared with direct fine-tuning, and an 11% relative reduction
compared with conventional continued pre-training.
2. Related Work
SSL methods such as wav2vec 2.0 [10], HuBERT [11], and
data2vec [12] learn strong speech representations from un-
labeled audio via masked prediction. These methods have
been scaled to multilingual settings, including XLS-R [2] and
MMS [1]. In current practice, multilingual foundation models
are adapted to ASR via supervised fine-tuning[1], sometimes
preceded by continual SSL on target-domain audio[13, 14].
Nevertheless, both strategies largely defer acoustic–symbol
alignment to the downstream stage.
To incorporate textual symbolic supervision earlier, UniS-
peech [7] integrates CTC into SSL training, and JUST [15]
explores joint SSL–supervised training for multilingual ASR.
While effective, these approaches are typically trained under
moderate multilingual settings, and are not explicitly formu-
lated as a continued pre-training strategy on top of large foun-
dation models. More recently, MAUBERT [16] performs multi-
lingual continued pre-training using articulatory features to in-
ject phonetic biases that improve cross-lingual robustness. In
parallel, modeling units (graphemes, phonemes, and unified ro-
manization such as Uroman [17]) determine the textual sym-
bolic space structure and shared pronunciation rules, which
can substantially affect cross-lingual sharing. Moreover, vec-
tor quantization introduces a shared discrete capacity that may
induce multilingual competition under long-tailed distributions.
Our work integrates these approaches by formulating multilin-
gual ASR adaptation as alignment-aware continual pre-training
for foundational SSL models, unifying this pre-training scheme
into a single adaptation stage and empirically analyzing how
textual symbolic space design and discrete modeling choices
impact cross-lingual transfer.

3. Method
We formulate multilingual ASR adaptation as a representa-
tion alignment problem. Given a multilingual foundation SSL
model pretrained on large-scale unlabeled speech, our goal is
to adapt it to symbolic prediction tasks while preserving cross-
lingual acoustic invariance.
Letxdenote an input waveform andH= [h 1,...,h T ]∈
RT×d denote contextual representations from the SSL encoder.
In standard fine-tuning, a mappingf:H→yis learned un-
der CTC supervision, whereyis a sequence of discrete textual
symbols. Under this paradigm, the acoustic-text alignment is
learned entirely during task optimization.
We instead introduce an intermediate alignment-aware con-
tinued pre-training stage to reshape representations before
fine-tuning. The overall framework consists of three stages:
(i) initialization from a multilingual SSL model (MMS), (ii)
alignment-aware continued pre-training with joint SSL and
CTC objectives, and (iii) CTC fine-tuning.

3.2. Modeling Units in Continued Pre-training
In alignment-aware continued pre-training, the modeling unit
defines the textual symbolic space to which acoustic representa-
tions are projected via the CTC objective. Unlike conventional
fine-tuning, where modeling units mainly affect output granu-
larity, here they directly govern how alignment constraints are
imposed on intermediate representations. As a result, the struc-
ture of the textual symbolic space influences not only recogni-
tion accuracy but also cross-lingual sharing and generalization.
We compare three modeling units under identical training
settings: native graphemes (Char), IPA phonemes, and unified
romanization (Uroman).
3.2.1. Grapheme (Char)
Grapheme modeling preserves each language’s original script
and aligns directly with ground-truth transcripts, providing pre-
cise supervision for in-language recognition. However, when

3.2.2. IPA Phoneme
IPA modeling maps speech into a shared phonetic inven-
tory, aiming to reduce script fragmentation and encourage
pronunciation-level alignment across languages. In this work,
IPA transcripts are generated automatically via grapheme-to-
phoneme (G2P) conversion usingPhonetisaurus[18] together
with multilingual G2P FST models fromLanguageNet[19].
The LanguageNet G2P resources cover 142 languages, with re-
ported phoneme error rates (PERs) ranging from 7% to 45%.
Despite offering a unified phonetic representation, IPA-
based modeling faces practical challenges in massively multi-
lingual settings. Significant allophonic variations exist across
different languages[20]. Only a partial overlap exists between
the languages supported by G2P resources and those used in
FLEURS, and conversion errors may introduce noise into the
CTC supervision signal. Moreover, the union of phoneme in-
ventories across many languages can become large and hetero-
geneous, reducing the compactness advantage typically associ-
ated with phoneme-based modeling.
3.2.3. Unified Romanization
Unified romanization constructs a shared symbolic space by
mapping diverse scripts into a common Latin-based represen-
tation. We useUroman[17] to convert texts from different
writing systems into a unified romanized form.
Compared with grapheme modeling, romanization in-
creases symbol sharing across languages by projecting hetero-
geneous scripts into a partially shared textual symbolic space.
Compared with IPA modeling, it avoids the dependency on
language-specific G2P resources. This shared symbolic space
encourages cross-lingual parameter sharing while preserving
sufficient orthographic information for language-specific dis-
crimination, providing a practical balance between language-
specific fidelity and scalable multilingual generalization.

3.3. Language-aware dual-codebook quantization
Multilingual speech data typically follow a long-tailed distribu-
tion, where a small number of high-resource languages dom-
inate the training corpus while many low-resource languages
contribute comparatively fewer samples. Under such imbal-
ance, a single shared discrete codebook may suffer from mul-
tilingual competition: dominant languages occupy a dispropor-
tionate portion of discrete tokens, limiting representational ca-
pacity for underrepresented or typologically distant languages.
This imbalance may bias the learned discrete space toward
majority-language acoustic patterns and weaken cross-lingual
robustness.
To alleviate this issue, we introduce a language-aware dual-
codebook quantization mechanism that explicitly factorizes the
discrete space into global and language-specific components.

From a representation learning perspective, the additive fu-
sion in Eq. 4 can be interpreted as decomposing the latent space
into a shared subspace and a residual subspace. Instead of forc-
ing all variation into a single discrete inventory, the model is al-
lowed to disentangle shared and language-specific components
explicitly. This structured decomposition mitigates multilingual
interference, reduces competition for discrete entries, and im-
proves representational fairness under long-tailed distributions.
Moreover, by constraining global patterns to be encoded
inC (g), the dual-codebook mechanism promotes more sta-
ble cross-lingual alignment during continued pre-training.
Language-specific variations are absorbed intoC (s,l) without
distorting the shared discrete structure, thereby strengthening
both transferability and robustness in multilingual ASR.
4. Experimental Setup
All experiments are conducted on top of the MMS 300M multi-
lingual SSL foundation model [1]. We evaluate on two comple-
mentary benchmarks.FLEURS[21] contains speech and tran-
scripts from102 languageswith diverse scripts and long-tailed
language distributions.MLS[22] (Multilingual LibriSpeech)
provides large-scale read speech across8 languages. We use
the10-hour subset (MLS-10h)for low-resource experiments,
and the full datasets of the 7 non-English languages for cross-
lingual transfer experiments.
We compare three adaptation strategies: (i)Direct fine-
tuning (B1)on labeled target data; (ii)Continual SSL
(B2), which performs self-supervised continued pre-training
on target-domain audio before supervised fine-tuning; and
(iii)Ours, which performsjoint SSL–CTC continued pre-
trainingwith random replacement (RR) followed by fine-
tuning. For fairness, B2 and our method use thesametarget-
domain audio during the continued pre-training stage, differ-
ing only in whether symbolic supervision (CTC) is introduced.
Joint pre-training runs for 20k update steps on 4 GPUs with
gradient accumulation 8, mask probabilityp= 0.05, replace-
ment probabilityp rr = 0.5, and Adam with peak learning rate
5×10 −4. Fine-tuning uses 2 GPUs with gradient accumula-
tion 4 and Adam, using 50k update steps on FLEURS and 20k
update steps on MLS-10h with peak learning rate3×10 −4.
We consider three modeling units: native graphemes (Char),
IPA phonemes (via G2P; with limited coverage on FLEURS),
and unified romanization (Uroman) [17]. For transfer evalua-
tion, we use the seven non-English MLS languages as contin-
ued pre-training sources and FLEURS as the target benchmark,
reporting results on both the seen subset (FLEURS-7lang) and
the unseen subset (FLEURS-Other).

5.1. Joint pre-training
Table 1 shows that continual SSL improves over direct fine-
tuning on FLEURS Test (47.2→43.4 WER), indicating that
additional acoustic adaptation helps reduce domain mismatch.
Joint SSL–CTC continued pre-training yields a larger improve-
ment (to 36.6 WER), suggesting that injecting symbolic con-
straints during representation shaping provides gains beyond
acoustic adaptation alone.
Importantly, our approach can operate in a continued pre-
training regime on top of a pretrained foundation model and
does not require retraining the SSL backbone from scratch. This
makes it computationally more efficient than prior joint-training
approaches while still achieving substantial performance gains.

5.4. Dual-codebook ablation
Table 4 reports the impact of dual-codebook quantization. Re-
placing a single shared codebook with the proposed dual-
codebook design reduces FLEURS Test WER from 36.6% to
35.5% (1.1% absolute reduction), with similar gains on the Dev
set. This gain can be attributed to reduced competition in mul-
tilingual discrete modeling. Under a single shared codebook,
high-resource languages tend to dominate code usage and lim-
iting representational capacity for low-resource or typologically
distinct languages. Such imbalance biases the discrete space to-
ward majority-language patterns and weakens cross-lingual ro-
bustness.
By contrast, the dual-codebook design decomposes the dis-
crete space into a global codebook for shared acoustic–phonetic
patterns and language-specific codebooks for residual varia-
tions. This structured factorization mitigates cross-language in-
terference and promotes clearer separation between shared and
language-dependent representations. The observed WER re-
duction therefore suggests that organizing discrete capacity ex-
plicitly during continued pre-training leads to better-aligned and
more transferable multilingual representations.

6. Conclusion
We proposed an alignment-aware continued pre-training frame-
work for multilingual ASR on top of foundation SSL mod-
els. By integrating joint SSL–CTC objectives, analyzing mod-
eling unit effects, and introducing structured dual codebooks,
we demonstrate that textual symbolic constraints during con-
tinued pre-training significantly improve multilingual ASR per-
formance. Future work may explore modeling units that dy-
namically integrate graphemes and phonemes, as well as scale
alignment-aware continual pre-training to much larger multilin-
gual speech datasets.
