Abstract
Neural audio codecs offer high compression efficiency but
introduce structured and non-linear distortions that severely de-
grade audio watermarks. To address this, we propose an adap-
tive codec-aware restoration framework that reconstructs codec-
distorted spectrograms prior to extraction. Our method employs
a ConvNeXt-based U-Net to recover fine-grained spectrogram
patterns and introduces a lightweight classifier to dynamically
select specialized restoration models for each codec. Exper-
imental results across various neural codecs demonstrate that
our model improves bitwise accuracy from near-random levels
to over 98% for RVQ-based codecs. Furthermore, by analyzing
the impact of quantizer configurations on watermark survivabil-
ity, we show that our method can effectively improve watermark
robustness in modern codec environments.

1. Introduction
Recent advances in generative artificial intelligence have sig-
nificantly transformed the way audio content is generated and
distributed. Modern speech synthesis and voice cloning sys-
tems are now capable of generating highly realistic speech that
closely resembles natural human recordings [1]. These tech-
nologies enable various applications, such as accessibility im-
provement, content production automation, and personalized
services. However, at the same time, they also introduce se-
rious risks including impersonation, misinformation, and unau-
thorized content manipulation [2].
As synthetic audio becomes increasingly indistinguishable
from real voices, the need for reliable verification and trace-
ability has rapidly increased. In response, watermarking has
emerged as a practical approach for identifying the origin of
generated audio [3, 4]. Audio watermarking embeds impercep-
tible information into signals while preserving perceptual qual-
ity. Recently, deep-learning-based watermarking methods have
demonstrated strong robustness against traditional signal pro-
cessing distortions such as additive noise, filtering, and lossy
compression.
Despite these advances, a new challenge has emerged due
to the widespread adoption of neural audio codecs. Neural
codecs, including EnCodec [5], DAC [6], and other learned
compression models, are increasingly used in real-world appli-
cations due to their superior compression efficiency compared
to conventional codecs. Unlike classical codecs that rely on
hand-crafted transforms, neural codecs compress audio through
learned latent representations and vector quantization. These

that are fundamentally different from conventional noise or fil-

Such distortions significantly degrade the subtle spectral
structures where watermark information is typically embedded

racy often drops to nearly random levels after passing through
neural codec compression, especially at low bit-rates [8]. Fur-
thermore, each codec affects embedded watermarks differently
depending on its architecture, number of quantizers, and train-
ing objectives. This heterogeneity makes it difficult for a single
restoration-based approach to generalize across codecs.
To address these challenges, we propose a codec-aware
adaptive watermark restoration framework. Instead of redesign-
ing the watermark embedding algorithm itself, we focus on
recovering watermark-relevant information before extraction.
Specifically, we insert a restoration network between the codec
output and the watermark extractor. The restoration network re-
constructs corrupted spectral details in the time–frequency do-
main, enabling more reliable extraction. In addition, we intro-
duce a lightweight codec classifier that dynamically selects a
specialized restoration model for each codec type. Experimen-
tal results demonstrate that our approach substantially improves
watermark extraction accuracy across various neural codecs,
confirming that restoration-based preprocessing is an effective
strategy for codec-robust watermarking.
2. Methodology
2.1. Watermarking Scheme
We employ the Timbre watermark model [9] as the underlying
watermarking framework, which embeds ownership informa-
tion into the spectrogram of the audio signal. Timbre employs a
repeated embedding strategy in which identical watermark fea-
tures are replicated along the time axis, resulting in high tem-
poral redundancy across spectrogram frames.
During the extraction process, the decoder aggregates these
features via average pooling across the time dimension. While
this mechanism provides inherent robustness against desyn-
chronization attacks such as cropping, it relies heavily on the in-
tegrity of fine-grained spectral structures, which are frequently
corrupted by the quantization processes of neural audio codecs.
2.2. Overall Pipeline
Instead of redesigning the watermark embedding scheme, we
formulate the problem as a restoration task. Our key hypothe-
sis is that watermark-related information is not completely de-
stroyed by neural codec compression but rather distorted in a
structured manner. Since the watermark is embedded across

2.3. Watermark Reconstruction with ConvNeXt U-Net
For restoration, we adopt a U-Net architecture inspired by self-
vocoding [10] approaches. Self-vocoding uses an enhancement
network to compensate for synthesis artifacts, and we apply
the same principle to compensate for codec-induced distortions.
The network follows a symmetric encoder–decoder structure
with skip connections, adopting the U-Net design employed
in Pix2Pix [11]. The encoder progressively downsamples the
spectrogram to capture high-level contextual features, while the
decoder reconstructs fine spectral details. Skip connections di-
rectly transfer low-level information from the encoder to the
decoder. This is particularly important for preserving weak wa-
termark patterns that may vanish during down-sampling.

blocks with ConvNeXt blocks [12]. The architecture of the
ConvNeXt block is illustrated in Fig. 2. A key motiva-

2.4. Adaptive Codec-Aware Restoration
Although a single restoration model improves performance, we
observe that different neural codecs introduce distinct distor-
tion patterns depending on their architecture and quantization
strategy. Consequently, a universal restoration network fails to
generalize well across diverse codecs. To address this limita-
tion, we adopt a codec-aware adaptive strategy. Specifically,
we train a dedicated restoration network for each neural codec.
Each model specializes in learning the distortion characteristics
of its corresponding codec, resulting in more accurate recon-
struction. During inference, a lightweight codec classifier first
identifies the applied codec. Based on the predicted label, the
corresponding restoration model is dynamically selected. In this
design, each restoration model serves as a specialized expert
for a specific codec-induced degradation, and only the selected
model is activated during inference.
3. Experiment
3.1. Experimental Setup
We adopt the Timbre watermarking scheme as the base wa-
termarking method. For training and evaluation, we utilize
the LJSpeech dataset [13], which consists of 13,100 short au-
dio clips from a single speaker. We partition the dataset into
training, validation, and test sets with a ratio of 8:1:1, respec-
tively. Restoration networks are trained on paired clean and
codec-distorted audio samples. Training is performed using a
batch size of 4 with gradient accumulation of 8. All experi-
ments are conducted on an NVIDIA RTX 4090 GPU, and each
model is trained for 200 epochs, selecting the best checkpoint
based on validation loss. We evaluate against a diverse set of
neural codecs, including EnCodec, DAC, AudioDec [14], Fun-
Codec [15], BigCodec [16], HiFi-Codec [17], SpeechTokenizer
[18], Mimi [19], UniCodec [20], StableCodec [21], and Wav-
Tokenizer [22].
Performance is measured using two metrics following. Bit-
wise Accuracy measures the percentage of correctly recovered
watermark bits, where 50% corresponds to random guessing.
Attribution Accuracy measures the proportion of test samples
for which all watermark bits are recovered correctly, reflecting
end-to-end watermark identification success [23].
3.2. Results
Prior to evaluating restoration performance, we verified that the
codec classifier achieved 100% accuracy under our evaluation
setting across all 11 neural codecs on the test set (1,310 sam-
ples per codec, 14,410 samples in total). This ensures that the
adaptive framework reliably identifies the codec type and routes
each input to the appropriate restoration model.

3.3. Ablation Study
To quantify the contribution of each architectural component,
we conduct an ablation study by progressively removing mod-
ules from the full framework, as summarized in Table 2. With-
out any restoration, watermark extraction accuracy remains
close to random guessing (59.01%). Using a single shared Con-
vNeXt U-Net without adaptive routing yields only a marginal
improvement (60.54%), indicating that a universal model strug-
gles to handle various codec distortions. Replacing ConvNeXt
with a basic CNN while retaining adaptive routing improves
accuracy to 70.44%, confirming that codec-specific specializa-
tion is highly effective. Finally, combining both ConvNeXt
and adaptive routing achieves the best performance (84.05%),
demonstrating that the two components are complementary:
adaptive routing enables codec-specific specialization, while
ConvNeXt provides stronger feature representation within each
specialized model.

4. Discussion
While our framework demonstrates robust performance on
RVQ-based codecs, we observe a clear performance limitation
with single-codebook models (Nq = 1) such as WavTokenizer.
We hypothesize that this limitation is related to an information
bottleneck effect [24]: whereas RVQ-based codecs preserve wa-
termark traces in residual layers, single-codebook codecs dis-
card fine-grained details, leaving insufficient residual signal for
restoration.
These findings suggest that our restoration-based strategy is
most effective when residual information is partially preserved,
and that fundamentally different approaches may be required
for extremely low bit-rate or single-codebook codecs.
From a practical standpoint, our adaptive framework offers
a notable extensibility advantage. While each codec requires
a dedicated restoration model, this design allows new codecs to
be seamlessly incorporated by training an additional specialized
model, without any modification to the existing watermarking
system or previously trained models. This makes our frame-
work well-suited for real-world scenarios where the codec land-
scape continues to evolve.
5. Conclusion
In this paper, we addressed the severe degradation of audio
watermark extraction caused by neural audio codec compres-
sion through a codec-aware adaptive restoration framework. In-
stead of redesigning the embedding scheme, we introduced a
ConvNeXt-based U-Net that reconstructs codec-distorted spec-
trograms prior to extraction, enabling seamless integration with
existing watermarking systems.
Extensive experiments across 11 neural codecs demonstrate
that our method consistently improves bitwise accuracy from
near-random levels to over 98% for RVQ-based codecs un-
der practical bit-rate conditions. Crucially, our analysis re-
vealed that restoration performance is fundamentally bounded
by the quantization structure: while structured distortions from
multi-codebook codecs are reversible, information erasure in
extremely low bit-rate single-codebook regimes presents a sig-
nificant challenge for post-hoc enhancement.
Overall, this work provides a practical solution for secur-
ing audio watermarks against neural codec distortions. Further-
more, our findings on the limits of post-extraction restoration
suggest that future watermarking schemes may benefit from be-
ing designed to withstand the specific quantization mechanisms
of neural compression.
