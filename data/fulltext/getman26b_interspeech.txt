Do Learned Layer Weights Reflect Pretrained Information Structure in
Self-Supervised Speech Models?

Abstract
Self-supervised speech models are commonly evaluated us-
ing learned weighted sums over frozen layers. Prior work con-
cluded these weights are uninformative, based on weak cor-
relation with single-layer performance. In contrast, we ex-
plore whether learned weights reflect the pretrained model’s
layerwise information content, measured by adjusted mutual in-
formation (AMI) between clustered layer representations and
phone or word labels. Across 13 SSL models, we find signifi-
cant correlations for most conditions, with strength depending
on supervision amount and pretraining objective. Weights af-
ter training with 10 minutes of labeled data consistently show
higher correlation with AMI than those from 1 hour. Con-
trastive models show strong correlation of up to 0.98, while
clustering-based ones yield weaker alignment. Despite prior
characterizations as unreliable, learned weights systematically
reflect pretrained information structure, particularly for con-
trastive models under limited supervision.
Index Terms: self-supervised learning, interpretation, auto-
matic speech recognition, layer weights
1. Introduction
Self-supervised learning (SSL) has enabled speech foundation
models whose internal representations encode different types
and amounts of acoustic and linguistic information at differ-
ent depths. Understanding this layerwise organization is an
active area of research approached from multiple perspectives.
Information-theoretic and correlation-based analyses have char-
acterized the content encoded at individual layers [1, 2, 3]. Sep-
arately, benchmarking frameworks such as SUPERB [4] and
ML-SUPERB [5] have established a protocol for downstream
evaluation that adopts learnable weighted sums over frozen
layers. Within this protocol, the learned layer weights have
been analyzed across tasks and models [5, 6, 7] and quantita-
tively compared to single-layer task performance [8, 2]. To-
gether, these studies establish that information in SSL models is
non-uniformly distributed across depth and that different down-
stream tasks benefit from different layers.
On the analysis side, [1] measured mutual information (MI)
between hidden representations and phone or word labels for
wav2vec 2.0 [9], finding non-monotonic trends where phonetic
and lexical content peaks at intermediate layers. Follow-up
work [2] extended the analysis to 11 SSL models using canoni-
cal correlation analysis (CCA), showing that layerwise patterns
differ systematically across pretraining objectives. These mea-
surements are conducted on frozen models, characterizing the
pretrained representations rather than downstream fine-tuning
effects.
A complementary line of work investigates how down-
stream systems combine layer representations. The SUPERB
framework [4] pairs a frozen SSL backbone with a lightweight
task head that receives a learned weighted sum of all layers.
[8] tested whether these learned weights reflect single-layer
downstream performance, finding correlations of onlyρfrom
0.39 to 0.71 with inconsistencies across tasks, and concluded
that “layer-weights are unreliable for interpreting the informa-
tion flow inside an SSL model.” [2] similarly reported a mean
ρ= 0.66between weights and task performance, characteriz-
ing weights as “relatively unreliable” compared to their CCA-
based analysis (ρ= 0.90). Both studies assessed weight in-
formativeness through correspondence with downstream per-
formance.
We ask a different question. Rather than testing whether
learned weights predict single-layer performance, we exam-
ine whether the full weight distribution reflects the pretrained
model’s layerwise information content, measured by adjusted
mutual information (AMI) [10] with phone and word labels.
Across 13 SSL models spanning three objective families
and three model sizes, we find statistically significant correla-
tions between ML-SUPERB ASR layer weights and layerwise
AMI for most model-condition combinations. The strength
of this alignment depends on two factors. First,supervi-
sion amount: weights learned after training with 10 min-
utes of labeled data show stronger alignment with AMI than
weights from 1 hour. Second,pretraining objective: con-
trastive models (wav2vec 2.0 family) show strong correlation
(meanρ= 0.86/0.91for phone / word AMI with 10min),
while clustering-based ones (HuBERT [11], WavLM [6]) yield
weaker and more variable correlations (meanρ= 0.67/0.77).
These results suggest that learned layer weights, despite prior
characterizations as unreliable, systematically reflect pretrained
information content, particularly for contrastive models under
limited supervision.
We make the following contributions:
1. We reframe prior characterization of trained layer weights as
uninformative by showing they reflect the pretrained model’s
layerwise information structure, as measured by significant
rank correlation with AMI across 13 SSL models spanning
three pretraining objectives and three model sizes.
2. We show that the weight distribution follows the pretrained
information structure most closely under limited supervision,
attenuating with additional labeled data.
3. We find that alignment strength varies systematically with
pretraining objective, with contrastive models yielding sub-
stantially stronger weight-AMI correlation than clustering-
based models.

2. Method
2.1. Layerwise information content
To characterize information encoded at each layer of a pre-
trained SSL model, we adopt the layerwise analysis protocol
of [1]. Specifically, for a model withLTransformer blocks,
representations are extracted from the convolutional feature en-
coder output (layer 0) and each Transformer layer (1,...,L),
yieldingL+ 1representation sets. Frame-level representa-
tions are then aggregated into segment-level embeddings using
forced alignments: phone representations are obtained by av-
eraging over the central third of each segment to limit coartic-
ulation at boundaries, and word representations are the mean
over the full aligned span. For each layer, the resulting segment
embeddings are clustered with k-means.
In [1], the dependence between cluster assignments and
true labels is measured using mutual information (MI). We in-
stead use adjusted mutual information (AMI) [10]. Raw MI
is sensitive to the size distribution of clusters: even with fixed
k, different layers produce different cluster size distributions,
and the expected MI under chance agreement varies accord-
ingly. AMI corrects for this by subtracting the expected MI
under chance agreement and normalizes by the maximum pos-
sible value, yielding scores between 0 for chance-level agree-
ment and 1 for a perfect match. This yields layerwise estimates
of phonetic and linguistic information content, derived solely
from pretrained representations and independent of downstream
optimization.
2.2. Learned layer weights
We obtain learned layer weights from the ML-SUPERB bench-
mark [5], which pairs a frozen SSL backbone with a shallow
downstream model. The architecture consists of a convolutional
downsample layer, followed by a two-layer Transformer with an
attention dimension of 256. The model receives a weighted sum
of all SSL layer outputs, with weights normalized via softmax.
These weights are initialized uniformly and optimized jointly
with the downstream model via CTC loss [12] for ASR. Since
the SSL model remains frozen during training, the weights con-
trol the relative contribution of each layer without modifying
the pretrained representations.
We use ML-SUPERB rather than the original SUPERB [4]
for several reasons. ML-SUPERB provides official training sets
at two supervision levels (10min and 1h) across three English
datasets, enabling controlled comparison of weight behavior un-
der different data regimes. It also defines a standardized multi-
lingual evaluation protocol, allowing future work to extend our
analysis to other languages.
2.3. SSL pretraining objectives
We evaluate models from three SSL objective families. All
three share a common architecture: a 7-layer convolutional fea-
ture encoder that downsamples raw audio into latent representa-
tions, followed by a stack of Transformer [13] encoder blocks.
Model scale varies by configuration:Basemodels consist of 12
layers with the hidden size of 768,Largemodels use 24 lay-
ers with the embedding dimension of 1024, andXLargemodels
have 48 layers with the dimension of 1280. During pretrain-
ing, spans of consecutive time steps are masked, and the model
learns to predict targets for the masked regions. The three fam-
ilies differ in how these targets are defined.
Contrastivemodels (wav2vec 2.0 [9]) learn contextual-
ized acoustic representations by solving a contrastive task over
masked speech frames, selecting the true quantized latent rep-
resentation from a set of distractors.Clustering-basedmodels
(HuBERT [11]) predict discrete pseudo-labels obtained by of-
fline k-means clustering of MFCC features or intermediate rep-
resentations.Clustering + denoisingmodels (WavLM [6]) ex-
tend the clustering objective with a denoising task, adding noise
or overlapping speech to inputs while predicting the pseudo-
labels of the original clean speech.
3. Experimental setup
Layerwise AMI.Following [1], we conduct layerwise analysis
on LibriSpeech [14]. Forced alignments are obtained from the
Montreal Forced Aligner [15, 16]. For each layer, segment rep-
resentations are clustered with mini-batch k-means (k= 500
for phones,k= 5000for words). Four train-test splits are sam-
pled for 39 phones and 500 words. Each split is clustered four
times, and AMI values are averaged across all splits and runs.
ML-SUPERB ASR.We run the ML-SUPERB monolingual
English ASR experiment using the official ESPnet [17] recipe
with default hyperparameters and the standard downstream ar-
chitecture. We follow the official benchmark protocol, ex-
cept that we apply layer normalization to each hidden layer
output before aggregation to ensure that the learned weights
reflect layer importance rather than differences in numerical
scale across layers. Training uses three datasets (MLS [18],
NCHLT [19], and V oxPopuli [20]), each with 10min and 1h
labeled training sets. The learned layer weights are averaged
across the three datasets for each supervision regime (standard
deviation<0.001across datasets).
Models.We evaluate 13 SSL models. Thecontrastivefamily
includes seven wav2vec 2.0 variants: wav2vec 2.0 Base and
Large pretrained on LibriSpeech (960h), wav2vec 2.0 Large
pretrained on Libri-Light (60kh) [21], and multilingual XLS-
R [22] and OmniASR [23] in Large and XLarge sizes, pre-
trained on 436kh and 4.3Mh of multilingual speech, respec-
tively. Theclusteringfamily includes HuBERT Base pretrained
on LibriSpeech, and Large and XLarge pretrained on Libri-
Light. Theclustering + denoisingmodels are WavLM Base
(LibriSpeech), WavLM Base+ (94kh mixed data from Libri-
Light, GigaSpeech [24], and V oxPopuli), and WavLM Large
(94kh mixed data).
Statistical analysis.Spearman’s rank correlationρis com-
puted between the learned layer weights and layerwise AMI for
each model-condition combination. Statistical significance is
assessed via permutation test with 5000 permutations.

4.2. Pretraining objective
Contrastive models achieve the strongest and most consistent
correlations, particularly with 10min of labeled data (mean
ρ= 0.86/0.91for phone / word AMI), with individual values
ranging from0.69to0.98. Alignment is observed across model
sizes (Base to XLarge) and substantially different pretraining
corpora, from 960h of English speech to 4M-hour multilingual
datasets. With 1h of labeled data, correlations decrease (mean
ρ= 0.65/0.70) but remain statistically significant for all con-
trastive models. As shown in Figure 1, both layer weights and
AMI yield non-monotonic trends across layers, increasing to-
ward intermediate-layer peaks and then decaying.
In contrast, clustering-based and clustering + denoising
models show weaker and more variable alignment. Two pat-
terns distinguish them from contrastive models. First, phone
and word AMI alignment can differ substantially within a
model. For instance, HuBERT-XL showsρ= 0.45for phone
but0.87for word AMI. Second, alignment varies more with
model size: Base models tend toward stronger correlations,
while larger models show weaker results: WavLM Large yields
the weakest phone AMI alignment overall (ρ= 0.53/0.32for
10min / 1h). Compared to contrastive models, clustering-based
models produce broader and more gradual layerwise trends in
both weights and AMI (Figure 1), and their weight-AMI cor-
respondence varies more across AMI type, model scale, and
supervision regime.
5. Discussion
Prior work found moderate correlations between learned layer
weights and single-layer task performance (ρfrom0.39to0.71
across tasks) and concluded that weights are unreliable indica-
tors of model behavior [8]. Notably, phoneme recognition, the
task most comparable to our ASR setting, yielded the weak-
est correlation (ρ= 0.39). We address a different question:
whether the weight distribution corresponds to the pretrained
model’s layerwise information content, as measured by AMI.
We find a stronger alignment, with contrastive models reaching
a meanρ= 0.86and0.91for phone and word AMI with 10min
of labeled data, and individual values up toρ= 0.98. This sug-
gests that the limited weight-performance relationship observed

The stronger alignment with less labeled data holds across
model families. In ML-SUPERB, the SSL backbone is frozen
and optimization proceeds only through a lightweight task head
trained with a CTC objective. Layer weights are therefore not
directly supervised but adjusted indirectly as part of end-to-
end ASR training. Under these constrained conditions, both
10min and 1h weights remain close to uniform (normalized
entropy>0.996), yet the observed deviations are systemati-
cally aligned with AMI rather than random, and all three non-
significant cases occur in the 1h condition. With limited su-
pervision, the weight distribution largely follows the pretrained
information structure, while increasing supervision results in
more concentrated weight distributions.
The alignment is strongest and most consistent for con-
trastive wav2vec 2.0 models, where it holds across seven vari-
ants differing in size, pretraining data scale, and mono- vs. mul-
tilingual pretraining. However, the strength of this alignment
depends on the pretraining objective: for clustering-based mod-
els (HuBERT, WavLM), it is weaker and more variable. This
difference may relate to how pretraining targets are derived:
wav2vec 2.0 jointly learns representations and quantization tar-
gets, while HuBERT and WavLM optimize against fixed offline
pseudo-labels. Whether this accounts for the weaker correspon-
dence remains an open question. Learned weights reflect layer-
wise AMI in contrastive models, while alternative information
measures may be needed to further explain weight selection for
other pretraining objectives.
Our analysis is restricted to a single downstream task and
correspondingly to phone and word AMI as the phonetic and
linguistic measures directly relevant to ASR. Non-ASR tasks
may rely on different aspects of the pretrained representations.
Extension to such tasks would require task-relevant information
measures and remains an important future work direction.
6. Conclusion
We have analyzed the learned layer weights of SSL speech mod-
els for frozen downstream ASR in ML-SUPERB. Across 13
models spanning contrastive and clustering-based objectives,
we show that the learned weights correlate significantly with
the pretrained model’s internal phonetic and linguistic infor-
mation flow, as measured by layerwise AMI. This alignment
is strongest for models with contrastive pretraining, holding
across seven wav2vec 2.0 variants of varying size and pretrain-
ing data. We further show that weight selection follows these
internal information patterns more strongly in low-resource set-
tings and attenuates with additional labeled data. Our findings
complement prior work: while weights may not reliably predict
single-layer downstream performance, their full distribution re-
flects pretrained information structure.
