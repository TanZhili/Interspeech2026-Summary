to Cantonese Tone-in-Noise Perception in Native Speakers

Abstract 
Despite superior pitch processing in tonal-language speakers, 
tone perception remains vulnerable in noise. Whether non -
linguistic pitch training can sharpen the psychophysical acuity 
underlying established tonal categories remains unexplored. 29 
native Cantonese adults completed 8 perceptual discrimination 
training sessions over 14 days, using pure tones between 126–
217 Hz. Near transfer was assessed via oddity tasks; far transfer 
via word identification in clean, 0 dB and −5 dB SNRs multi-
talker babble noise, including a novel-speaker test. Results 
showed: (1) discrimination thresholds decreased for level and 
contour stimuli; (2) word identification improved more 
markedly in noise, with benefits on T1, T3 and T4 generalising 
to a novel speaker, while T2, T5 and T6 exhibited limited 
measurable gains. These findings support a condition -
dependent vertical transfer model and suggest that tones in 
crowded tone space require targeted training beyond domain-
general pitch training. 
Index Terms: Cantonese tone perception; Non-linguistic Pitch 
Training; Perceptual Learning; Speech-in-Noise 
1. Introduction 
Previous studies have suggested that linguistic and non -
linguistic pitch processing draw on shared perceptual 
mechanisms [1], particularly at early auditory stages [2]. Tone 
language speakers, for instance, exhibit enhanced pitch 
perception not only for non-speech stimuli such as pure tones 
and musical intervals but also for unfamiliar tonal contrasts in 
non-native languages, indicating that lifelong tonal experience 
shapes domain-general pitch representations [3]. While some 
studies emphasize the relative autonomy of speech and non-
speech tone processing, they nonetheless acknowledge shared 
low-level perceptual substrates[4, 5]. Indeed, recent work 
demonstrates that lower-level acoustics underlie higher-level 
phonological categorization in tone perception [6]. These 
findings collectively motivate the hypothesis that non-linguistic 
acoustic training may enhance linguistic pitch processing 
through shared psychophysical mechanisms. 
Despite superior pitch processing competence, lexical tone 
perception does not seem to be immune to signal degradation 
among tone language speakers. Cross-linguistically, under 
adverse listening conditions (particularly multi-talker babble 
noise), fundamental frequency (F0) cues critical for tone 
identification become informationally masked as competing 
lexical and tonal content depletes processing resources and 
disrupts target F0 extraction [7, 8, 9, 10, 11]. Given that F0 
carries crucial lexical contrast in tone languages, such masking 
poses substantial communicative challenges.

If this bottleneck resides at the level of low -level pitch 
resolution, targeted psychophysical training should strengthen 
pitch representations and improve tone-in-noise perception. 
Perceptual learning evidence is consistent with this prediction: 
spectral-temporal training improves speech intelligibility in 
noise [12, 13], and non-speech F0 discrimination training can 
transfer speech perception in noise among native tone language 
speakers [14]. The evidence showing that strengthened low-
level processing can support higher-level perception when 
signals are degraded is termed “Vertical Transfer” [14].  Within 
this framework, near transfer denotes gains on new tasks within 
the same psychophysical domain, whereas far transfer denotes 
gains on qualitatively different tasks that draw on the trained 
representations. These findings collectively predict that non-
linguistic pitch training could benefit native tone perception in 
noise via far transfer. 
Furthermore, Cantonese tone perception appears inherently 
more vulnerable than other tonal languages such as Mandarin 
[15]. For healthy Cantonese young adults, tone identification 
drops substantially in noise, particularly for tones whose inter-
tone distances are smaller. Even in the quiet condition, isolated-
syllable identification is far from ceiling—accuracy for T3 and 
T6 reaches only approximately 70% and 42%, respectively. 
Noise further exacerbates the bidirectional T2–T5 confusion, 
while T4, as the sole falling tone, remains stable across 
conditions [16]. This vulnerability stems from the crowded tone 
space of Cantonese [17]. Its six-tone system relies on both pitch 
height and pitch contour for lexical contrast [18, 19, 20, 21]: 
three level tones (T1: high-level [55], T3: mid-level [33], and 
T6: low-level [22]) are distinguished by relative F0 height, 
while contour tones (T2: high-rising [25], T4: low-falling [21], 
T5: mid-rising [23]) are further differentiated by F0 movement 
[17, 22, 23]. Because listeners must combine static height and 
dynamic contour cues while also accounting for talker- and 
context-driven variability [24], tone categories are prone to F0 
overlap [25]. This is most problematic for tones where small 
inter-tone distances promote confusion and contribute to the 
ongoing merger patterns [17]. Context can mitigate this 
ambiguity, but its benefit is limited when signal quality is poor 
or masking noise is strong [26, 27]. Developmentally, 
Cantonese-speaking children acquire the use of native-language 
context for tone identification later than their Mandarin -
speaking counterparts, likely reflecting the greater perceptual 
demands [28]. 
 Prior work on native Cantonese listeners has investigated 
whether non-linguistic pitch training transfers to lexical tone 
perception in quiet, typically showing limited improvement in 
discriminating native tonal contrasts [4]. It remains unclear 
whether this lack of improvement reflects a ceiling effect in 
quiet conditions, where the signal is already clear, or whether 
non-linguistic training has a limited impact on language -
specific tone processing. This uncertainty highlights the need to

explore whether such training can enhance lexical tone 
perception, particularly when tone categories are already well-

improves tone identification in noise can help clarify whether 
training potentially sharpens the psychophysical representation 
of tonal categories. If non-linguistic pitch training results in 
robust gains detectable under masking across all tonal 
categories, this would support the idea that quiet conditions 
simply reflect a ceiling effect. In this case, speech-in-noise 
difficulties likely stem from reduced pitch resolution, which 
non-linguistic training could enhance. Conversely, if no 
improvement is observed, it would suggest that non-linguistic 
training lacks the capacity to address higher-level linguistic 
demands. We therefore test whether (1) non-linguistic training 
can sharpen psychophysical pitch acuity and (2) this sharpening 
improves lexical tone perception in adverse listening 
conditions. 
2. Methods  
2.1. Participants 
Thirty-three adult participants aged 19 to 32 years (Mean = 
22.34 years, SD = 3.24 years) were recruited in the Hong Kong 
Special Administrative Region (Hong Kong hereafter) via 
poster advertisements on the university campus. All 
participants were native speakers of Cantonese. Hearing 
sensitivity was assessed using pure-tone audiometry, and all 
participants exhibited normal auditory thresholds. None of the 
participants reported a history of neurological, developmental, 
or cognitive disorders. Five participants reported prior formal 
musical training; however, none had engaged in mu sical 
practice within three years prior to participation, and none 
identified themselves as musicians. Four participants withdrew 
from the experiment before completion, leaving data from 
twenty-nine participants (12 females) for inclusion in the final 
analyses. 
2.2. Training Materials and Procedure 
The experiment consisted of three phases: a pre-training phase, 
an online training phase, and a post-training phase, with both 
pre- and post-training phases conducted in a soundproof room. 
The pre-training phase included three sessions of a word 
identification test and three sessions of a pitch sensitivity test 
(see Sections 2.2.1 and 2.2.2 for details). This was followed by 
an eight-session online pitch training programme completed at 
home, employing an ABX discrimination paradigm. The post-
training phase mirrored the pre-training one, including three 
sessions each of the word identification and pitch sensitivity 
tests. Additionally, three word-identification sessions using 
stimuli produced by two novel talkers were included as a 
generalization test in this phase.  
2.2.1. Word Identification Test 
The word identification task closely followed the procedure 
described in Tao et al. [29], with minor adaptations. The stimuli 
consisted of Cantonese monosyllables that contrasted in lexical 
tone while sharing the same segmental structure. Three base 
syllables, namely, /fan/, /fu/, and /ji/, were selected to sample 
major regions of the Cantonese vowel space and to minimize 
segmental confounds [30]. An additional syllable, /se/, served 
as a context stimulus to familiarize listeners with each speaker’s 
pitch range and to facilitate pitch normalization [31, 32]. Each 
syllable was produced with the six Cantonese tones. Stimuli 
used in pre- and post-training word identification tests were 
recorded by two native Hong Kong Cantonese speakers (one 
male, one female), yielding 48 tokens (four syllables × six tones 
× two speakers). In the post -training phase, an extra 
generalization test was included in which the identification task 
employed stimuli produced by two additional, untrained native 
talkers (one male, one female), allowing assessment of training 
generalization across speakers. The three word identification 
sessions consisted of three listening conditions: a no -noise 
condition and two noise-masked conditions, in which stimuli 
were presented with 6-talker babble noise at signal-to-noise 
ratios (SNRs) of 0 dB and -5 dB. All stimuli were normalized 
to 70 dB based on mean square amplitude and to a duration of 
450 ms using Praat. On each trial, participants heard one 
stimulus and identified its tone category by selecting one of six 
visually presented word options corresponding to the six tonal 
variants of the same base syllable, using a six-alternative 
forced-choice response format. Practice trials were provided 
prior to the experiment to familiarize participants with the task.  
2.2.2. Pitch Sensitivity Test 
The pitch sensitivity task consisted of two subcomponents 
targeting sensitivity to level tones and contour tones, 
respectively. Both subcomponents employed a three-interval 
oddity paradigm: on each trial, participants heard three pure-
tone stimuli and indicated which interval contained the tone that 
differed from the other two. The task was administered via a 
custom HTML-based web interface in a soundproof laboratory. 
Sensitivity to level tones was assessed using a pure-tone 
discrimination task in which difficulty was parametrically 
manipulated based on semitone (st) differences. A single 
reference frequency was defined as the mean of two previously 
established pitch ranges, yielding a base frequency of 173.2 Hz, 
averaged across the F0 of  all speakers reported in [25]. Adapted 
from Zatorre et al. [33] and Giuliano et al. [3], we designed ten 
difficulty levels, corresponding to progressively smaller pitch 
differences between the reference tone and the comparison tone 
(2, 1.5, 1, 0.7, 0.5, 0.3, 0.2, 0.1, 0.05, and 0.03 st). All 
comparison stimuli were generated within lower and upper F0 
bounds (in Hz), calculated by averaging the corresponding male 
and female bounds reported in the same study, resulting in a 
range of 127.67–217.03 Hz [25]. The stimulus generation 
procedure is illustrated in the Supplementary Materials.  
Sensitivity to contour tones was assessed using a dynamic 
pitch discrimination task in which difficulty was manipulated 
by varying the duration of the frequency modulation window. 
The pitch range was identical to that of the level stimuli, with a 
schematic illustration of the stimulus generation procedure 
provided in the Supplementary Materials. Contour tones were 
created via sinusoidal modulation of the F0 contour, with rising 
tones spanning a sine phase of 1.5π–2.5π and falling tones 
spanning 0.5π–1.5π. This manipulation was adapted from prior 
work on dynamic pitch perception [34]. Ten difficulty levels 
were designed by scaling the modulation window relative to a 
standard stimulus (100%). The standard stimulus contained a 
400-ms modulation window centered within a 450-ms token, 
leaving 25-ms pre- and post-modulation plateaus. For 
comparison stimuli, the modulation window was proportionally 
shortened to 10%–95% of the standard duration while 
remaining temporally centered; smaller percentages (i.e., 
shorter windows) resulted in steeper F0 slopes and thus lower 
task difficulty. For example, at difficulty level 3 (30% 
modulation window), the modulation window lasted 120 ms 
(400 ms × 30%).  
The duration of every stimuli was normalized to 450 ms 
(sampling rate: 44.1 kHz), with an inter-stimulus interval (ISI) 
of 1000 ms between the three stimuli in each oddity-task trial 
[33]. For both subcomponents, task difficulty was adjusted 
using an adaptive 2-down-1-up staircase procedure. Each run 
terminated after ten reversals. Individual just noticeable 
differences (JNDs) were estimated online based on the final six 
reversal points of the adaptive track [35]. For level tones, JNDs 
were indexed by the minimal st differences between two sounds 
while for contour tones, JNDs were defined as the minimal 
modulation-window duration required for reliable contour 
discrimination. Smaller JNDs indicated higher pitch sensitivity. 
2.2.3. Online Training Session 
Online training was completed on participants’ own devices via 
a web-based interface, using wired headphones. Training began

within 48 hours after the pre-training phase, was conducted for 
eight sessions within two weeks, and was followed by the post-
training phase within 48 hours of the final session. Each training 
session included both level -tone and contour -tone 
subcomponents, with their order randomized across sessions. 
Training employed an ABX discrimination task, in which 
participants indicated whether the third stimulus matched the 
first or the second. Stimulus generation and difficulty 
manipulation followed the same principles as in the pitch 
sensitivity task, with difficulty indexed by st differences for 
level tones and modulation window duration for contour tones. 
Trial-by-trial feedback was provided: participants were 
informed of response correctness, and incorrect trials were 
followed by three repetitions of the same stimulus configuration 
[33]. Task difficulty was adjusted using a 2-down-1-up adaptive 
staircase, and individual JNDs were estimated from the final six 
reversal points of each run. 
3. Results 
3.1. Within-training Changes in JNDs 
Performance changes during the online training phase are 
illustrated in Figure 1. They were examined separately for level 
and contour tones using repeated-measures ANOVAs with 
Session (1 – 8) as a within-subject factor. For level tones, the 
effect of Session was not significant for higher thresholds, F(7, 
186) = 1.25, p = .28, nor for lower thresholds, F(7, 186) = 0.59, 
p = .77. Planned comparisons between the first and final 
training sessions did not reach significance (higher: p = 1.00; 
lower: p = .25). For contour tones, different learning patterns 
were observed for rising and falling pitch contours. For rising 
tones, a significant main effect of Session was found, F(7, 188) 
= 2.74, p = .01, indicating changes in JNDs over the course of 
training. Bonferroni-corrected pairwise comparisons identified 
a reliable difference between Session 1 and Session 3 (p = .04), 
whereas the planned comparison between the first and final 
sessions was not significant (p = .79), suggesting a non-
monotonic learning trajectory. In contrast, falling tone JNDs 
did not show a significant overall effect of Session, F(7, 187) = 
1.64, p = .13. Nevertheless, a planned comparison revealed a 
significant reduction in JNDs between the first and the final 
training session, F(1, 27) = 6.14, p = .02.

3.2. Pitch Sensitivity Test (Oddity Task) 
Comparisons of JNDs between pre- and post-training pitch 
sensitivity tests using oddity tasks are shown in Figure 2. For 
level tones, a Session (pre-training vs. post-training) × 
Direction (lower vs. higher threshold) repeated -measures 
ANOVA showed a significant main effect of Session (F(1, 27) 
= 4.24, p = .049, ηp² = .136), with JNDs decreasing from pre-
training to post-training tests. Neither the main effect of 
Direction nor the Session × Direction interaction was 
significant (both ps > .05).  
For contour tones, another Session (pre-training vs. post-
training) × Direction (rising vs. falling) repeated-measures 
ANOVA on JNDs revealed a robust main effect of Session (F(1, 
27) = 35.00, p < .001, ηp² = .57), with JNDs decreasing from the 
pre-training to the post-training test. The Session × Direction 
interaction was significant (F(1, 27) = 4.22, p = .05, ηp² = .14). 
Follow-up comparisons confirmed significant JND reductions 
for both contour types (rising: t(27) = 4.79, p < .001; falling: 
t(27) = 5.51, p < .001). Additionally, the JND of rising contours 
was smaller than that of falling ones at pre-training test (t(27) = 
−2.91, p = .01), whereas this difference was insignificant at the 
post-training test (p = .57).

3.3. Word Identification Test 
Word identification accuracy across tests and noise conditions 
is presented in Figure 3, which was analyzed using a repeated-
measures ANOVA with Session (pre-training test, post-training 
test, and generalization test), Noise Condition (clean, 0 dB 
SNR, and -5 dB SNR), and Tone Category (T1, T2, T3, T4, T5, 
and T6) as within-subject factors. All main effects and 
interactions were statistically significant, including the three-
way interaction among Session, Noise Condition, and Tone 
Category (all ps < .01).  
The main effect of Session (F(1.44, 38.94) = 49.67, p 
< .001, ηp² = .65) was significant. Post-hoc comparisons showed 
that identification accuracy was lower in the pre-training than 
in both the post-training and generalization tests (both ps < .01). 
Accuracy was slightly but reliably higher in the post-training 
than generalization test (p < .01). There was a strong main effect 
of Noise Condition (F(1.60, 43.20) = 125.28, p < .01, ηp² = .82). 
Accuracy was highest in the clean condition, intermediate at 0 
dB SNR, and lowest at -5 dB SNR (all ps < .01). The main effect 
of Tone Category was also significant (F(3.44, 92.94) = 20.27, 
p < .001, ηp² = .429), as T1 was identified more accurately than 
all other tone categories (all ps < .01). Accuracy did not differ 
reliably between T2 and T3, nor between T5 and T6, with T4 
showing intermediate performance.

4. Discussion and Conclusion 
The present study examined whether non -linguistic pitch 
training can sharpen the psychophysical acuity underlying 
already-established native tone categories, and whether such 
sharpening translates into measurable gains in lexical tone 
perception under adverse listening conditions. Three key 
findings collectively support near transfer at the psychophysical 
level and conditional cross-domain far transfer that emerges 
primarily when the perceptual processing is stressed by signal 
degradation. First, for near transfer measured by pitch oddity 
tasks, JNDs for both level and contour subcomponents 
decreased significantly after eight sessions of adaptive ABX 
pitch discrimination training, confirming robust near transfer 
and validating the training protocol. Second, for far transfer 
indexed by lexical tone identification tasks , the most 
pronounced improvements emerged almost exclusively under 
noise-degraded conditions, particularly at moderate noise levels 
(0 dB SNR), whereas limited reliable gains were observed in 
the noise-free condition, with the sole exception of the low-
level tone (T6). Third, Tone-specific training effects: Tones 
occupying distinct pitch space (i.e., T1, T3, T4) showed 
significant improvement that generalized to novel talkers, 
whereas tones sharing overlapping tone distribution (i.e., T2, 
T5, T6) exhibited limited gains restricted to the trained talker.  
Successful near transfer is supported by the significant pre-
to-post JND reductions on the oddity task , confirming our 
hypothesis that eight sessions of adaptive training improved 
pitch resolution for both level and contour tones. For level pure 
tones, benefits extended bidirectionally. No within-session 
significant progress was observed, yet reliable pre-to-post gains 
still emerged, suggesting either a task-specific ceiling of static 
pitch discrimination or offline consolidation between sessions, 
consistent with the broader perceptual learning literature [36, 
37]. For contour tones, JNDs decreased in both directions, with 
larger gains for falling contours eliminat ing the pretest 
directional asymmetry. This is probably due to the low-level 
perceptual salience advantage enjoyed by rising contours [38],
