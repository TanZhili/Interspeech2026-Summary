Robust Streaming ASR with Decoupled Separation and Recognition
Yufeng Yang1, Cheng Yu1, Vahid Ahmadi Kalkhorani1, DeLiang Wang2
1 Department of Computer Science and Engineering, The Ohio State University, USA
2 School of Data Science, The Chinese University of Hong Kong, Shenzhen, China
yang.5662@osu.edu
Abstract
Real-time speech transcription that is robust to background
noise, room reverberation, and interfering speakers is critical
for many real-world automatic speech recognition (ASR) appli-
cations. To address these interferences, multi-condition train-
ing (MCT) is commonly employed for training an ASR model,
but it requires large-scale training data and degrades recogni-
tion performance on clean speech. In this work, we propose
a decoupled framework that leverages an online speech sep-
aration frontend, and a streaming ASR backend trained only
on clean speech, for robust streaming ASR. We benchmark the
proposed approach on LibriSpeech, CHiME-4, and LibriCSS
datasets, and demonstrate that the decoupled framework con-
sistently outperforms the MCT-based baselines for streaming
ASR. Our approach is also shown to generalize to large pre-
trained speech models, offering a modular approach for robust
streaming ASR that avoids task-specific re-training and MCT.
Index Terms: CHiME-4, LibriCSS, online speech separation,
robust ASR, streaming ASR
1. Introduction
Over the past decades, automatic speech recognition (ASR)
has evolved from Gaussian mixture model (GMM) and hidden
Markov model (HMM) systems [1, 2] to deep learning based
end-to-end architectures [3, 4], leading to substantial improve-
ment in recognition accuracy and reaching human-level perfor-
mance in relatively clean conditions [5]. In modern speech ap-
plications, streaming ASR has become increasingly needed for
voice assistants, real-time captioning, and spoken dialogue sys-
tems, where low latency and reliable transcription are both im-
portant [6].
The transition toward streaming ASR has been marked by
a series of architectural advancements, including recurrent neu-
ral network transducer (RNN-T) [7] and chunk-based attention
mechanisms [8]. The evaluation of streaming ASR systems
is mainly focused on the word error rate (WER) and algorith-
mic latency [9]. However, real-world deployment is subject to
background noise, room reverberation, and interfering speak-
ers, and streaming ASR in such acoustic conditions is underex-
plored with existing systems. While general ASR robustness
has been examined through frameworks such as Speech Ro-
bust Bench [10], a benchmark for evaluating the robustness of
streaming ASR remains lacking. This gap can be problematic
as streaming models, constrained by limited future context, of-
ten exhibit different failure modes under noise compared with
offline models. Thus, we investigate streaming ASR systems
beyond clean-speech datasets to evaluate their robustness.
To mitigate the impact of acoustic interference, the most
prevalent approach is multi-condition training (MCT), where an
ASR model is exposed to a large, diverse amount of background
noise during training. While MCT improves generalization,
it often fails to reach the performance of clean-trained mod-
els when tested on clean speech. An alternative approach in-
volves integrating a speech separation (SS) frontend. However,
the SS frontend and ASR backend are subject to a mismatch
effect, where the separated speech deviates from the ASR train-
ing data. In recent years, as SS progresses, prior studies [11,12]
find that decoupled SS and ASR trained only on clean speech
can outperform MCT-based systems.
In this paper, we present a decoupled framework for ro-
bust streaming ASR, consisting of an online SS frontend and
a streaming ASR backend trained only on clean speech. We
evaluate the system comprehensively and benchmark the re-
sults on both simulated and real data. Specifically, we inves-
tigate online SS frontends such as open-sourced DPDFNet [13]
and TF-CrossNet [14]. For the ASR backend, we propose
FastMambaformer, a novel architecture built upon FastCon-
former [15, 16] and leveraging Mamba [17] to improve the
modeling capability in a Conformer [18] encoder. In addition,
we employ multiple representative pre-trained streaming ASR
models, such as NeMo [19] pre-trained FastConformer [15,16].
We train all models on LibriSpeech [20] and test additionally
on CHiME-4 [21] and LibriCSS [22] datasets, demonstrating
the cross-corpus generalizability of the decoupled framework.
We show that, given a sufficiently strong online SS frontend,
we can eliminate MCT with a much smaller cost of ASR train-
ing, and much more flexibility.
The main contributions are summarized as follows. First,
we establish a benchmark for robust streaming ASR by com-
prehensively evaluating multiple online SS and streaming ASR
models under streaming constraints (zero lookahead). Second,
we propose FastMambaformer, a new streaming ASR model
that outperforms FastConformer. Third, we demonstrate that
a decoupled system, utilizing a strong online SS frontend and
clean-trained ASR backend, is able to outperform MCT-based
models in streaming conditions, which has not been reported in
prior studies. Fourth, we show that the benefit of the decou-
pled framework is architecture-agnostic by integrating different
frontend-backend model architectures. This suggests a promis-
ing direction for improving robustness without task-specific re-
training or fine-tuning, and the potential to benefit audio-based
foundation models with an SS frontend.
The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. Sec-
tion 2 describes the architectures of the frontends and backends.
Section 3 describes the experimental setup and implementation
details. Section 4 presents the results and comparisons. Con-
clusions and discussions are made in Section 5.

2. Methods
2.1. Overall Decoupled Framework
The comparison between the decoupled framework and the
MCT-based system is shown in Figure 1. The online SS
model and MCT-based ASR model are trained on a comparable
amount of data. The MCT-based model mitigates the interfer-
ence but achieves suboptimal performance on clean speech. In
the decoupled framework, the online SS frontend and streaming
ASR backends are trained separately, with full flexibility for im-
proving either model when a more advanced model is available,
without additional task-specific re-training or fine-tuning.
2.2. Online Speech Separation Frontend
2.2.1. DPDFNet
We employ DPDFNet [13], an online SS model that extends
the DeepFilterNet2 architecture [23] with dual-path blocks in
the encoder. It strengthens long-range temporal and cross-band
modeling while preserving the original framework compared
with DeepFilterNet2. The model uses grouped temporal con-
volutions and dual-path recurrent units, merges high-frequency
bands through an equivalent rectangular bandwidth filter bank,
and applies temporal recurrent attention.
2.2.2. TF-CrossNet
We utilize TF-CrossNet [14] as another SS frontend. TF-
CrossNet consists of a cross-temporal module, a cross-band
module, and positional encoding. It performs complex spectral
mapping by predicting the real and imaginary (RI) parts of the
short-time Fourier transform (STFT) of clean speech from the
stacked RI parts of the STFT of noisy speech [24, 25]. The sep-
arated waveform is generated by performing an inverse STFT
on the separated RI parts. The TF-CrossNet is designed to per-
form offline SS. By adding causal attention masks and making
all convolution layers causal, TF-CrossNet is modified into an
online SS model with zero frame lookahead.
2.3. Streaming ASR Backend
2.3.1. FastMambaformer
We propose FastMambaformer, a new ASR architecture based
on the FastConformer [15, 16] backbone, which is a state-of-
the-art open-sourced streaming ASR model. We replace the
convolutional module with Mamba [17] to leverage better se-
quence information than the convolutional module, in addi-
tion to the multi-head attention mechanism. Mamba is a se-
lective state-space model (SSM), which is designed to enable
efficient, linear-time sequence modeling by parameterizing the
state-space dynamics as functions of the input at each time step.
Unlike traditional time-invariant SSMs, where state transition
and output matrices remain fixed, Mamba’s parameters are dy-
namically generated from the current input, allowing the model
to selectively propagate or attenuate relevant information across
long contexts. This gives better sequence modeling capability
than the convolutional module in FastConformer.
2.3.2. SimulStreaming
SimulStreaming [26] is a streaming ASR model built on
Whisper [5] and combines Simul-Whisper [27] and Whisper-
Streaming [28], leveraging the knowledge from the Whisper
large-v3 model but adapting it for streaming ASR, which is the
state-of-the-art Whisper variant for streaming ASR. The adap-
tation is done by segmenting the input speech using a buffering
mechanism and splitting the continuous audio into short, incre-
mental chunks that are appended into a rolling audio buffer. At
each update step, the accumulated buffer is encoded, and the de-
coder generates output tokens conditioned on the partial audio
and previously decoded text, following a simultaneous decod-
ing policy to decide how much of the decoded hypothesis can be
emitted without waiting for future frames. The model maintains
multiple internal buffers to handle overlapping contexts across
chunks and to preserve previously decoded text for continuity.
3. Experimental Setup
3.1. Datasets
To evaluate the robust streaming ASR systems, we experiment
on the LibriSpeech [20], CHiME-4 [21], and LibriCSS [22]
datasets. All models are trained only on LibriSpeech and
evaluated on three datasets. This provides insights into the
cross-corpus generalizability and the robustness to unseen back-
ground noise, room reverberation, and overlapping speakers for
each system. The sampling rate for all utterances is 16 kHz.
For LibriSpeech, the training data is generated by mixing
all 960 hr training sets with noise segments randomly sam-
pled from the 10k non-speech sounds of a sound effect library
(https://sound-ideas.com) with signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) uni-
formly selected in [-5, 0] and [0, 10] dB ranges, with 50% prob-
ability for each range. Validation data is generated by mixing all
development datasets with the factory noise from the NOISEX-
92 dataset [29] at -5 dB SNR. The evaluation sets are generated
by mixing the test-other set with ADTBabble and ADTCafete-
ria noises (https://auditec.com) at{-5, -2, 0, 2, 5, 10}dB SNR
levels.
We evaluate on the CHiME-4 dataset, which comprises
simulated and real noisy speech using the Wall Street Journal
(WSJ) [30] text material. The test set consists of 1320 simu-
lated and 1320 real noisy utterances from 4 different speakers.
Real data is recorded in noisy environments (bus, cafe, pedes-
trian area, and street) spoken by real talkers. Simulated data
is generated by mixing recorded speech via a close-talking mi-
crophone and separately recorded noises. We select the official
1-ch track data for evaluation.

tion, which is more suitable for monaural streaming ASR. From

3.2. Implementation Details
3.2.1. Frontend Configuration
For DPDFNet, we utilize the pre-trained dpdfnet8 model 2 with
3.54M parameters for the best quality, following the official
script.
For TF-CrossNet, we train two models, one online with
zero lookahead, and one offline with full-context, denoted as
oTF-CrossNetandTF-CrossNet, respectively. Both models
are trained on noisy LibriSpeech, and the training data is gener-
ated dynamically for every epoch. During training, a 4-second
segment is randomly selected for all utterances. We use 12 TF-
CrossNet blocks for each model, with hidden size set to 192,
squeeze dimension set to 16, convolutional feedforward dimen-
sion set to 384, and 4 self-attention heads. Two models have
7.95M parameters and are both trained for 50 epochs with a
batch size of 32. We utilize the Adam optimizer with a maxi-
mum learning rate of 2e −4. The learning rate starts from 2e −4
for 16 epochs, and then gradually decays to 2e −5. Models are
trained on 8 NVIDIA H100 GPUs.
3.2.2. Backend Configuration
For FastMambaformer, we follow the same configuration
with [16] except integrating a Mamba block in each Conformer
block to replace the convolutional module. For each Mamba
block, the SSM state expansion factor is set to 16, the local
convolution width to 4, and the block expansion factor to 2.
We train two FastMambaformer models, one on clean speech
and the other with MCT, denoted asclean-trainedandnoisy-
trained, respectively. Both models have 130M parameters. The
clean-trained model is trained on clean LibriSpeech only. The
noisy-trained model serves as the baseline with MCT on both
clean and noisy speech. To make the baseline training data
comparable with the decoupled system, the noisy-trained ASR
model sees the same duration of data as the online SS fron-
tend and the clean-trained ASR backend training data. We
use the same configuration as [16], and use the Nvidia NeMo

4. Results and Discussions
4.1. Results on LibriSpeech
First, we compare the results on the LibriSpeech test-other
set. In [16], FastConformer trained on LibriSpeech achieves
10.6%/9.5% WER with 0 ms lookahead with connectionist tem-
poral classification (CTC)/RNN-T decoder. Clean-trained Fast-
Mambaformer outperforms it with 10.3%/9.1% WER, demon-
strating the effectiveness of leveraging Mamba. Noisy-trained
model achieves 12.5%/10.9% WER, higher than the clean-
trained model, showing that MCT degrades performance on
clean speech.
For SS metrics, the average short-time objective intelligi-
bility (STOI) [31] and perceptual evaluation of speech quality
(PESQ) [32] improvement of TF-CrossNet is 21% and 1.26. For
online models, oTF-CrossNet and DPDFNet achieve 18% and
12% improvement in STOI, and 0.89 and 0.48 improvement in
PESQ, respectively. Table 1 presents the average WER compar-
ison between different systems on ADTBabble and ADTCafete-
ria noises. Without a frontend, the NeMo pre-trained model and
SimulStreaming all outperform the clean-trained model due to
their larger training set. The noisy-trained baseline outperforms
all other models significantly with a 36.9% WER, showing the
effectiveness of MCT to mitigate background noise.

WER, outperforming the NeMo pre-trained model of 29.85%
WER. With oTF-CrossNet, the system achieves 24.56% WER,
even outperforming the noisy-trained baseline of 26.17% WER.
This finding aligns with the results in Section 4.1. The de-

4.3. Results on LibriCSS
Similar to Section 4.2, we load models trained on LibriSpeech
and evaluate on LibriCSS utterance-wise evaluation. With-
out frontend processing, SimulStreaming outperforms all other
models because it has more training data. On LibriCSS, the
clean-trained and noisy-trained models both outperform the
NeMo pre-trained model, and the noisy-trained model has a
lower WER on average. DPDFNet degrades the performance
of all ASR backends, showing the outcome of the frontend
processing artifact. However, with oTF-CrossNet, all mod-
els achieve a performance improvement in separating over-
lapping speakers. For the clean-trained backend, coupling
with oTF-CrossNet yields a WER reduction from 26.51% to
22.93%. NeMo pre-trained model achieves 20.09% WER, sig-
nificantly lower than the 45.04% WER without frontend. With
oTF-CrossNet, SimulStreaming achieves 20.75% WER from
23.06% without frontend. The findings align with those in Sec-
tion 4.2.
5. Concluding Remarks
In this work, we address robust streaming ASR with a decou-
pled framework where online SS frontend and streaming ASR
backend are trained separately, with the streaming ASR model
trained on clean speech only. We propose a new ASR model
architecture, FastMambaformer, leveraging the strength of the
FastConformer and Mamba. The proposed framework outper-
forms the baseline system trained with MCT. We demonstrate
that the decoupled framework works on both simulated and real
data, and benchmark the performance on CHiME-4 and Lib-
riCSS datasets. The benchmark can be compared for online SS
frontend performance, streaming ASR backend performance, or
the entire robust streaming ASR system. The evaluation results
on two open-sourced pre-trained ASR models show the poten-
tial of online SS as a frontend for modern audio language mod-
els.
