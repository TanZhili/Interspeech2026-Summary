Abstract
This article presents the SSPNet Speaker Personality Corpus
Version 2 - an enhanced edition of the SSPNet Speaker Per-
sonality Corpus, the most widely used public benchmark for
speech-based Automatic Personality Perception. The main im-
provements are: (1) all speech samples are fully transcribed
(the original corpus included only audio); (2) personality an-
notations use a 100-point scale (instead of the original 9-point
scale); and (3) the corpus includes two rater groups: one that un-
derstands the language of the recordings and one that does not.
These enhancements enable multimodal approaches (previously
not possible with the original), facilitate regression methodolo-
gies (hindered by the coarse 9-point scale), and support inves-
tigations into how language comprehension affects personality
perception (previously infeasible). The release also provides
unimodal and multimodal baselines with reproducible experi-
mental protocols: SPC V2 Repository.
Index Terms: Social Signal Processing, Automatic Personality
Perception, Computational Paralinguistics
1. Introduction
More than a vehicle for words, the human voice is a mirror of
emotion, mood, and individuality [1, 2, 3]. Within its rhythm,
tone, and nuances lie the subtle cues that allow listeners to form
impressions about a speaker spontaneously [4, 5]. These im-
pressions, though subjective, tend to be consistent across lis-
teners and interplay with dimensions such as trust, likability,
and engagement in social interactions [6, 7, 8]. Understand-
ing the association between such impressions and speech is a
key question in psychology, linguistics, and computing [9, 10].
In psychology, the process is known associal perception- the
mechanism by which people infer others’ apparent traits from
observable cues such as voice [4, 11]. In computing, this corre-
sponds toAutomatic Personality Perception(APP), the task of
predicting the traits that listeners attribute to speakers they hear
for the first time [9, 12].
The SSPNet Speaker Personality Corpus (SPC), part of the
Interspeech Speaker Trait Challenge [13], is the most widely
used benchmark for APP. One of the defining characteristics of
the SPC is that its personality raters did not understand French,
the language spoken by the speakers. As a result, the person-
ality ratings were largely based on nonverbal aspects of speech
rather than on linguistic content. While this design choice made
the SPC a distinctive benchmark for APP, it also limited the
development of multimodal approaches that integrate language
(what people say) with paralanguage (how they say it). In ad-
dition, it prevented any investigation into the interplay between
language and paralanguage in APP. To address the above lim-
itations, this paper introduces the SSPNet Speaker Personality
Corpus Version 2 (SPC V2), a new and enhanced version of the
original SPC with the following improvements:
• The SPC V2 includes automatic transcriptions of the audio
clips (the original SPC included only audio), thereby en-
abling the development of multimodal approaches.
• The personality annotations in SPC V2 are obtained from two
groups of assessors: one comprising individuals who under-
stand what the speakers are saying, and another comprising
individuals who do not. This design makes it possible to an-
alyze the impact of language comprehension on APP.
• The personality annotations use a 100-point scale (the orig-
inal corpus used a 9-point scale), providing a more fine-
grained measure of perceived personality and supporting both
classification and regression approaches.
• The data distribution includes clearly defined experimental
protocols (not available with the original corpus), ensuring
full reproducibility of experimental results.
• The SPC V2 distribution package includes implementations
of the baseline approaches to be used for performance com-
parison.
Overall, these enhancements do more than simply add
new material to the original SPC; they open the door to re-
search directions that have not yet been explored. Because
the voice is a powerful carrier of social information, it is es-
sential to examine the channels through which such impres-
sions are formed [1, 14]. Personality is conveyed through a
complex interplay of verbal (language) and nonverbal (paralan-
guage) cues [9, 15]. Research suggests that paralinguistic cues
often have a stronger impact on first impressions [7, 12, 15], as
they remain accessible even in the absence of language compre-
hension. However, the relative contribution of each modality
to perceived personality is still not fully understood, particu-
larly when listeners differ in their level of language comprehen-
sion [9, 16]. Advancing our understanding of this interaction is
therefore crucial for both theories of social perception and the
development of APP systems.
The remainder of this paper is organized as follows: Sec-
tion 2 describes the SPC V2, Section 3 presents the baseline
experiments and results, and Section 4 draws some conclusions.
2. The SSPNet SPC V2
The 640 audio clips included in SPC V2 are taken from the
original SPC. Each clip is 10 seconds long and is taken from
news broadcasts of Radio Suisse Romande, a Swiss radio chan-
nel. The language spoken in the clips is French. The speaker
set includes all individuals who participated in the news pro-

The distribution of speakers across clips is included in the data

The personality assessments are based on theBig Five
traits, one of the personality models most widely used in psy-
chology and computing [9, 17, 18]. The Big Five model rep-
resents personality as a five-dimensional vector, a format par-
ticularly suitable for computational analysis. Each component
of the vector corresponds to one of the Big Five traits:Open-
ness(tendency to be curious and intellectually open),Conscien-
tiousness(tendency to be rigorous, organized, and planful),Ex-
traversion(tendency to be sociable, energetic, and expressive),
Agreeableness(tendency to be kind, cooperative, and trusting),
andNeuroticism(tendency to be emotionally sensitive or easily
stressed) [17]. The higher the value of a facet, the stronger is
the tendencies of the trait in an individual.
The personality annotations in SPC V2 are different from
those in the original SPC as follows: The key improvement
is the inclusion of two groups of annotators. The first group
consists of 100 French-speaking raters who understand the lex-
ical content of the recordings (55 male, 44 female, and 1 pre-
ferred not to say). The second group consists of 101 English-
speaking raters who do not understand the lexical content of
the recordings (50 male, 50 female, and 1 preferred not to say).
This design makes it possible to analyze the effect of language
comprehension on APP. The annotation process was conducted
throughProlific 1, which is an online interactive web-based plat-
form. The 640 clips in the corpus were randomly divided into
10 groups of 64 clips each. Each group was randomly assigned
to a pool of 20 raters, including 10 from the French-speaking
group and 10 from the English-speaking group. During the as-
sessment, raters were presented with the items of theBig Five
Inventory–10(BFI-10) [19], a widely used personality assess-
ment questionnaire, and were asked to assign a score between 0
(“the speaker does not reflect the described trait”) and 100 (“the
speaker strongly reflects the described trait”). English-speaking
raters were presented with the original BFI-10 items [19], while
French-speaking raters received the validated French adapta-
tion [20]. The ratings provided by the 10 raters evaluating the
same clip were averaged to obtain the final score for each of the
Big Five personality traits.
The personality scores allow the APP task to be cast as a
regression. In addition, in line with the literature (see, e.g., [12,
21, 22]), the scores have been binarized to frame the APP task as
a binary classification: for every trait, a clip has been assigned
to classHighif its score is greater or equal to the median and to
classLowotherwise.

3. Baseline Approaches and Results
Figure 1 shows the six approaches included with SPC V2
as reference baselines for performance comparison. All ap-
proaches use the same underlying representation strategy. For
audio, speech signals are encoded as sequences of embeddings
X={x 1,...,x N }using Whisper, whereNis the number
of embeddings. These sequences are then segmented into sub-
sequences of lengthM(frames), starting at regular time in-
tervals ofM/2, resulting in 50% overlap between consecutive
frames. The value ofMis a hyperparameter selected through
cross-validation (see below). In parallel, automatic transcrip-
tions of the clips (see item 1(b) in Section 2.1) are used to de-
rive linguistic representations. These transcripts are first tok-
enized with the KerasTokenizer(vocabulary indexing), and
then converted into a sequence of embeddingsZ=z 1,...,z T
using Word2vec [23], whereTis the total number of tokens.
Whisper and Word2vec were selected among the representa-
tions listed in item 1(d) as they achieved the best empirical per-
formance; results obtained using other features are included in
the release for comparison.
TheSframes extracted from each clip are used as input
to two unimodal approaches. The first employs an Long Short
Term Memory Network (LSTM) that classifies each frame into
one of two classes - Low or High (see Section 2). The output
is a sequence of probabilitiesp 1,...,p S, wherep k is the pos-
terior probability that framekbelongs to class High. A clip is
labeled High if more than half of its frames havepk >0.5. The
average of the frame-level probabilities provides an estimatepP
of the posterior probability that the entire clip belongs to class
High. The second unimodal approach uses an LSTM to predict
a scorer k for each frame. The average of these frame-level
scores yields the clip-level scorer P .
The next two unimodal approaches rely on linguistic infor-
mation. They take as input the full sequenceZof token em-
beddings (see above). Because clips contain relatively few to-
kens (with a maximum observed length of 47), there is no need
to segmentZinto frames. Instead, the entire sequence is fed
into two LSTMs: one that outputs a posterior probabilityp L for
class High, and the other outputs a predicted scorer L (Fig. 1).
These four unimodal approaches form the basis of two mul-
timodal systems. The first multimodal method concatenates the
hidden-layer outputs of (1) the LSTM trained to classify audio
frames and (2) the LSTM trained to classify the token sequence
Z. The resulting sequence of concatenated vectors is passed to
a dense layer with a softmax output, producing a sequence of
multimodal posteriorsp ′

teriors exceed 0.5, the clip is assigned to class High. In parallel,
the same concatenated sequence is passed to another dense layer
(followed by a softmax) that outputs predicted scoresr ′

average of these produces the clip-level scorer(see Figure 1).
3.1. Experiments and Results
All experiments were conducted using a five-fold cross-
validation protocol. The folds were created randomly, with
the only constraint being that all clips from the same speaker
were assigned to a single fold (the lists of clips in each fold are
provided in the corpus distribution to ensure reproducibility).
This design guarantees speaker independence, meaning that no
speaker appears in both the training and test sets. As a result, the
models learn to generalize personality traits rather than memo-
rize speaker identity. Hyperparameter tuning was performed us-
ing a nested cross-validation setup. In each iteration, three folds
were used for training, one for validation, and one for testing.
This approach enabled model selection while preserving an un-
seen test set. Across the five iterations, each fold served once as
the validation set and once as the test set.
For the paralanguage-based models, two hyperparameters
were optimized: the number of neurons in the LSTM layers (32,
64, 128, 256, 512) and the frame lengthM(50, 100, 150, 200).
For the language-based models, only the number of LSTM units
was tuned, using the same range of values. The length of se-
quenceZwas fixed atT= 47, the maximum number of tokens
extracted from any clip (shorter sequences were padded with
null vectors). Hyperparameter tuning was performed separately
for each personality trait and each annotator group (French-
speaking vs. English-speaking). Once the optimal configuration
was determined based on the highest validation performance,
the final experiments were conducted using these settings within
a five-fold cross-validation framework, with four folds used for
training and one fold for testing.

4. Conclusion
This paper introduced theSSPNet SPC V2, a new, improved
corpus for the APP task based on the widely used SSPNet
Speaker Personality Corpus. The SPC V2 addressed two main
goals: (i) to widen the spectrum of problems that can be ad-
dressed with the data and (ii) to improve the reproducibility and
benchmarking of the results obtained over the Corpus. For the
first goal, the SPC V2 paves the way forward to addressing
multiple scientific and technological questions, including the
role of language understanding and cultural differences in APP,
the roles of language and paralanguage in impression forming,
the development of multimodal APP approaches, and other ap-
plications. For the second goal, the SPC V2 distribution in-
cludes experimental protocols and baseline approaches that en-
sure full reproducibility of the results provided in this work.
The benchmarking protocol ensures that all results obtained on
the SPC V2 can be rigorously compared with each other. To
this end, SPC V2 is a valuable replacement of the original one
that will enable development and benchmarking of new APP
methodologies, while opening up new research avenues.
