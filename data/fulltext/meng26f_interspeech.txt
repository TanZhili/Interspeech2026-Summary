Abstract
Snore sounds are clinically informative acoustic biomarkers, yet
robust and transparent automatic classification remains chal-
lenging. We propose an explainable Siamese framework for
four-class snore classification that treats similarity as deci-
sion evidence. A lightweight convolution module learnsℓ 2-
normalised embeddings using joint semi-hard triplet and class-
balanced cross-entropy training. Classification is performed via
a non-parametric Class Centroid aligned with metric learning.
A support-based explanation mechanism relates predictions to
reference examples and visually comparable time–frequency
activation patterns. On the Munich-Passau Snore Sound Cor-
pus, macro-recall improves from 0.401 (wav2vec2) to 0.638 and
remains robust under additive noise. Deletion and perturbation
tests validate explanation faithfulness and stability, demonstrat-
ing interpretable similarity-based reasoning for clinical sleep
assessment.

1. Introduction
Snore sounds are informative acoustic biomarkers that corre-
late with upper–airway obstruction patterns and sleep-related
breathing disorders. Automatic classification of snore events
into clinically meaningful categories can assist screening, lon-
gitudinal monitoring, and model-based clinical decision sup-
port. However, achieving robust real-world performance re-
mains challenging due to non-stationary noise, device and envi-
ronment variability, class imbalance, and distribution shift. Be-
yond predictive accuracy, clinical workflows also demand trans-
parency: Models should provide human-understandable evi-
dence rather than behave as black boxes [1]. Recent reviews
further highlight the growing demand for mechanism-oriented
analysis and excitation localisation in clinical snore assessment,
rather than mere disorder detection [2].
Recent deep learning approaches have demonstrated strong
performance in audio event recognition using end-to-end con-
volutional networks trained with cross-entropy loss [3]. While
effective, softmax-based classifiers provide limited insight into

Metric learning and Siamese networks offer a complementary

In this work, we propose an explainable Siamese frame-
work for four-class snore classification that jointly optimises
semi-hard triplet loss and class-balanced cross-entropy to learn
ℓ2-normalised embeddings from log-Mel spectrograms [5]. At
inference time, classification is primarily performed using a
non-parametric Class Centroid rule in the learnt embedding
space, which assigns each query to the nearest class proto-
type under Euclidean distance. A softmax head is additionally
evaluated as a parametric baseline for comparison, while in-
terpretability is achieved via a support-based, feature-level ex-
planation mechanism that attributes each decision to concrete
reference examples and aligned time–frequency activation evi-
dence, rather than relying on class-level prototype voting. Un-
like post-hoc visualisation alone, the proposed explanation de-
sign is further evaluated through quantitative faithfulness (dele-
tion) analysis and perturbation-based stability testing, enabling
structured validation of interpretability.
We evaluate the proposed framework on the Munich-Passau
Snore Sound Corpus (MPSSC) [6] under a four-class taxon-
omy using macro-averaged recall as the primary metric [7]
to emphasise minority-class performance. Experimental re-
sults demonstrate that the learnt metric-enhanced embedding
improves clean-condition macro-recall from 0.401 (wav2vec2
baseline) to 0.638, while maintaining competitive robustness
under moderate additive noise. In addition, quantitative explain-
ability analyses confirm that high-evidence regions contribute
disproportionately to model confidence and remain structurally
stable under realistic perturbations.

We reconceptualise similarity in Siamese networks as evidence
for model decisions and propose an explainable framework that
couples structured embedding learning with feature-level in-
terpretability. (2) We introduce and quantitatively validate a
support-based, feature-level explanation mechanism grounded
in query–support similarity and visually comparable activation

2. Related Work
2.1. Snore Sound Classification and Datasets
Early snore-analysis studies relied on hand-crafted time-
frequency descriptors and classical classifiers under controlled
recording conditions. For instance, wavelet-based acoustic
features have been explored for VOTE classification of snore
sounds, where VOTE denotes the four excitation locations:
Velum, Oropharyngeal walls, Tongue base, and Epiglottis [8].
Subsequent work further validated excitation-location classi-
fication in larger clinical cohorts using multi-feature acous-
tic analysis, establishing the VOTE taxonomy as a clinically
grounded categorisation framework [9]. With the advent of
deep learning for audio, convolutional neural networks (CNNs)
operating on log-Mel spectrograms have become a dominant
paradigm for acoustic event recognition, enabling end-to-end
training and improved robustness to noise and device variabil-
ity [3]. For clinically meaningful snore categorisation, pub-
licly available datasets such asMPSSCprovide multi-class
taxonomies and diverse recording environments. However,
these datasets also expose practical challenges, including se-
vere class imbalance, heterogeneous recording devices, non-
stationary background noise, and limited sample size typical of
clinical biomedical data.
2.2. Deep Models for Audio Event Recognition
Convolutional backbones, ranging from VGG-like architectures
to lightweight CNNs, remain competitive for short-window
spectrogram modelling, whereas attention-based models have
shown advantages in large-scale audio tagging with long tem-
poral contexts [10]. Recently, pre-trained foundation models
such as wav2vec 2.0 [11] have demonstrated strong represen-
tation learning capability in speech and audio tasks through
large-scale self-supervised training. However, when applied
to small-scale, clinically constrained biomedical datasets, such
models may suffer from domain mismatch and limited inter-
pretability [12–14]. To mitigate data scarcity in snore sound
classification, generative augmentation approaches have been
explored. For example, Snore-GANs employ a semi-supervised
conditional generative adversarial framework to synthesise real-
istic snore signals and improve classification performance under
limited training data conditions [15]. Nevertheless, these ap-
proaches primarily focus on performance enhancement rather
than on structuring interpretable embedding geometry.
Despite strong performance with cross-entropy objectives,
conventional softmax-based classifiers provide limited trans-
parency regarding their decision mechanisms and may exhibit
sensitivity to spurious correlations under domain shift. Regu-
larisation strategies such as spectrogram augmentation and test-
time augmentation can mitigate over-fitting [5], but do not ex-
plicitly structure the embedding space for similarity-based rea-
soning or interpretability [16].
2.3. Siamese Networks and Metric Learning
A Siamese network consists of two or more branches with
shared parameters that encode different input samples into a
common embedding space. Rather than directly predicting class
labels from each input independently, it learns representations
such that the distance between embeddings reflects the similar-
ity between the corresponding samples. As the branches share
the same encoder, the resulting embeddings are directly compa-
rable under a predefined distance metric.
Metric learning constructs such an embedding space by
mapping acoustically similar signals close together while sep-
arating dissimilar ones by a margin. Contrastive and triplet
losses are widely adopted in speaker verification and audio re-
trieval. They are often combined with class-balancedP×M
batch construction, wherePdenotes the number of classes sam-
pled per batch andMdenotes the number of examples sampled
from each class, to stabilise hard-example mining [17]. Margin-
based classification heads can further enhance discriminability
when integrated with cross-entropy objectives [18–20].
While these approaches improve representation quality and
class separation, most prior work focuses on discriminative per-
formance rather than on how similarity is formed and expressed
within internal representations. The learnt embedding geometry
alone does not automatically yield transparent explanations un-
less it is explicitly linked to interpretable evidence in the input
domain.
2.4. Prototype-Based and Case-Based Inference
Example-based explanations have been explored in model-
agnostic explainable artificial intelligence (XAI) frameworks
such as LIME [21], which approximate local decision behaviour
using representative samples. Prototype-based and case-based
methods [22] perform classification by comparing test embed-
dings to reference examples or class representatives, such as
nearest class centroids,k-nearest neighbours (kNN), or Maha-
lanobis nearest-class means [23, 24]. These approaches pro-
vide intuitive decision heuristics and are widely used in metric-
learning evaluation.
However, class-level distance aggregation or neighbour vot-
ing alone offers limited insight into the feature-level acoustic
evidence that drives similarity. Such aggregation may obscure
heterogeneous intra-class structures or cross-category overlaps.
Furthermore, post-hoc saliency visualisations on spectrograms
often rely on heuristic design choices and can exhibit instabil-
ity under domain shift [25]. A structured connection between
similarity-based inference and interpretable time-frequency ev-
idence remains under-explored.
2.5. Imbalanced Learning in Acoustic Classification
Long-tailed class distributions are prevalent in clinical audio
tasks. Class-balanced re-weighting schemes, effective-number
weighting [19], and logit-adjusted losses reduce bias toward
majority classes, while batch-level sampling strategies that en-
force per-class balance improve the availability of informative
positives and negatives for metric learning. These techniques
typically improve macro-averaged recall, which better reflects
clinical utility than overall accuracy [26].
2.6. Positioning of our Work
Our work builds upon metric learning and Siamese architec-
tures for snore sound representation, but differs from prior ap-
proaches in its explicit integration of structured interpretability.

We jointly optimise semi-hard triplet loss and class-balanced

To stabilise metric learning, each batch uses aP×Mconstruc-

3.6. Support-based Feature-level Explanation
While Siamese networks are often interpreted through class-
level distances or prototype-based decision rules, our objective
is to explain model decisions by explicitly analysing the simi-
larity mechanism inherent to Siamese inference. Rather than at-
tributing predictions to abstract class prototypes or aggregated
distance scores, we ground explanations in concrete support
examples and visually comparable feature-level activation pat-
terns. This design connects embedding geometry with time-
frequency activation patterns, transforming similarity-based
reasoning into a structured and interpretable mechanism rather
than a post-hoc visualisation [28].
3.6.1. Support Set Construction
For each class, we construct a compact support set composed of
representative training examples selected based on embedding-
space proximity to class centroids [23]. Specifically, samples
with minimal distance to their respective class mean embed-
dings are chosen to capture typical acoustic patterns. These
supports remain fixed during inference and are used exclusively
for interpretability without influencing the model’s prediction
rule. By anchoring explanations to real audio instances that are
geometrically central in the learnt embedding space, the result-
ing interpretation remains intuitive, example-driven, and struc-
turally consistent with similarity-based inference.
3.6.2. Query-Support Similarity
Given a query audio segmentxq and a support examplexs, their
normalised log-Mel representations ˜Xq and ˜Xs are mapped by
the shared encoder toℓ 2-normalised embeddingsz q =f θ( ˜Xq)
andz s =f θ( ˜Xs), respectively. Their embedding-space dis-
tance is defined as
d(zq,zs) =∥z q−zs∥2,(8)
which is monotonically related to cosine similarity underℓ 2
normalisation. This hyper-spherical embedding geometry facil-
itates distance-based reasoning and enables similarity ranking
among support examples.

Macro-averaged recall is reported as the primary metric [7]
