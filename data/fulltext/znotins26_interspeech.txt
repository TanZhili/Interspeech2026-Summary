Abstract
This paper investigates automatic speech recognition
(ASR) for rich medical transcription in a low-resource setting.
We compare end-to-end ASR models trained to produce format-
ted reports with a two-stage pipeline combining verbatim tran-
scription and LLM-based post-editing. To address limited train-
ing data, we introduce an LLM-driven data curation approach
that converts legacy verbatim transcripts into formatted data,
and we augment training with LLM-corrected pseudo-labeled
data. Our experiments, using 50 hours of manually transcribed
medical dictation and an additional 75 hours of LLM-corrected
pseudo-labeled audio, show that domain-adapted foundation
models significantly improve transcription quality across word
error rate, punctuation accuracy, and medical entity recogni-
tion. The results demonstrate that high-quality medical ASR
with rich transcription is achievable for low-resource languages
using limited domain data.

1. Introduction
Automatic speech recognition (ASR) has become an estab-
lished technology for clinical documentation in high-resource
languages: from radiology to doctor-patient conversations. Re-
cent advances in speech representation learning and multilin-
gual ASR models, such as Wav2Vec2.0 [1], XLS-R [2], Whis-
per [3], MMS [4], and Canary [5], have substantially low-
ered the barrier to building ASR systems through cross-lingual
transfer and fine-tuning. This shift is particularly impactful for
low-resource languages such as Latvian, an Indo-European lan-
guage of the European Union with approximately 1.5 million
native speakers. However, domain adaptation remains signifi-
cantly more challenging than general-purpose language adapta-
tion: medical speech datasets are scarce, terminology is highly
specialized, and clinically usable output must follow strict for-
matting conventions.
A key requirement in medical ASR is the production of rich
transcriptions – structured and formatted reports that include
punctuation and casing, the use of abbreviations, acronyms,
and symbols, numeric expressions (values, dates, ranges), mea-
surement units, and other domain-specific notation. Traditional
medical ASR pipelines typically address this using a verbatim
transcription system followed by post-processing modules such
as punctuation restoration and inverse text normalization (ITN).
Although hybrid rule-based and machine learning ITN systems
can achieve high accuracy, they require extensive engineering
and maintenance, which is not a feasible and sustainable ap-
proach for low-resource languages with small markets.
For Latvian, several hundred hours of general-domain
open-data and academically licensed speech corpora are avail-
able for supervised ASR training, while medical-domain adap-
tation relies on limited-access datasets of only a few dozen
hours. Without domain adaptation, even strong general-domain
models such as LATE [6] degrade substantially on medi-
cal speech, with WER increasing from 3–13% on general-
domain content to 12–46% in medical reporting scenarios [7].
These scenarios include medical dictation in visual imaging and
histopathology, filling in electronic health records and discharge
forms, as well as transcription of doctor–patient conversations.
In this paper, we investigate domain adaptation for Lat-
vian medical ASR under strict data constraints (a legacy 50-
hour medical speech corpus), focusing on two strategies for rich
transcription. First, we fine-tune selected base models – Whis-
per, Canary, Gemma-3n [8] – for end-to-end production of for-
matted transcripts. Second, we evaluate a two-stage approach
combining the fine-tuning of selected base models – Wav2Vec2-
BERT [9] and Whisper – for verbatim transcription with a text-
only large language model (LLM) that performs formatting and
limited correction of the verbatim transcripts. To mitigate the
scarcity of formatted training data, we introduce an LLM-driven
data curation approach that converts legacy verbatim transcripts
into paired verbatim–formatted transcripts.
Our main contributions: (1) we compare end-to-end ASR
with a pipeline combining verbatim ASR and LLM-based post-
editing for Latvian medical transcription in a low-resource set-
ting; (2) we propose an LLM-driven approach for supervised
data curation that converts legacy verbatim transcripts into rich
transcriptions and generates LLM-corrected pseudo-labels; (3)
we evaluate both strategies using standard ASR metrics together
with formatting- and domain-aware error metrics.
2. Related Work
For high-resource languages, ASR has long been used in clini-
cal documentation workflows to reduce reporting time. For low-
resource languages, there is typically a substantial gap in the
ASR accuracy and formatting quality of medical transcripts.
Legacy medical ASR systems are based on hybrid architec-
tures, consisting of a separate acoustic model combined with a
domain-adapted language model, a pronunciation lexicon, and a
rule-based formatting post-processor. Such multi-step pipelines
have been implemented for medical ASR in Latvian [10], Es-
tonian [11] and other low-resource languages, where perfor-
mance depends heavily on targeted resource creation and labor-
intensive domain adaptation.
Recent work increasingly explores the adaptation of mod-
ern end-to-end ASR models to the medical domain. Exam-
ples include Whisper fine-tuning for French radiology transcrip-
tion [12], the creation of Polish medical speech datasets for

LLMs are increasingly used for post-processing ASR out-

also been explored for dataset expansion and annotation as-
sistance, including pseudo-label generation and human-in-the-
loop frameworks for efficient speech dataset curation [18, 19].
For Latvian, medical ASR research has focused on creat-
ing domain resources and adapting systems to radiology dicta-
tion. Early work constructed radiology speech and text corpora
to enable acoustic and language model adaptation [20], which
were later integrated into practical transcription systems [21].
These hybrid systems used rule-based rewriting grammars to
convert verbatim transcripts into structured reports and achieved
word error rates of roughly 20–30% depending on modality and
recording conditions [10]. Subsequent work explored semi-
supervised adaptation using untranscribed medical audio, re-
porting WERs of 25% for clinical discharge forms, 20% for
psychiatry, and 37% for radiology [22]. More recently, the
adaptation of multilingual foundation models, particularly fine-
tuned Whisper models, has significantly improved general Lat-
vian ASR [7, 6], while LLM-based post-processing has been
explored for correcting and formatting ASR transcripts [23].
3. The LVMED Dataset
For this study, we used a 35-hour Latvian radiology speech cor-
pus [20], which we extended with recordings from histopathol-
ogy, clinical discharge, and surgery, resulting in 50 hours of
medical dictations (see Table 1) with legacy verbatim transcripts
containing spoken punctuation commands. In addition to the
verbatim transcripts, the dataset contains parallel final docu-
ments produced by human transcriptionists. These documents
do not fully correspond to the spoken content, which may in-
clude disfluencies, transcription instructions, false starts, self-
corrections, and post-transcription additions.
We simplified the original annotations to retain only verba-
tim and formatted forms for abbreviations, numbers, punctua-
tion commands, and interrupted words. Each utterance is repre-
sented as[<formatted>|<verbatim>], enabling deter-
ministic conversion between the two forms, facilitating align-
ment, and allowing evaluation of individual replacements.
Formatted transcripts are generated using Gemini 3 Pro,
with manual verbatim transcripts and final documents provided
as input pairs. The LLM restores domain-specific terminology,
code notation, punctuation, and casing. To minimize hallucina-
tions and unintended modifications, the prompt was iteratively
refined using evaluation-driven adjustments and few-shot exam-
ples; the final prompt contains about 50 examples. Generated
outputs are automatically validated against the original verba-
tim transcript to ensure annotation consistency. If mismatches
are detected, the LLM is retried up to five times, each time with
the diff and a reminder to follow the instructions more strictly.
Only a small number of cases required manual review.
For detailed evaluation, we automatically annotate medical
named entities using the same LLM. XML tags are added for
four categories: anatomy (ANA), medical condition (COND),
medication (MED), and treatment procedures (PROC). A vali-
dation loop ensures that no unintended changes are introduced
into the transcripts. Sentence-level timestamps are added using
a CTC-based forced aligner.1

We followed a similar approach to expand the dataset
with 75 hours of partner recordings. We generated verbatim
transcripts with our strongest ASR model and used an LLM,
together with human-edited medical reports, to post-correct
both ASR errors and formatting (terminology, notation, casing,
punctuation), producing LLM-corrected pseudo-labeled data.
We then filtered this data by discarding recordings with WER
above 15%, computed between the ASR transcript and the
LLM-corrected annotation.
4. Evaluation
Evaluating end-to-end ASR with rich transcription is challeng-
ing because errors may arise at multiple levels: lexical recog-
nition, inverse text normalization (e.g., numbers and abbre-
viations), spoken punctuation and formatting commands, and
domain-specific notation. Minor recognition differences can
lead to alignment shifts, and multiple orthographic variants may
be acceptable (e.g., alternative number formats or abbreviation
expansions). As a result, standard WER alone does not ade-
quately reflect domain-specific performance. To assess these
aspects, we use several complementary metrics.
All metrics are computed using a fuzzy token-level align-
ment based on the Levenshtein distance. Text is tokenized into
words and punctuation marks, except for numbers, dates, and
codes containing internal punctuation, which are treated as sin-
gle tokens. Substitution costs are weighted by relative edit
distance, and substitutions are allowed only between word to-
kens or punctuation tokens. This procedure is similar to the
punctuation-aware evaluation proposed by Meister et al. [24].
Based on this alignment, we report the following metrics:

• WER: computed on formatted transcripts, including written
forms of abbreviations and numbers, but ignoring punctua-
tion and casing.
• V-WER: verbatim WER, computed against verbatim annota-
tions to evaluate verbatim ASR models.
• CER: character error rate without normalization.
• PER: punctuation error rate, computed as WER over tokens
where the reference or hypothesis token is punctuation [24].
• PC-ER: punctuation command error rate, computed over ref-
erence punctuation command tokens only; counts substitu-
tions and deletions without penalizing possible insertions.
• N-ER: number error rate over reference numeric tokens.
• MC-ER: medical concept error rate over reference named en-
tity tokens, with separate results for each entity type.
5. Experiments
5.1. ASR Fine-Tuning
We evaluate several multilingual foundation models and fine-
tune them for rich medical transcription using the LVMED
dataset. Our selection of base models includes Whisper [3],
Wav2Vec2-BERT [9], Canary-1B-v2 [25], and Gemma-3n [8].
Wav2Vec2-BERT extends the Wav2Vec2.0 [1] and XLS-R [2]
architectures and is pretrained on a substantially larger multilin-
gual speech corpus. Gemma-3n is a multimodal LLM based on
the MatFormer architecture, with conditional audio and vision
modules, and is one of the few open-source LLMs with native
audio support and a tokenizer that reasonably covers Latvian;
we evaluate the 4B and 2B versions.
The fine-tuned models are compared to state-of-the-art
general-purpose baseline models supporting rich Latvian tran-
scription, including the original Canary and Whisper models,
and the Whisper-based LATE model for Latvian [6].
For fine-tuning, we apply a two-stage training strategy:
models are first trained on general-domain annotated Latvian
data2 for 10k steps and then adapted to the LVMED dataset for
5k steps. For most models, we use FP16 training, a batch size of
32, and a learning rate of2×10 −5 in the first phase, reduced by
half during domain adaptation. For Wav2Vec2-BERT, we use a
learning rate of5×10 −5 in the first phase, while Gemma-3n
is trained with BF16 and an initial learning rate of5×10 −5.
All models are trained on a single A100 80GB GPU. We mod-
ify the default Whisper generation configuration by removing
non-special suppressed tokens to allow generation of additional
punctuation symbols.
5.2. LLM-Based Formatting
We additionally evaluate the two-stage approach: (1) fine-tuned
ASR for verbatim transcription followed by (2) text formatting
using fine-tuned LLMs. Verbatim ASR models use the same
fine-tuning setup as the formatted ASR models, unless marked
otherwise.
For text formatting, we fine-tune Gemma-3 1B, 4B, and
12B models [28], since they perform well on Latvian bench-
marks.3 The formatting models are trained on pairs of verbatim
and formatted transcripts. We use full fine-tuning with a batch
size of 16 and a learning rate of1×10 −5.

To improve robustness to ASR errors, we augment the train-
ing data with ASR-generated transcripts. ASR models are
trained on one portion of the training data and used to gen-
erate hypotheses on a non-overlapping portion; only samples
with WER below 10% are retained. This exposes the LLM to
realistic ASR noise and allows limited error correction during
formatting. Including samples with larger ASR errors led to
degraded results due to increased hallucinations.
The text formatting dataset contains about 30,000 transcrip-
tion pairs corresponding to audio segments of up to 30 seconds.
5.3. Learning Curves
To analyze data scaling effects, we trained ASR models on sub-
sets of the training data (1/8, 1/4, 1/2, and full data). Exper-
iments were conducted with various Whisper base model ver-
sions and Wav2Vec2-BERT. We compared end-to-end Whisper-
based models with the two-stage approach using Gemma-3 1B,
4B, and 12B models for text formatting, analyzing performance
trends across model sizes and training data scales (see Figure 1).
6. Results and Discussion
Table 2 summarizes the performance of baseline and fine-tuned
models on the medical test set. General-domain ASR systems
perform poorly in this setting due to substantial formatting and
terminology errors, highlighting the difficulty of medical dicta-
tion with structured formatting and domain-specific vocabulary.
Domain-specific adaptation substantially improves perfor-
mance across all models. Whisper-based models show the
best results among end-to-end systems. The best configura-
tion,ft-whisper-large-v3, reaches 11.1% WER, while adding 75
hours of pseudo-labeled data further reduces WER to 10.6%,
more than6×lower than the original Whisper model. Similar
improvements are observed for the punctuation (PC-ER) and
domain-specific (MC-ER) metrics. In contrast, skipping the
first-stage general-domain Latvian adaptation and fine-tuning
Whisper directly on LVMED yields worse performance. This
suggests that the general-domain Latvian data are helpful, given
the limited size of the in-domain medical dataset.
The two-stage ASR+LLM approach achieves competitive
results. The best configuration,ft-whisper-large-v3withft-
gemma3-12b, reaches 11.2% WER, closely matching the end-
to-end model, and provides the best punctuation command
recognition result (PC-ER of 2.1%).
Performance improves consistently as training data in-
creases for all models (Figure 1). The LLM-based approach
performs better with less data but reaches similar performance
to end-to-end models with sufficient in-domain data.
Overall, the results show that domain adaptation is critical
for medical ASR, that end-to-end and ASR+LLM models are
competitive when trained on comparable in-domain data, and
that pseudo-labeled data can provide further gains.

Another group of errors involves word boundaries and mor-
phology. Multi-word expressions may be merged and com-
pounds may be split. In other cases, correct words appear in in-
correct grammatical forms that are often pronounced similarly.
Errors also involve numbers, ranges, and alphanumeric
codes: the system may split codes incorrectly, miss range for-
matting, vary between Roman and Arabic numerals, or alternate
between verbatim and numeric forms of small ordinal numbers.
Finally, many errors originate from spontaneous dictation
and disfluencies. Overall, most residual errors stem from for-
matting decisions, terminology variation, and characteristics of
spontaneous dictation rather than lexical substitutions.
8. Limitations
While our approach significantly improves performance, sev-
eral limitations remain. End-to-end ASR and LLM-based post-
processors are inherently prone to hallucinations, which, in a
clinical context, can lead to critical misrecognitions of dosages
or drug names. This work relied on existing verified reports for
data curation. While LLMs can be used to generate or proof-
read annotations where such reports are unavailable, this would
likely require increased manual verification to ensure medi-
cal accuracy. Furthermore, while agentic loops and few-shot
prompting were used to guide the LLM, maintaining absolute
consistency in automated annotations remains a challenge.
9. Conclusion
This study demonstrates that high-quality ASR for low-resource
rich medical transcription is achievable with limited annotated
data by combining speech foundation models with LLM-driven
curation. Our fine-tuned Whisper model reached a 10.6% WER,
representing a sixfold improvement over general-domain base-
lines and significantly reducing medical concept error rates.
The results highlight the value of LLM-based formatting and
pseudo-labeling for reusing legacy data and improving domain
adaptation when in-domain resources are scarce. This reduces
the manual annotation burden for specialized rich-transcription
tasks in low-resource languages such as Latvian.
