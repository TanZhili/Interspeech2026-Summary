PhonePrune: One-shot Phoneme-Aware Pruning for Large-scale ASR Models
via Phoneme Set Generation and Calibration

Abstract
Large-scale Automatic Speech Recognition Models face de-
ployment challenges due to massive parameter counts. While
one-shot pruning compresses them, conventional methods in-
discriminately discard low-magnitude parameters, degrading
fine-grained phonetic distinctions. We conceptualize the sub-
networks of these delicate weights as the Phoneme Ticket
Hypothesis, arguing they must be preserved. We propose
PhonePrune to preserve these weights via Phoneme Set Genera-
tion and Phoneme-aware Calibration. Evaluated on LibriSpeech
and Common V oice (English, Korean, Japanese) at 50% spar-
sity, PhonePrune demonstrates strong results specifically on the
Korean and Japanese datasets, yielding 13.41% and 13.83%
Word Error Rate reductions over Distil-Whisper, respectively.
Our success stems from preserving fragile parameters shared
by these languages, such as subtle spectral variations in Korean
and precise temporal durations in Japanese.

1. Introduction
Large-scale automatic speech recognition (ASR) models, such
as Whisper [1], have been actively studied, yet their massive
number of parameters severely hinders practical deployment.
Therefore, compressing these ASR models has been actively
explored in various studies [2, 3, 4, 5, 6]. For practical ASR
applications, one-shot pruning has emerged as a highly efficient
strategy that directly reduces the size of the model without ex-
pensive retraining pipelines [7, 8, 9].
Recent efforts have successfully adapted one-shot pruning
to ASR models, as demonstrated by promising methods such
as SparseWav [8]. Magnitude-based pruning relies on the as-
sumption that smaller weights are less important, indiscrimi-
nately discarding low-magnitude weights. Within ASR mod-
els, however, these low-magnitude weights are distinct from
those capturing general acoustic features [10, 11, 12]. Some
of them are critical for recognizing fine-grained phonemes dur-
ing the speech recognition. This vulnerability becomes highly
evident, as visualized by the red boxes in Figure 1, where
magnitude-based pruning fails to recognize these segments. As
highlighted by recent studies such as DyPCL [13], future ap-
proaches must account for these subtle phonetic distinctions
in speech recognition. This limitation raises a research ques-
tion:How can we identify and preserve these highly vulnerable,
low-magnitude weights encoding fine-grained phonemes during
Large-scale ASR model compression?To address this question,

2. Related Work
2.1. Large-scale ASR Models Pruning
Recent compression of ASR models spans unstructured, struc-
tured, and dynamic approaches. Within unstructured meth-
ods, PARP [7] introduces weight-level sparsity across various
speech tasks but suffers from high sparsity (50%). SparseW A V
[8] overcomes this by adaptively determining sparsity ratios
for efficient one-shot compression. Among structured meth-
ods, PruneSLU [9] enables on-device deployment by combining
layer-wise and vocabulary pruning with knowledge distillation.
Furthermore, recent dynamic approaches [3, 4] optimize com-
putation on the fly by adapting the model structure to context-
level information, such as speaker characteristics and language,
during inference. Despite optimizing loss and hardware effi-
ciency, these existing methods prune low-magnitude weights
or structural blocks based solely on magnitude or context. We
diverge from these conventional approaches by prioritizing a
phonology perspective. Specifically, our method aims to pre-
serve phoneme tickets, the essential parameters for phonemes
that standard pruning systematically discards.
2.2. Phonetic Cues in ASR
Research on phonetics in ASR has largely focused on probing
and interpretability to understand how models encode linguis-
tic units. Early probing studies [10] demonstrate that unsuper-
vised speech representations intrinsically encode basic phone
classes alongside speaker and language traits. Building on this,
recent works [11] reveal that self-supervised models develop
subspaces in early layers to capture both low-level phonetic and
abstract phonemic distinctions. Furthermore, feature attribution
analyses [12] identify the specific acoustic cues modern ASR

systems rely on. Notably, these studies expose a natural vulner-
ability: low-energy categories, such as non-sibilant fricatives,
exhibit inherently lower saliency in model representations. Be-
yond descriptive analysis, some approaches leverage phonetic
granularity for training. For instance, DyPCL [13] employs dy-
namic phoneme-level contrastive learning to discriminate sub-
tle phonetic similarities and improve dysarthric speech recog-
nition. However, integrating such phoneme awareness into
model compression remains unexplored. Previous interpretabil-
ity studies remain at the level of phonetic probing through post-
hoc analysis, and training-based methods such as DyPCL do not
address pruning. We bridge this gap. To preserve vulnerable
phoneme tickets during compression, PhonePrune directly uses
gradient sensitivity, derived from phoneme contrastive triplets
via a Phoneme Set Generation strategy, as a pruning criterion.
3. Phoneme-aware Pruning
3.1. Overview
In this paper, we propose PhonePrune, a novel phoneme-aware
pruning approach that improves speech recognition under high
sparsity by preserving phoneme tickets for low-magnitude allo-
phones without extensive retraining. As illustrated in Figure 2,
our pipeline operates in two sequential stages: (1) generating a
specialized candidate pool of phoneme triplets based on phonol-
ogy constraints (Section 3.2), and (2) calibrating pre-trained
weights based on their extracted phoneme relevance (Section
3.3).
3.2. Phoneme Set Generation
To accurately isolate the weights that function as phoneme tick-
ets, we cannot rely on unstructured speech datasets, which
entangle diverse acoustic features such as background noise.
Moreover, a naive phoneme-based approach computing sensi-
tivity solely on isolated target phonemes would fatally assign
high importance scores to irrelevant parameters responding to
generic voice activity or shared acoustic characteristics. To
overcome this, we draw inspiration from our previous work,
DyPCL [13], which successfully employs dynamic phoneme-
level contrastive learning to discriminate subtle phonetic simi-
larities.

serves as an acoustic negative, introducing a hard signal that

Calibration Setup.We compute gradient sensitivity using a
calibration dataset of 128 contrastive triplets, manually curated
according to our method. Based on the phoneme set investi-
gated in recent feature attribution literature [12], we utilize 25
English phonemes (11 vowels, 6 plosives, and 8 fricatives), se-
lecting corresponding phonemes for Korean and Japanese to
closely match these proportions and characteristics. To yield
128 triplets, we allow variations in the allophone and acous-
tic negative samples, maintaining the categorical proportions as
closely as possible to the initial set. To source these phonemes
and enable accurate temporal masking (M t) for gradient com-
putation, we utilize TIMIT [23] for English, and extract precise
boundaries for Korean and Japanese using the Montreal Forced
Aligner (MFA) [24]. By calibrating the model on these audio
data, we preserve the weights crucial for fine-grained phoneme
distinctions.
Metrics.We measure model performance using zero-shot Word
Error Rate (WER) on each test set and report the average WER
(Avg. WER) for overall evaluation. Statistical significance is
assessed via the Wilcoxon signed-rank test [25].
Baselines.The methods compared with our model are cate-
gorized as follows: Random,L 0 (regularization pruning) [26],
L1 (magnitude pruning), and OBS [27] for unstructured prun-
ing baselines, and Quantized Whisper [28], Distil-Whisper [5],
and uDistil-Whisper [6] for representative compressed whisper
models. Following the experimental setups of previous studies
[5, 6], all pruning experiments on the whisper-large [1] were
conducted at a 50% sparsity to match the 756M parameters of
the compressed whisper models.

4.3. Ablation Study
We investigate the effect of calibration sample size (N) on prun-
ing performance. Table 2 shows that increasingNconsistently
improves the WER. Notably, a significant WER reduction oc-
curs whenNincreases from 16 to 32, proving that even a lim-
ited amount of phoneme contrastive data effectively preserves
phoneme tickets. AsNincreases to 128, the model shows a
continuous improvement in performance in all evaluated lan-
guages. This tendency demonstrates that providing a larger set
of calibration samples allows the phoneme-aware calibration
mechanism to more precisely capture and preserve crucial pho-
netic parameters, thereby further mitigating performance degra-
dation during compression. We also analyzeγ, which balances
the generic weight magnitude and our phoneme focus score. Ta-
ble 3 shows that extreme values severely degrade performance:
γ= 0.0reverts to standard magnitude pruning, whileγ= 1.0
completely ignores essential generic acoustic features. Setting
γ= 0.5balances global weight importance and phonetic dis-
criminability, achieving the lowest WER across all datasets.
5. Conclusion
We propose PhonePrune to improve ASR compression by pre-
serving fine-grained acoustic distinctions. Combining Phoneme
Set Generation and Phoneme-aware Calibration, it protects low-
magnitude phoneme tickets essential for confusable phonemes.
Evaluations on LibriSpeech and Common V oice demonstrate
that our method achieves significant improvements over base-
lines under extreme sparsity by preserving fragile phonetic fea-
tures critical for Korean and Japanese.
