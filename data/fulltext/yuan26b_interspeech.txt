Abstract
Recent generative speech enhancement methods based on lan-
guage and diffusion models achieve strong perceptual qual-
ity but are more susceptible than discriminative approaches
to speech-like hallucinations under low SNRs and transient
noise. We propose DelayGSE, a text-aware generative
speech enhancement framework built on a multi-codebook lan-
guage model for denoising, dereverberation, and audio super-
resolution. DelayGSE conditions on noisy-speech STFT fea-
tures and Whisper encoder representations, and models multi-
ple discrete codebooks in a delayed manner to stabilize gen-
eration. A text-aware mechanism suppresses hallucinations,
while an importance-aware codebook weighting strategy bal-
ances perceptual fidelity and semantic consistency. Experi-
ments demonstrate state-of-the-art performance, with ablations
showing effective hallucination suppression and a 15.8% rela-
tive word error rate reduction. Audio samples are available at
https://delaygse.github.io/.
Index Terms: generative speech enhancement, text-aware
modeling, hallucination suppression
1. Introduction
Speech enhancement aims to improve the clarity and intelligi-
bility of speech signals and is fundamental to real-time com-
munications, entertainment, and human–computer interaction.
With the rapid progress of deep learning, substantial advances
have been achieved over the past decades. Early approaches
mainly relied on masking- or mapping-based strategies, where
neural networks estimate time–frequency masks or directly map
noisy speech to clean targets [1]. Later, generative adversarial
networks (GANs) introduced perceptually driven training ob-
jectives, yielding more natural-sounding enhanced speech [2].
Recently, generative models—in particular diffusion mod-
els and large language model (LLM)–style architectures—have
reshaped speech synthesis and inspired a new paradigm for
speech enhancement [3, 4, 5, 6]. Instead of explicitly suppress-
ing noise or reconstructing waveforms, generative speech en-
hancement aims to model the underlying distribution of clean
speech and generate enhanced outputs with both high intelli-
gibility and perceptual quality. This shift has demonstrated
the potential to approach studio-level audio quality, often sur-
passing the perceptual limits of traditional discriminative meth-
ods [7, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12, 13].
Existing generative speech enhancement methods can be
broadly divided into two categories. The first category oper-
ates in continuous acoustic feature spaces, such as short-time
Fourier transform (STFT) spectra, and is largely motivated by

diffusion and probability flow models developed for image gen-
eration. These methods treat noisy speech as a corrupted obser-
vation and learn a reverse generative process that maps noise
distributions back to clean speech [5, 7, 8]. Representative
examples include StoRM, which combines discriminative pre-
dictors with diffusion-based refinement to improve robustness
under extremely low-SNR conditions [5], and FlowSE, which
adopts conditional flow matching to learn a continuous trans-
formation path from noisy to clean speech with only a few sam-
pling steps, making it attractive for low-latency scenarios [8].
The second category is based on discrete token modeling,
inspired by the success of LLMs and neural audio codecs. In
this formulation, speech enhancement is cast as a conditional
sequence modeling problem: noisy speech serves as condition-
ing input, and an autoregressive or masked generative model
predicts sequences of clean speech tokens that are subsequently
decoded into waveforms [6, 10, 11, 12, 13]. While these ap-
proaches often achieve strong perceptual quality, recent studies
report a consistent degradation in semantic accuracy, with in-
creased word error rates (WER) compared to the original noisy
speech, regardless of the specific generative strategy or speech
representation used [11].
Despite their perceptual advantages, generative models are
prone to speech-like hallucinations under low-SNR or transient
noise, producing fluent but semantically incorrect or unintel-
ligible speech [11, 14]. To address this limitation, we pro-
poseDelayGSE, a text-aware generative speech enhancement
framework based on multi-codebook discrete representations.
By introducing delayed text supervision, the approach con-
strains semantic generation while preserving generative flexi-
bility, improving intelligibility and robustness without sacrific-
ing perceptual quality. An importance-aware codebook weight-
ing strategy is further adopted to balance perceptual quality,
intelligibility, and speaker similarity during training. Textual
guidance can be optionally applied at inference to improve ro-
bustness under extremely low-SNR conditions. Moreover, De-
layGSE supports denoising, dereverberation, and audio super-
resolution within a unified framework.
The main contributions of this work are summarized below:
• A delay-based text-aware generative speech enhancement
method is presented, which mitigates hallucination artifacts
by adopting a semantic-before-acoustic generation order.
• An importance-aware weighting strategy for multi-codebook
modeling is introduced to balance perceptual quality, intelli-
gibility, and speaker similarity during training.
• A unified generative framework for multiple speech restora-
tion tasks is demonstrated, enabling both speech enhance-
ment and audio super-resolution within a single model.

2.2. Audio Codec
Audio language models require a front-end codec that com-
presses continuous waveforms and quantizes them into dis-
crete tokens suitable for sequence modeling. Neural audio
codecs (NACs)—end-to-end convolutional autoencoders with
residual vector quantization (RVQ)—achieve high-fidelity re-
construction at low bitrates while emitting discrete codebook
indices [17]. Recent surveys systematically compare discrete
audio tokenizers across speech, music, and general audio, high-
lighting the relationship between token quality and downstream
task performance [18].
To preserve high-frequency details and enable broader ap-
plicability, we employ the 44.1 kHz Descript Audio Codec
(DAC) as the front-end audio representation. In our simulation
pipeline, all clean speech signals are sampled above 24 kHz,
with a substantial portion at 44.1 kHz. Operating in this high-
rate discrete token space allows the proposed framework to
naturally support both speech enhancement and audio super-
resolution within a unified generative model.

2.3. Delay Pattern
Language modeling over discrete tokens derived from neural
codecs typically employs multi-codebook parallel or hierarchi-
cal prediction schemes. To model cross-codebook dependen-
cies effectively within a single-stage autoregressive decoder,
MusicGen introduces fixed-stride delays across codebooks, en-
abling higher-level codebooks to be predicted conditional on
lower-level ones [19]. This delay regime balances inference
parallelism and output quality, and has been adopted by sub-
sequent speech generation models such as Parler-TTS [20] and
V oiceCraft [21].
A different notion of delay arises in end-to-end spoken dia-
logue systems. Moshi formulates dialogue as speech-to-speech
generation, but first predicts time-aligned text tokens as an inter-
mediate semantic plan (the so-called inner monologue), before
emitting acoustic tokens [22]. In this formulation, text predic-
tion is explicitly delayed ahead of acoustic generation, enforc-
ing a semantic-before-acoustic generation order.

complementary delay strategies into our framework:codebook-
level delayandtext-first multi-task delay.

2.5. Importance-aware Codebook Weighting
In RVQ, lower-level codebooks tend to play a more critical role
in speech intelligibility, whereas higher-level codebooks pri-
marily affect perceptual quality and timbre fidelity [18]. Mo-
tivated by this observation, we propose anincremental contri-
butionstrategy to estimate the relative importance of each RVQ
layer and derive the corresponding loss weightswℓ (Fig. 2).
Specifically, we randomly sample 100,000 audio clips and
encode them intoLRVQ layers. We first obtain a baseline score
by decoding the audio after randomizing all codebook layers.
Then, starting from the first layer, we progressively preserve the
ground-truth codes of the firstℓlayers while randomizing the
remaining layers, and compute the reconstruction scores. The
importance of theℓ-th codebook is defined as the incremental
score improvement introduced by retaining this layer. Finally,
these incremental gains are averaged over the sampled set and
normalized to produce the training weightswℓ.

3. Experiments
3.1. Experiment Setup
The autoregressive model was initialized from Qwen 2.5
0.5B [23]. The input noisy speech was sampled at 16 kHz, and
STFT was computed using a Hann window withnfft = 512and
hop size 256, followed by a 4-layer Conformer encoder. Train-
ing used only cross-entropy loss, with the Whisper encoder
frozen and the STFT encoder jointly trained with the autore-
gressive model. Optimization employed Adam with a learning
rate of5×10 −4, 5,000-step warm-up, cosine decay, dynamic
batching of∼40 s audio per batch, and 300,000 training steps.
Following Section 2.5, perceptual quality, speaker similar-
ity, and intelligibility were used to determine the loss weights
wℓ, yielding [0.30, 0.13, 0.12, 0.10, 0.08, 0.09, 0.09, 0.06,
0.03]. The text loss weight was set toλ text = 0.3.
3.2. Datasets and Evaluation metrics
Our training data was acquired through simulation. As indi-
cated in Tables 1 and 2, the clean data amount to more than
30,000 hours, while the noise data total approximately 600
hours. In addition, we produced 700,000 reverberated samples
by simulating environments of large, medium, and small meet-
ing rooms.
The evaluation set comprised the public datasets DNS Chal-
lenge [26], VCTK-DEMAND [27], and URGENT 2025 (a sub-
set containing only Chinese and English) [28], together with an
internal meeting-room dataset of 258 recordings. The internal
recordings were captured in several large, medium, and small
conference rooms at close-talk, 3 m, 5 m, and 8 m distances
under quiet, steady-state, and transient noise conditions.
Perceptual quality was evaluated using DNSMOS
P.808 [29], while intelligibility was measured by the word error
rate (WER) with the FireRedASR [30]. Speaker similarity
(SIM) was measured by the cosine similarity between em-
beddings extracted from the enhanced audio and a registered

3.3. Results and Analysis
We compared DelayGSE with three representative open-
source generative speech enhancement models:StoRM[5],
FlowSE[8], andLLaSE-G1[12], covering diffusion- and
LLM-based paradigms. An internally trainedGAN-based
baseline with a CMGAN-like architecture was also in-
cluded [32]. In addition, several ablated variants of the pro-
posedDelayGSEframework were evaluated:
•DGSE-EW:DelayGSEwithEqual-Weight training, where
all RVQ codebooks share equal CE loss weights.
•DGSE-IW:DelayGSEwithImportance-Weight training us-
ing the scheme in Section 2.5.
•DGSE-IW+T:DGSE-IWaugmented with delayedText-
aware supervision.
•DGSE-IW+TG:DGSE-IW+Twith inference conditioned
onGround-truth text.
Table 3 summarizes the objective evaluation results. The
GAN baseline shows stable performance under noise and re-
verberation but consistently lags behind LLM-based models in
perceptual quality, indicating limited fine-grained modeling ca-
pacity. Diffusion-based methods (StoRM and FlowSE) achieve
competitive results but degrade noticeably under reverberation,
leading to inconsistent performance across datasets. LLaSE-G1
obtains high perceptual scores on several benchmarks, yet in-
curs substantially higher WER, reflecting a preference for wave-
form naturalness over linguistic fidelity.
In contrast, DelayGSE achieves a more balanced perfor-
mance profile. The importance-weighted variant (DGSE-IW)
consistently attains the best or near-best perceptual quality and
timbre similarity while maintaining competitive WER across all
conditions. Compared with continuous-feature generative mod-
els, DelayGSE exhibits improved robustness to noise and rever-

Ablation studies further validate each design choice.
Importance-aware weighting outperforms equal-weight train-
ing by accounting for unequal semantic contributions across
RVQ layers and mitigating gradient conflicts. Delayed text-
aware supervision reduces WER by 15.8% relative by enforcing
a semantic-before-acoustic generation order, which constrains
downstream acoustic prediction and effectively suppresses hal-
lucinations with minimal perceptual impact. Conditioning on
ground-truth text at inference yields a larger WER reduction of
33.1%, representing an upper bound on intelligibility restora-
tion under severe degradation. Additional tests with random
text confirm that the model primarily relies on acoustic cues
when inputs are reliable and selectively leverages textual infor-
mation only under highly distorted conditions.
Overall, these results show that DelayGSE achieves a su-
perior trade-off among perceptual quality, robustness, and in-
telligibility. By integrating importance-aware weighting and
delayed text-aware supervision within a discrete generative
framework, the proposed method effectively bridges robust
GAN-based baselines and perceptually strong LLM-based ap-
proaches.
4. Conclusion
We present DelayGSE, a generative speech enhancement frame-
work with delayed text-aware supervision and importance-
aware codebook weighting. Experiments show that delayed
text-aware supervision reduces hallucination-like artifacts and
improves recognition accuracy, while codebook weighting ac-
celerates convergence and enhances perceptual quality. The
model also supports optional text conditioning for severely de-
graded speech. Future work will focus on reducing latency and
extending the approach to broader multilingual scenarios.
