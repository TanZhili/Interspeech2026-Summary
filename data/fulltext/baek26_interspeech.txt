SPARK: Efficient Audio-Text Matching for User-Defined Keyword Spotting
via Spiking Neural Networks
Seung-Yeop Baek1,∗, Sangho Han2,∗, Joon-Hyuk Chang1,2,∗∗

Abstract
With the increasing prevalence of voice-driven interaction,
keyword spotting (KWS) has become an essential component
of hands-free control. This has led to a growing demand for
user-defined KWS, allowing users to customize target keywords
via text. While various models have emerged to support this,
their high computational costs and energy consumption make
real-world deployment challenging. In response, we introduce
SPARK, a SPike-driven Audio-text matching framework for
eneRgy-efficient user-defined Keyword spotting. SPARK em-
ploys a spike-driven attention mechanism to enable end-to-end
processing within the spiking domain, replacing heavy floating-
point operations with low-cost accumulate operations. Our
experimental results on the LibriPhrase test dataset demon-
strate that SPARK achieves competitive performance with a
2.1 times reduction in parameter count and a 21.7 times re-
duction in energy consumption compared to its artificial neural
network counterpart.

1. Introduction
Keyword spotting (KWS) [1, 2, 3] refers to the task of detecting
whether a predefined keyword is present in an input utterance. As
voice-enabled devices become increasingly widespread, KWS
has become an essential part of everyday life. Simultaneously,
there is a growing demand for personalized wake words that
users can define to suit their individual preferences.
User-defined keyword spotting (UDKWS) enables flexible
interfaces by allowing users to set custom keywords [ 4, 5].
Although a variety of models have been developed to support
the enrollment of new target keywords [6, 7, 8, 9, 10], such sys-
tems require users to record spoken examples of their custom
keywords. Recently, several studies [11, 12, 13, 14, 15, 16] have
explored methods that support text-based keyword enrollment,
determining whether the input utterance matches the enrolled
keyword. However, artificial neural network (ANN)-based mod-
els rely on intensive multiply-accumulate (MAC) operations,
which lead to unnecessary memory overhead and hinder efficient
“always-on” deployment on resource-constrained devices.
To address this, spiking neural networks (SNNs) [17, 18, 19]
provide a promising energy-efficient alternative; they naturally
leverage event-driven and sparse computation to replace power-
intensive MAC operations with low-cost accumulate (AC) oper-
ations. Accordingly, we propose SPARK, a spike-driven audio-
text matching framework for energy-efficient user-defined key-
word spotting. To the best of our knowledge, SPARK is the

The main contributions of this work are as follows:
• We propose SPARK, the first SNN-based UDKWS framework.
Unlike conventional ANN-to-SNN conversion approach,

• As shown in Fig. 1, our experimental results on benchmark

2. Related Work
2.1. User-defined Keyword Spotting
Unlike closed-set KWS [1, 2, 3], UDKWS is typically formu-
lated as a verification task that determines whether the input au-
dio matches the enrolled text sequence. In recent years, various
studies on UDKWS have emerged. Among them, [12, 13] used
cross-modal matching to align audio and text representations in
a shared latent space, while [14, 15] further refined this approach
through phoneme-level supervision to better distinguish hard
negative samples with similar pronunciations.
2.2. Spiking Neural Networks

remarkable energy efficiency by computing information through
sparse, asynchronous binary “spikes” [ 17, 18, 19]. To model
these dynamics, the widely adopted leaky integrate-and-fire (LIF)

2.3. SNNs in KWS
Recently, various studies [22, 23, 24, 25] have applied SNNs to
KWS tasks by leveraging their energy-efficient properties. How-
ever, these existing SNN architectures predominantly formulate
KWS as a closed-set classification task, categorizing input ut-
terances into a fixed set of predefined classes. This paradigm
strictly limits the system to a predefined vocabulary, making it
incapable of enrolling custom keywords dynamically. While
conventional ANN-based systems have successfully advanced
to UDKWS by enabling text-based keyword enrollment without
requiring audio templates [12, 14], such an open-vocabulary ca-
pability has remained unexplored within the SNN domain. To
address this limitation, we extend SNN-based KWS to open-set
scenarios, demonstrating that our framework achieves both com-
petitive verification performance and significant computational
efficiency compared to conventional ANN baselines.
3. Proposed Method
In this section, we introduce our proposed method, SPARK. It
comprises three main modules: spiking audio and text encoders,
spiking pattern extractor, and pattern discriminator. The overall
architecture of SPARK is illustrated in Fig. 2. During training,
we optimize two binary cross-entropy (BCE) losses computed at
the utterance and phoneme levels.
3.1. Model Architecture
Spike-Driven Self-Attention (SDSA).In SPARK, capturing in-
trinsic phoneme correlations and audio-text alignment is crucial.
Thus, we adopt spike-driven self-attention (SDSA) [26] as the
foundational block for both the spiking text encoder (STE) and
spiking pattern extractor (SPE).
The conventional Transformer [27] computes attention via
dot products, requiring power-intensive floating-point matrix
multiplications. Spikformer [28] replaces these with sparse ad-
ditions but still computes an N×N attention map, retaining
quadratic complexityO(N 2). SDSA [ 26] overcomes this via
a multiplication-free “Mask & Add” mechanism, which masks
padded regions and computes attention scores by accumulating
spikes along the temporal axis. This reduces the complexity to a
linear scaleO(N)while preserving modeling capability:

4. Experiments
4.1. Datasets
Following [14], we trained on theLibriPhrasedataset [ 12], con-
structed from thetrain-clean-100andtrain-clean-360subsets
ofLibriSpeech[ 32]. It contains 800k samples, with 200k for
each phrase length from 1 to 4 words. The maximum audio
duration and phoneme-sequence length were 2 seconds and 35,
respectively. For evaluation, we used theLibriPhrasetest set
constructed from theLibriSpeech train-other-500subset.
4.2. Experimental Setup
The input audio was pre-processed into 40-bin log-mel spec-
trograms using a 25 ms window and a 10 ms frame shift. The
embedding dimensionD was set to 128, and we employed PLIF
neurons asSN withVreset = 0. The SEE module was config-
ured with PConv (kernel size 1) and DConv (kernel size 7). The
model was trained for 100 epochs with a batch size of 256 using
the AdamW optimizer [ 33] with a learning rate of 1×10 −3.
The simulation stepSwas set to 8, and theλwas set to 0.3.
4.3. Evaluation Metrics
KWS performance was measured using the area under the curve
(AUC) and the equal error rate (EER). To evaluate the com-
putational efficiency of the proposed SNN model, we adopted
synaptic operations (SOPs) [ 34], which are the SNN counter-
part to floating-point operations (FLOPs) in ANNs (FLOPs≈
2×MACs ). Following [26, 29], SOPs are FLOPs scaled by the
spiking rate, counting only actual operations. Theoretical energy
consumption was estimated based on the 45 nm CMOS process
[35], where the energy cost of an AC operation, EAC, is 0.9
pJ, and that of a MAC operation,EM AC, is 4.6 pJ. The energy
consumption for both models is calculated as follows:

5. Results
5.1. Main Results
In Table 1, we compared the KWS performance, complexity, and
energy consumption of SPARK with ANN baselines [ 12, 14].
The results demonstrated that SPARK achieved an efficient trade-
off between KWS performance and low computational cost.
While PhonMatchNet [ 14] achieved the best AUC and EER
scores, SPARK surpassed CMCD [ 12] and maintained com-
petitive results, achieving an AUC of 99.07% and an EER of
3.97% on LE. Beyond these competitive detection capabilities,
the primary advantage of SPARK was reflected in the complexity
and energy consumption metrics. Although PhonMatchNet [14]
used a heavy pre-trained audio encoder module [ 36], SPARK
is constructed as a native end-to-end spiking architecture with-
out relying on any pre-trained audio encoders. Consequently,
SPARK requires only 18.44µJ per inference, achieving a 6.8
times reduction in energy consumption relative to CMCD [12]
(125.12µJ ), an ANN baseline that also operates without pre-
trained models, and a 21.7 times reduction relative to PhonMatch-
Net [14] (400.66µJ ). This substantial efficiency was enabled by
spike-driven modules (SAE, STE, SPE), which largely replace
power-intensive MAC operations with low-cost AC operations.
Fig. 3 presents the average spike rate of each module, re-
flecting the proportion of essential features propagated to sub-
sequent layers. The SAE exhibited the lowest rate (4.55%),
as silent regions produced no threshold-crossing events, mini-
mizing computation during inactive intervals. The STE rates
(4.58%–24.68%) indicated that attention gates were selectively
activated by relevant phoneme patterns, allowing only signals
exceeding the spiking threshold to propagate further. The SPE
(14.88%–23.57%) selectively suppressed less relevant features,
passing only evidence critical for verification. The GSN showed
the highest spike rate (43.76%), preserving global evidence for
the final verification decision. However, because it accounts for
only a small fraction of the model, its contribution to the overall
energy overhead remains minimal. This sparsity enables SPARK
to maintain low energy consumption across all modules.

5.2. Ablation Study
As reported in Table 2, we validated the SPARK architecture by
selectively replacing individual spiking modules with their ANN
counterparts from PhonMatchNet [14].
Necessity of Spiking Audio Encoder (Variant 1).Reverting
the SAE to ANN degraded KWS performance to EER of 5.07%,
increasing energy consumption to 291.32µJ . This highlights
the necessity of our SEE module for efficient audio encoding.
Significance of Spiking Text Encoder (Variant 2).Replacing
the STE with ANN reduced KWS performance (EER: 4.83%),
demonstrating that the STSA module effectively extracts context-
aware features from static phoneme embeddings.
Synergy of End-to-End Spiking Encoders (Variant 3).
Applying ANNs to both audio and text encoders slightly im-
proved performance but drastically increased energy consump-
tion to 291.04µJ . These results demonstrate the architectural
synergy between the two encoders, where the low-power advan-
tages are fully preserved only when both modules operate within
the spiking domain.
Efficiency of Spiking Pattern Extractor (Variant 4).Substitut-
ing the SPE with ANN provided a performance gain. However, it
sharply increased energy consumption to 150.89µJ , confirming
the substantial energy efficiency of our spike-driven attention
mechanism.
These results demonstrated that incorporating dense ANN
operations severely increased energy consumption, suggesting
that SPARK provides a strong trade-off for always-on devices.
6. Conclusion
In this paper, we proposed SPARK, the first SNN-based UDKWS
framework that enables text-based enrollment through spike-
driven audio and text encoders, a spiking pattern extractor, and a
GSN-based pattern discriminator. Experimental results demon-
strated that SPARK achieved competitive performance with a 2.1
times reduction in parameter count and a 21.7 times reduction in
energy consumption compared with its ANN counterpart, provid-
ing a lightweight, always-on solution for resource-constrained
edge devices.
