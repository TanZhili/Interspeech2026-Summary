Abstract
End-to-end acoustic named entity recognition (NER) in-
tegrates speech transcription and entity detection within a
unified model, avoiding error propagation in cascaded ASR-
NER systems. However, organization entities remain signif-
icantly harder to detect due to multi-word spans, acronyms,
and lexical variability. We present a systematic analysis of
organization recognition failures in acoustic NER, revealing
that conventional cross-entropy training inadequately models
entity span dependencies. To address this limitation, we
propose an organization-aware Whisper-based acoustic NER
framework combining LLM-based targeted semantic augmen-
tation, category-specific boundary supervision, and a structure-
constrained entity learning (SCEL) objective that enforces en-
tity span consistency during decoding. Experiments demon-
strate substantial improvements in organization recognition
while maintaining strong ASR performance, outperforming in
entity-recognition for several other existing approaches.
Index Terms: Named-entity recognition, Automatic speech
recognition, Organization entity modeling, Whisper, LLM.
1. Introduction
In natural language understanding (NLU), extracting the seman-
tic information from the input enhances human-to-computer
interactions. Named-entities (NEs), describing persons, loca-
tions, organizations, or some non-constituting rare words con-
tain one of the major semantic contexts along with other lex-
ical components, such as parts-of-speech, intent, and action
words [1, 2, 3, 4]. Traditionally,named-entity recognition
(NER) has been formulated as a sequence labeling task in the
text domain [5, 6, 7]. However, with the increasing adoption
of voice-driven interfaces in smart devices and conversational
applications, extracting named entities directly from speech, re-
ferred to asacoustic NER, has become an important and emerg-
ing research direction [8, 9, 10, 11, 12].
Extracting entities directly from speech introduces unique,
non-trivial challenges compared to text, primarily due to the
absence of explicit punctuation, acoustic-domain ambiguity
among homophones, and the presence of environmental noise
and channel-related distortions in speech signals. Primarily,
acoustic NER systems are developed in a pipeline approach.
The speech signal is first transcribed into text using an au-
tomatic speech recognition (ASR) system [13, 14], and the
resulting transcript is then processed by a text-domain NER
model [9]. Although widely adopted but this two-stage ap-
proach has several limitations. First, being multi-stage, the effi-
ciency of the first-stage ASR systems becomes too critical con-
cerning error propagation to the subsequent NER model. Since
most NER models are trained on clean, human-generated text,
they are often not robust to the recognition errors present in
ASR transcripts [15, 16, 17]. Second, the pipeline requires
training and maintaining two independent systems operating
across different modalities, preventing joint optimization to-
ward a unified objective [18, 19].
Regarding these, limited yet notable research has been con-
ducted in recent years to develop an end-to-end acoustic model
for detecting NEs directly from speech. One of the early at-
tempts was proposed by Ghannay et al. [9], who developed
an entity-aware ASR framework for French. In their approach,
named entities were embedded into the transcription sequence
by surrounding entity spans with special tokens, enabling the
ASR model to jointly perform transcription and entity detection.
They collected open-source French ASR datasets and manually
annotated the NE words in groundtruth references by surround-
ing them with special⟨NE⟩tokens. In a similar approach,
Yadav et al. [10] proposed an acoustic NER model based on
Deep Speech 2 [20] for English. In [21], acoustic NER is ex-
plored in Mandarin by proposing the AISHEL-NER database.
In recent years, Yu et al. [12], have developed LLM and TTS
based benchmark for semi-supervised annotation of English and
Mandarin acoustic NER. In [11], Whisper [22] was extended to
recognize open-type NEs via prompt-driven fine-tuning across
multiple English datasets.
Upon analyzing these limited but emerging acoustic NER
literature, we observe one common pitfall: Organization entities
are consistently more challenging to detect than person or loca-
tion entities. To our knowledge, no prior work in the acoustic
domain has explicitly attempted to investigate and address this
issue. Towards this, we first develop a Whisper-based entity-
aware joint ASR and NER framework covering three common
named entities, person, location, and organization. Thereafter,
we systematically study all organization-class failure cases in
the validation set and identify several key aspects that lead to
consistent poor performance, including multi-word spans, fre-
quent use of acronyms, and high lexical variability with many
out-of-vocabulary terms. Moreover, organization entities often
contain non-entity words within their spans, making boundary
detection more ambiguous in speech than in text.
To address these challenges, we propose a structured
Whisper-based acoustic NER framework incorporating both
data-centric and optimization-centric improvements. Specif-
ically, we introduce targeted semantic augmentation and
organization-aware training strategies, together with a struc-
tural loss formulation, to improve entity boundary prediction
and reduce tag confusion. Experimental results show that the

2. Organization-aware structured acoustic
NER framework
2.1. Diagnosing organization entity weaknesses
To investigate the root causes of the organization entity’s in-
ferior recognition, using our Whisper-based entity-aware ASR
baseline (cf. 3), we perform inference using our validation set
utterances. Analyzing these results leads us to several key find-
ings that are particularly predominant for the organization NEs.
• Organization names often consist of multi-word spans, which
may even include non-entity tokens within them (e.g., World
Health Organization, Bavarian Motor Works).
• Organizations frequently appear in abbreviated forms (e.g.,
WHO, BMW), while the full-form expansion may appear in
other contexts, introducing lexical ambiguity during training.
• Organization entities are prone to appear as out-of-vocabulary
(OOV) terms during evaluation, since new institution names are
often underrepresented in training corpora.
Finally, acoustic NER models must infer entity boundaries
directly from speech without access to punctuation or capital-
ization cues, leading to boundary instability in which entity
spans are either truncated or extended beyond their correct lim-
its. Motivated by these observations, we design a category-
aware training framework that introduces organization-specific
supervision and structured optimization objectives to improve
entity span modeling.
2.2. Targeted semantic augmentation
LLM-based organization entity restructuring (abbreviation
expansion, lexical variation):The acoustic NER dataset we
use [10], although rich in<loc>and<per>entities, contains
comparatively limited coverage of<org>entities in both fre-
quency and structural diversity. This imbalance leads to reduced
exposure of organization patterns during training. Moreover,
organization entities frequently appear as multi-word spans and
often contain abbreviations, which increases the likelihood of
incorrect boundary predictions or missing<end>tokens in
acoustic NER outputs.
To mitigate these issues, we propose an LLM-assisted
semantic augmentation pipeline to expand the diversity of
organization entity expressions. We employ the 14B pa-
rameter instruction-tuned language modelPhi-3-medium-128k-
instruct 1 as a controlled sentence rewriter to generate semanti-
cally equivalent variants of organization-tagged transcripts. As
illustrated in Figure 1, the augmentation process follows a two-
stage prompting strategy.
In the first stage, the model generates paraphrased variants
of<org>tagged sentences while preserving entity spans. The
prompts encourage lexical variation and abbreviation expan-
sion (e.g.,ICC→International Cricket Council). However,
direct LLM generation occasionally introduces tag inconsisten-
cies. Therefore, in the second stage, the same model is used as
a grammar and tag-correction module. By providing both the
original and generated sentences, the model refines the entity
annotations and restores correct tagging boundaries. The en-
tire output then goes through a rule-based cleanup to remove
malformed annotations, such as incorrect tags (missing<end>
token, incorrect tokens such as<organ>).
TTS-based acoustic synthesis for LLM-augmented
texts:The semantically augmented and validated sentences are
subsequently converted back to speech using the XTTS-2 text-
to-speech (TTS) model [23]. During synthesis, the entity to-
kens are removed from the TTS inputs. To maintain acoustic
diversity, synthesis is conditioned on multiple speaker profiles
sampled from the original datasets. The synthesized utterances
are then incorporated into the acoustic NER training data.
2.3. Category-aware training strategy
Conventional acoustic NER frameworks use a category-
agnostic<end>token for all entity types, which can lead to
ambiguous boundary predictions, particularly for multi-word
organization entities. The proposed<org end>token allows
the model to explicitly learn category-specific boundary pat-
terns for organization spans. During inference, this token is
collapsed back to the standard<end>token to maintain com-
patibility with existing evaluation protocols.

3. Experimental setup
Database details:We use the English acoustic NER database
presented in [10], containing 150 hours of manually anno-
tated NE spans covering person (<per>), location (<loc>),
and organization (<org>) categories. For the majority of
experiments, we use a common entity end boundary token
(<end>). For category-aware supervision, we use an addi-
tional (<org end>) token during training. This database is
constructed from LibriSpeech [24] and Common V oice [25] and
provides 38,891 unique named entities. We follow a random
90 : 5 : 5split for training, validation, and evaluation while
ensuring balanced coverage of each NE category.
Framework consideration:Following the ASR literature, we
choose Whisper-small [22] as the backbone architecture. Due to
computational constraints, we perform the experiments on the
Whisper-small. The main purpose of this study is to improve
entity consistency regularization, without confounding our con-
tributions with exploring architectural variations. To preserve
the pre-trained ASR representations, the encoder is kept frozen
during training. For the decoder, we empirically freeze the first
nine layers during fine-tuning. Training is performed using the
Adam optimizer for up to 50k steps with a learning rate5e −4
and 2k warm-up steps. The weights of the SCEL losses are
tuned using the validation set.
Performance metrics:Performance is evaluated using ASR
(excluding tags) word error rate (WER) and named entity recog-
nition precision, recall, and F1-score. We also report category-
wise NE performance. Since the main objective of this work is
to improve organization entity recognition, the organization F1-
score is treated as the primary metric, while ensuring no degra-
dation in overall ASR performance or in other NE categories.

the proposed structured, entity-focusing developments achieve
a delicate balance between ASR and entity recognition perfor-

5. Conclusion
We focus on improving end-to-end acoustic NER performance
for the organization entity category. Organization recognition
is more challenging than other NE categories, primarily due to
its multi-span structure with intervening non-entity words. To
address this, we propose a structure-constrained entity learning
framework with entity modeling and structural regularization
losses. In addition, we introduce LLM-based targeted semantic
augmentation and explicit end-boundary modeling for organi-
zation entities. The proposed methods significantly improve or-
ganization recognition compared to existing approaches, while
also achieving the lowest WER and consistent performance
across other NE categories. Future work will explore fur-
ther improvements for organization recognition and extend the
framework to multilingual acoustic NER.
