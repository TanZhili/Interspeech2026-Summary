Abstract 
We examine continuation rises in Cypriot Greek (CYG) and 
Athenian Greek (ATG) as a case study for comparing and 
evaluating methods of dynamic data analysis applied to contact-
induced intonational variation. We analyse contemporary data 
from young speakers using two approaches: Legendre 
Polynomial modelling and Generalized Additive Mixed 
Modelling (GAMM). Both methods converge in identifying 
two distinct CYG patterns: a low-nuclear ATG-like contour 
(CYG-nl), and a high-nuclear variant (CYG-nh) with reduced 
global slope and increased cur vature. While polynomial 
modelling captures global geometric properties of the contour, 
GAMM specifies differences in relative time, especially around 
the nuclear high target. The comparison shows that 
complementary modelling systems can shed light on different 
aspects of the phonetic reali zation and phonological 
interpretation of intonation in contact scenarios. 
Index Terms: Prosodic contact, variation, multilingualism, 
Cypriot Greek, dynamic data analysis approaches 
1. Introduction 
In situations of linguistic contact, prosodic patterns from the 
relevant linguistic systems may coexist, intertwine or otherwise 
influence one another, producing variability that is challenging 
for both phonological analysis and statistical modelling. By 
taking a methodological perspective, we examine variation in 
continuation rises in Cypriot Greek (CYG), compared with 
Athenian Greek (ATG), as a case study of contact-induced 
prosodic variation. We analyse these continuation rises using 
two modelling approaches , Legendre Polynomial and 
Generalized Additive Mixed Modelling, with the aim of not 
only comparing methods, but also reflecting on how these 
different analyses shed light on the phonetic realization and 
phonological interpretation of intonation in the context of 
contact. We explore the extent to which each can model the 
continuous nature of f0 changes and assess whether either one 
is better suited to this task or whether the two approaches are 
ultimately equivalent, or even complementary. 
1.1. Background 
1.1.1. Analysing dynamic data 
Acoustic parameters of speech such as f0, intensity, and spectral 
structure are continuous and thus vary over time, reflecting the 
dynamic nature of speech production . Any linguistic 
interpretation of these dynamic parameters depends on the 
interpretation of time series trajectories, for which different 
linguistic frameworks propose different approaches, some 
incorporating the dynamicity, and others abstracting away from 
it. Within the Autosegmental-Metrical (AM) framework, 
meaningful events such as pitch accents and edge tones are 
modelled as discrete tonal targets (e.g., L, H) associated with 
metrically defined positions, with the observed f0 dynamic 
contour arising through phonetic interpolation between these 
turning points [1], [2], [3]. Even within this static target-based 
view, however, the initial (and arguably ultimate) empirical 
object of analysis is a continuous signal whose shape reflects 
the alignment, scaling, and interaction of tonal targets across 
time, aspects that themselves carry phonological, pragmatic 
and/or sociophonetic meaning [4], [5], [6], [7]. 
Nevertheless, methodological approaches that break down 
continuous prosodic data into static targets at discrete 
landmarks often fail to capture the functional organization of 
pitch contours. In recognition of this, over the past two decades, 
dynamic data-analytic techniques have been increasingly 
adopted in laboratory phonology and experimental phonetic 
investigations of prosody (see [8] for a recent review). These 
techniques allow researchers to model entire contours as 
continuous functions, quantify communicatively meaningful 
differences in curvature and temporal alignment  with the 
segmental string, and thus statistically evaluate systematic 
variation across speakers, conditions and linguistic varieties. 
The proliferation of techniques available offers powerful new 
insights; however, choosing between available techniques 
requires careful consideration of the nature of the research 
question posed, and the benefits and limitations of different 
approaches in their application to that question. 
The very use of dynamic modelling raises an important 
question: what exactly is being compared when entire f0 
trajectories are statistically model led? From an AM 
perspective, the objects of phonological analysis are discrete 
tonal targets and their alignment relations, while the continuous 
f0 curve is seen as their phonetic implementation. Dynamic 
statistical models, however, examine the surface contour 
differently, creating a potential mismatch between the  
analytical insights and level of representation captured by the 
model, and the type and level of representation posited by 
theoretical frameworks such as AM. A Legendre Polynomial fit 
or a GAMM smooth provide a mathematically precise 
description of curvature across time, but they do not distinguish 
between phonologically contrastive targets and phonetic 
interpolation. Any comparison of analytic approaches must 
make explicit which aspects of the continuous f0 trajectory are 
being foregrounded and how these relate to phonological 
interpretation. All modelling begins with the same acoustic 
signal, but different techniques highlight different properties of 
that signal, such as global shape, local alignment, scaling, and 
temporal interaction, and so provide different kinds of evidence 
to be used for phonological analysis. However, the issue is not 
whether one analyses phonetic or phonological aspects per se, 
but how particular representations of the phonetic  contour 
inform possible phonological interpretations. This distinction 
becomes important when the same surface trajectory could arise 
from different configurations of tonal targets, scaling  and 
alignment, or when distinct surface contours arise from a 
common underlying structure. 
These questions go to the heart of the relationship between 
phonological structure and phonetic implementation for any 
linguistic system. However, the issue becomes even more 
complex in contact situations such as the CYG variety  
examined here, where, for a given speech community, multiple

phonological structure and phonetic form, including contour

1.1.2. Cypriot Greek 
CYG is a variety of Modern Greek [9], [10] spoken on the 
island of Cyprus and elsewhere in diasporic communities. CYG 
speakers are bi(dia)lectal [11], [12] between CYG and a Cypriot 
variant of Standard Modern Greek, the former acquired at home 
and the latter taught in formal education. The two varieties are 
functionally differentiated within a diglossic arrangement [13], 
[14]. Following the population displacements of 1974, regional 
subvarieties are said to have levelled, giving rise to a 
Pancyprian koine marked by structural mixing and covert 
prestige [12], [15], [16]. Despite much shared lexico-
grammatical material, the two varieties are known to diverge 
across all levels of linguistic analysis [9], [17]. 
Previous research on pre-1974 CYG [18] investigated the 
intonation of continuation rises in archival recordings and 
compared their f0 trajectories with those of ATG, mainland 
Turkish, and Asia Minor Greek. Using a polynomial modelling 
analysis, [18] identified the co-existence of two functionally 
equivalent but phon ologically distinct continuation-rise 
patterns, differing in contour shape and temporal alignment, 
and demonstrated that while one of these two patterns was 
similar to ATG (as expected, given the genetic relationship with 
ATG), the other was Turkish -like. These findings were 
interpreted as evidence for contact-induced variation rather than 
resulting from CYG system-internal variation. The logic behind 
this conclusion depended on considering not just possible 
endogenic explanations for the additional variant, but also 
exogenous influences, e.g. through linguistic contact, for which 
quantitative phonetic comparison was more pertinent, and 
ultimately illuminating. 
1.2. Aims 
This study revisits this contact-induced phenomenon in CYG, 
using contemporary data from younger speakers and semi -
spontaneous tasks, and extends the earlier analysis through a 
methodological comparison between Legendre Polynomial and 
Generalized Additive Mixed Modelling (GAMM). 
2. Methods 
2.1. Data collection 
We elicited and analysed speech productions from the varieties 
of interest: 8 speakers of ATG (6F, 2M), and 13 speakers of 
CYG (10F, 3M, from Nicosia). Participants were aged 18-30 
and completed a detailed sociolinguistic questionnaire. A range 
of tasks were completed, including semi -spontaneous 
conversation and a map-task (see [18] for more details). 
An utterance was classified as a continuation rise if it 
occurred in a broad-focus declarative, was non-final, and ended 
in a high boundary tone, optionally followed by a brief pause. 
In ATG, the continuation rise tune consists of a L* nuclear pitch 
accent aligned with the stressed vowel, followed by a simple f0 
rise ending in a H− phrase accent [18], [19], [20]. In CYG, 
besides the ATG-like tune, labelled CYG-nl here, a second 
pattern is identified in which the nuclear vowel aligns with a 
high tonal target rather than a low one before the final rise 
(CYG-nh) [18]. 
Target phrases were annotated in Praat [21] as intervals 
extending from the onset of the prenuclear syllable to the offset 
of the phrase. The Region of Interest (RoI) thus began prior to 
the stressed syllable of the nuclear word and extended to the end 
of the phrase. This ensured that the entire f0 movement 
associated with the nuclear accent and the phrase edge was 
captured. 
2.2. Polynomial Modelling 
For each utterance, f0 was extracted in Hz at 10 ms intervals 
using ESPS get_f0 [22], and pitch errors manually inspected 
and corrected. Values obtained in Hz were subsequently 
converted into semitones by token. A 10th-order polynomial 
was fitted to each contour using GNU Octave’s polyfit function 
[23]. The RoI was then modelled with a 4th-order polynomial, 
subsequently expressed in the orthogonal Legendre basis (cf. 
[24]). This approach is particularly suited to modelling 
continuous f0 contours, even in data containing substantial gaps 
due to voiceless segments. It is also ecologically valid as 
listeners do not perceive such small gaps in the f0 signal. The 
resulting five coefficients (c₀–c₄) of the polynomial capture 
global contour properties: mean f0 height (c₀), slope (c₁), 
curvature (c₂), and higher order N-like and M/W-like shapes. 
(c₃, c₄). Coefficients c₁–c₃ were selected for statistical analysis 
as they showed more distinct patterns compared to others and 
were entered as dependent variables in linear mixed-effects 
regression (LMER) models (lmerTest package, [25]), including 
variety*tone (ATG, CYG-nl, CYG, nh) as a fixed effect and 
speaker as a random effect with by-speaker variety*tone slopes. 
2.3. GAMM 
To evaluate whether the findings are robust across analytic 
frameworks, we additionally modelled the same data using 
GAMM, (e.g., [26], [27], [28] for recent studies). GAMM, like 
Polynomial modelling, is well-suited for modelling f0 contours 
because it estimates smooth underlying functions rather than 
treating measurements as independent time points. However, 
while both methods interpolate across gaps in the data, GAMM 
alone uses a penalised spline framework whereby smooth 
functions are estimated using flexible basis functions with an 
added penalty that controls excessive curvature and prevents 
overfitting. The absence of observations simply removes local 
constraints on the smooth, allowing the trajectory to be 
determined by neighbouring data points and global smoothness 
penalties. When functional random effects are included, the 
estimated trajectory through a gap additionally reflects speaker- 
or item-specific f0 patterns learned from other, complete 
observations [28], [29], [30], [31], [32]. 
Consistent with the polynomial method described above, f0 
was sampled in Hz at 10 ms intervals and converted to 
semitones to ensure comparability across tokens and speakers. 
The data extraction was conducted in Praat through R [33] 
using the praatsauce function from the sauceshelf package [34]. 
The extracted data were subsequently checked manually for 
octave jumps and other tracking errors. 
We fitted two GAMM models via bam() (mgcv package; 
[29]) following [31]. The full model incorporated a 
variety*tone factor as a parametric term and a by-factor smooth 
over normalised RoI duration; the reduced model excluded this 
factor. Models were compared using Maximum Likelihood 
(ML) to test overall factor significance. To account for speaker-
specific variation and mitigate residual autocorrelation, we 
included random smooths for speaker and token using factor-
smooth interactions in both models. All terms utilised cubic 
regression splines. Significant localised differences between

3.1. Results of polynomial analysis 
Consistent with the findings from archival recordings in [18], 
contemporary CYG data exhibit two distinct contour types. 
LMER models fitted on the polynomial coefficient data found 
a significant effect of variety*tone for all three coefficients: 
F(2,21.41) = 74.872, p < .001 for c₁; F(2,21.11) = 9.892, p < 
.001 for c₂; F(2,21.32) = 52.245, p < .001 for c₃. Post-hoc tests 
revealed that CYG-nh differs significantly from both CYG-nl 
and ATG (p < .05 at least) in all cases, whereas ATG and CYG-
nl do not differ significantly from each other. These results 
indicate CYG-nh contours are characterized by reduced overall 
slope (lower c₁), greater curvature (higher c₂), and more 
pronounced N-like configuration (higher c₃) relative to the other 
two categories, replicating the presence of two phonetically 
distinct continuation-rise patterns in contemporary speakers. 
These results are illustrated in Figure 2, with the three 
Legendre coefficients plotted in a three-dimensional space. 
CYG-nh forms a distinct cluster from CYG-nl and ATG. 
Specifically, c₁ values are lower for CYG-nh, clustering closer 
to, or below, zero, indicating reduced overall tilt of the contour 
or even a reversal in global slope direction. In addition, c₂ 
values are higher for CYG-nh, consistent with a more overall 
U-shaped contour, and c₃ values are also elevated relative to the 
other two categories, which cluster around zero, suggesting a 
more pronounced N-shaped configuration. Furthermore, we 
observe a negative correlation between c₁ and c₃, indicating 
greater contour complexity (higher c₃ values) associated with a 
reduction in overall slope (lower c₁ values); in other words, the 
more N-shaped a curve is, the less tilted it is.

4. Methods evaluation 
Both Polynomial Modelling and GAMM are designed to 
capture nonlinear contour dynamics and thus go beyond point-
based analyses of tonal targets. In the present study, both 
methods converge on the same core finding for CYG: CYG-nh 
constitutes a contour type distinct from both CYG-nl and ATG, 
while the latter two remain largely overlapping. The polynomial 
analysis identifies this distinction in terms of global contour 
properties—reduced overall slope and increased curvature and 
N-shaped configuration in CYG-nh—whereas the GAMM 
analysis localizes the divergence to specific temporal regions 
around the early high target and portions of the final rise. 
Despite these differences, the results are empirically compatible 
and demonstrate the existence of two distinct continuation-rise 
patterns in contemporary CYG, a result consistent with the 
findings from archival recordings in [18]. 
In terms of similarities between methods, Polynomial 
Modelling requires the polynomial order to be determined a 
priori, and GAMM also necessitates the pre-categorization of 
tokens, here informed by qualitative inspection of the contours. 
Choosing between the models therefore depends on theoretical 
or impressionistic assumptions about the contour’s structure, 
for example whether it is likely to involve a simple rise, a rise-
fall, or more complex patterns. In contact contexts where prior 
research may suggest the presence of distinct contour types, 
such hypothesis-driven specification may be analytically 
appropriate. 
Regarding method-specific advantages, since Polynomial 
Models represent each curve as a single global function over the 
RoI, they are arguably most useful for evaluating overall 
phonetic similarity. In contact situations, where the question 
may be whether a phonetic pattern has been adopted as a whole, 
such global comparison is especially informative. Rather than 
isolating local turning points, Polynomial Modelling captures 
the overall geometric structure of the contour. The Legendre 
coefficients provide a low-dimensional representation of this 
structure, with orthogonal parameters approximating slope, 
curvature, and higher-order shape characteristics. Differences 
between curves are reflected indirectly in contrasts among 
coefficients, allowing global properties such as overall rise 
versus fall, U-shaped versus ∩-shaped curvature, and degree of 
inflection, to be quantitatively compared. Furthermore, 
examining the distribution of coefficients across tokens can 
indicate distinct global contour types or unmodelled 
heterogeneity within a variety. 
The strength of Polynomial modelling in capturing global 
contour structure also results in certain constraints. Having to 
specify the polynomial degree in advance limits flexibility. 
Increasing the order to accommodate more complex shapes can 
lead to instability and overfitting. As a global function, a 
polynomial fit is less sensitive to localized temporal 
differences, which can be problematic when phonological 
interpretation depends on precise alignment with segmental 
landmarks or stress, unless extra analyses of alignment are 
conducted. Additionally, Polynomial models do not natively 
account for hierarchical structure, random effects, or temporal 
autocorrelation, all of which are relevant in the modelling of 
linguistic data. They are also sensitive to edge effects, reducing 
reliability near phrase boundaries, potentially critical when 
analysing nuclear or boundary tones. Thus, while Polynomial 
modelling excels at summarizing contour shape, it is less suited 
to capturing how f0 curves interact with linguistic structure over 
time. It is thus most informative when contours are to be viewed 
as a global phonetic object rather than as a tune governed by 
segmental and grammatical structure. 
In contrast, GAMM provides a flexible, spline-based 
estimation of contour shape, that allows localized examination 
of temporal structure. It facilitates the identification of turning 
points, relative timing of extrema, and local scaling differences, 
which in turn allows for directly relating these to phonological 
targets and alignment patterns. GAMM also naturally 
incorporates random effects, random smooths and temporal 
dependence within a unified framework. Its spline-based 
structure tends to behave more stably near boundaries, which is 
more useful when modelling contours in relation to hierarchical 
prosodic structure. Difference smooths allow an explicit 
localization of curve differences, which reveals not only 
whether contours differ but also where along the time axis these 
differences occur. When supplemented with temporal 
alignment information, such as the timing of s yllable 
boundaries and intonational targets, such information can 
provide comparisons of tonal alignment and scaling. These 
properties make GAMM particularly suited for system-internal
