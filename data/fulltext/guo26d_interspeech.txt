Lightweight Convolutional Front-ends for Real-time Framewise Phoneme
Recognition in Cochlear Implants
Yuchu Guo1, Leslie M. Collins 2, Boyla O. Mainsah 2
1 Duke Kunshan University, China
2 Duke University, Durham, NC, USA
yuchu.guo@dukekunshan.edu.cn, leslie.collins@duke.edu, boyla.mainsah@duke.edu
Abstract
Cochlear implants (CIs) are life-changing devices for individu-
als with severe hearing loss. Improving real-time speech en-
hancement is vital for speech perception in noisy and rever-
berant environments, yet many neural models are incompati-
ble with strict on-device latency and causality constraints. This
work investigates lightweight, causal convolutional front-ends
for real-time phoneme classiﬁcation for CI applications. We
compare 1D-time/frequency and 2D time-frequency structures,
with standard, dilated, and deformable convolutions, paired
with various sequence model backends. Results show adding
convolutional front-ends improve phoneme classiﬁcation per-
formance. In simulated deployment on a low-power STM32
microcontroller, 1D structures satisfy sub-10ms latency with
low memory footprint and dilated convolutions offer an efﬁcient
means to expand receptive ﬁeld in a predictable manner. These
ﬁndings provide practical insights into architectural trade-offs
for model deployment in CI speech processing systems.
Index Terms: cochlear implants, framewise phoneme recogni-
tion, causal speech processing, computational linguistics
1. Introduction
Cochlear implants (CIs) are medical devices that restore hear-
ing to individuals with moderate to severe sensorineural hearing
loss [1]. Although modern CI systems enable most users to per-
ceive speech and environmental sounds in quiet, understanding
speech in noisy or reverberant environments remains a major
challenge. Current commercially available CIs rely on front-
end signal preprocessing techniques, such as directional micro-
phones [2] and signal-to-noise-based noise reduction [3], which
are not as effective when speech and noise sources are collo-
cated, in non-stationary noise or reverberant settings [4, 5, 6].
Time-frequency (TF) masking is a widely studied approach
for speech enhancement, in which the TF representation of
speech is element-wise multiplied by a gain matrix to suppress
distortion-dominated speech regions [7]. Early TF mask esti-
mation methods for CI applications were primarily statistical-
based, leveraging features such as kurtosis [8], linear prediction
residual-to-reverberant ratios [9], or signal-to-distortion ratios
[10]. While these techniques have low complexity for on-device
CI deployment, blind implementation was challenging as they
often required environment-speciﬁc tuning and exhibited lim-
ited robustness across diverse acoustic conditions.
Advances in machine learning have led to signiﬁcant
improvements in speech enhancement and speech recogni-
tion. Convolutional neural networks, which are typically two-
dimensional (2D), are used for initial encoding of compressed
TF features. Recurrent neural networks (RNNs) [11], includ-
ing long short-term memory (LSTM) [12] and gated recurrent
unit (GRU) models [13], have demonstrated strong capabilities
in modeling temporal dependencies in speech. More recently,
attention-based models [14] and state-space models (SSMs)
[15] have emerged as powerful sequence modeling frameworks,
offering improved long-range context modeling.
Despite these advances, deploying deep neural network
(DNN) models on CI hardware remains an unmet goal due to
constraints on available computing resources. CI processors op-
erate at a frame level under real-time requirements with no ac-
cess to future context, making causality and low latency ( ≤10
ms) essential design considerations. Architectural choices that
are effective in ofﬂine, cloud-based, or high-capacity resource
settings are therefore unsuitable for on-device CI deployment.
Most modern hearing aids that are labeled “AI-powered” use
AI during development or for automated setting adjustments
based on acoustic scene classiﬁcation [6]. While there are a
few commercially available hearing aids with embedded DNNs
(typically 3-4M parameters) for real-time speech enhancement
[16, 17], there is currently no commercially available CI with
on-device DNN-based speech enhancement.
For real-time feasibility and practical deployment, there
is increased focus on lightweight speech enhancement mod-
els with causal framewise implementation directly within the
CI sound processing pipeline [18, 19, 20]. To improve ro-
bustness, phoneme-based TF mask estimation relies on frame-
wise phoneme predictions to weight outputs from phoneme-
speciﬁc mask estimation experts [20, 21]. Even at relatively low
phoneme classiﬁcation accuracies (30-40%), signiﬁcant gains
in speech enhancement can be achieved when compared to
conventional (phoneme-independent) TF mask estimation [21].
However, technical benchmarking for potential deployment of
speech enhancement models in CI embedded settings has been
lacking. There is a need to investigate how alternative architec-
tural choices affect performance under real-time constraints.
In phoneme-based TF mask estimation, the phoneme classi-
ﬁer performance is the main limiting factor [21]. Using a causal
phoneme classiﬁcation model as a representative neural net-
work for CI applications, we systematically investigate archi-
tectural design choices for real-time framewise phoneme clas-
siﬁcation. We study the impact of lightweight, strictly causal
convolutional front-end designs and their interaction with dif-
ferent sequence model back-ends. Our goal is to provide prac-
tical insights into architectural trade-offs that balance accuracy,
efﬁciency, and deployability for real-time CI speech processing
in challenging acoustic environments.

ii) Sequence Model Back-Ends
We pair convolutional front-ends with lightweight unidirec-
tional sequence model back-ends: LSTM, GRU and Mamba.
For base models, hidden dimensions were set to 123 for LSTM
and 117 for GRU, following prior studies [21, 23]. For small
and large models, hidden dimensions are set to 32 and 200,
respectively. For small and large models, hidden dimensions
are set to 32 and 200, respectively. For state-space modeling,
we implement a strictly unidirectional TF-Mamba block fol-
lowing [33], with hidden dimension set to 32. These modular
components evaluate front-end robustness across diverse tem-
poral modeling strategies. Attention-augmented variants (e.g.,
LSTM+Attn) integrate a multi-head causal self-attention layer.
Causal masking enables dynamic feature weighting within strict
real-time hardware constraints.
iii) Model Training
Experiments were implemented in PyTorch and models
were trained using an NVIDIA RTX A5000 GPU. Models were
trained using cross-entropy loss and stochastic gradient descent
with a learning rate of 1 × 10−5 and momentum of 0.9. Train-
ing samples are segmented into 2-second sequences. Training
was stopped when validation accuracy improvements fall below
0.001% over 10 consecutive epochs.
iv) Deployment Simulation
Deployment experiments on a microcontroller unit (MCU)
were conducted using the STM32Cube.AI Developer Cloud
platform [34, 35]. The trained models were exported to ONNX
format and uploaded to STM32Cube.AI Developer Cloud. On-
device inference estimates were measured on the STM32F746G
board, which has similar MCU speciﬁcations previously used in
hearing-aid research [36] and provides a proxy embedded eval-
uation setting for CIs. The STM32F746G MCU has 1 MB ﬂash
memory and 340 KB of RAM.
Technical Note. All baseline and convolutional variants of
LSTM- and GRU-based models were successfully deployed.
However, deformable convolution models, all attention-based
architectures and all Mamba-based models could not be evalu-
ated due to current limitations of the deployment platform.
3. Results
3.1. Effect of Convolutional Front-end and Depth
We ﬁrst evaluated whether incorporating a causal convolu-
tional front-end improves phoneme classiﬁcation performance
and how different convolutional dimensions affect performance.
Table 1 compares base LSTM and GRU models without and
with convolutional front-ends at varying depths. All convo-
lutional front-ends provide consistent improvements in frame-
level phoneme classiﬁcation accuracy over baseline sequence
models. The most noticeable performance increase from the
base model is observed with 1L in Conv1D-T and Conv2D and
with 2L in Conv1D-F, and performance trends are mixed with
additional depth. For example, for phoneme classiﬁcation accu-
racy with Conv1D-F, standard models generally peak at around
L = 2 or L = 4, suggesting that full frequency coverage of the
log-compressed FFT features is not strictly necessary to extract
relevant phonetic features useful for phoneme classiﬁcation.
