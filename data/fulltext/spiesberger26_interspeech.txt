Abstract
Hormonal changes throughout the menstrual cycle can affect
speech production. However, studies investigating differences
in acoustic parameters have found inconsistent results. This
could be because changes are likely subtle and multivariate.
We therefore apply handcrafted and embedding-based features,
combined with machine learning methods. We use a German
read speech dataset of 76 participants in two cycle phases (ovu-
lation and luteal). Additionally, we investigate whether speaker-
level accuracy is correlated with hormonal levels, the magnitude
of hormonal changes between phases, or age. Our results show
small, non-significant effects of loudness, formant amplitudes,
H1-H2, and spectral flux. We achieve 62.5 % accuracy with
handcrafted features, but chance-level performance with learnt
embeddings. Speaker-level accuracies show no correlation with
hormonal levels or age. We suggest using more cycle phases
and personalisation techniques in future research.
Index Terms: menstrual cycle tracking, hormonal changes,
computational paralinguistics, speaker state analysis
1. Introduction
The menstrual cycle is characterised by systematic hormonal
fluctuations. This cycle is commonly divided into two distinct
phases: the follicular phase, which starts with the first day
of menstruation and ends at ovulation, and the luteal phase,
which starts at ovulation and ends with the next menstrua-
tion. Across these phases, the levels of estrogen, progesterone,
follicle-stimulating hormone, luteinising hormone, and testos-
terone vary substantially.
These hormonal changes have a notable impact on the hu-
man body, including an impact on the speech apparatus. In par-
ticular, sex hormone receptors have been found on the vocal
folds [1, 2]. Following up on that, Shoffel-Havakuket al. [3] re-
ported structural changes of the larynx that correlated with fluc-
tuations of progesterone. The presence of hormonal receptors
and structural changes throughout the cycle suggests that hor-
monal variability can affect acoustic speech characteristics, pro-
viding a foundation for research on cycle-related vocal changes.
Despite this physiological foundation, research investigat-
ing voice changes across the menstrual cycle has produced
mixed results. Several studies found systematic differences in
the voice throughout the menstrual cycle. For example, lower
pitch in the late luteal phase than in the fertile phase has been
reported multiple times [4, 5, 6, 7] and differences in minimum

Importantly, results of studies that compared the voices of
naturally cycling women (i. e., individuals with regular men-
strual cycles and no hormonal contraceptive use) with those of
users of hormonal contraceptives suggest that these changes are
linked to hormonal fluctuations. Arruda et al. [12] found that
naturally cycling women showed changes in roughness, ten-
sion, and instability that were absent in contraceptive users.
In their study, estradiol levels were associated with impair-
ments in voice quality, vocal deviation, tension, and instabil-
ity. Similarly, several studies report greater vocal stability
among users of oral contraceptives compared to naturally cy-
cling women [13, 14, 15].
At the same time, other studies have not detected systematic
changes in acoustic parameters between cycle phases (e. g., [16,
17, 18]). Thus, despite physiological plausibility and empirical
support, the evidence remains inconclusive.
One possible explanation for these inconsistencies lies in
methodological limitations. Many previous studies relied on
small sets of predefined acoustic measures and primarily used
inferential statistics (such as t tests and ANOV A). Such ap-
proaches may not be sufficient to capture subtle and potentially
multivariate acoustic patterns associated with hormonal varia-
tions. Moreover, most research has been focused on group-level
mean differences, without evaluating whether cycle phase can
be predicted at the individual level.
Machine learning approaches provide a complementary
methodological framework. Instead of testing predefined acous-
tic parameters individually, these approaches can model high-
dimensional representations and capture complex feature inter-
actions. This makes them particularly suitable for detecting
subtle patterns that may not manifest as strongly as univariate
effects. However, only a few studies have applied these methods
to voice-based research on the menstrual cycle. In one recent
example, Spiesberger et al. [19] achieved classification accu-
racies for menstruation versus ovulation around 60 % using a
limited set of eight acoustic features, suggesting moderate pre-
dictive value.
This study goes beyond previous work by investigating
whether systematic acoustic differences between ovulation and
the luteal phase can be identified using both traditional statisti-
cal analysis and machine learning approaches. We focus specif-
ically on the ovulation and luteal phases, as they represent con-
trasting hormonal profiles – peak estrogen around ovulation ver-
sus elevated progesterone in the luteal phase – making them

Building on the work of Spiesberger et al. [19], we use
a much larger feature set, as well as embedding-based repre-
sentations. For this, we examine two complementary acous-
tic representations: the handcrafted EGEMAPS [20] fea-
ture set and embedding-based representations derived from
WAV2VEC 2.0 [21]. While EGEMAPS captures interpretable
acoustic parameters, WAV2VEC 2.0 embeddings, originally
trained for speech emotion recognition, have been shown to en-
code rich paralinguistic information beyond what handcrafted
features capture. This makes them a potentially valuable tool
for detecting physiological state differences.
In addition to overall classification performance, we extend
previous work by exploring inter-individual variability in pre-
dictability. Specifically, we test whether speaker-level classifi-
cation accuracy is associated with absolute hormone levels and
with the magnitude of hormonal change between phases. Mean
hormonal levels across phases represent the general hormonal
exposure, while the difference between phases measures the
hormonal change, allowing us to disentangle these two potential
sources of variability. We hypothesise that speakers with more
pronounced hormonal fluctuations will exhibit stronger contrast
between phases, leading to higher classification accuracy. Fi-
nally, given previous findings suggesting age-related effects on
fundamental frequency [18], we explore whether there is a cor-
relation between age and predictive performance.
2. Dataset
This study uses recordings of read speech from the ovulation
and the luteal phases. To estimate the times for the recordings,
participants were asked to record their cycle lengths over three
months. The recordings were timed for reverse cycle days 16-
18 and reverse cycle days 4-11 for ovulation and luteal phase,
respectively. The recordings are part of a larger dataset. For a
detailed description of the dataset, see [18, 22].
We included 76 participants between 18 and 44 years
(M = 29.4, SD = 7.9), who had recordings available from both
phases. All of them were native German speakers, reported reg-
ular menstrual cycles, and were not using hormonal contracep-
tives at the time of the study. All participants spoke the same
15 sentences at both time points 1. The recording order (ovu-
lation first vs luteal first) was balanced across participants. In
addition, saliva samples were collected to determine estradiol,
progesterone, and testosterone levels, which we use to correlate
speaker-level accuracies.
The recordings were split at the sentence level by one an-
notator using manual segmentation based on waveform and au-
ditory inspection, resulting in 15 recordings per participant per
cycle phase. For this, the full utterance of each sentence was
used, including errors (e. g., mispronunciations, restarts). This
resulted in 2 277 audio files with a total duration of 84.35 min
(M = 2.22 s, SD = 0.58 s).

Comparative Analysis. We performed a comparative anal-
ysis of EGEMAPS features to investigate differences in dis-
tribution between the ovulation and luteal phases. We used
Wilcoxon signed-rank tests with an alpha level of 0.05. Mul-
tiple testing was corrected using the Bonferroni-Holm method.
Given the limitations of p values, we also report effect sizes
(rank-biserial correlation) for all features showing at least a
small effect (|r| > 0.2).
Classification. Before model training, we standardised all
features. For the classification of ovulation versus luteal phase,
we assessed the predictive performance of different classifiers
on both feature sets separately – eXtreme Gradient Boosting
(XGB OOST ) using XGBOOST -V3.2.0 and Support Vector Ma-
chine (SVM), Random Forest (RF), and Logistic Regression
(LR) using SKLEARN -V1.8.0. For hyperparameter search and
training and testing, we used a nested cross-validation with a
leave-one-group-out design for the outer loop and a group k-
fold for the inner loop, with k = 3 folds. Hyperparameters were
tuned using grid search; the full search space is listed in Table 1,
all other hyperparameters were left at their defaults.
Evaluation. Given the balanced nature of the dataset, we
used accuracy scores for evaluation. Using majority voting, we
created one prediction per person per phase and model and eval-
uated model performance by computing 95 % confidence inter-
vals using bootstrap resampling with replacement (1000 itera-
tions). In addition, we calculated speaker-level accuracies for
each speaker separately, using all their sentences (before ma-
jority voting) as independent evaluation samples, and report de-
scriptive statistics.

higher values in the luteal phase include several loudness, spec-

5. Discussion
This study provides an assessment of acoustic changes between
the ovulation and luteal phase of the menstrual cycle using both
handcrafted and embedding-based features.
Taking a closer look at the EGEMAPS features that show
at least a small effect, we would like to highlight several points.
Table 2 shows higher formant amplitudes in the luteal phase,
reflecting stronger resonance energy and pointing to increased
vocal intensity. This is complemented by an increase in loud-
ness features in the luteal phase. However, these findings stand
in direct contrast to Pavela Banai [8], who found loudness to be
lowest in the luteal phase. This discrepancy may be due to the
technical setting or reflect differences in speech task and phase
determination methodology. Additionally, we find higher spec-
tral flux values and, like Narasimhan and Pooja [6], also higher
H1-H2 values in the luteal phase.
Regarding the classification tasks, we found a moderate
predictive value of 62.5 % (chance level: 50 %) forEGEMAPS
features, using Random Forest and Logistic Regression, and
492
thereby achieved similar results as Spiesberger et al. [19] for
the distinction between menstruation and ovulation. In contrast,
embedding-based representations did not outperform chance.
One possible reason lies in the nature of the WAV2VEC 2.0
model used in this paper. Not only was this pretrained on En-
glish data and an emotion recognition task, but it also shows
robustness to gender variability [21]. Given that hormone lev-
els in males and females differ strongly, it could be that these
features are not suited to modelling more granular variability
caused by the menstrual cycle.
The lack of significant correlations of speaker-level accura-
cies across phases with hormonal levels and age may, in part,
be due to the limitations of Pearson’s r, which captures only
linear relationships. An exploratory investigation of boxplots
showing the speaker-level accuracy scores using three different
age groups suggests a possible non-linear relationship between
age and classification accuracy, though the sample size was in-
sufficient to test this. However, the findings regarding hormone
levels align with Fischer et al. [5] and Weirich and Simpson
[18], who found that neither estrogen nor progesterone levels
predicted the changes in fundamental frequency. Additionally,
it is interesting to note that even speakers who showed a drop in
progesterone level between the ovulation and the luteal phase
did not necessarily show worse results on the accuracy score.
This suggests that changes in progesterone level are not the only
reason for changes in vocal features.
The variability in speaker-level accuracies indicates that the
strength of using acoustic signals for predicting menstrual cy-
cle phases differs substantially across individuals, with some
reaching a speaker-level accuracy of 100 % and others scoring
as low as 0 % (always predicting the wrong phase). The high
correlation of speaker-level accuracies across classifiers sug-
gests that classification difficulty is a property of the speaker
rather than the classifier used. This could be due to a variety
of factors, for example, individual differences in hormonal sen-
sitivity, differences in the magnitude of laryngeal changes, or
behavioural/emotional factors not captured in this study. As
a result, we hypothesise that personalisation techniques which
‘normalise’ to a speaker baseline (e. g., during menstruation
when estrogen, progesterone, and testosterone levels are all at
their lowest) could improve predictive performance in the fu-
ture. While we used two phases for the current study, future
studies should consider sampling in more cycle phases or col-
lecting daily recordings, possibly over several cycles, in order
to look at individual changes.
Another important consideration is feature selection. While
we focused on widely used acoustic descriptors, other measures
such as voice onset time (VOT) have previously been shown to
vary across the cycle [24, 25]. Even though Hilliger et al. [26]
could not confirm these results for their data, as they did not
find any significant differences in VOT between cycle phases,
integrating further features, such as VOT, could nevertheless en-
hance the predictive capability of models.
This study has several limitations worth noting. These in-
clude restricted interpretability of loudness levels, as loudness
analysis was not planned at the time of recording and there-
fore not controlled for. Differences could therefore be due to
the technical settings, though these should be random, as the
recording order was counterbalanced. Additionally, we did not
exclude participants with unusual hormonal patterns, such as
a drop in progesterone in the luteal phase, which could indi-
cate an anovulatory cycle or incorrectly assigned phase labels.
The segmentation was performed manually by a single anno-
tator based on waveform and auditory inspection; future work
should assess inter-annotator reliability to ensure segmentation
consistency. Further, as we used read speech only, our findings
may not generalise to spontaneous or conversational speech.
Our study also highlights the scarcity of comparable
datasets. The current dataset [18], shared under a data trans-
fer agreement, is unique due to its sensitivity, and no public
alternatives exist. The field would benefit from additional mul-
tilingual datasets to assess the generalisability of these effects
across languages and populations. This is also necessary to un-
derstand whether found effects are due to dataset-specific pat-
terns or actual physiological changes.
Finally, future research could explore predicting hormonal
levels or their changes directly from speech, rather than classi-
fying cycle phase. As cycle phase labels are an approximation
of the underlying hormonal processes, they may not always ac-
curately reflect physiological state. For example, progesterone
levels may not increase in the luteal phase of anovulatory cy-
cles. This approach might, therefore, reveal stronger acoustic
correlates of hormone dynamics and better account for inter-
individual differences. In addition, this could also be a possi-
ble alternative to detect hormonal imbalances and health-related
changes in hormonal levels, as conventional hormone measure-
ment approaches require laboratory analysis and are not suited
for continuous monitoring.
6. Conclusion
This year’s conference is dedicated to the celebration of all
voices. However, many models continue to show lower per-
formance for women’s voices compared to men’s, and research
addressing women-specific questions, such as the relationship
between voice and hormonal changes, remains limited.
In this work, we investigated how vocal parameters re-
late to the menstrual cycle. We found small effects for loud-
ness features, formant amplitudes, H1-H2, and spectral flux be-
tween ovulation and luteal phase, though statistical tests were
not significant after correcting for multiple testing. In addition,
our results show that, while WAV2VEC 2.0 embeddings did not
achieve results significantly above chance for distinguishing be-
tween ovulation and luteal phase, handcrafted acoustic features
can moderately predict these cycle phases. Interestingly, indi-
