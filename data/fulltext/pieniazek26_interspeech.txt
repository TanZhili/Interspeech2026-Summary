Abstract
Incorrect production of sibilant consonants is among the most
common speech disorders in children, and its effective treat-
ment depends on early diagnosis. This study proposes and in-
vestigates a method for detecting the incorrect place of artic-
ulation in productions of Polish voiceless retroflex fricatives
and affricates in child speech. The approach combines con-
volutional autoencoders (classical, sparse, and multi-task vari-
ants) for feature extraction with support vector machines. The
speech corpus included children aged 4–8 years: 149 speakers
for fricative and 151 for affricate sounds. The best-performing
models employ multi-task learning achieving sensitivities of up
to 84.32%. The results demonstrate that autoencoder-based em-
beddings are effective for feature extraction and classification in
challenging pediatric speech datasets.

1. Introduction
1.1. Background
The correct articulation of speech sounds constitutes one of the
fundamental determinants of effective verbal communication
and, consequently, of an individual’s proper functioning within
society [1]. The most critical stage in the acquisition of accurate
articulation occurs in early childhood, when initial phonological
systems are established, and the speech motor apparatus under-
goes dynamic development [2]. The complexity of speech de-
velopment during this period significantly influences the emer-
gence of early articulation disorders. One of the most frequently
observed speech disorders is a form of dyslalia manifested by
the incorrect production of sibilant sounds [3]. Effective ther-
apeutic intervention depends largely on early and accurate di-
agnosis. However, timely assessment is often hindered by the
limited availability of speech-language pathologists.
1.2. Related works
The rapid advancement of digital signal processing techniques
has facilitated the development of novel systems and algo-
rithms for the automatic assessment of pronunciation accuracy.
Among the widely applied approaches are machine learning
methods, particularly Support Vector Machines (SVMs) [4, 5],
which typically operate on carefully engineered acoustic fea-
tures extracted from speech signals. In recent years, more
sophisticated classification frameworks have increasingly re-
lied on deep learning architectures, including convolutional
neural networks (CNNs) [6, 7, 8], also for the detection of

speech signal is commonly transformed into a time-frequency
representation—most often a spectrogram—enabling the model
to learn discriminative patterns directly from structured input
data. Systems incorporating CNN-based architectures have
demonstrated high effectiveness for speech disorder detection
and pronunciation assessment [11, 12]; however, their perfor-
mance may be limited on small or imbalanced datasets. These
findings highlight the significant potential of CNNs and related
approaches in automated speech diagnostics.
A specific class of neural networks based on the convo-
lution operation are the convolutional autoencoders (CAEs).
CAEs are typically trained using unsupervised learning tech-
niques, with the primary objective of learning compact and in-
formative representations from unlabeled data [13]. Architec-
turally, they are built upon an encoder-decoder framework. The
encoder maps the input data into a lower-dimensional latent
representation, often referred to as a bottleneck or embedding
space. Subsequently, the decoder reconstructs the input data
from this compressed representation, aiming to reproduce the
original signal as accurately as possible [14]. The learned low-
dimensional representations, owing to their compactness and
potentially discriminative power, can be further used as input
features for separate classification models [15].
Chen et al. [16] proposed a cascade architecture composed
of multiple autoencoders operating on mel-frequency cepstral
coefficients (MFCCs) and reported an accuracy of up to 97%
in a voice disorder detection task. In their framework, the latent
representations extracted by the stacked autoencoders were sub-
sequently classified using a single softmax layer, demonstrating
the diagnostic usefulness of learned embeddings.
Another noteworthy approach involves the use of sparse au-
toencoders, which incorporate sparsity constraints to ensure that
only a subset of hidden neurons is activated for a given input
sample [17]. Such regularization encourages the learning of
more distinctive and structured latent representations. Xiong et
al. [18] employed sparse autoencoders to extract latent feature
vectors from acoustic speech signals for the detection of Parkin-
son’s disease. Their method incorporated transfer learning tech-
niques and utilized, among other classifiers, SVMs, achieving
an accuracy of approximately 85%.
A further promising direction is the integration of autoen-
coders within a multi-task learning framework. While dimen-
sionality reduction performed by the encoder is inherently an
unsupervised learning process, augmenting the bottleneck rep-
resentation with a supervised classification objective encour-
ages the latent space to become class-discriminative. Sztahó
et al. [19] adopted this strategy for voice pathology detection.

the bottleneck layer were jointly optimized for both reconstruc-

1.3. Contribution
This study aims to present a method for classifying place
of articulation articulation in children’s productions of Polish
retroflex sibilants /ù/ and />tù/. The performance of three variants
of convolutional autoencoders was investigated: a classic con-
volutional autoencoder (CAE), a sparse convolutional autoen-
coder (SCAE), and multi-task convolutional autoencoder (MT-
CAE). In each case, the neural network architecture served as
feature extractor, while classification was performed using an
SVM operating on the latent space representations. To the best
of our knowledge, a comparative analysis of autoencoder-based
classifier models trained specifically on recordings of children’s
speech has not yet been reported in the literature. Conducting
such evaluation provides insight into whether latent embedding
vectors are capable of capturing diagnostically relevant spectral
features derived from speech spectrograms, thereby enabling
the detection of articulation errors in pediatric speech. This task
is particularly challenging due to the inherently high variability,
low repeatability, and development instability characteristic of
children’s speech production.
2. Methods
2.1. Speech corpus
The speech corpus used in this study originates from a large-
scale research project dedicated to the investigation of articu-
lation development in preschool children. All acoustic signals
were collected using multimodal acquisition system comprising
15 spatially distributed microphones an a synchronized video
recording module. A comprehensive description of the acqui-
sition protocol and the structure of the database is provided
in [21]. The recordings were obtained from children aged 4 to 8
years attending 5 kindergartens and 1 primary school located in
Poland. Prior to participation, written informed consent was ob-
tained from the children’s legal guardians, and verbal assent was
secured from the children themselves. Each participant com-
pleted a picture-naming task designed to elicit the production of
Polish sibilant sounds across a range of lexical items. All speech
samples were evaluated by an experienced Speech-Language
Pathologist (SLP). The assessment aimed to determine whether
each child’s pronunciation conformed to age-appropriate norms
of sound production and to identify the place of articulation
(PoA) for each realized sibilant.
This study focuses on two polish sibilant consonants char-
acterized by a retroflex PoA: /ù/ and / >tù/. Any realization of
these phonemes with PoA other than retroflex was considered
indicative of incorrect pronunciation. To ensure high acous-
tic quality, only recordings obtained from the centrally posi-
tioned electret microphone (Panasonic WM-61) located clos-
est to child’s speech apparatus were included in the analysis.
All signals were recorded with a 16-bit resolution and a sam-
pling rate of 44.1 kHz. Two principal PoA categories were ex-
amined: retroflex, regarded as the normative articulation, and
dental, which represents the most frequently observed non-
normative realization in articulation disorders affecting these
sounds. Other, substantially less frequent PoA variants were ex-
cluded from the present analysis. The detailed composition of
the speech corpus is summarized in Table 1. For the phoneme
/ù/, a total of 1,746 productions were collected from 149 speak-
ers, including 1,414 retroflex and 332 dental realizations. For
/>tù/, 586 productions were obtained from 151 speakers, com-
prising 484 retroflex and 102 dental realizations. The observed
class imbalance reflects the naturally lower prevalence of ar-
ticulation disorders relative to normative pronunciation in the
examined population.
2.2. Signal preprocessing
Signal preprocessing commenced with the manual segmenta-
tion of audio fragments containing the target sibilant produc-
tions (/ù/ or / >tù/) from the original recordings. The segmen-
tation was performed by an expert to ensure precise isolation
of individual phoneme realizations. Each extracted signal was
amplitude-normalized to [0, 1] range and transformed from the
time domain to time-frequency domain. Spectrograms were
generated using a 20 ms Hamming window with 50% frame
overlap. The resulting time-frequency representations were re-
sized to a resolution of 64×64 pixels and exported in PNG for-
mat. These spectrogram images constituted the input data for
training autoencoders. All preprocessing procedures were im-
plemented in MATLAB R2025a.
2.3. Convolutional autoencoders
To identify the most effective system for detecting incorrect
PoA in children’s speech, three variants of CAEs were eval-
uated: a classical convolutional autoencoder, a sparse convo-
lutional autoencoder, and muti-task convolutional autoencoder
incorporating a classifier connected to bottleneck layer. All
model variants were trained and evaluated using 10-fold cross-
validation. To prevent speaker-specific characteristics from in-
fluencing performance estimates, a speaker-independent data
division strategy was adopted. Specifically, all sound produc-
tions originating from a given speaker were assigned exclu-
sively to either the training or the testing subset within each
fold. This procedure ensured that the models learned generaliz-
able features associated with specific PoAs rather than proper-
ties of individual speakers.

2.4. Classification
The final stage of the proposed framework involved an SVM
classifier operating on the latent representation extracted from
each CAE variant. To ensure methodological consistency and
reliable performance estimation, the SVM models were trained
and evaluated using the same speaker-independent 10-fold
cross-validation data partitions as those applied during autoen-
coder training. Exactly the same samples were used to train the
SVM as were used to train its corresponding autoencoder. Prior
to classifier training, all latent vectors were normalized to stan-
dardize their scale. To address class imbalance, class weight-
ing was incorporated into the SVM training procedure, thereby
penalizing misclassification of minority-class samples more
strongly. All SVM classifiers employed a radial basis function
(RBF) kernel. To optimize classification performance, a hyper-
parameter search was conducted over the kernel regularization

evaluation metrics obtained from the SVM classifiers reflect the

2.5. Statistical analysis
To assess the influence of the model type on prediction qual-
ity, the non-parametric Kruskal-Wallis test was performed. This
test was chosen due to the lack of normality in the data distribu-
tion within groups, as verified by the Shapiro-Wilk test and the
heterogeneity of variances, confirmed by Levene’s test. The in-
dependent variable was the model category, whereas the depen-
dent variables were the evaluation metrics, namely sensitivity
and accuracy.
3. Results
To eliminate degenerative solutions resulting from extreme hy-
perparameter configurations, classifiers with both sensitivity
and accuracy below 50% were excluded from comparative anal-
ysis. This criterion was used to exclude models in which the de-
cision hyperplane assigned all samples to a single class. The ap-
plication of this procedure resulted in the exclusion of approxi-
mately two-thirds of trained models for each analyzed sibilant.
The primary evaluation metric considered in this study was
sensitivity, which reflects the model’s ability to correctly iden-
tify pathological cases. Overall accuracy wes also reported
to provide a broader assessment of classification performance.
Sensitivity was prioritized because the primary objective of the
proposed system is the reliable detection of pathological speech
with predominantly normative speech data. The remaining met-
ric offers complementary insight into the overall discriminative
capability and balance of the classifiers.
Ten classifier configurations achieving the highest sensi-
tivity for the /ù/ and / >tù/ sound are presented in Table 2. For
the sibilant /ù/, the most sensitive classifier incorporated MT-
CAE, achieving sensitivity of 84.31% and overall accuracy of
72.68%. The second-highest sensitivity was also obtained us-
ing an MTCAE-based model, while the third position was oc-
cupied by a sparsity-constrained autoencoder, reaching a sen-
sitivity of 79.82%. Overall, the top-performing models for /ù/
were predominantly based on MTCAE and SCAE architectures,
with only a single classical CAE appearing among the ten most
sensitive classifiers. All selected classifiers exceeded 78% sen-
sitivity and demonstrated satisfactory accuracy. In contrast, the
results for the />tù/ sound exhibited a different pattern. All ten of
the most sensitive classifiers were based on MTCAE architec-
ture, with sensitivity values reaching up to 84.31%. This sug-
gests that the unsupervised autoencoders are less effective for
this particular sibilant.
To assess the influence of the autoencoder variant on system
performance, the results obtained across different SVM models
and kernel hyperparameter configurations were summarized us-
ing box plots (Figure 2). These visualizations illustrate the dis-
tribution of evaluation metrics across all trained classifiers. To
determine whether the type of autoencoder had a statistically
significant effect on performance, a non-parametric Kruskal-
Wallis test was conducted for each evaluation metric.

4. Discussion & limitations
The primary objective of this study was to demonstrate that
CAEs can serve as effective components of system designed to
classify correct sound articulation in speech therapy diagnosis.
In one of our previous studies [22], we employed SVM clas-
sifier operating on the / >tù/ sound using MFCCs. Despite per-
forming hyperparameter grid search in a manner analogous to
the procedure adopted in the presented work, our previous most
effective classifier achieved a sensitivity of 71.58%, which is
substantially lower than the 84.31% obtained using the MTCAE
proposed in this study.
Ding et al. [23] investigated the performance of well-
established neural networks, such as ResNet, for the binary clas-
sification of pronunciation correctness, achieving sensitivity of
up to 80%. The best-performing models presented in this work
slightly outperformed those networks. However, it should be

When interpreting the presented results, several limitations
of this study should be acknowledged. First, the dataset was rel-

negatively affect overall performance. Second, the dataset was
imbalanced, with a substantially higher number of normative
realizations than pathological ones. This disproportion may re-

ciation, increasing the risk of biased decision boundaries that
favor the majority class. Another limitation concerns the small
input representation: the architecture required spectrograms re-
sized to64×64×3pixels, potentially leading to a loss of
spectral information and reducing autoencoders’ performance.
5. Conclusions
This study presented novel method for the detection of the in-
correct place of articulation in Polish sibilant consonants pro-
duced by children. The proposed framework combined convo-
lutional autoencoders for feature extraction with support vector
machines trained on latent representations derived from the au-
toencoder’s embedding space. Several variants of the autoen-
coder architecture were evaluated, including classical, sparse,
and multi-task.
The experimental results indicate that, for the /ù/ sibilant,
SCAE achieved higher average sensitivity across hyperparam-
eter configurations. In contrast, for the / >tù/ sound, the MT-
CAE demonstrated clear superiority, consistently outperform-
ing other variants in both sensitivity and accuracy. Notably, for
both phonemes, the single best-performing model incorporated
the multi-task learning strategy. These findings suggest that
convolutional autoencoders are capable of encoding diagnosti-
cally relevant spectral information related to the correctness of
PoA in children’s sibilant production.
