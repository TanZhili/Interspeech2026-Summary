Abstract
Speaker embeddings are foundational to voice technologies like
speaker verification, diarization and target speaker extraction.
The embeddings must remain robust to environmental noise
and invariant to spoken language. In this paper, we propose
a speaker embedding model based on the ReDimNet architec-
ture, which has shown state-of-the-art performance on V ox-
celeb. To improve the robustness of speaker embeddings under
cross-lingual and diverse acoustic conditions, model is trained
on a large-scale multilingual corpus with extensive noise and
reverb augmentation. Further, a post-network is trained with
triplet loss to disentangle speaker identity from linguistic con-
tent, thereby encouraging language-invariant representations.
The proposed model is evaluated on the TidyV oice Challenge
dataset. Under the matched and mismatched language scenar-
ios, Our model achieves an Equal Error Rate (EER) of 1.58%,
demonstrating strong robustness to both noise and language
variability.

1. Introduction
Speaker verification systems are widely deployed in voice-
driven authentication and security contexts such as banking,
smart home devices, and contact center platforms. Robust
speaker representations are equally important for speaker di-
arization, where recordings must be partitioned by speaker iden-
tity, and for target speaker extraction, where a specific voice
must be isolated from interfering sources in noisy real-world
environments.
Early speaker recognition approaches modeled speaker-
specific acoustic distributions via Gaussian Mixture Models
with a Universal Background Model (GMM-UBM) [16], and
the i-vector [17] framework subsequently introduced com-
pact fixed-length representations by projecting sufficient statis-
tics into a low-dimensional subspace [5]. Deep neural net-
works [22, 26, 20] then superseded this pipeline, learning
speaker-discriminative features directly from audio using clas-
sification objectives [26]. The loss function evolved from stan-
dard cross-entropy toward angular margin losses, which directly
shape the geometry of the embedding hypersphere. Additive
angular margin in particular improved class separability sub-
stantially [7]. While these approaches perform well in mono-
lingual and clean conditions, their performance degrades un-
der acoustic mismatch, including noise [25], reverberation, and
cross-lingual scenarios [23]. To improve robustness, multi-
condition data augmentation and adversarial training have been
explored [26, 18].
The cross-lingual problem manifests in two distinct failure
modes [21]. First, different speakers sharing a language may be
incorrectly accepted because common phonetic patterns domi-
nate the embedding. Second, the same speaker using two differ-
ent languages may be incorrectly rejected because the language
shift alters acoustic features substantially. Both failure modes
trace to the same root cause: the embedding encodes linguistic
content that is irrelevant to speaker identity.Several research di-
rections address this through multilingual data. Large-scale cor-
pora such as V oxCeleb have enabled models to learn from more
varied conditions [6]. The TidyV oice dataset [1], constructed
from Mozilla Common V oice [2], provides a structured multi-
lingual benchmark covering four evaluation conditions: same
speaker same language, same speaker different language, dif-
ferent speaker same language, and different speaker different
language. The TidyV oice 2026 Challenge uses this benchmark
to specifically assess cross-lingual speaker verification across
4,474 speakers and 40 languages, spanning 457 hours of audio.
This work investigates a progressive training strategy for
language-invariant speaker embeddings using the TidyV oice
dataset [1]. We adopt ReDimNet-B6 [3] as the backbone be-
cause of its efficient design and strong speaker verification per-
formance. The model is adapted to the multilingual cross-
lingual setting, and metric learning is subsequently applied to
further refine the embedding space toward language invariance.
Acoustic augmentation is performed using the MUSAN cor-
pus [8] and room impulse response recordings [9] for extract-
ing robust acoustic invariant speaker embeddings. The remain-
der of this paper is organized as follows. Section 2 describes
the proposed two-stage progressive training methodology, cov-
ering the ReDimNet-B6 backbone, ArcMargin fine-tuning, and
the cross-lingual triplet projection network. Section 3 details
the experimental setup, including the TidyV oice dataset, feature
extraction, and evaluation protocol. Results and analysis are
presented in Section 4 on both the development set and the two
blind evaluation sets. Finally, Section 5 concludes the paper and
outlines directions for future work.
2. Proposed Methodology
We propose a two-stage progressive training framework target-
ing both language invariance and acoustic robustness. Noise
robustness is addressed at the data level through augmentation
with additive noise and room impulse responses, while lan-
guage invariance is pursued through the staged training proce-
dure described below.

2.1. Backbone: ReDimNet-B6
We selected the B6 configuration of the Reshape Dimensions
Network (ReDimNet) [3] as the backbone after evaluating sev-
eral open-source speaker embedding architectures. ReDimNet
spans 1M to 15M parameters in a parameter-efficient design.
The B6 variant shows state-of-the art performance with an EER
of 0.3% on V oxCeleb evaluation pairs. ReDimNet-B6 accepts
72-dimensional log-mel filterbank features and processes them
through alternating 1D and 2D residual blocks that reshape fea-
ture dimensions, followed by attentive statistics pooling to ag-
gregate frame-level representations into a single utterance em-
bedding. This hybrid design captures temporal dynamics via
1D convolutions and spectro-temporal structures via 2D convo-
lutions, yielding rich and robust speaker representations.
2.2. Gradient Reversal Exploration
We explored a gradient reversal strategy to suppress language
information in the embeddings, inspired by domain-adversarial
training [14] and speech disentanglement studies [15]. A lan-
guage identification branch was attached to the ReDimNet-B6
encoder via a GRL, which acts as identity in the forward pass
and scales gradients by−λduring backpropagation to enforce
language invariance. However, this adversarial objective did not
improve cross-lingual verification and therefore excluded from
the final system.

speaker identity similarity. Given an anchor utterancea, a pos-

We freeze Stage 1 and train a lightweight convolutional pro-
jection network (256.13 K parameters, 27.36 M MAC/s) on top
of its fixed 192-dimensional embeddings, preserving the mul-
tilingual speaker knowledge already encoded in the backbone
while reshaping the embedding space for cross-lingual invari-
ance. The full two-stage pipeline is illustrated in Fig. 1, and
the triplet training setup across three shared-weight branches is
shown in Fig. 2.
2.4.1. Projection Network Architecture
The projection network as shown in Stage 2 of Fig. 1, refines
Stage 1’s 192-dimensional embeddings through a residual de-
sign. The input is processed through a linear projection in paral-
lel with a stack of three 1D convolutional layers, having channel
sizes of 64, 128, and 256 and kernel sizes of 5, 5, and 3, respec-
tively. Each convolutional layer is followed by batch normaliza-
tion and a ReLU activation. The resulting features are passed
through adaptive average pooling and a fully connected layer
to produce a 256-dimensional output. Afterℓ 2 normalization,
all embeddings are projected onto a unit hypersphere, ensuring
that the triplet loss operates within a geometrically consistent
space [11]. This residual structure preserves the original em-
bedding content while the convolutional path refines it toward

2.4.2. Triplet Construction
The three triplet branches share identical weights as depicted in
Fig. 2. The effectiveness of triplet learning largely depends on
negative selection. For each triplet, the anchor is a randomly
sampled utterance from a speaker. The positive is drawn with
equal probability from either the same speaker–same language
or same speaker–different language setting, ensuring balanced
exposure to intra-lingual and cross-lingual variations. The neg-
ative is sampled either from a different speaker sharing the an-
chor’s language or from a different speaker in any language. In-
cluding same-language negatives explicitly mitigates language-
induced false positives arising from shared phonetic character-
istics.

3. Experimental Setup
3.1. Dataset and Training Configuration
All experiments use the TidyV oice dataset [1], sampled at
16kHz, with data augmentation as explained in Stage 1. We ex-
tract 72-dimensional log-mel filterbank features using a 25 ms
frame length and 15 ms frame shift, followed by cepstral
mean normalization. Stage 1 training follows the WeSpeaker
toolkit [13] setup with SGD (momentum 0.9, weight decay
2×10 −5, lr= 10 −3). Stage 2 optimizes the projection net-
work with Adam (lr= 10 −3) while keeping the Stage 1 fully
frozen.
3.2. Evaluation Setup
Verification scores are computed using cosine similarity be-
tweenℓ 2-normalised enrolment and test embeddings. We re-
port EER and minDCF (P target = 0.01,C FA =C Miss = 1) on
the development set, broken down by the four trial-pair cate-
gories (SS-SL, SS-DL, DS-SL, DS-DL), and on two blind eval-
uation sets described in Section 4.2. We further conducted a
robustness analysis of the speaker embeddings on the develop-
ment dataset by introducing acoustic perturbations, specifically
additive noise and reverberation.

4.2. Blind Evaluation Set Results
The TidyV oice Challenge includes two blind test sets: eval-A,
where enrolment utterances come from seen languages and test
utterances from 38 unseen languages, and eval-U, where both
enrolment and test utterances are drawn from unseen languages,
representing a stricter zero-shot setting. Table 2 presents blind
results on eval-A and eval-U. On eval-A (seen-language enrol-
ment), Stage-2 reduces overall EER from 9.06% to 4.81%, with
consistent improvements across all trial types, including a large
gain in the hardest cross-lingual case (10.08% to 4.90%). On
the more challenging eval-U (fully unseen languages), overall
EER drops from 11.60% to 7.01%, with substantial reductions
across conditions, particularly for cross-lingual trials (12.39%
to 8.06%). These results confirm that the proposed two-stage
fine-tuning significantly improves robustness to unseen lan-
guages while maintaining stable performance across trial cat-
egories.

5. Conclusion
We present a two-stage progressive training framework for
cross-lingual speaker verification that disentangles language-
dependent features from speaker identity by combining in-
domain backbone adaptation with metric learning. Our ap-
proach improves the challenge baseline EER from 3.07% to
1.75% through fine-tuning of ReDimNet-B6, and further to
1.58% using a lightweight projection network trained with
cross-lingual triplet loss and noise and reverberation augmen-
tation, thereby improving the system’s robustness to acoustic
variations. Additionally, a balanced cross-lingual triplet sam-
pling strategy helps mitigate language-induced errors.
