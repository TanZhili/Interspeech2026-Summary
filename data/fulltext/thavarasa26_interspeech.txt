Abstract
Speech Emotion Recognition (SER) advancements are con-
strained by high-resource bias, limiting global access. To ad-
dress this, we introduce KuralHub 1, an open-science initiative
democratizing SER for under-resourced languages. We evaluate
11 self-supervised models (WavLM, HuBERT, Wav2vec 2.0,
Whisper) across 33 datasets in 29 diverse languages. Beyond
standard benchmarking, our study reveals a critical typological
bias: cross-lingual transferability is dictated more by structural
linguistic properties than pre-training data volume. While mod-
els easily adapt to Mandarin or Amharic, they systematically
fail on the Dravidian family. Crucially, we demonstrate that
simply scaling model parameters fails to resolve this bottleneck.
By exposing these linguistic capability frontiers, KuralHub pro-
vides a vital roadmap for developing equitable, typologically-
aware speech technologies for all speakers.

1. Introduction
Speech Emotion Recognition (SER) constitutes a critical com-
ponent in the advancement of natural human-computer interac-
tion systems. As computational devices become increasingly
integrated into our daily lives, the ability to recognize and ap-
propriately respond to human emotional states has emerged
as a key factor in creating intuitive and effective interfaces
[1]. While significant progress has been made in SER tech-
nologies for high-resource languages, the linguistic diversity of
our global society—with over 7,000 languages spoken world-
wide—presents substantial challenges for developing truly in-
clusive emotional intelligence systems [2].
The disparity in SER performance across languages stems
from several factors. High-resource languages enjoy ample
data, funding, and tools, while low-resource languages lack
such support [3]. Cultural differences in emotional expression,
such as prosody and speech rate, further hinder model general-
ization across languages [4]. Phonological and acoustic varia-
tions also influence how emotions are conveyed and perceived
[5]. Moreover, methods effective for one language family may
not transfer well to others, especially typologically distant ones
[6].
Recent advances in self-supervised learning have pro-
duced powerful pretrained speech models such as HuBERT [7],
Wav2Vec 2.0 [8], WavLM [9], and Whisper [10]. These models,
trained on vast quantities of unlabeled speech data, have demon-
strated remarkable transferability to various downstream tasks,

2. Related Work
2.1. Speech Emotion Recognition (SER)
Speech Emotion Recognition (SER) is an important component
of human-computer interaction systems. Early SER methods
relied on handcrafted acoustic features such as pitch, energy,
duration, and spectral characteristics [11]. Recently, deep learn-
ing methods have achieved better performance by learning emo-
tional representations from spectral features such as MFCCs
and log-Mels [12]. Modern SER systems commonly use CNN,
RNN, and hybrid architectures [13]. However, SER remains
challenging due to speaker, language, and cultural variations,
especially in cross-lingual settings [14].
2.2. Supervised Learning Models for Speech
Self-supervised learning has revolutionized speech processing
through pre-trained models such as HuBERT [7], Wav2Vec 2.0
[8], WavLM [9], and Whisper [10]. HuBERT employs masked
prediction of clustered speech units using the BERT framework.
Wav2Vec 2.0 utilizes contrastive learning to identify correct
quantized speech representations. WavLM extends these ap-
proaches by incorporating both contrastive learning and speech
unit masking with denoising objectives. Whisper, trained on a
diverse multilingual dataset with ASR as its primary task, of-
fers potential advantages for cross-lingual applications. Fine-
tuning approaches typically fall into vanilla fine-tuning (VFT)
and parameter-efficient fine-tuning (PFT) categories [12].
2.3. SER Datasets and Benchmarks
SER development relies heavily on emotional speech datasets,
with a significant disparity in availability across languages [14].
Notable datasets include IEMOCAP [15], RA VDESS [16], and
MSP-PODCAST [17] for English; EMO-DB [18] for German;
and CASIA [19] for Mandarin [11]. These datasets vary in
design characteristics, including number of speakers, record-

3. Methodology
3.1. Language Selection and Dataset Collection
We identified the top 70 most widely spoken languages using
the Ethnologue database [24] and surveyed the availability of
emotional speech datasets for each. The complete survey is
available on the official KuralHub website 2. Among them, 45
languages had at least one emotional speech dataset, and 29 met
our criteria for experimental evaluation. Table 1 summarizes
these selected languages, their datasets, and associated meta-
data.
Datasets were collected through a systematic search of aca-
demic repositories and public resources. We selected corpora
that (i) contained clearly annotated emotional labels, (ii) in-
cluded at least three target emotions (among Anger, Happi-
ness, Sadness, Neutral, and Fear), and (iii) provided a mini-
mum of 50 utterances per emotion. The chosen datasets vary
in recording conditions, elicitation methods (acted vs. sponta-
neous), speaker demographics (age, gender, culture, etc.), and
emotion taxonomies (categorical vs. continuous), enabling a
comprehensive evaluation of model robustness and generaliza-
tion across varied linguistic and acoustic conditions.
3.2. Model Architecture
We adopted a transfer learning approach by leveraging sev-
eral state-of-the-art pretrained speech models as feature ex-
tractors. Specifically, we utilized the following mod-
els: HuBERT (hubert-base-ls960 3, hubert-large-ls960-ft 4)
[7], Wav2Vec 2.0 (wav2vec2-base 5, wav2vec2-large-960h 6,
wav2vec2-large-lv607, wav2vec2-xls-r-1b 8, wav2vec2-xls-r-
300m9) [8], WavLM (wavlm-base-plus 10, wavlm-large 11) [9],
and Whisper (whisper-small 12, whisper-large 13) [10]. These
models were used in a frozen configuration to retain their
learned acoustic representations, which significantly reduces
the need for large amounts of labeled data during fine-tuning.
To adapt these representations for emotion classification,
we appended a lightweight classification head on top of the
frozen feature extractor. The classification head consisted of

This head maps the extracted speech embeddings to emo-

was trained end-to-end with only the classification head being

3.3. Fine-tuning Process
We split each language dataset into 70% training, 15% valida-
tion, and 15% testing. The pretrained feature extractors were
kept frozen, and only the classification head was trained. Audio
was resampled to 16 kHz, normalized with Librosa and passed
through the backbone to extract embeddings.
The classification head was trained using cross-entropy loss
and the Adam optimizer for up to 30 epochs, with early stopping
based on validation loss.
3.4. Evaluation Methodology
We evaluated model performance using standard classification
metrics: precision, recall, F1-score, and accuracy. These were
computed on the held-out test set, with results reported both
per emotion and as macro and weighted averages to account for
class imbalance.

The evaluation focused on key emotions present across all
datasets. This methodology allowed us to assess not only over-
all accuracy but also how well the model performed across in-
dividual emotional classes. Early stopping was based on vali-
dation performance to avoid overfitting.

Our analysis reveals significant differences in how pre-trained
models detect emotions across languages. Figure 1 shows a

These findings indicate that pre-training duration alone
does not determine model quality. Structural linguistic prop-

for self-supervised learning (SSL) models to learn. In contrast,
languages with high dialect variation and spontaneous speech
introduce acoustic noise that can reduce performance.

A major finding is the consistent underperformance on Dravid-
ian languages. Kannada (µ= 0.275,σ= 0.085,P 25 = 0.23,
P50 = 0.26,P 75 = 0.30), Tamil (µ= 0.306,σ= 0.088,
P25 = 0.24,P 50 = 0.29,P 75 = 0.38, max =0.44), and Tel-
ugu (max =0.53) are among the worst-performing languages.
Table 2 strongly demonstrates model–language specializa-
tion, proving that no single model is universally optimal and
emphasizing the necessity of adaptive, language-aware model
selection for multilingual SER.
The very small standard deviation in Kannada and Tamil
indicates that almost all models fail consistently on these lan-
guages. This suggests structural limitations in current SSL
architectures. Dravidian languages are morphologically com-
plex and highly agglutinative, with many retroflex consonants.
These characteristics require fine-grained temporal modeling.
Current architectures using strided CNN encoders may lose im-
portant phonetic information.

4.3. Model Architecture and Parameter Scaling
Our benchmark evaluated 11 pre-trained models across 33
datasets. Whisper models achieved the highest average F1-
score (µ whisper−small = 0.70,µ whisper−large = 0.70),
followed by HuBERT-base (µ= 0.68) and WavLM models
(µwavlm−large = 0.64,µ wavlm−base−plus = 0.63). Figure 3
shows architectural differences. Whisper and HuBERT models
showed strong robustness across languages, while wav2vec 2.0
models showed weaker multilingual generalization.
Figure 3:Mean F1-score of models across architecture families.
To analyze architectural effects, we fitted a linear mixed-
effects model:
F1∼Family+ (1|Dataset) + (1|Language)(1)
The reference family achieved a mean F1-score of0.524. Com-
pared with this baseline, Wav2Vec 2.0 showed significant per-
formance degradation (β=−0.195,p < .001). WavLM
(β= 0.091,p < .001) and Whisper (β= 0.170,p < .001)
achieved higher F1-scores. About 40% of total variance was ex-
plained by dataset and language differences, confirming strong
linguistic and dataset influence.
Interestingly, larger models within the same family did not
always perform better. For example, wav2vec2-base (µ= 0.58,
σ= 0.17) outperformed wav2vec2-large-960h (µ= 0.30,σ=
0.11) and wav2vec2-large-lv60 (µ= 0.25,σ= 0.10).
At raw parameter scale, no significant linear relationship
existed between model size and F1-score (r= 0.0351, 95%
CI[−0.0681,0.1376],p= 0.5047). Since parameter growth
is exponential, we evaluatedlog(ModelSize). This showed
a significant negative correlation (r=−0.1769, 95% CI
[−0.2748,−0.0753],p= 0.0007). Spearman correlation
also confirmed moderate performance decline with model size
(ρ=−0.3440,p= 1.61×10 −11).
Another mixed-effects model was fitted:
F1∼log(ModelSize) + (1|Dataset) + (1|Language)(2)
The fixed effect oflog(ModelSize)was significantly nega-
tive (β=−0.0481, SE =0.0125,p= 0.000125). Increasing
parameters by a factor ofe(2.718×) reduced F1-score by about
4.8 points on average. Dataset-level variance (Var = 0.01134)
dominated language-level variance(≈0), mainly because 25
of 29 languages were represented by only one dataset. Thus,
parameter scaling alone does not improve multilingual emotion
recognition.

5. Conclusion, Limitations and Future
Work
We introduce KuralHub, a large-scale initiative exposing sys-
temic inequities in multilingual SER. Benchmarking 11 mod-
els across 29 languages reveals a critical paradigm shift: cross-
lingual transferability is bottlenecked by structural linguistic ty-
pology, not merely pre-training volume or parameter scale. Cru-
cially, some language families (e.g., Dravidian) appear to lie
near a ”capability frontier” where current SOTA architectures
systematically fail.
To ensure computationally equitable evaluation, we relied
on frozen last-layer features, which may underrepresent par-
alinguistic cues embedded in intermediate layers. Further-
more, dataset conditions (e.g., acted vs. spontaneous speech)
drive 40% of performance variance, complicating pure zero-
shot comparisons.
Achieving inclusive SER requires moving beyond blind pa-
rameter scaling. We urge the community to leverage KuralHub
to pioneer typologically-aware pre-training and dynamic, layer-
wise fine-tuning, ultimately dismantling these architectural bi-
ases to guarantee equitable access to speech emotion intelli-
gence systems for all speakers.
