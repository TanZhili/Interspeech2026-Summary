Abstract
Many low-resource languages in Northeast India lack native
scripts, limiting speech technology development. This work
presents text-to-speech resources for two Manipuri tribal lan-
guages, Tangkhul (9.58h) and Maring(10.79h), based on stu-
dio recordings in their standard dialects. The dataset consists
of read speech collected from textbooks, storybooks, and Bible
translations, together with an automatic preprocessing pipeline
for segmentation and normalization. We train monolingual
character-level TTS systems using Tacotron 2 and FastSpeech
2 and evaluate them with objective and subjective metrics. The
results show that these corpora and baseline systems can sup-
port TTS development for low-resource Latin-script languages
of Manipur and provide a useful starting point for future work.

1. Introduction
Text-to-speech (TTS) synthesis is a process that transforms
written text into utterances that sound natural. This allows
for the creation of educational content, the preservation of lan-
guages, and the development of accessibility tools. Many works
has been done on TTS [1] even on low-resource languages [2].
Such models require sufficient data [3] and good model to train.
The synthesized speech are rated through naturalness, intelligi-
bility and sometimes expressiveness. A simple TTS starts from
single speaker to multispeaker and multilingual models. Re-
cently zero-shot TTS like Adaspeech 4 [4] are also very popular
which can generate speech of a speaker from very low data.
Since the colonial era, all 34 tribal languages in Manipur,
India [5] have mostly employed an adapted form of the Latin
(Roman) script for educational and literary purposes. Each lan-
guage possesses multiple dialects across regions, and in the ab-
sence of standardization, the orthographies differ among publi-
cations. A shortage of data and its ambiguity hinder the corpus
aggregation necessary for developing a viable text-to-speech
model. Tangkhul and Maring are two Tibeto-Burman languages
used by tribal groups in Manipur. The speakers of Tangkhul lan-
guages total approximately 178 thousands, while the speakers
of Maring languages number around 26 thousands, categorizing
Maring as endangered language [6]. The standard Latin alpha-
bet, which consists of 26 letters, is insufficient to express the
extensive inventories of consonants, vowel contrasts, and tones,
as indicated by media. To guarantee phonemic characters, di-
acritics or special characters are implemented. The objective
of this paper is to construct an initial speech corpus and train

Models: There has been many recent works on TTS for
both autoregressive models such as Tacotron 2 [7] and non-
autoregressive model such as FastSpeech 2 [8]. Tacotron 2 has
been used to build custom TTS models for Malay [9], Kannada
[10], Sanskrit [11], Punjabi and Urdu [12] either trained from
scratch or using transfer learning. Similarly FastSpeech 2 is
trained on Arab Speech [13]. No prior work addresses TTS for
Manipur’s Latin Script tribal languages, nor provides reusable
pipelines for similar Tibeto-Burman contexts.
Datasets: There already exist TTS datasets for Indic lan-
guages, such as IndicTTS [14], which has about 10 hours of
speech for each of the 13 languages, and IndicV oices-R [15],
which has speech from Manipuri (Meitei) as well as other Indic
languages with few resources. These resources are beneficial to
mainland Indic languages, but there aren’t any datasets for Ma-
nipur’s hill tribal languages, such as Tangkhul-Naga and Mar-
ing, even though they are both Tibeto-Burman languages and
have similar data limits. Regional attempts, like the North-East
India corpora, mostly focus on ASR instead of TTS. This leaves
a big gap for these unscripted, Latin-adapted tribal languages
that have complicated phonotactics.
The following outlines our contributions: We have con-
structed the first 9-hour monolingual TTS dataset for the
Tangkhul language. We have also developed the first 10-
hour high-quality monolingual TTS dataset for the Maring lan-
guage. Furthermore, we have implemented TTS models for
both Tacotron 2 and FastSpeech 2 in these languages. To fa-
cilitate the creation of datasets for low-resource languages, we
are also releasing an open pipeline for dataset processing.

2.1. Text corpus
The data collected from the web was insufficient and unsuitable
for our text corpora. Although some data was found, it was in
PDF format and contained only images. Instead of using those
data, we obtained students’ textbooks and additional literature
books.
Textbooks are the primary source of our text data, followed
by storybooks [19], poetry books, and Bible translations . The
dialect used in textbooks is selected as the standard for our
dataset. Because the annotators are native speakers of the lan-
guage, they can catch misspellings and minor errors. Inap-
propriate text was excluded from recording. The handling of
extended characters is a specific instance in the Tangkhul lan-
guage. The three versions of ‘a’ that represent three separate
phonemes are ‘a’, ‘a’ with a bar above (represented by ‘¯a’), and
‘a’ with a bar below (made up of two characters).
2.2. Speech Recording
The recording was conducted in a studio with low ambient noise
levels. To ensure optimum quality, each recording was limited
to a maximum of two hours per day, with short intermissions in
between. The exact same recorder was being used consistently
during the session. The device used was the Tascam DR-05X
Stereo Audio Recorder and recording was done in mono chan-
nel at 48 kHz.
The speech recordings were contributed by two native
speakers. A 23 years old female Tangkhul speaker, familiar with
the textbooks used in the recording and who also speaks the
standard dialect spoken in Ukhrul headquarters, the main town,
was the selected speaker. Similarly, we also selected a 24 years
old female Maring speaker who can fluently speak the standard
dialect featured in the more recently published textbooks. Both
speakers were instructed to prioritize natural speech over pre-
cise word-by-word pronunciation. If a mistake occurred, it was
corrected before proceeding, with audio handling done later to
clip errors and select the best recordings.
2.3. Audio Preprocessing
Once the recording is finished, an automatic segmentation
pipeline has been implemented by incorporating existing signal
processing methods.
Using voice activity detection, the raw audio files are anal-
ysed to identify regions that contain silence by comparing the
energy level to a threshold. Silence is applied to segments that
are quieter than -40 dB to ensure that soft speech is still cap-
tured. The timestamps are recorded for potential segmentation
after the raw V AD regions are identified. The preceding and
trailing silences are also specified to accommodate room tone
during the recording of the timestamps in accordance with Ta-
ble 1, ensuring that the recording sounds natural. A loop is ini-
tiated to combine these segments in order to accommodate the
minimum and maximum durations of two or more adjacent seg-
ments, using these timestamps. This ensures that the segmenta-
tion logic doesn’t force the audio to be split into extremely short
segments which will leave it unintelligible.

2.4. Annotation and Quality Control Process
The annotators are thereafter provided with these parts for si-
multaneous annotation. Additionally, they were instructed to
discover problematic segments and inconsistencies for quality
assurance. Upon completion of the annotation, quality control
activities involve manually concatenating adjacent audio to en-
hance its alignment with standard speech patterns. Text normal-
ization is also performed in quality control, where all abbrevia-
tions and numbers are identified and subsequently expanded af-
ter hearing monitoring of the speech. The annotators and quality
control experts are the same individuals who are native speakers

3. Experiments
3.1. TTS Models
For our experiment, we used TTS models which generate mel
spectrogram from the input text. V ocoders are then used to get
the final speech.
Tacotron 2: Tacotron 2 [7] is an autoregressive (AR) model
that applies location-sensitive attention. In this encoder-decoder
configuration, the prenet is positioned at the decoder’s input,
forcing the decoder to avoid overfitting on precise preceding
mel frames. The postnet following the decoder improves the re-
sulting mel spectrogram. It gives spectral information and aids
in the reconstruction of the fine structure of distinct language
units. A directed attention loss is also included.
FastSpeech 2: FastSpeech 2 [8] is a non-autoregressive
model that predicts mel spectrograms simultaneously using a
feedforward transformer. Despite the increased amount of pa-
rameters, the architecture allows for shorter training times. It
uses attention in conjunction with positional encoding. Fast-
Speech 2’s duration predictor can detect tonal rhythms. The
F0 predictor models basic frequency or pitch without the expo-
sure biases found in AR models. The loss criteria include Mean
Squared Error (MSE) and duration loss.
V ocoder: Style MelGAN [20] is a version of HiFi-GAN
that uses style tokens to make expressive waveforms. It uses
a global style vector to add prosodic variety, which keeps the
dialectal timbre. The vocoder uses mel spectrograms as input to
make speech. These are made by Tacotron 2 and FastSpeech 2.

The discussion on results of the various evaluations leads to
the following:
Tangkhul: A particular instance for Tangkhul is that, as we
trained the model using characters, the letter ’a’ with a bar be-
low comprises two distinct characters. When we include only
the bar, it is identified as a non-silent character, which can occa-
sionally produce incomprehensible speech. Figure 2 illustrates
the attention plot of a random audio file with a duration of 7
seconds. The diacritics in Tangkhul languages indicate phonetic
distinctions that are fully represented by our trained model. We
eliminated the discrepancies and trained a basic model that fails
to recognise the distinction for shorter durations. The tonal dis-
tinction also influences the duration, as noted during evaluation.
Maring: The synthesised speech is challenging to compre-
hend, according to the MOS raters. Upon observation, we deter-
mined that this is due to the dialect differential. Maring has not
had a standard dialect until recently, in contrast to the Tangkhul
language, which has been using a standard version of the di-
alect for a long time. This is also evident during data collection,
as textbooks were first published not long ago. Nevertheless,
the raters (those who speaks different dialect than the recorded
version) are capable of comprehending the speech after the text
in the synthesised speech is provided. The attention plot of a
random audio file of 15 seconds is depicted in Figure 3.
Discussion on Script: Despite sharing the same Latin
script, Engish trained TTS fails on Tangkhul/Maring due to
orthographic and phonetic divergence. The high presence of
tones in these languages is completely ignored as Engish speech
doesn’t use tone except for stress.
5. Conclusion and Future Work
The experimental results demonstrate the need to acquire high-
quality datasets in tonal languages. TTS models trained on
a particular language cannot replace other languages, even if
the scripts are similar. It should also be noted that dialects
have a significant impact on languages that have numerous di-
alects. The objective evaluations of unseen text versus seen
text demonstrate the limitations of MOS evaluation by a rater
whose dialect differs from the speaker’s. High-quality mod-
els were successfully developed for Manipur’s endangered lan-
guages, Maring and Tangkhul. The distinction between tonal
languages and non-tonal languages has also been found in the
synthesised speech of existing models in English-trained pre-
trained models. The data curation and processing pipeline will
help improve resources for additional tribal languages.
One of the major challenges in scaling TTS systems is the
inability to capture the full phonetic diversity of multiple lan-
guages, even those belonging to the same language family. To
address this challenge, a shared phoneme space that can ac-
commodate a larger set of related languages should be devel-
oped. Modern state-of-the-art TTS models incorporate pitch
modeling techniques that effectively capture tonal information
and prosodic variations. Real-time deployment of these systems
can promote the adoption of speech technologies for very low-
resource languages and improve speech technologies in general.
