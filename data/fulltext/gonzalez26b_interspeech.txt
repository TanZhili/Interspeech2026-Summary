Abstract
Speech embeddings have become a central component of mod-
ern automatic speech recognition and speech-language models;
however, their linguistic interpretability is still under active re-
search. This study adopts a diagnostic approach to examine
the extent to which speech embeddings encode linguistic fea-
tures. We analyse linear associations between pretrained acous-
tic embeddings and a set of core acoustic, phonetic, and lin-
guistic structure features. The results show that embeddings
encode acoustic and phonetic properties, particularly tempo-
ral dynamics, spectral structure, and voice-quality related fea-
tures, while higher-level linguistic structure is represented more
weakly. Lexical complexity shows a measurable association,
whereas morphological and syntactic complexity show limited
linear accessibility. These findings suggest that embeddings
capture linguistic information in a distributed and gradient man-
ner, with strongest sensitivity to properties closely tied to signal
realisation.
Index Terms: speech embeddings, linguistic features, phonet-
ics, phonology, lexical complexity
1. Introduction
Recent advances in speech and language technologies have been
driven by the widespread adoption of embedding-based rep-
resentations. Learned from large-scale acoustic and linguistic
data, embeddings provide dense, multidimensional representa-
tions that encode rich information about speech and language
structure [1, 2, 3]. Prior work has demonstrated that such rep-
resentations capture a wide range of linguistic properties, span-
ning acoustic, phonetic, and higher-level linguistic information,
even when trained with weak or indirect supervision [4, 5, 6, 7].
Embedding representations have become foundational across a
broad spectrum of downstream applications and research do-
mains, as in Automatic Speech Recognition (ASR) [8], linguis-
tic typology [9, 10], speaker and accent classification [11], di-
alect modeling [12], and speech processing for under-resourced
and zero-resource languages [13, 14, 15].
A key conceptual shift underlying embedding-based mod-
els is their departure from linguistic unit representation anal-
yses, which typically operate over discrete units such as
phonemes, morphemes, or syntactic categories, which are
treated as explicit targets or annotations. In contrast, modern
acoustic-to-language models rely on vectorial representations
learned through optimisation objectives such as masked lan-
guage modelling, next-token prediction or speech-to-text de-

into numerical spaces distributed across populations of simple
computing units, allowing information from multiple linguistic
levels to be integrated within a unified representational frame-

This unified perspective has proven particularly powerful
for capturing language as a multidimensional and intercon-
nected system rather than as a collection of isolated compo-
nents. Large-scale models, such as Whisper, have achieved
speech recognition performance comparable to human-level ac-
curacy, despite the absence of explicit phonemic or grammati-
cal representations during training [17]. Moreover, combining
acoustic and linguistic embeddings within a shared representa-
tional space has been shown to facilitate the study of relation-
ships across traditionally distinct levels of linguistic analysis,
including phonetics, lexical structure, and discourse-level infor-
mation [16].
These approaches suggest that embeddings encode complex
linguistic structure in a distributed manner that transcends in-
dividual symbolic categories. At the same time, linguistic re-
search and applied speech sciences continue to rely heavily on
the analysis of interpretable linguistic units, since they help in
the understanding of language behaviour within a speech com-
munity [18, 19], which is central to the study of language vari-
ation, identity, and change [20, 21, 22, 23]. Similarly, clini-
cal phonetics and speech pathology [24, 25] depend on identi-
fying systematic deviations in particular linguistic dimensions
[26, 27, 28], while language documentation and revitalisation
efforts often target specific phonetic-phonological phenomena
[29, 30].
2. Aim of Research
This study examines how speech embeddings encode linguisti-
cally meaningful information by identifying quantifiable linear
associations between pretrained embeddings and established
linguistic features. We adopt a diagnostic perspective, probing
what linguistic information is captured and how it is distributed
within the representation space rather than recovering parame-
ters directly.
Pretrained embeddings offer key advantages over tradi-
tional acoustic measures. Unlike pitch or formant frequencies,
which rely on strong modeling assumptions and are sensitive to
variability, embeddings are learned from large-scale, heteroge-
neous data and optimised to capture invariant structure across

We calculate linear associations between embeddings and
linguistic features as a principled means of probing represen-
tational structure. The goal is not one-to-one correspondences
between dimensions and units, but distributed relationships be-
tween embedding subspaces and linguistic values. This char-
acterises how linguistic information is organised within high-
dimensional spaces, contributing to the interpretability of large-
scale speech models and its application in speech studies.
3. Methodology
3.1. Speech Dataset
The analyses were conducted using the FLEURS dataset [31], a
multilingual speech corpus covering over 100 languages across
diverse linguistic families. It provides high-quality read speech
recordings (with transcriptions) of a consistent set of prompts,
enabling controlled cross-linguistic comparisons, suitable for
investigating how acoustic embeddings encode linguistic fea-
tures in a cross-linguistic context. For this study, we focused
on a subset of 36 languages for which complete metadata and
high-quality audio were available. The final data analysed con-
sisted of 43,185 recordings (mean=1,200 recs per language,
sd=348 across all dataset), and a total duration over 136 hours
(mean=3.78 per language, sd=1.21 across all dataset). The data
was split into train (n=35,647, 83%) and test (n=7,538, 17%)
splits.
3.2. Features Extracted
The selected features are widely used in both linguistic research
and speech technology, allowing the present analysis to bridge
theoretical interpretability and applied relevance. While no fea-
ture set can fully capture the complexity of spoken language, the
chosen features provide a both compact and informative rep-
resentation of key linguistic dimensions. The feature set was
organised into three broad categories: core acoustic features,
acoustic-phonetic features, and higher-level linguistic structure.
All features were extracted from a custom python script, which
useslibrosa[32] andparselmouth[33] (Praat [34] in Python)
libraries. All values were calculated on the full duration of each
selected recording from the dataset.
3.2.1. Acoustic Features
Core acoustic features were selected to characterise low-level
properties of the speech signal that are sensitive to both lin-
guistic and extra-linguistic factors. These includedJitterand
Shimmer, which capture cycle-to-cycle variability in fundamen-
tal frequency and amplitude, respectively, and are widely used
in phonetic research, clinical voice assessment, speaker recog-
nition, and emotion analysis [35, 36].Spectral Flatness,zero-
crossing rate(ZCR),Spectral Centroid,Spectral Bandwidth,
andSpectral Rolloffwere included to capture spectral shape,
noisiness, and energy distribution [37, 38]. These features are
commonly employed in speech pre-processing, voice activity
detection [39], robustness enhancement in ASR [40], and the
analysis of speaking style and vocal effort [41, 42], and they
have been shown to be informative for both linguistic and meta-
linguistic variation [43, 44].
3.2.2. Phonetic Features
Acoustic-phonetic features were selected to reflect properties
that have direct linguistic relevance while remaining grounded
in the acoustic signal. The present study does not treat these
features as ground-truth representations of linguistic units, but
rather as linguistically motivated proxies that enable systematic
comparison with embedding representations. These included
segmental and suprasegmental duration measures, speech rate,
pitch range, and acoustic energy.Speech Ratewas calculated
as syllables per second, computed using peak detection in the
amplitude envelope, following established approaches that ex-
ploit the energetic prominence of vowel nuclei across languages
[45, 46, 47].
This method relies on minimal language-specific assump-
tions and generalises well across typologically diverse lan-
guages, making it particularly suitable for large-scale compar-
ative analysis.Pitch Rangeand theRoot Mean Square Er-
ror(RMSE) were included due to their well-documented links
to prosody, emphasis, affect, and pragmatic meaning, as well
as their relevance in speech recognition and expressive speech
modeling [48, 41, 42, 49].
3.2.3. Linguistic Structure Features
To capture higher levels of linguistic organisation, additional
features were selected to reflect morphological, lexical, and
syntactic structure. For this, we carried out the automatic tag-
ging using theStanza toolkit[50], which has a reported over-
all accuracy of 92.49% for universal parts-of-speech tagging
(UPOS), and 92.78% for lemma tagging [50]. Morphologi-
cal complexity was quantified asUPOS Entropy, a measure of
UPOS distributional complexity, alongside lemma-based inflec-
tional complexity (Lemma Complexity) to capture the richness
of morphological paradigms. Syntactic complexity was cap-
tured throughClause Complexitymeasures, reflecting the de-
gree of hierarchical structuring in sentence formation. Lexical
complexity was calculated using thecorrected type-token ratio
(CTTR), a widely adopted metric for assessing lexical diversity
that has recently been applied to both human language data and
large language models [51, 52].
3.3. Speech Embeddings
We adoptW2V-BERT 2.0[53] to obtain the embedding of
a speech utterance due to its strong empirical performance
across various downstream tasks including speech classifica-
tion, speech recognition and speech separation [54, 31].W2V-
BERT 2.0is aConformer-basedspeech representation model
pretrained using the self-supervised learning paradigm. The
model learns contextualised speech representations from raw
audio via masked prediction objective without requiring la-
belled data.W2V-BERT 2.0has approximately 600M parame-
ters and was trained on 4.5M hours of audio covering more than
143 languages . Given a speech utterance as input, the model
produces a sequence of frame-level embeddings of dimension
1024.
Since the number of time steps varies across different ut-
terances, we apply median pooling over the temporal dimen-
sion to obtain a fixed-dimensional utterance-level embedding.
WhileW2V-BERT 2.0does not impose a maximum input au-
dio length, the quadratic complexity of theTransformerself-
attention presents practical challenges for the model to process
long audio. As a result, in our experiments, long utterances are
segmented into fixed-length chunks (10 seconds) before being

the median over the segment embeddings.

4. Results
4.1. Overall Results
Across all feature categories, the results indicate that speech
embeddings encode linguistically relevant information in a sys-
tematic but distributed manner, although the strength of the as-
sociations varied substantially across domains (See Table 1 and
Figure 1). It is important to note that the observed patterns do
not show one-to-one correspondences between embeddings and
specific linguistic parameters. Instead, they point to graded and
feature-dependent relationships, consistent with the assumption
that embeddings capture linguistic structure in a multidimen-
sional and non-discrete way. Features grounded in acoustic and
acoustic-phonetic properties show stronger linear associations
than features at higher levels of the linguistic structure, includ-
ing morphological and lexical features.
4.2. Acoustic Features
Shimmer(R 2=0.585) andFlatness(R 2=0.558) showed the
highest explained variance, suggesting that amplitude variabil-
ity and spectral noisiness are particularly salient in the em-
bedding space. Measures related to spectral shape and en-
ergy distribution, includingCentroid(R 2=0.396),Bandwidth
(R2=0.374), andRolloff(R 2=0.339), also showed moderate to
strong associations, pointing to a robust encoding of frequency-
domain characteristics. ZCR (R 2=0.508) demonstrated a com-
parable level of predictability, consistent with its role as a coarse
indicator of voicing and signal complexity.Jitter(R 2=0.365),
showed a moderate association, indicating that micro-variations
in fundamental frequency are partially reflected in embeddings,
though less strongly than amplitude-based or spectral measures.

4.3. Phonetic Features
Acoustic-phonetic features showed consistently strong linear
associations with embeddings, indicating the relevance of
phonetic structure in embedding representations.Duration
(R2=0.510), emerged as one of the most strongly encoded fea-
tures, exhibiting high explanatory power. This finding aligns
with the central role of temporal structure in speech processing
and suggests that embeddings are highly sensitive to timing-
related cues.Speech Rate(R 2=0.321) also showed a substantial
association, indicating that rhythmic and syllabic organisation
is reflected in the embedding space.
RMSE (R 2=0.196) showed a weak but consistent rela-
tionship with embeddings, supporting the view that loudness-
related information is encoded, albeit in a distributed manner.
Pitch Range(R 2=0.146) demonstrated another weak but still
observable relationship, suggesting that suprasegmental varia-
tion is encoded, though less directly than temporal features.
These patterns support the assumption that embeddings cap-
ture phonetic information that is closely tied to articulation and
prosodic organisation, even when such features are not explic-
itly modeled during training.
4.4. Linguistic Structure
Higher-level linguistic features showed weaker and more vari-
able linear associations with embeddings, though several mea-
sures showed meaningful explanatory power. Lexical diversity
(CTTR) displayed a clear and robust association (R 2=0.427),
suggesting that embeddings encode information related to lexi-
cal richness and vocabulary distribution. This result is notable
given that CTTR is derived from textual structure rather than
directly from the acoustic signal, indicating that embeddings
capture aspects of linguistic organisation beyond purely pho-
netic content. Morphological complexity measures showed no
meaningful associations.UPOS Entropydid show an observ-
able relationship though its trajectory was positive (R2=0.039),
suggesting that category-level morphological variation is virtu-
ally not reflected in acoustic embeddings. Similarly, lemma-
based morphological complexity showed no meaningful lin-
ear association (R 2=0.005), indicating that fine-grained inflec-
tional richness is not readily accessible through linear projec-

5. Discussion
The present findings provide empirical evidence that speech
embeddings encode linguistically relevant information in a sys-
tematic and distributed manner, with clear differences in how
information is represented across linguistic domains. In line
with the theoretical foundation of this study, the results support
the view that embeddings do not mirror traditional linguistic
units one-to-one, but instead reflect graded associations with
acoustic, phonetic, and, more indirectly, linguistic-structural
properties. At the acoustic and acoustic-phonetic levels, the
stronger linear associations observed for features such as du-
ration, speech rate, spectral measures, shimmer, and jitter, indi-
cate that embeddings retain substantial information about tem-
poral dynamics, spectral structure, and fine-grained signal vari-
ability. These properties are central to phonetic realisation and
articulation, suggesting that embeddings are particularly well
suited for capturing aspects of speech that emerge directly from
the acoustic signal.
Results highlight the relatively stronger encoding of shim-
mer and jitter, which are features traditionally associated with
voice quality and clinical pathology. This suggests potential
avenues for extending their use beyond established domains.
Their prominence in the embedding space raises the possibility
that such measures may also be informative for studying socio-
phonetic variation, speaker-specific patterns, or stylistic modu-
lation, areas where these features have received comparatively
limited attention. This aligns with broader trends in speech re-
search that advocate for re-examining established acoustic fea-
tures in new theoretical and empirical contexts, such as work
exploring the linguistic relevance of MFCCs and other signal-
derived representations (e.g., [57, 58, 59, 60]).
The results further indicate that speech embeddings cap-
ture phonetic information more robustly than higher-level lin-
guistic structure. While lexical complexity, measured via
CTTR, showed a clear association with embeddings, morpho-
logical and syntactic complexity measures exhibited substan-
tially weaker relationships. This asymmetry suggests that infor-
mation about linguistic structure is encoded indirectly, mediated
through its phonetic and prosodic realisation, rather than be-
ing explicitly represented in embeddings. This may be because
the attention mechanism in the transformer is fulfilling the role
of encoding structural information, while the median pooling
applied to the embeddings may suppress linguistic information
encoded in the relationships between embeddings. Importantly,
the fact that lexical complexity can be accessed through embed-
dings has practical implications for linguistic research, partic-
ularly for under-resourced languages where reliable transcrip-
tions are scarce.
Finally, these findings do not suggest that embedding-based
analyses should replace research grounded in linguistic units.
Rather, they point toward a complementary approach in which
embeddings and other linguistic representations are combined
strategically, depending on the research question. For certain
phenomena, particularly those involving acoustic realisation,
embeddings may offer clear advantages. For others, especially
those requiring explicit structural representation, unit-based lin-
guistic analysis remains indispensable. Future work can ben-
efit from integrating multiple types of embeddings, including
word, sentence, and speech-level representations, to better cap-
ture higher-order linguistic structure.
6. Conclusions
This study on embeddings and linguistic features advances
our understanding of how linguistic information is encoded
in speech embeddings. We have systematically examined
their linear associations with acoustic, phonetic, and linguistic-
structural features. In a broader sense, this work highlights the
potential for linguistics to leverage methodological advances
from speech technology and artificial intelligence in an in-
formed manner. This encourages interdisciplinary collabora-
tion and methodological exchange, where embedding-based ap-
proaches can complement other linguistic analyses and expand
the scope of empirical investigation. Such integration is partic-
ularly relevant for the study of under-resourced languages and
diverse speech communities, where scalability is essential.
These findings should be interpreted with caution. The re-
sults are conditioned on the specific dataset, feature selection,
and embedding model employed in this study, and alternative
features or models may reveal different patterns of association.
In particular, embeddings derived from other architectures or
training objectives may encode linguistic structure in distinct
ways. Nevertheless, the consistency of the observed patterns
provides potential contributions in the broader research direc-
tion and motivates further exploration of embedding-linguistic
relationships.
