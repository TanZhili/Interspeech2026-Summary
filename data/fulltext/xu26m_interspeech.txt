Learning speaker identities in dialogue: Conversational familiarisation
modulates response bias and confidence in voice recognition
Tianze Xu ID 1,∗∗, V olker Dellwo ID 1
1 Department of Computational Linguistics, University of Zurich, Switzerland
tianze.xu@uzh.ch, volker.dellwo@uzh.ch
Abstract
In everyday communication, voice learning occurs in conver-
sational dialogue, yet most experimental research relies on iso-
lated, explicitly memorised speech. We examined how conver-
sational familiarisation and attentional focus influence subse-
quent voice recognition. Two hundred English-speaking par-
ticipants listened either to a coherent four-speaker dialogue or
to a scrambled version and were instructed to attend to speaker
identity, speech content, or simply listen without a specific fo-
cus. Participants then completed a voice recognition (old/new)
task. Results revealed no main effects of stimulus type or focus.
Critically, familiarisation stimulus interacted with ground truth:
dialogue familiarisation increased recognition of old speakers
and confidence in those responses, but also increased incorrect
endorsements of new speakers as old, suggesting a shift in re-
sponse bias. These findings demonstrate how conversational
context systematically modulates voice recognition decisions.
Index Terms: voice recognition, naturalistic stimuli, attention
in learning, auditory memory
1. Introduction
In everyday life, voice learning and recognition typically oc-
cur implicitly in conversational exchanges [1]. For example,
when getting to know new colleagues, listeners acquire their
voices incidentally through dialogue, without explicitly trying
to memorise them, yet are still able to recognise them in later
interactions. Conversational speech is rich in prosody and un-
folds across turns, providing a context in which listeners natu-
rally track vocal identity cues. Such ecologically valid learning
environments align with recent trends in cognitive science re-
search that emphasise the use of naturalistic, continuous, and
non-task-based stimuli [2].
However, most previous studies on voice recognition, par-
ticularly those developing assessment tools, have relied on
highly controlled stimuli, such as isolated vowels or short sen-
tences repeated many times [3, 4]. For example, the Jena V oice
Learning and Memory Test (JVLMT) [4] uses pseudo-sentences
that follow English phonotactics and consists of three phases:
a learning phase in which participants hear target voices and
complete an immediate recognition trial; a repetition phase in
which the target voices reproduce the sentences; and a test
phase involving a three-alternative forced-choice (3AFC) task
with target voices and foils. Although such designs provide
strong experimental control, they differ sharply from the im-
plicit, context-rich voice learning that occurs in natural con-
versation. Crucially, this discrepancy raises a theoretical ques-
tion: do laboratory-based tests capture the same mechanisms

as the JVLMT minimise linguistic variability, reduce memory
load, and encourage intentional memorisation, they may esti-
mate an upper bound on recognition ability rather than the pro-
cesses engaged in spontaneous dialogue, where identity cues
are variable, less salient, and often encoded incidentally.
Previous research has examined how attentional focus dur-
ing voice learning influences subsequent recognition, yet the
methodologies and findings vary considerably across studies.
One major source of divergence concerns the operationalisa-
tion of non-intentional learning. In some experiments, listeners
were told to expect a memory test but were misled about its true
target (e.g., instructed to focus on message content rather than
speaker identity) [6, 7]. In others, listeners were not informed
of any upcoming recognition test at all, thereby aiming to cap-
ture genuinely incidental voice learning [8]. More recent studies
have introduced more sophisticated designs, such as directed-
forgetting paradigms with electroencephalography [9] or tasks
embedding voice exposure under different attentional demands
[1]. Across studies, results have been mixed: some report an ad-
vantage for intentional over non-intentional learning [6, 9], oth-
ers find no reliable difference between the two [7, 8, 10], and a
few have observed superior performance under non-intentional
learning conditions [1]. Moreover, because much of the early
work was motivated by forensic applications, the emphasis has
traditionally been on measuring performance after a single ex-
posure rather than on understanding the cognitive mechanisms
that support voice learning in more naturalistic settings [9].
The difference between voice recognition in laboratory set-
tings and everyday situations has long been acknowledged [5],
yet it remains unclear how voice learning depends on the nat-
uralness of the stimuli and listeners’ attentional focus during
familiarisation. To systematically examine this question, the
present study adopted a learning–recognition paradigm with a
2×3between-subjects design (Figure 1). During familiari-
sation, participants were exposed to the voices of four speak-
ers, and both familiarisation stimulus and focus were manip-
ulated, yielding six groups. Familiarisation stimulus had two
levels: natural dialogue or scrambled speech (which disrupted
discourse coherence and approximated the isolated materials
used in previous studies [3, 4]). Familiarisation focus had three
levels: speaker identity, speech content, or undirected. Each
participant was randomly assigned to one of the six conditions.
After familiarisation, participants completed tests assessing re-
call of both the content and the voices (four learnt targets and
four novel foils). Analyses focused on voice recognition perfor-
mance and response confidence using frequentist and Bayesian
linear mixed-effects models (LMMs) and generalised LMMs
(GLMMs). Signal detection theory (SDT) metrics were also
analysed to examine response bias and sensitivity. The con-

2. Methods
2.1. Participants
A total of 203 native speakers of Northern American English
residing in North America were recruited via the online plat-
form Prolific [11]. Three participants were excluded due to
a clear misunderstanding of the task, as indicated in the post-
task debriefing questionnaire. Thus, data from 200 participants
were included in subsequent analyses (125 female, 73 male,
two non-binary; aged 22–55 years,M= 39.56,SD= 9.19).
Participants reported normal hearing and no history of speech,
language, cognitive, or psychological disorders. The Edin-
burgh handedness inventory [12] indicated that most partici-
pants were right-handed (n= 176; 88%), with smaller num-
bers of ambidextrous (n= 14; 7%) and left-handed partici-
pants (n= 10; 5%). All participants provided written informed
consent prior to participation and received monetary compensa-
tion. The study was approved by the Research Ethics Commit-
tee of the Faculty of Arts and Social Sciences at the University
of Zurich (Registration No. 25.10.16).
2.2. Stimuli
The familiarisation material consisted of a conversational dia-
logue between four friends discussing whether a balloon would
burst in outer space, designed to be engaging and naturalis-
tic for listeners. The dialogue opened with greetings in which
each of the four speakers was explicitly named, simulating real-
life conversational openings. Speakers took turns throughout
the exchange (six turns per speaker) and maintained consis-
tent personas. Each speaker produced six turns and a total of
181 phonetic syllables, ensuring equal exposure across listen-
ers. The voice recall test used a separate set of materials for
each speaker: four Harvard sentences and an additional four
short phrases extracted from other sentences [13].
The familiarisation and test speech were synthesised with a
state-of-the-art multi-speaker text-to-speech system, VibeV oice
[14]. This system learns the acoustic characteristics of each ref-
erence speaker from real human samples and generates highly
natural speech that preserves speaker identity. Evaluations with
human listeners indicate that VibeV oice produces high-quality
speech with appropriate prosody, emotional nuance, and smooth
conversational dynamics. Using synthesised speech derived
from real human voices allows us to retain the advantages of
large, previously established speech datasets for the reference
speakers while providing substantial flexibility in manipulating
and extending the experimental materials.
Reference voices for the four target and four foil speakers
(two female and two male in each set) were selected to max-
imise acoustic distinctiveness using x-vector speaker embed-
dings. X-vectors are fixed-dimensional representations derived
from deep neural networks trained for speaker identification and
have been shown to capture speaker-specific information in a
robust and noise-tolerant manner [15, 16]. To identify suitable
voices, we drew on natural speech recordings from the UCLA
Speaker Variability Database [17], using the subset of native
speakers of North American English (N= 127; 64 female,
63 male; aged 18–29 years,M= 20.43,SD= 2.03) recall-
ing a neutral conversation. Each speaker was represented by
a single x-vector embedding extracted from this recording us-
ing the SpeechBrain toolkit (Version 1.0.3) [18] in Python. To
quantify between-speaker distinctiveness, we computed pair-
wise cosine distances among x-vectors separately for each gen-
der. We then searched for the most square-like quadruples of
speakers in this distance space, defined as two disjoint pairs (A,
B) and (C, D) whose within-pair distances (AB, CD) were ap-
proximately matched and whose four cross-pair distances (AC,
AD, BC, BD) were approximately equal within predefined tol-
erances. This procedure yielded a set of four speakers with ap-
proximately symmetric separations in the embedding space for
each gender, while maintaining controlled variation in between-
speaker distinctiveness. The reference speech from these speak-
ers was then supplied to the synthesiser to generate the famil-
iarisation dialogue and test speech clips.
2.3. Procedure
The experiment (Figure 1) was implemented in Gorilla [19].
After providing consent and completing a language background
questionnaire and the Edinburgh handedness inventory, partici-
pants completed three sound checks: the first verified that audio
playback in the browser was functioning normally; the second
screened out non-headphone users using a dichotic pitch test
[20]; and the third allowed participants to adjust the volume
to a comfortable level. The main task began with a familiari-
sation phase. Participants were randomly assigned to hear ei-
ther a 3-min conversational dialogue (dialogue speech) or its
scrambled counterpart (scrambled speech), and were instructed
either to memorise who was speaking (speaker identity), what
was being said (speech content), or to listen carefully (undi-
rected). Following familiarisation, participants completed a
content recall test consisting of 20 yes/no comprehension ques-
tions about whether specific information had been mentioned,
along with two vigilance checks. The subsequent voice recall
test presented short speech clips produced by either target or
foil speakers, and participants indicated whether each voice had
been heard during familiarisation (yes/no). Each speaker con-
tributed eight trials, and eight additional vigilance checks were
embedded. After every trial in both tests, participants rated their
confidence on a 9-point Likert scale. Instructions for the con-
tent and voice recall tests were displayed for 90 s and were im-
mediately followed by the trials to ensure identical learning–
recognition intervals across participants. Trial order was fully
randomised, with vigilance checks inserted at a fixed rate, and

between participants. After the task, participants completed a
debriefing questionnaire describing their understanding of the
task. The experiment lasted about 30 min.
2.4. Data analyses
Analyses focused on voice recognition performance. Partici-
pants whose accuracy on the content recall test fell below .20,
who failed more than one vigilance check in the content test, or
who failed more than two vigilance checks in the voice test were
excluded from all analyses. Trial-level correctness was anal-
ysed using a GLMM with a binomial logit link, implemented
in thelme4package (Version 1.1-35.1) [21] in R. Fixed effects
were familiarisation stimulus (dialogue vs. scrambled), famil-
iarisation focus (identity, content, or undirected), ground truth
(old vs. new speaker), and their full interactions, as well as
test material type (phrase vs. sentence). Random intercepts
were included for participants and items. Random slopes for
ground truth by participant were retained following backward
model selection based on likelihood ratio (LR) tests [22, 23],
implemented using thebuildmerpackage (Version 2.12) [24].
To quantify the strength of evidence for effects of interest, we
also fitted a Bayesian GLMM with the same structure using the
brmspackage (Version 2.22.0) [25], with weakly informative
default priors. Bayes factors (BFs) were computed via model
comparison [26] for each fixed effect.
To characterise the effects of the experimental manipula-
tions on confidence ratings, we fitted an LMM on trial-level cer-
tainty ratings, since such ratings on a 9-point scale approximate
a continuous variable and are commonly analysed using Gaus-
sian models [27]. Fixed effects included familiarisation stim-
ulus, familiarisation focus, ground truth, response correctness,
and their full interaction, as well as test material type. Ran-
dom intercepts were included for participants and items, with
a random slope for ground truth and correctness by participant
retained following backward model selection. A corresponding
Bayesian model was also fitted, with BFs calculated.
Furthermore, SDT metrics were analysed to assess response
bias and sensitivity. Specifically,d ′ quantified listeners’ abil-
ity to discriminate old from new voices by comparing hit and
false-alarm rates, whereascindexed response bias; we addi-
tionally computed a confidence-based sensitivity metric, area
under the curve (AUC), by treating trial-wise confidence rat-
ings as graded evidence: ratings were assigned a positive sign
foroldresponses and a negative sign fornewresponses, and
AUC was calculated as the rank-based probability that signal
trials received stronger evidence than noise trials [28]. Separate
analyses of variance (ANOV As) were fitted to participant-level
d′ andcvalues, with familiarisation stimulus and familiarisation
focus entered as between-subject factors. For each frequentist
ANOV A, we conducted a corresponding Bayesian ANOV A us-
ing default Jeffreys–Zellner–Siow (JZS) priors and computed
BFs relative to the intercept-only model [26].

3.1. Trial-level correctness
Three participants were excluded due to misunderstanding of
the task. The remaining 200 participants achieved a mean ac-
curacy of 0.67 (range 0.63–1.00,SD= 0.17) in the voice re-
call test. GLMMs on trial-level correctness revealed no sig-
nificant main effects: familiarisation stimulus (χ 2(1) = 0.81,
p=.367; BF= 0.21), familiarisation focus (χ 2(2) = 0.79,
p=.673; BF= 0.01), ground truth (χ 2(1) = 2.04,p=.153;
BF= 0.41), or test material type (χ 2(1) = 0.00,p=.953; BF
= 0.19). The two-way interactions familiarisation stimulus×
familiarisation focus (χ 2(2) = 2.14,p=.343; BF= 0.039)
and familiarisation focus×ground truth (χ 2(2) = 3.58,p=
.167; BF= 1.50) were not significant. However, familiarisa-
tion stimulus×ground truth was significant (χ 2(1) = 13.27,
p < .001; BF= 297.41). The three-way interaction did not
reach significance (χ2(2) = 4.41,p=.110; BF= 0.17).
Post-hoc pairwise comparisons with Holm correction clari-

p=.002). In contrast, for new-speaker trials, accuracy was
lower following dialogue than isolated familiarisation (odds ra-

3.3. Participant-level sensitivity and bias
ANOV As on participant-level SDT measures revealed patterns
for response sensitivity and bias. For sensitivity indexed by
d′, there was no significant effect of familiarisation stimulus
(F(1,194) = 0.59,p=.442; BF= 0.20) or familiarisation
focus (F(2,194) = 0.33,p=.722; BF= 0.07), and no inter-
action (F(2,194) = 0.91,p=.402; BF<0.01). In contrast,
response bias (c) showed a significant effect of familiarisation
stimulus (F(1,194) = 13.26,p < .001; BF= 60.07), but no
effect of familiarisation focus (F(2,194) = 1.99,p=.140;
BF= 0.251) and no interaction (F(2,194) = 1.83,p=.163;
BF= 7.06). Confidence-based sensitivity (AUC) showed a
marginal effect of familiarisation stimulus (F(1,194) = 3.33,
p=.070; BF= 0.62), with no effect of familiarisation focus
(F(2,194) = 0.72,p=.490; BF= 0.09) and no interaction
(F(2,194) = 1.74,p=.178; BF= 0.02).
4. Discussion
Using speech materials generated by a state-of-the-art multi-
speaker synthesiser that preserved the identity of real human
reference speakers, the present study examined whether listen-
ers’ ability to learn and recognise voices is shaped by (a) the nat-
uralness of the familiarisation stimulus (dialogue vs. scrambled
speech) and (b) attentional focus during familiarisation (speaker
identity, speech content, or undirected). No significant main ef-
fects of familiarisation focus were observed. However, famil-
iarisation stimulus interacted with the ground truth of the test
items: listeners familiarised with dialogue were more accurate
on old-speaker trials but less accurate on new-speaker trials,
whereas listeners familiarised with scrambled speech showed
lower hit rates but better rejection of new speakers.
Analyses of confidence ratings further clarified the mech-
anisms underlying these behavioural patterns. Based on con-
verging evidence from frequentist and Bayesian analyses (i.e.,
where BFs also supported the effects), confidence differed be-
tween old- and new-speaker trials, and this difference depended
on response correctness and familiarisation condition. When re-
sponses were correct, confidence was higher for old- than new-
speaker trials, whereas when responses were incorrect, confi-
dence was lower for old than new trials. Importantly, this pat-
tern was further modulated by familiarisation stimulus. Fol-
lowing dialogue familiarisation, the difference between old and
new trials was amplified: correct responses to old speakers (rel-
ative to new trials) were associated with higher confidence than
after scrambled familiarisation, whereas incorrect responses to
old speakers were associated with lower confidence. There-
fore, following dialogue familiarisation, listeners appeared to
rely more strongly on familiarity-based signals when judging
speaker identity. This resulted in stronger subjective evidence
for previously encountered voices, increasing confidence for
correct identifications of old speakers.
Importantly, SDT analyses confirmed that these pattern pri-
marily reflects a shift in response bias rather than a change in
overall perceptual sensitivity for recognising voices. Sensitivity
(d′) did not differ as a function of familiarisation stimulus or fo-
cus, indicating that the ability to correctly recognise voices was
comparable across conditions. By contrast, response bias (c)
differed reliably between familiarisation stimulus conditions,
with dialogue familiarisation producing a more liberal bias. In-
terestingly, confidence-based sensitivity (AUC), which quanti-
fies graded evidence from confidence ratings [28], showed a
marginal effect of familiarisation stimulus. Future studies with
larger samples may clarify whether conversational exposure in-
deed yields changes in confidence-based sensitivity.

iarisation increasedoldresponses is that coherent conversa-
tional structure promotes richer or more integrated voice repre-
sentations. Dialogue provides discourse-level coherence, which
may facilitate the integration of identity cues across utterances.
