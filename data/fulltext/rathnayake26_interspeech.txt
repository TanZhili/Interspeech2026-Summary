Abstract
This paper introduces P ¯a-Kakare, the first emotional speech
database for te reo M ¯aori, the Indigenous language of New
Zealand. Unlike existing emotional speech resources that rely
on presumed universal emotions, this corpus uses 16 categories
identified through prior community-based research, ensuring
cultural relevance. The database contains 3,840 high-quality
recordings from four professional M ¯aori actors, with 15 sen-
tences per emotion, designed to capture both neutral-context
and emotion-salient content. We describe emotion selection,
corpus design, recording procedures, and database validation.
A preliminary acoustic analysis shows systematic differences
in fundamental frequency, intensity, and speech rate across the
emotion categories. As one of the first emotional speech cor-
pora for an Indigenous Pacific language, P¯a-Kakare supports the
analysis of M¯aori emotional expression and encourages further
research on emotional speech in under-represented languages.

1. Introduction
Speech Emotion Recognition (SER), which involves identify-
ing emotions from speech, is a popular research area aimed at
making human-computer interaction more naturalistic. Such re-
search mostly focuses on a few well-resourced languages such
as English, French, German, and Mandarin [1, 2, 3], leaving In-
digenous and low-resource languages under-represented. Te reo
M¯aori (the M¯aori language; henceforth M ¯aori), the Indigenous
language of New Zealand, is one such example and currently
lacks an annotated emotional speech database to support the
development of emotion-related technologies. We address this
gap by presenting the first M ¯aori emotional speech database,
developed through a culturally informed, community-oriented
approach, building on previous work on M¯aori emotions [4].
M¯aori is the only official Indigenous language of New
Zealand. According to the 2023 New Zealand Census, 4.3%
of the population (just over 200,000) report being able to con-
verse in M ¯aori, and only 18.6% of M ¯aori can speak in te reo
M¯aori [5]. The significant decline is mainly due to historical
colonial practices that discouraged the use of M ¯aori and pro-
moted English. Since the 1970s, however, M¯aori has undergone
extensive revitalisation efforts [6]. As part of these initiatives,
language resources and technology development have become
increasingly important. Recent efforts focus on areas such as
pronunciation aids [7], text-to-speech systems [8, 9], language
detection tools [10], automatic speech recognition, and part-of-
speech tagging [11, 12, 13]. However, understanding the nature

of emotion production in M ¯aori and, subsequently, developing
emotion-related technologies has yet to be explored.
A recent review [14] highlights three key challenges in SER
research for Indigenous and low-resource languages. Firstly,
there is a lack of language resources to support the development
of emotion-related technologies. Secondly, emotional speech
corpora often rely on universal emotion categories, despite on-
going debate about their cross-cultural validity and the need
for community-defined categories. Thirdly, limited Indigenous
community involvement in technology development can result
in resources that do not align with cultural values or emo-
tional frameworks. To address these challenges, we designed
our M¯aori emotional speech database using culturally grounded
methodology. We include M ¯aori-defined emotion categories
(see Section 2.1) and incorporate M ¯aori involvement through-
out the research process, including active participation and ad-
herence to cultural protocols. Notably, half of our research team
identifies as M¯aori, ensuring cultural insight at every stage.
This study is also the first to examine the acoustic prop-
erties of emotional expression in te reo M ¯aori. Previous work
has shown that M¯aori and New Zealand Europeans exhibit dif-
ferent patterns of emotional reactions to social situations [15],
suggesting that the acoustic expression of emotion may also dif-
fer from patterns reported in other languages. To address this,
we analyse how emotions are acoustically expressed in M ¯aori
speech. Fundamental frequency, speech rate, and intensity are
prominent acoustic features for differentiating emotions across
languages [16, 17]. We investigate whether similar acoustic pat-
terns emerge in M¯aori speech or whether distinct M¯aori-specific
patterns are observed. Taken together, this paper reports two
key contributions: (1) the development of the first culturally
relevant emotional speech database for M¯aori, and (2) a prelim-
inary acoustic analysis of emotions in M¯aori speech.
2. Corpus development
2.1. Emotion selection
The first step in developing an emotional speech database is de-
termining which emotions to include. Most existing studies,
regardless of language, adopt a similar set of categories such
as happiness, sadness, anger, fear, disgust, and surprise [14].
Although many studies do not explicitly justify their category
choices, these selections are typically based on Ekman’s dis-
crete emotion model [18], which is assumed to be universal.
The universality of these emotion categories has been ques-
tioned, as experiments designed to verify it often lack cultural
appropriateness and community involvement [19, 20, 21]. Sev-
eral studies argue that emotion categorisation can be shaped by
culture. For M ¯aori, emotional expressions are deeply embed-
ded within cultural practices and frameworks [22]. Previous

Rathnayake et al. [4] conducted a rigorous community-
oriented analysis and identified 16 emotion categories in M¯aori
speech that are required for meaningful emotion analysis. These
categories are: ngenge (tired), haumaru (safe), aroha (love),
huakore (hopeless), p ¯ouri (sad), harikoa (happy), manawanui
(serious), pai (good, well), kaik ¯a (impatient), whai whakaaro
(curious), whakarihariha (disgusted), whakah ¯ıh¯ı (proud), h ¯oh¯a
(annoyed), h¯ıkaka (excited), ohorere (surprised), ¯awangawanga
(anxious). The English translations for the M ¯aori terms given
here are close equivalents but do not capture the full cultural
or emotional nuances. These emotion categories were derived
from a questionnaire that used M¯aori community media as elic-
itation stimuli, followed by focus groups with M ¯aori speakers
to refine and consolidate the categories. While some categories
align with Ekman’s basic emotions (e.g., harikoa, p¯ouri), others
are not widely represented in current emotion literature, yet they
are significant to M ¯aori (e.g., kaik ¯a, aroha). We adopted these
categories to develop our database, ensuring cultural relevance.
2.2. Corpus design
We developed an acted emotional speech database rather than a
natural corpus because acted recordings provide greater control
over recording conditions. Acted speech enables high-quality
audio captured in a controlled environment, one emotion at a
time, without speaker overlap, simplifying annotation and im-
proving consistency. Although induced emotional speech cor-
pora, where participants are placed in contextual scenarios, can
produce more naturalistic emotional speech, we avoided these
methods due to the ethical concerns that can arise when attempt-
ing to provoke strong emotional states. Since this is the first at-
tempt to create an emotional speech corpus for M ¯aori, starting
with an acted corpus is a practical and effective approach.
2.3. Speakers
We recorded four professional M¯aori voice actors (2 male and 2
female). The actors were 27–40 years old. Professional actors
were selected because they are extensively trained in emotional
expression, which helps them better simulate emotions and fa-
cilitates their easy identification [2]. All actors were compen-
sated at their standard hourly rates.
2.4. Script design
Each emotion category had 15 sentences. Five of the sen-
tences differed across the 16 emotion categories and contained
emotion-specific context. The other 10 sentences were neutral
in context, meaning they contained no emotionally salient terms
and were consistent across all emotion categories. We included
both types to enable future studies on the role of semantic influ-
ence in emotional expression. For neutral sentences, we added
a leading phrase, as previous work has shown that actors find
it difficult to reach an emotional state without emotion-salient
words [17]. These leading phrases were excluded from our
analysis. Table 1 provides example sentences for a few emo-
tions with the two types of sentences we used. On average, the

2.5. Recording flow
Recording sessions began with a karakia (M¯aori cultural prayer)
to respect cultural protocols and establish a culturally inclusive
environment, allowing the actors to portray the emotions more
effectively. The instructions for the actors were also given in
M¯aori. Actors were instructed to portray emotions naturally,
without exaggeration. To minimise hand gestures and body
movements, they were advised to use speech as the primary
vehicle for expressing emotions. They were also advised to
maintain a consistent posture and distance from the microphone
(approximately 15 cm) throughout the session. The first author
monitored the session to ensure technical quality, and the third
author, a M ¯aori researcher, was present throughout to clarify
any doubts regarding the emotions or the script. A warm-up
task was performed using three emotions at different intensity
levels: pai, h ¯oh¯a, and p ¯ouri, which also helped calibrate the
software and check for clipping or other technical issues.
In the main recording, a set of related images (visual aids)
was shown to help actors immerse themselves in the target emo-
tion, following methods used in prior work [17, 25]. Sentences
were then presented one by one, and actors were asked to de-
liver each using the specified emotion. At least two takes were
recorded per sentence, with additional takes made if needed.
The entire procedure was repeated on a separate day to account
for potential psychophysiological variations in speakers arising
from unforeseen prior events [17]. Overall, the corpus contains
3,840 utterances (16 emotions × 15 sentences × 2 takes × 2 days
× 4 actors). It is 3 h 45 min long, with utterance durations rang-
ing from 1.16 to 9.68 seconds, and has a file size of 3.31 GB.
2.6. Technical details
Recordings were conducted in the Faculty of Arts and Educa-
tion Recording Studio, University of Auckland. A talk-back
device connected to earphones was used to communicate with
the actor. Actors were recorded using a SHURE SM7B Dy-
namic Microphone and a Sony HXR-NX5P Camera. Audio was
recorded using Pro Tools software and saved with a 44.1 kHz
sample rate, 16-bit PCM encoding, mono channel and W A V
format. Recordings were manually segmented into sentences
in Adobe Audition and Premiere Pro.

2.7. Accessibility
M¯aori values are central to our research process, particularly
in maintaining data sovereignty, which refers to managing
data and information in accordance with the laws, practices,
and customs of the nation or community where it is located
[26]. In line with the principle of kaitiakitanga (guardianship),
we consider ourselves guardians, not owners of the recorded
data. Accordingly, the database will be governed by a Kaiti-
akitanga license [27], ensuring culturally appropriate use and
protection of the resource. The dataset will include the la-
belled audio recordings, transcripts, emotion annotations, per-
ception test data, and extracted acoustic features. Prospec-
tive users must apply to the research team. Access is granted
only to those who demonstrate an understanding of the cul-
tural significance of the data, with all decisions made in con-
sultation with M ¯aori experts on the team. More details are
available at:https://speechresearch.auckland.
ac.nz/maori-emotions.
3. Corpus validation
A perception test was conducted to evaluate whether the actors
produced the intended emotions in a way that listeners could re-
liably identify. Six test versions were created, each containing
48 sentences, for a total of 288 sentences evaluated (7.5% of the
corpus). Sentences were selected to represent all actors and to
ensure a balanced distribution of emotions. Each sentence was
rated by three participants, and 18 participants took part in to-
tal. The test was conducted online via Qualtrics [28], allowing
remote participation. Participants were required to be famil-
iar with M ¯aori and at least 18 years old. To reduce the cogni-
tive load of identifying all 16 emotions simultaneously, we split
them into two subsets, each containing a mix of emotions across
different arousal levels and valence (positive and negative), as
well as those corresponding to presumed basic emotions. Only
one subset was presented to each participant, and an equal num-
ber of participants rated each subset. This study was approved
by the University of Auckland Ethics Committee (Ref. 25668).
The perception test yielded an overall recognition accuracy
of 36.15% for the eight-class classification task (with 38.89%
and 33.41% for the two subsets), well above the 12.5% chance
level. Recognition accuracy varied by actor: 51% and 40%
for the female actors, and 30% and 26% for the male actors.
While above chance, these rates remain modest, consistent with
prior findings that larger emotion sets, particularly those involv-
ing finer-grained categories beyond the six basic emotions, are
harder for listeners to discriminate [29].

4. Preliminary acoustic feature analysis
4.1. Feature extraction
We analysed three acoustic features to investigate how emo-
tions are expressed in M¯aori: fundamental frequency, mean in-
tensity, and speech rate. The fundamental frequency (f0), the
primary acoustic correlate of perceived pitch for each utter-
ance, was extracted using Praat’s Autocorrelation algorithm via
Python Parselmouth library [31]. Mean intensity, representing
the perceived loudness of the vocalisation for each utterance,
was also computed with Parselmouth. Speech rate was calcu-
lated in morae per second, as M ¯aori is generally described as
a mora-timed language [32]. Although the role of the mora in
rhythm remains unclear [33], for this preliminary analysis, we
adopted mora-based measurements because the mora provides
a linguistically meaningful unit for examining M ¯aori speech.
A mora is defined as a short vowel, optionally preceded by a
consonant. Morae counts were determined manually for each
sentence and divided by the utterance duration.
Many emotion studies use the label ‘neutral’ as a baseline,
but perception tests show that it produces mixed perceptual re-
sponses and is often interpreted as slightly negative [34]. This
may occur because actors are unsure how to express true emo-
tional neutrality [34]. As an alternative, the RA VDESS dataset
uses the emotion label ‘calm’ as its baseline, which is percep-
tually similar to neutral but tends to have a slightly positive

4.2. Results and discussion
Figures 2, 3, and 4 present the distributions of mean f0, in-
tensity and speech rate across the 16 emotion categories. The
half-violin plots for f0 and intensity show male and female dis-
tributions separately, with wider areas indicating higher sample
density and circular markers denoting gender-specific means.
Speech rate values are presented as mean estimates with associ-
ated confidence intervals. Emotions in all figures are ordered by
arousal, as detailed in the approximate mapping in Section 3.
In figures 2 and 3, we observe patterns consistent with prior
work on arousal-related prosody [16, 29]. High-arousal emo-
tions such as h ¯ıkaka (excited) and ohorere (surprised) show
elevated mean f0 and intensity, whereas lower-arousal emo-
tions like haumaru (safe) and ngenge (tired) show lower values.
Kaik¯a (impatient) shows comparatively higher f0 and inten-
sity values, suggesting that M¯aori speakers may express it with
higher arousal than expected. Although intensity clearly differ-
entiates the emotions, it also shows substantial within-emotion
variability. This variability likely reflects differences in how
strongly actors expressed emotions despite instructions not to
exaggerate, as well as small shifts in their distance from the
microphone. Although we attempted to control this, actors nat-
urally moved into comfortable postures over time.
