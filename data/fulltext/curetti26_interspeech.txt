Towards an understanding of prosodic cue weighting for turn-end classification 
in older adults with varying hearing abilities 
Lorenza Zaira Curetti1, Hae-Sung Jeon2, Lauren V. Hadley1 
1 Hearing Sciences - Scottish Section, School of Medicine, University of Nottingham, Glasgow, UK 
2 School of Psychology and Humanities, University of Lancashire, Preston, UK 
zaira.curetti@nottingham.ac.uk, HJeon1@lancashire.ac.uk, lauren.hadley1@nottingham.ac.uk 
 
Abstract 
In conversation, interlocutors use prosody to identify  when a 
partner’s utterance is complete. Sensorineural hearing loss may 
limit access to relevant prosodic cues or alter their weighting. 
This exploratory study examined if older listeners with hearing 
loss differ from those with typical hearing in their use of 
temporal, intensity, and pitch -related information to judge 
utterance completion. Participants classified declarative 
questions as finished or continuing based solely on prosody.  
The acoustic properties of the utterances were quantified  in 
terms of boundary strength (final  word duration and intensity) 
and pitch movement (duration and excursion of final rise). Both 
listener groups classified utterances above chance. However, to 
identify finished utterances, listeners with typical hearing relied 
on duration and intensity while those with hearing loss relied 
on pitch movement. We suggest that hearing loss reshapes the 
prosodic dimensions listeners rely on for turn-end perception. 
Index Terms: turn-end prediction, prosody, hearing loss 
1. Introduction 
Smooth conversational turn-taking relies on a listener’s ability 
to predict when a speaker’s turn will end [1]. This prediction is 
largely mediated by the perception of phrase boundaries, where 
prosodic cues signal whether an utterance is complete or 
projects further talk  [2]. More variable turn taking timing for 
interlocutors with hearing loss could indicate a reduced ability 
to interpret these cues [3]. In the present study, we therefore 
examine how older adults with and without hearing loss use 
prosodic cues to determine whether an utterance has finished. 
Prosodically, phrase boundaries are marked by a multitude 
of acoustic cues, including pitch movements (e.g., rises at the 
end of declarative questions), final lengthening, and intensity 
modulation [4], [5], [6]. In English, strong intonational phrase 
(IP) boundaries typically show greater phrase-final lengthening 
and weakening (i.e., increased duration and reduced intensity), 
than weaker intermediate phrase (ip) boundaries  [4], [5], [6], 
[7]. Pitch movements further support such boundary distinction. 
Contour shape and excursion are primary cues to boundary 
contrasts in English [8], with utterances approaching an end  
often exhibit ing final pitch movements that differ 
systematically from those  that are continuing  (e.g., longer or 
larger rises for complete declarative questions). Together, such 
cues contribute to listeners’ judgments of completion  in 
perceptual tasks , helping them disambiguate turn ends from 
continuations, e.g., [1]. 
Hearing loss may alter the perception and thus use of 
various prosodic cues , with reduced spectral resolution and 
altered dynamic range affect ing access to both pitch and 
intensity information [9]. Indeed hearing loss has  been linked 
to reduced prosodic sensitivity for emotion recognition [10], as 
well as to intonational and stress contrasts [11]. Yet  h earing 
loss does not affect all acoustic dimensions equally: while 
reduced spectral resolution and impaired temporal fine structure 
processing can limit sensitivity to pitch, sensitivity to slower 
temporal-envelope cues is often relatively preserved  [9], [12]. 
Furthermore, p erceptual decisions about turn completion can 
emerge from a variety of  acoustic dimensions , and h ow 
listeners weight these  dimensions depends on their perceptual 
reliability. For example, when a cue is uninformative or highly 
variable, listeners may dynamically reweight their relative 
contribution in favour of cues that are the most consistent [13], 
[14]. Evidence suggests that listeners with hearing loss and 
cochlear implant users  can shift cue -weighting strategies to 
facilitate speech perception [15], [16], [17] , including relying 
more on temporal -envelope information and down -weighting 
fine pitch variation. Thus, hearing loss may not simply reduce  
the ability to use prosodic information; it may alter the acoustic 
dimensions listeners use to make perceptual judgments. 
Turn-taking offers a particularly informative context for 
examining potential shifts in cue weighting, as predicting turn 
completion requires rapid integration of multiple prosodic 
dimensions in real time.  Although conversational timing 
differences have been documented in individuals with hearing 
loss [3], the perceptual basis of these differences remains 
unclear. If turn -ends can be signalled through temporal, 
amplitude, and pitch-related information, then limited access to 
any such cues may either lead to poorer identification of turn 
ends, or to changes in how turn ends are identified. 
In this exploratory study, we address this gap by directly 
testing how hearing ability affects the use of different prosodic 
cues for classifying turn ends . Listeners with differing hearing 
abilities judged whether short utterances were complete or were 
going to continue . The acoustic properties of the stimuli were 
parameterised along dimensions associated with utterance-final 
boundary strength (final word duration and intensity), and pitch 
movement (duration and excursion of the final pitch rise) . By 
linking perceptual classifications to these acoustic dimensions, 
we aimed to assess not only whether classification behaviour 
differs across groups, but also whether any differences reflect 
shifts in the relative weighting of prosodic cues. 
2. Methods 
2.1. Participants 
Participants were recruited  online via Prolific (prolific.co). A 
total of 112 met the inclusion criteria ( aged 55–75, English 
nationality, native English speaker, resident in England ), and 
passed data -quality screening  (see Section 2.3). Of these, 55 
had typical hearing (TH; 2 7 male; mean age 61.3 yrs) and 57 
had hearing loss ( HL; 32 male; mean age 60.5 yrs ). The HL 
group included 14 bilateral hearing aid users (average 9.1 yrs of 
use; range 1–35 yrs). Participants completed the study unaided. 
2.2. Stimuli 
The materials comprised 40 items, each realised in two prosodic 
versions (finished and continuing), yielding 80 recorded stimuli 
in total. All utterances were declarative questions recorded by a 
male native speaker of Standard Southern British English, who 
had expertise in intonational analysis and read the questions at  
a comfortable speaking rate.

Each item contained a one - to three -syllable target word  
stressed on the first syllable. In the finished stimuli the target 
word occurred in utterance -final position (e.g., She plays 
football?), whereas in the continuing stimuli it  occurred at a 
continuation point (e.g., She plays football for England?). The 
continuing stimuli were truncated after the target word to make 
them semantically identical to the finished ones while 
preserving their original prosodic properties. Stimulus versions 
were designed to contrast two prosodic properties: (1) the 
strength of the prosodic boundary at the right edge of the target 
word, and (2) the trajectory of pitch movement across the target 
word. For finished items, the target word was aligned with the 
right edge of an IP, whereas in continuing items it was aligned 
with the right edge of an ip and showed no substantial final 
lengthening or pause. 
In both conditions, the speaker kept the intonation of the 
words preceding the target word as consistent as possible, 
maintaining a gently rising intonation (‘She plays’ in Fig. 1). or 
all stimuli, t he stressed syllable of the target word was pitch 
accented, carrying a fall-rise f0 contour; the contour shape was 
held constant within each finished-continuing pair. In the 
finished versions, the accent was followed by a steeper and 
more immediate rise (end of ‘football’ in Fig. 1A). In the 
continuing versions, by contrast, this accent was followed by a 
rise forming a plateau or gentle upward trajectory, leading into 
the utterance-final rise (‘for England’ in Fig. 1B). The speaker 
aimed to produce consistent prosodic differences between the 
finished-continuing stimuli across all pairs.  All stimuli were  
loudness-normalised to −23 Loudness Units Relative to Full 
Scale (per [18] ), and gated with 5 ms onsets and offsets  to 
remove edge transients.

2.3. Procedure 
The study was  part of a larger online study comprising two 
sessions (~20 min each). This data was collected in the first 
session. Participants were asked to wear headphones and set 
volume to a comfortable level at the start of the experiment. 
2.3.1. Digit Triple Test (DTT) 
Hearing was assessed using an online DTT [19], in which 
listeners listen to and then recall  quasi-random digit triplets 
presented in speech -shaped noise. Digits ranged from zero  
(spoken as ‘oh’) to nine, excluding the disyllabic ‘seven’. There 
were 120 possible triplets, selected at random. The signal -to-
noise ratio (SNR)  was adapted towards an individual’s 
threshold following a one -up/one-down procedure across 25 
trials, starting at −16 dB and ranging  from −25 to −6 dB. 
Participants entered their responses using an on-screen keypad. 
The DTT score was defined as the mean SNR over trials 7-25. 
Participants were classified as TH or HL based on a DTT 
score cutoff of −17.1, corresponding approximately to a 20 dB 
high-frequency pure-tone loss [19]. The median DTT score for 
the whole sample was −16.9 dB (range −24.3 to −6 dB), 
confirming balanced groups despite variation in listening setup. 
2.3.2. Classification task 
The classification task comprised three practice trials followed 
by 44 experimental trials. Of these, 40 were main trials, and 
four were catch trials that served as audibility/attention checks. 
All trials required participants  to press a Start button to 
begin stimulus presentation. The main trials included 20 
finished and 20 continuing stimuli. Stimulus version (finished/ 
continuing) was counterbalanced such that each participant 
heard only one version of each target word . Trial order was 
randomised, with the constraint that  no more than three 
finished/continuing stimuli could occur consecutively. After 
stimulus playback, participants had to judge whether the talker 
sounded like “they are going to continue talking (you're only 
hearing the first part of a longer sentence that has been cut), or 
whether they are finished” . They indicated their choice by 
selecting: ‘Talker will continue’ or ‘Talker is finished’ . The 
stimuli could be replayed without limit. 
Catch trials occurred every seven main trials, and differed 
only by including a 200 ms 1 kHz tone, for which participants 
were instructed to press a 'beep' button. The same catch stimuli 
were used for all participants, with version ( 2 finished, 2  
continuing) and order randomi sed. Only participants that 
identified three out of the four catch trials were retained. 
2.3.3. Prosodic measures 
Four acoustic measures were extracted from each stimulus to 
capture the key prosodic differences between finished and 
continuing stimuli: 
• Ttw: the duration of the target word (ms). 
• dBtw: the mean intensity of the target word (dB). 
• Trise: the time from the onset of the final upward  pitch 
movement to its maximum (ms). 
• STrise: the excursion in Trise (semitones ST). 
Ttw and dBtw were selected as acoustic correlates of 
boundary strength at the right edge of the target word  [4], [5]. 
Since intonational movement can extend beyond  lexical word 
boundaries, the target word's edges were adjusted where 
necessary to ensure the relevant pitch contour was fully 
included. Ttw and dBtw were then extracted from these adjusted 
intervals using ProsodyPro in Praat [20], [21]. To characterise 
pitch movement, we used Trise (the duration of the utterance’s 
final rise) and STrise (its pitch excursion).

2.3.4. Statistical analysis 
Performance was evaluated separately at the  participant and 
item levels. First, sensitivity and response bias were estimated 
separately for TH and HL using the dprime function of the 
psycho package in R v4.5.2 [22], [23] . Because the task 
required listeners to detect prosodic boundary cues, finished 
stimuli (with stronger boundaries) were treated as signal and 
continuing stimuli as noise. Group differences were assessed 
using Mann -Whitney U tests, and performance relative to 
chance using Wilcoxon signed -rank tests against zero, 
implemented with the wilcox.test function. 
Second, to assess which prosodic measures predicted 
listeners’ performance, we fitted rank-based regression models 
using the Rfit package [24], separately for each listener group 
(TH, HL) and stimulus version (finished, continuing). Cue use 
within each listener group was assessed separately due to 
limited power for higher-order interaction terms. Finished and 
continuing stimuli were analysed separately as they differ 
systematically in their prosodic structures, and modelling them 
together would risk masking condition-specific effects. 
The full model included predictors from both prosodic 
dimensions: accuracy ~ dBtw × Ttw + Trise × STrise. Accuracy 
was defined as the proportion of correct responses per item, and 
all prosodic measures were z-transformed prior to modelling.  
Interactions were included to account for non -additive effects 
within each prosodic dimension.  Although some predictors 
were moderately correlated (see Table 1), none exceeded 
conventional thresholds for problematic multicollinearity (ρ > 
0.8), and variance inflation factors from equivalent linear 
models were all below 1.8 . All predictors were therefore 
retained in the initial models. 
Model selection proceeded via backward elimination using 
drop-in-dispersion tests using the drop.test function. The final 
models therefore retained only predictors whose removal 
significantly reduced model fit.  
Where significant interactions were found, simple slopes 
analyses were conducted following  [25]. This involved re -
centring one predictor at ±1 standard deviation from the mean. 
The model was then re-estimated to evaluate the effect of the 
primary predictor at lower and higher levels of the interacting 
variable. Statistical significance was evaluated at α = 0.05. 
3. Results 
3.1.1. Sensitivity and response bias 
Sensitivity did not significantly differ between groups (p = 
0.12), and both groups performed above chance (TH: d' = 0.28, 
p < 0.001; HL: d’ = 0.10, p = 0.03), with mean accuracy levels 
of 61% and 54%, respectively. 
Response bias also did not differ between groups (p = 0.74). 
However, both groups showed a small but significant tendency 
to identify utterances as finished (TH: C = −0.08, p = 0.016; 
HL: C = −0.09, p = 0.015). 
3.1.2. Item-level predictors 
For continuing stimuli, no predictors reached significance in 
either group (all p ≥ 0.32), and overall model fit was weak (TH: 
R² = 0.07; HL: R² = 0.09), indicating that the prosodic measures 
did not reliably predict responses in this condition.  
By contrast, both  group’s finished models resulted in 
moderate fit (TH: R² = 0.28, p = 0.007; HL: R² = 0.24, p = 
0.005), but incorporated different prosodic cues (see Table 2).

4. Discussion 
This study examined how older adult listeners with differing 
hearing abilities  classify utterances as finished or continuing 
based solely on prosodic information. Listeners heard naturally 
produced declarative questions  ending at a n IP  boundary 
(finished), or semantically identical utterances truncated at an 
ip boundary (continuing). We assessed overall sensitivity (d’) 
to acoustic markers of (1) boundary strength at the right edge of 
the utterance-final target word (the target word’s duration Ttw 
and intensity dBtw), and (2) the trajectory of pitch movement 
across the target word (the final rise ’s duration Trise and pitch 
excursion STrise). We then examined how these cues were 
associated with classification accuracy within each group. 
Both groups showed above -chance sensitivity, indicating 
that prosodic cues alone were sufficient to support 
classification. Additionally, both groups showed a slight bias 
towards classifying items as finished. Given that IP boundaries 
are typically marked by stronger and more consistent acoustic 
cues than ip boundaries [6], [7], this bias is not surprising in a 
decontextualized listening task . In the absence of lexical, 
syntactic, or pragmatic context, listeners may be more inclined 
to treat a clearly marked prosodic contour as complete (since it 
did indeed end at that point) . Under such conditions, listeners 
may primarily be scanning for completion cues, rather than cues 
specific to continuation (which are more subtle) . This lack of 
use of a specific ‘completion template’ may  also explain why 
no single acoustic measure consistently predicted continuing 
responses. Crucially, however, despite comparable sensitivity 
and bias  between groups , they differed in the acoustic 
dimensions predicting their responses. This suggests that rather 
than hearing loss leading to a general loss of sensitivity to 
prosodic information, it alters the prosodic cues listeners use. 
For TH listeners, dBtw showed a significant negative main 
effect, with lower intensity values increasing the likelihood of 
correctly identifying  finished items. This is consistent with 
descriptions of phrase -final weakening, where intensity often 
trails off  towards the end of complete utterances [6]. 
Interestingly, however, intensity did not operate independently 
of duration. The significant dBtw × Ttw interaction indicates that 
the effect of intensity was modulated by  the duration  of the 
target word. For longer target-word durations (solid line in Fig. 
2), the effect of intensity was absent. However, for shorter 
target-word durations (dashed line in Fig. 2), increased intensity 
was associated with a substantial drop in finished accuracy. 
One plausible interpretation is that listeners treated duration 
and intensity as complementary cues to boundary strength.  
Final lengthening is among the most robust  correlates of 
prosodic boundaries [4], [5] . Thus,  substantial word -final 
lengthening may have served as a sufficiently strong cue to 
completion, reducing the need to rely on intensity. A similar 
interdependence has been reported in prominence perception, 
where duration and intensity jointly contribute to stress 
judgments, but intensity rarely functions as a reliable cue on its 
own [26]. In contrast, it was when the target words were shorter 
that listeners relied more strongly on intensity, with lower 
intensity values  (consistent with a phrase -final ‘trailing off ’) 
supporting finished judgments. This pattern aligns with models 
of speech perception in which multiple acoustic dimensions are 
integrated probabilistically, and listeners increase reliance on 
secondary cues when primary cues are weak or ambiguous [13], 
[14]. This interpretation may also explain why pitch -rise 
measures did not significantly predict TH listeners’ responses , 
since in other prosodic contexts  (e.g., statements) a larger or 
sharper rise can reflect a continuation cue [7], [8] . The 
ambiguity of this cue may thus have led to down-weighting.  
Unlike TH listeners, HL listeners did not show sensitivity 
to intensity or duration differences. While the former may have 
been expected, the latter was more surprising. Reduced 
dynamic range and altered loudness growth associated with 
sensorineural hearing loss may diminish the perceptual salience 
of amplitude -based cues  [9], leading to  down-weighting of 
intensity cues in favour of alternative dimensions. However, the 
absence of a durational effect was unexpected  given that 
sensitivity to slower temporal -envelope cues (such as overall 
word duration ) is often relatively preserved in sensorineural 
hearing loss  [9]. A speculative explanation for such lack of 
reliance on these cues could be that both word lengthening and 
phrase-final weakening require an internalised expectation 
against which the heard input can be compared  (i.e., a 
prediction of how long or loud the word should have been). This 
is a relatively complex cognitive process, as a word can only be
