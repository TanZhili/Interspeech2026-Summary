Beyond Mimicry: Constrained Exploration with GRPO for Joint Multi-Talker
ASR and Diarization under Unknown Speaker Counts
Yunrui Cai1, Dingdong Wang1, Lingwei Meng1, Xixin Wu1, Zhiyong Wu1,2, Helen Meng1,∗∗
1 The Chinese University of Hong Kong, Hong Kong SAR, China
2 Shenzhen International Graduate School, Tsinghua University, Shenzhen, China
{yrcai,ddwang,lmeng,wuxx,zywu,hmmeng}@se.cuhk.edu.hk
Abstract
Joint multi-talker ASR with speaker attribution and timestamps
is particularly challenging when the speaker count is unknown
and the output must follow a strict structure. We observe
that Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT)-only alignment of Speech-
LLMs is brittle under dense overlaps, often leading to burst
hallucinations and malformed speaker/timestamp tags. We
propose a unified two-stage generative framework: (i) CoT-
augmented SFT that infers the global speaker count before tran-
scription, and (ii) GRPO-based constrained exploration guided
by a Multi-dimensional Constraint-Aware Reward (MCAR)
that directly optimizes permutation-invariant accuracy while
enforcing counting, temporal, and structural constraints. On
Libri3Mix and a Dynamic-Mix (2+3) set (speaker count un-
known at test time), our model achieves 14.52%/9.24% cpWER
and 1.95%/1.12% WDER, respectively, with GRPO yielding a
35%/54% relative cpWER reduction over SFT+CoT.
Index Terms: Speech-LLM, Multi-Talker Speech Recognition,
Speaker Diarization, Reinforcement Learning
1. Introduction
The recent advancements in Speech Large Language Mod-
els (SpeechLLMs), such as Qwen-Audio [1], SALMONN [2],
and Kimi-Audio [3], have demonstrated unprecedented zero-
shot capabilities in single-speaker automatic speech recogni-
tion (ASR) and general audio understanding. However, the
“cocktail party problem” [4], characterized by overlapping
speech from multiple talkers, remains a fundamental challenge.
Crucially, resolving this acoustic interference requires tack-
ling the joint task of multi-talker ASR and speaker diariza-
tion—simultaneously decipheringwhatwas said,whosaid it,
andwhenit occurred.
To explicitly model this joint task, traditional multi-talker
systems often rely on complex, cascaded pipelines involv-
ing speech separation, single-talker ASR, and clustering-based
speaker diarization [5], which are highly susceptible to error
propagation. To alleviate this, recent end-to-end models uti-
lizing Permutation Invariant Training (PIT) [6] or Serialized
Output Training (SOT) [7] have significantly simplified the
modeling pipeline. Specifically, highly optimized architectures
such as the SOT-Conformer [8], Whisper-Sidecar [9] and UME
[10] have pushed the boundaries of these paradigms, achiev-
ing remarkable transcription and diarization accuracy on over-
lapping speech. While these specialized models excel within
their designated frameworks, extending the powerful, reason-
ing and generative capabilities of foundational SpeechLLMs to
this complex scenario remains largely unexplored.

Fundamentally, off-the-shelf SpeechLLMs fail on overlap-
ping speech due to severe acoustic interference. Recent pio-
neering efforts (e.g., MT-LLM [11], CMT-LLM [12], JEDIS-
LLM [13]) mitigate this by splicing specialized multi-talker au-
dio front-ends into the LLM, typically aligning the modalities
via parameter-efficient techniques like Low-Rank Adaptation
(LoRA) [14]. To preserve end-to-end elegance, a natural al-
ternative is direct Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) to sequential-
ize overlapping signals via text-driven supervision. However,
SFT exhibits critical flaws in dynamic scenarios with unknown
speaker counts, and suffers from severe text hallucination [15]
and illogical temporal boundaries [16]. These failures are diffi-
cult to fix by further SFT: teacher forcing never trains the model
on its own malformed generations, while the key objectives are
sequence-level and non-differentiable. We therefore adopt re-
inforcement learning to perform constrained, on-policy explo-
ration over complete hypotheses and directly optimize these
task-level constraints.
To break this bottleneck, we propose a parameter- and data-
efficient two-stage framework that aligns SpeechLLMs with the
physical realities of overlapping speech 1. First we construct
a carefully curated, fine-grained SFT dataset. Crucially, we
introduce a Chain-of-Thought (CoT) reasoning prefix into the
training target, prompting the model to explicitly estimate the
speaker count before transcribing. Stage 2 introduces Group
Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) [17] to the multi-talker
domain, which has recently been explored in simple ASR [18].
Unlike data-hungry SFT, this phase relies on self-generated roll-
outs and active exploration. We guide this exploration with
a multi-dimensional reward mechanism that directly optimizes
Permutation-Invariant WER, rewards CoT-accuracy, enforces
structural logic, and penalizes hallucinations. This GRPO
alignment effectively shifts the learning paradigm from passive
mimicry to constrained exploration, enabling acoustic scene de-
duction with limited overhead.
Our main contributions are summarized as follows:
• We are the first to successfully adapt and optimize
SpeechLLMs for complex multi-talker scenarios—including
2-speaker, 3-speaker, and challenging dynamic mix-
tures—using a combined SFT and GRPO training paradigm.
• We unify Multi-talker ASR and Speaker Diarization into a
single end-to-end SpeechLLM reasoning process. Empow-
ered by a fine-grained reward engine, we effectively miti-
gate the severe hallucination and timestamp degradation is-
sues prevalent in pure SFT models.
• Experimental results demonstrate that our approach signifi-
cantly expands the dense-overlap acoustic comprehension ca-
pabilities of SpeechLLMs.

2. Methodology
To tackle the complexities of dynamic multi-talker scenarios,
we propose a two-stage adaptation and exploration framework.
We first formulate the joint ASR and diarization task via a
CoT reasoning mechanism, and subsequently adapt the Speech-
LLM through SFT, followed by GRPO guided by a Multi-
dimensional Constraint-Aware Reward (MCAR) engine.

2.2. Model Architecture & LoRA Adaptation
We build our framework upon a foundational SpeechLLM ar-
chitecture (e.g., Qwen-Audio/Omni [1, 19] series), comprising
an audio encoderE θ, a modality projectorP ϕ, and a large lan-
guage modelM ψ. To break the physical limitations of acous-
tic entanglement in overlapping speech without incurring pro-
hibitive computational costs, we employ a universal Low-Rank
Adaptation (LoRA) strategy. Distinct from conventional text-
only alignment, we apply LoRA modules universally across

front-end, while strictly preventing catastrophic forgetting of
the LLM’s inherent linguistic and CoT reasoning capabilities.
2.3. Stage 1: Constraint-Aware Supervised Fine-Tuning
In the first stage, the primary goal is to establish the fundamen-
tal CoT logic and structural templates. To force the SpeechLLM
to rely on acoustic features rather than spurious sequential bi-
ases, we employPermutation-Invariant Prompting. For each
audio sample withNspeakers, the transcription segments are
randomly shuffled among theN!possible permutations. The
model is optimized using the standard auto-regressive negative
log-likelihood lossL SFT . While SFT successfully initializes
the CoT format and XML-style tags, it intrinsically suffers from
exposure bias. When confronted with variable speakers, pure
SFT models exhibit continuous hallucinations, temporal degra-
dation, and logical inconsistencies between the predicted CoT
count and the actual generated segments.
2.4. Stage 2: GRPO via Constrained Exploration
To alleviate the hallucination avalanche and sequence-bias in-
herent in teacher-forcing, we transition from passive imitation
to active exploration via Group Relative Policy Optimization
(GRPO), which eliminates the memory-intensive value model
by estimating the baseline directly from a group of generated
outcomes.

2.5. Multi-dimensional Constraint-Aware Reward
The core innovation of our RL stage lies in the MCAR engine. It
evaluates the dynamic multi-talker outputs through an exhaus-
tiveN!permutation search and enforces five orthogonal physi-
cal and logical constraints:
1. CoT Counting Reward (Rcot):To stabilize exploration
early in training, we provide a dense reward if the model cor-
rectly deduces the acoustic complexity. We parse the integer
Npred from the CoT prefixCand compare it against the ground
truthNtrue. A substantial positive reward (+1.0) is granted for
an exact match.
2. Dynamic Semantic Fidelity (Rsem):Standard WER is
highly ambiguous when speaker identities are permuted. We
implement a dynamic Permutation-Invariant WER (PI-WER,
equivalent to the evaluation metric cpWER) that traverses all
N!combinations between the predicted and reference segments
to find the optimal alignment path. To prevent gradient insta-
bility from unbounded errors, PI-WER is mapped into an expo-
nentially decaying reward surface:
Rsem = exp(−γ·PI-WER N! (yi,Yref ))(4)
3. Time-Aligned Accuracy (R time):Based on the op-
timal alignment path discovered by PI-WER, we compute the
absolute Time Error (TE) between the generated and reference
timestamps. We tolerate minor deviations (τ= 0.2s) and re-
ward temporal precision via:
Rtime = exp(−max(0,TE−τ))(5)
4. Fine-Grained Burst Penalty (Rburst):Acoustic mask-
ing often triggers auto-regressive collapse in SpeechLLMs,
causing cascading text hallucinations [20, 21]. Because stan-
dard WER treats scattered and consecutive errors equally, it
fails to penalize these localized avalanches. To suppress this,
we trackL burst, the maximum contiguous length of insertion
and substitution errors derived from the PI-WER alignment. We
impose a severe penalty for ungrounded generation exceeding a
tolerance thresholdτb:
Rburst =−λ b·max(0,L burst−τb)(6)
whereτ b (e.g.,τ b = 2) permits minor, acoustically plausi-
ble misrecognitions, but aggressively penalizes rambling. This
explicitly forces the policy to concisely close the speaker tag
(⟨/spk⟩) when acoustic grounding is lost.
5. Structural & Temporal Logic (R logic):We enforce
strict adherence to the XML formatting and physical con-
straints.R logic heavily penalizes missing tags, conversational
verbosity outside tags, and logically impossible temporal inver-
sion (ts≥t e). Furthermore, severe logical divergence—such
as predictingN pred = 3in the CoT but generating only 2
speaker tags—triggers a catastrophic penalty.
The final scalar rewardR i is the weighted sum of these
five components. This multi-dimensional formulation strictly
guides the SpeechLLM to align its generative distribution with
the complex, dynamic constraints of multi-talker overlapping
speech.
3. Experiments
3.1. Experimental Setup
3.1.1. Datasets
We comprehensively evaluate our framework on the widely
adoptedLibri2MixandLibri3Mixdatasets [22]. Formally,
these datasets combine clean utterances from LibriSpeech [23]
with WHAM! noise [24] to simulate realistic scenarios.
To explicitly assess the model’s reasoning capacity on dy-
namic, unknown speaker counts, we further construct a novel
Dynamic-Mix (2+3)evaluation set by uniformly sampling and
shuffling test utterances from both Libri2Mix and Libri3Mix.
We use these benchmarks as controlled diagnostic settings to
isolate dense overlap and unknown speaker-count reasoning.
Finally, the GRPO policy is optimized on a heavily curated
subset of 10,000 highly overlapped speech samples, which are
strictly sampled from the official training splits.
3.1.2. Implementation Details
Our experiments are conducted on the Qwen2.5-Omni-7B foun-
dational model using the MS-Swift framework. We apply Low-
Rank Adaptation (LoRA) [14] universally across all linear lay-
ers within the audio encoder, modality projector, and the LLM,
setting rankr= 16,α= 32, and dropoutp= 0.05.
During the SFT stage, the model is trained for 5 epochs with
a global batch size of 128 and a learning rate of2×10 −5 . In
the GRPO stage, the learning rate is decayed to1×10 −6 and
βis set to0.04. For each audio query, the active policy samples
G= 8independent rollouts, with the clipping parameterϵset
to0.2. The decay factor in Eq. (4) is set toγ= 1.0. For the
fine-grained burst penalty (R burst), we set the tolerance win-
dowτ b = 2tokens and the penalty scaleλ b = 1.0. The five
rewards are equally weighted to compute the total advantage.
3.1.3. Evaluation Metrics
To evaluate both transcription fidelity and diarization accuracy,
we employ the following metrics:
•cpWER (Concatenated minimum-Permutation WER):
The primary joint metric for multi-talker ASR, computing
the minimum Word Error Rate across allN!speaker-to-
transcript permutations. Note that this cpWER is mathemati-
cally equivalent to PI-WER utilized in ourRsem reward.
•WDER (Word Diarization Error Rate):Traditional
pipelines rely on frame-level Diarization Error Rate (DER).
However, generative SpeechLLMs operate on discrete se-
mantic tokens rather than continuous acoustic frames, mak-
ing frame-level metrics highly susceptible to boundary quan-
tization artifacts. WDER can evaluate text-level speaker con-
fusion by calculating the percentage of correctly transcribed
words assigned to incorrect speakers.
•TE (Timestamp Error):The average absolute deviation be-
tween the predicted and ground-truth temporal boundaries.
•CoT-Acc (Chain-of-Thought Accuracy):Specifically for
the Dynamic-Mix setting, measuring the exact-match accu-
racy of the predicted speaker count prior to text generation.

3.3. Ablation Study on MCAR Constraints
Table 3 isolates the contribution of each component within our
MCAR reward engine on the Dynamic-Mix set. The removal of
the CoT Counting Reward triggers the most catastrophic degra-
dation. Coupled with the drop in CoT-Acc, this validates that
explicitly rewarding global acoustic scene deduction is a prereq-
uisite for accurate autoregressive transcription. Similarly, omit-
ting the DynamicN!Evaluation disables permutation-invariant
optimization, drastically corrupting speaker tracking and caus-
ing the cpWER to surge to16.56%.
Furthermore, disabling the Fine-Grained Burst Penalty de-
grades cpWER to11.84%, confirming its critical role in sup-
pressing localized hallucination avalanches and auto-regressive
collapse. Finally, removing the Time-Aligned Accuracy or
Structural Logic rewards yields minor but consistent regres-
sions. This indicates that while they are not the primary drivers
of semantic accuracy, these components act as essential regu-
larizers, rigidly enforcing temporal formatting and preventing
structural degradation during active RL exploration.

4. Conclusion
In this paper, we introduced a two-stage generative framework
that empowers SpeechLLMs to multi-talker overlapping speech
tasks. By integrating Chain-of-Thought (CoT) reasoning with
Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO), our model tran-
sitions from passive text mimicry to active acoustic scene de-
duction. Evaluated on the LibriMix and Dynamic-Mix bench-
marks, this unified approach achieves competitive cpWER and
diarization precision (WDER) without relying on external V AD
or clustering modules. For future work, we plan to scale this
RL paradigm to larger real-world conversational datasets like
AMI and AliMeeting. We will also explore finer-grained re-
ward mechanisms to enhance temporal resolution and extend
this generative approach to broader tasks.
