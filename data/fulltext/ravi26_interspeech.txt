Abstract
Confidence scores in Automatic Speech Recognition (ASR) are
required to assess the correctness of the predicted text. Class
probabilities from overconfidentASRdecoders do not reflect
prediction accuracy. Auxiliary Confidence Estimation Model
(CEM) based on binary target scores does not reflect the cor-
rectness of partially correct transcripts.CEMbased on tempo-
ral continuous target scores suffers from inaccurate alignment.
We observe the diminishing class probabilities in true-class
probability-basedCEM. To address the limitations of the State-
of-the-Art (SOTA)CEM, we propose a novel continuous target
score, namedRanD. Then, we presentRanD-basedCEMfor
fourASRarchitectures: Connectionist Temporal Classification
(CTC), Recurrent Neural Network-Transducer (RNN-T), Token
Duration Transducer (TDT), and Attention-based Encoder De-
coder (AED). Experiments with Hindi and EnglishASRshow
that our approach outperformsSOTA CEMand also generalises
to mismatched domains.
Index Terms: Automatic Speech Recognition, Confidence
Estimation, Connectionist Temporal Classification, Recurrent
Neural Network Transducer, Token and Duration Transducer,
Attention-based Encoder Decoder
1. Introduction
Confidence estimation improves the trustworthiness of Auto-
matic Speech Recognition (ASR) models. Confidence scores
can be estimated at the token level, word level or utterance level.
Word-level confidence scores play a significant role not only in
downstream and upstream tasks, such as speech translation [1]
and depression detection [2], but also in voice assistants and ap-
plications that transcribe speech instructions. Confidence scores
will aid in handling uncertain predictions through automatic er-
ror correction [3] and disfluency detection [4].
Maximum class probabilities from theASRdecoder are the
confidence scores in [5] [6] [7]. Due to the overconfident nature
of deep learning models, the probability mass tends to accumu-
late towards a class, making it challenging to determine correct-
ness. Transformations, such as entropy and temperature scaling
[8] [9], are also not effective for addressing the skewed proba-
bility distribution. The literature also includes trainable auxil-
iary Confidence Estimation Models (CEM) that extract various
intermediate embeddings from theASRto train it. Trainable
CEMare based on binary or continuous target scores.
For binary target scores, the target score for correct words
is ’1’, and the other predicted words (inserted and substituted
words) are assigned ’0’. The correct, substitution and insertion
markers are assigned using Levenshtein alignment. [10], [11],

as Language Model (LM) and acoustic features, duration, prob-

using binary target scores. [15] trains Long Short Term Mem-

features from theASR, like attention embeddings,LMfeatures,
probability distribution and top-k class probability values, to
train theCEMbuilt using a fully connected layer and a residual-
energy model, respectively. [21] use theCEMapprooach in [19]
and [20] and add out-of-domain audio data to train the mod-
els. [22] builds multilayer perceptron and transformer-based
CEMforCTCusing binary targets. To predict word-level confi-
dence, binary targets assign a score of ’0’ to partially correctly
predicted words, which does not effectively reflect the degree
of correctness. Hence,CEMmodels trained using continuous
target scores emerged, where the target captures the degree of
correctness of the predicted words.
Our previous works, [23] [24] train theCEMusing continu-
ous target scores. [23] proposes a target score,TeLeS, which
is a combination of scores that quantifies temporal and lexical
similarity between the actual and predicted words. The tempo-
ral score is approximated using the forced alignment. Errors in
timestamp estimation propagate into the target score. [24] uses
a target score,TruCLeS, that is based on true class probabil-
ity and lexical similarity. We examine the true class probability
range of correct and incorrect tokens. As seen in Figure 1 (token
probabilities are from the HindiASRmodel [25] and evaluated
on the KathBathKBdataset [26]), the probabilities of incorrect
tokens diminish due to overconfidence as the number of classes
increases, approaching the binary target score. Consequently,
the model’s confidence estimates are not well-calibrated. This
collapse undermines calibration, as the model can not reliably
distinguish between mildly and severely incorrect tokens.

3. Experimentation & Evaluation
3.1. Experimental Settings
We evaluate the proposedCEMusing pre-trainedASRmod-
els available in theNeMo[29] framework. For theCTC-
ASRmodel, we employ the Hindi Conformer-CTCmodel [25].
For theRNN-Tarchitecture, we use the conformer-transducer
model [30]. Theparakeet-TDTmodel [31] is adopted for
theTDTarchitecture, and theCanary-Flashmodel [32] is
used to evaluate theAEDmodel.
The model-basedCEMapproaches are trained on a subset
of the dataset used to train the correspondingASRmodel. To
assess the efficacy of theCEM, we evaluate it on both in-domain
and mismatched-domain test sets relative to theCEMtraining
set. ForCTC, theKBHindi dataset [26] is used to train the
CEM, and thePBHindi speech dataset [33] is used as the mis-
matched dataset. For the other threeASRarchitectures, the Lib-
riSpeech English [34] dataset is used to train theCEM, and the
Pure SetNPTEL[35] and Svarah Indian English dataset [36] are
used as the out-of-domain datasets. The observed Word Error
Rate (WER) and Character Error Rate (CER) on the experimental
datasets are given in Table 1.

We compare the proposedCEMwithSOTAapproaches. The
Maximum Class Probability (MCP) is used as the token confi-
dence in [5][6][7]. The secondSOTAmethod [9] interprets the
entropy of the token-level class probability distribution as the
confidence score. TheMCPand entropy values at the token level
are aggregated to derive the confidence of the predicted words
in the output text. TheMCP- and entropy-basedCEMmethods
are used as baseline approaches for all theASR.
TheSOTAmodel-basedCEMapproaches are trained us-
ing either binary or continuous target scores. The Multi-layer
perceptron and Transformer-basedCEM(M-CEM&T-CEM) in
[22], trained using binary target scores, serve as baselines for
theCTCmodel. The Subword-based trainableCEM(S-CEM)
model with binary targets in [20] serves as a baseline for the
RNN-Tmodel. TheCEMin [19] for theAEDarchitecture is also
trained using binary target scores. We train theCEMusing bi-
nary target scores to evaluate theTDT-CEM’s performance. We
also compare our approach withSOTAmodel-based methods,
namelyTruCLeS(CTC&RNN-Tmodel) andTeLeS(CTC),
which are trained using continuous target scores.
TheRanD-CEMmodel forCTChas fully connected layers
with 512, 256, and 128 neurons and a sigmoid output layer, with
Rectified Linear activation function (ReLU). ForRNN-Tand
TDT, theRanD-CEMcomprises two bidirectionalLSTMlayers
with 512 hidden units. It also has a fully connected layer with
1024 neurons and a sigmoid activation function. For theAED
model, theRanD-CEMuses a feedforward network of size 256
withReLUactivation. The network further has a linear output
layer with a sigmoid activation function. TheRanDmodels are
trained for 50 epochs using the Adam optimizer with a learning
rate of 0.0001. Time- and frequency-masking are applied to the
audios during training. We setα= 0.5.
The estimated confidence is evaluated using: Mean Ab-
solute Error (MAE∈[0,1]↓), Kullback-Leibler Divergence
(KLD∈[0,∞]↓), Jensen-Shanon Divergence (JSD∈[0,1]↓),
Normalized Cross Entropy (NCE∈[−∞,1]↑) [37], Expected
Calibration Error (ECE∈[0,1]↓), Area Under the Receiver
Operating Characteristic curve (AUROC∈[0,1]↑), and Area
Under the Precision-Recall Curve (AUPRC∈[0,1]↑).

As observed from the model-basedCEMapproaches trained

non-trainableCEM, as the former is trained on speech represen-
tations to predict label correctness. However, it does not differ-

mixture of correct and substituted tokens.
The model trained with continuous target scores achieves
better results than the one trained with binary targets.

4. Conclusion & Future Work
Interpreting probabilities and their statistical transformations as
confidence scores is unreliable because theASRneural archi-
tecture’s training mechanism leads to overly confident predic-
tions. The otherSOTAtrainable approaches, such as binary
target scores, are coarse and fail to capture partial correctness
in the predicted words. In addition, the temporal alignment
score cannot be used with allASRarchitectures. In this pa-
per, we analysed the limitations of true-class-probability-based
approaches for trainingCEM. To address the challenges, we
proposeRanD-CEM, which leverages probability rank and dis-
tance between the predicted and actual probability distributions;
hence, it is more discriminative and calibrated than other ap-
proaches. We evaluate the proposed model across fourASR
models and observe that it achieves better results thanSOTA.
Further, we plan to explore extending theCEMto predict deleted
words by observing the representations of words or segments
around deletions. TheCEMleverages rich representations to es-
timate the confidence of predictions. However, in low-resource
ASR, it may not be possible to get rich representations. Our fu-
ture work will focus on probing confidence estimation in low-
resourceASRand fine-tuning theCEMfor this.
