Abstract
This work presents high-quality Text to Speech (TTS) sys-
tems for Amharic and Afan Oromo, two widely spoken but
under-resourced languages in Ethiopia using the SpeechT5,
a Transformer-based self-supervised model. A phonetically
rich and diverse text corpus was created, then professionally
recorded in a studio to produce 200 hours of high-quality
speech data (100 per language). To resolve linguistic com-
plexities such as gemination and context-sensitive pronuncia-
tion in Amharic,we created a special dataset and extended the
training to 113 hours. We evaluated the models both objec-
tively and subjectively. Human raters assigned mean opinion
scores of 4.65 for Amharic and 4.43 for Afan Oromo, high-
lighting the naturalness, intelligibility, and accurate pronuncia-
tion of the models. These results demonstrate that linguistically
informed data combined with self-supervised learning enables
robust, natural-sounding TTS for low-resource languages.

1. Introduction
Spoken languages all over the world exceed 7,000 , yet only a
small fraction are supported by modern speech technologies[1,
2].Most speech processing applications such as Automatic
Speech Recognition (ASR) and Text-to-Speech (TTS) are avail-
able primarily for high-resource languages like English, Chi-
nese, and Japanese. One of the most significant barriers limiting
speech technology development for the majority of the world’s
languages is the lack of linguistic resources and computational
infrastructure [2]. This is the case in Ethiopia, where there is a
greater diversity of languages in comparison to the speech mod-
ern applications[1].
TTS synthesis refers to the process of artificially con-
verting written text into spoken words.It’s used in many ev-
eryday applications, like virtual assistants, screen readers for
people with visual impairments, and voice features in mobile
apps. Historically, two primary technologies have been used
for TTS: concatenative synthesis and formant (rule-based) syn-
thesis.Concatenative synthesis works by piecing together real
recordings of speech, which can sound very natural but it needs
a lot of high-quality labeled data and doesn’t easily adapt to dif-
ferent speaking styles[3]. Formant synthesis, on the other hand,
is resource efficient and flexible but tends to sound robotic and
requires complex rule-based modeling of prosody and pronun-
ciation [4].
For languages with limited linguistic resources like
Amharic and Afan Oromo, traditional TTS methods such as

concatenative and formant-based synthesis have often proven
impractical or produced limited results. These methods typ-
ically require either large, carefully labeled datasets or hand-
crafted linguistic rules both of which are difficult to obtain for
under-resourced languages. While a few early attempts were
made to develop TTS systems for Amharic and Afan Oromo
using concatenative, formant based, and statistical approaches,
they were constrained by limited data, low output quality, and
a lack of scalability [3, 5, 4]. In most cases, the available
corpora were small, domain-limited, and designed primarily
for speech recognition rather than speech synthesis. More-
over, these systems often ignored non-standard words, complex
prosodic structures, or required extensive feature engineering,
making them unsuitable for practical deployment[6]. Given the
limitations of these conventional methods, and the growing de-
mand for accessible and natural-sounding speech technologies,
there is a strong need for more robust and scalable solutions .
In Ethiopia, there have been growing efforts to prepare
speech corpora for local languages These include the cre-
ation of a 20-hour Amharic speech corpus [1], the develop-
ment of corpora for four Ethiopian languages Amharic, Afan
Oromo, Tigrigna, and Wolaytta under Addis Ababa Univer-
sity’s thematic research program [2], and a 100- hour Amharic
dataset that incorporates the earlier 20-hour resource. More re-
cently, the Ethiopian Artificial Intelligence Institute (EAII) has
launched large-scale projects to collect and prepare speech cor-
pora for Amharic, Afan Oromo, Tigrigna, Afar and Somali, sup-
ported by its high-performance computing infrastructure [7].
However, almost all of these speech resources were designed
primarily for Automatic Speech Recognition (ASR) tasks. To
the best of the authors’ knowledge, no studio quality, TTS spe-
cific corpus has yet been developed for Ethiopian languages
highlighting a critical gap in the development of high-quality
speech synthesis systems.
To address this gap, this work presents high-quality, multi-
speaker TTS systems for Amharic and Afan Oromo using
SpeechT5 [8], a Transformer-based, self-supervised model. A
200-hour clean, studio-recorded speech dataset was prepared
100 hours per language alongside text normalization, Amharic
transliteration, and speaker embeddings. An additional dataset
was created for Amharic to handle gemination and context-
sensitive pronunciations.
This study demonstrates that by combining carefully cu-
rated data with state-ofthe- art self-supervised architectures, it is
possible to develop natural and intelligible speech synthesis sys-
tems for low-resource languages. The models developed in this
work lay the foundation for expanding TTS technology to more
Ethiopian languages and making speech applications more in-
clusive and accessible.

1.1. Afan Oromo TTS Systems
The first known attempt at TTS for Afan Oromo was by Morka
Mekonnen [9] using diphone-based concatenative synthesis.
The system achieved 43.3% intelligibility for unfamiliar listen-
ers and 83.3% for listeners who had heard the samples multiple
times. The performance suffered due to segmentation errors and
lack of smooth transitions between diphones.
Samson Tadesse [10] also used concatenative synthesis
with RELP coding. His system achieved 75% intelligibility
for diphones and 54% for triphones, with MOS scores of 3.03
(diphones) and 2.2 (triphones) for intelligibility, and 2.65 and
2.02 for naturalness, respectively. The small diphone database
and absence of prosodic modeling contributed to limited output
quality.
The most recent effort was by Wosho[11], who imple-
mented an HMM-based statistical parametric TTS system. Us-
ing 400 training and 10 test sentences, his system achieved
MOS scores of 4.1 (naturalness) and 4.3 (intelligibility)—the
highest reported for Afan Oromo to date. However, the system
did not handle NSWs (abbreviations, numbers, acronyms), and
relied heavily on hand-engineered features and a small dataset,
limiting generalizability and robustness.
Most existing TTS systems for Amharic and Afan Oromo
suffer from major limitations: small or domain-specific
datasets, lack of speaker diversity, poor modeling of prosody,
and limited ability to handle non-standard word forms. Addi-
tionally, they depend on outdated or labor-intensive modeling
techniques and do not leverage recent advances in deep learn-
ing.
1.2. Neural and Multilingual Approaches
Recent trends in TTS have shown that end-to-end neural
approaches, particularly those using self-supervised learning
and Transformer architectures (e.g., Tacotron, FastSpeech,
SpeechT5)[8]), outperform traditional models in both qual-
ity and scalability. Recently, Meta evaluated MMS (Mas-
sively Multilingual Speech) models[12] for low-resource lan-
guages, reporting promising performance for Amharic and Afan
Oromo: CER around 5% for Amharic and MOS scores of
4.21–4.23 for Afan Oromo. However, these models often focus
on aggregate multilingual performance, with limited handling
of language-specific phonological phenomena, highlighting the
need for targeted datasets and speaker aware modeling in under
resourced Ethiopian languages.
2. Data Preparation
Developing a high-quality TTS system requires a well-prepared
dataset consisting of clean text and high-quality speech record-
ings. For Ethiopian languages such as Amharic and Afaan
Oromo, publicly available TTS corpora are limited. Therefore,
a key contribution of this work is the collection and preparation
of a studio-quality, multi-speaker TTS dataset. This section de-
scribes the text and speech data collection process, followed by
preprocessing steps to ensure data quality and training suitabil-
ity.
2.1. Text Data Collection
To build a diverse and representative text corpus, sentences
were collected from a variety of sources, including books, news
articles, school materials, and conversational texts. The goal
was to capture a broad range of vocabulary, sentence struc-
tures, and linguistic features for both languages. After collec-
tion, the text was cleaned by removing duplicates, incomplete
sentences, non-linguistic symbols, and other inconsistencies.
Special care was taken to retain proper nouns, numerals, and
context-sensitive terms to preserve pronunciation richness.
For Amharic, additional processing was required to translit-
erate text from the Ge’ez (Fidel) script to a standardized Latin-
based representation. This transliteration facilitates easier tok-
enization and improves model compatibility without losing lin-
guistic fidelity.
2.2. Speech Data Collection
After text preparation, corresponding speech was recorded in a
quiet, studio-quality environment to ensure clean and consistent
audio. For each language, two speakers one male and one fe-
male were selected to capture speaker variability. The Amharic
dataset contains 82,865 utterances totaling 100 hours, while the
Afaan Oromo dataset includes 78,013 utterances totaling 100
hours. All recordings were captured at 16 kHz, 16-bit resolu-
tion, mono channel, using high-quality microphones and saved
in .wav format.
To address specific linguistic phenomena in Amharic, such
as gemination and context-sensitive word pronunciation, we ad-
ditionally prepared a set of sentences designed to explicitly in-
clude these linguistic patterns. These sentences contained ho-
mographs, geminated words, and context-dependent pronunci-
ations in diverse linguistic contexts. The resulting recordings
were collected under the same studio conditions as the main
dataset. This additional targeted recording resulted in 13 hours
of high-quality speech data, which was incorporated into the
training set, bringing the total Amharic dataset to 113 hours.
2.3. Data Preprocessing
A series of preprocessing steps were applied to ensure consis-
tency and training stability.
Text Data:Text normalization was performed to convert
non-standard forms such as numbers, dates, abbreviations, and
symbols into their full spoken equivalents. Since the SpeechT5
tokenizer does not support digits, all numerical forms were writ-
ten in words. The SpeechT5 tokenizer was used for both lan-
guages.
For Amharic, all text was transliterated from Ge’ez script
to Latin characters using a standardized mapping. Additional
cleaning removed duplicates, unnecessary punctuation, special
characters, and incomplete lines.
Special attention was given to language-specific phonetic
and contextual features. In Amharic, homographs, words with
identical spelling but different meanings and pronunciations de-
pending on context or gemination, pose a significant challenge
for TTS systems. For example, the word ”Gena” may refer to
“Christmas,” “still early,” or “there is more,” depending on con-
text. Such ambiguity makes correct pronunciation prediction
difficult. To address this issue, we constructed a targeted dataset
containing homographs in diverse contextual sentences. This
targeted data was included in the additional 13-hour Amharic
corpus, increasing the total dataset to 113 hours. The enriched
dataset improved the model’s ability to correctly disambiguate
and pronounce context-sensitive words.

3. Method
The proposed Text-to-Speech system is built by fine-tuning
SpeechT5, a unified Transformer encoder–decoder model that
learns shared representations for text and speech. For the TTS
task, input text is first tokenized and passed through a Text En-
coder Pre-Net. The encoded representations are processed by
the Transformer encoder–decoder to model contextual depen-
dencies. The decoder then generates an 80-dimensional log
Mel-spectrogram through a Speech Decoder Pre-Net, which is
further refined by a Post-Net. Finally, a neural vocoder converts
the refined spectrogram into a 16 kHz speech waveform.
Figure 1:Architecture of the proposed multi-speaker SpeechT5-
based TTS system.
We initialize from a SpeechT5 base model pre-trained on
speech–text data and fine-tuned on English LibriTTS, which is
then adapted to our dataset. During preprocessing, all audio was
resampled to 16 kHz, transcripts were normalized for tokenizer
compatibility, and sequences longer than 200 tokens were re-
moved to ensure stable training. The model was optimized us-
ing the Adam optimizer with a learning rate of1×10−5, mixed-
precision training, and gradient accumulation.
To support multi-speaker synthesis, 512-dimensional x-
vector speaker embeddings were extracted for each utterance
and used to condition the decoder. This enables the system
to capture speaker-specific acoustic characteristics and gener-
ate consistent, speaker-aware speech. During inference, speaker
embeddings are selected based on the target speaker identity
used during training.
4. Evaluation
4.1. Objective Evaluation
To assess model performance, Mean Squared Error (MSE) on
spectrogram predictions was used as the primary training ob-
jective. Increasing the dataset size had a clear impact on gen-
eralization. For Afaan Oromo, expanding training data from
50 to 100 hours reduced validation loss from 0.3996 to 0.3662.
For Amharic, loss decreased from 0.3915 (50 hours) to 0.3710
(100 hours), and further to 0.3675 after including the additional
13 hours of targeted homographs and geminated words. This
demonstrates that both dataset size and linguistic quality di-
rectly improve model accuracy.
4.2. Subjective Evaluation
Objective metrics alone cannot fully capture naturalness or in-
telligibility in TTS systems. Therefore, a Mean Opinion Score
(MOS) evaluation was conducted with ten expert native speak-
ers who assessed 250 synthesized utterances per language for
naturalness, intelligibility, and pronunciation accuracy using a
5-point scale (1 = Bad, 5 = Excellent).
MOS results are reported together with 95% confidence in-
tervals computed over listener ratings to reflect statistical vari-
ability. Each utterance was rated independently by the listeners
in a randomized order to avoid bias, and ratings were averaged
across all evaluators.
The inclusion of the 13-hour targeted Amharic dataset led
to substantial improvements in pronunciation and overall qual-
ity, increasing the overall MOS from 4.12 to 4.65. As shown in
Table 1, both the Amharic and Afaan Oromo systems achieved
high perceptual quality, with overall MOS scores of 4.65 [4.60,
4.70] and 4.43 [4.37, 4.49], respectively.

5. Discussion
The evaluation results indicate that both the quantity and quality
of the dataset have a substantial impact on TTS performance for
Ethiopian languages. For Amharic, adding 13 hours of targeted
data containing homographs and geminated words significantly
improved pronunciation and overall naturalness, as reflected in
the MOS increase from 4.12 to 4.65. This demonstrates that ad-
dressing specific linguistic phenomena is important for improv-
ing speech synthesis in languages with complex phonological
structures. Compared to previous Amharic TTS systems, which
typically reported MOS scores between 2.8 and 4.0 and often
struggled with gemination and prosody modeling, our approach
shows consistent improvements across perceptual quality met-
rics.
The SpeechT5 model, with its unified Transformer en-
coder–decoder architecture, effectively leverages shared text-
speech representations, allowing the system to generalize
across diverse linguistic inputs. In addition, the use of 512-
dimensional x-vector speaker embeddings enabled the model
to capture speaker-specific characteristics, producing natural,
consistent voices and supporting multi-speaker synthesis. For
Afaan Oromo, the multi speaker setup using x-vector embed-
dings effectively captured speaker variability, enabling high-
quality speech synthesis. Previous Afan Oromo TTS models
reported MOS scores around 3.0–4.3, often limited by small
datasets and single-speaker recordings, whereas our method
achieves both higher naturalness and intelligibility while sup-
porting multiple voices.
Overall, the study emphasizes that under-resourced lan-
guages benefit not only from more data but from linguistically
informed data, appropriate model architectures, and speaker
conditioning that represent the unique characteristics of the lan-
guage. The results also highlight that modern neural TTS sys-
tems, combined with carefully curated multi-speaker datasets,
outperform earlier statistical or concatenative approaches for
these languages. At inference time, text normalization is ap-
plied to convert numbers, symbols, and abbreviations into spo-
ken forms. However, homograph disambiguation remains a
challenging problem, and in this work we assume standardized

input text. Developing a full linguistic front-end for automatic
disambiguation is left for future work.
6. Conclusion
This study presents a high-quality TTS system for Ethiopian
languages, focusing on Amharic and Afaan Oromo. By curat-
ing a large, linguistically informed dataset, including 113 hours
for Amharic with an additional 13 hours of targeted data con-
taining homographs and geminated words, we demonstrate that
both dataset size and quality critically influence pronunciation,
naturalness, and intelligibility in TTS systems.
Fine-tuning the SpeechT5 Transformer-based model with
x-vector speaker embeddings enables natural and speaker-
consistent speech synthesis. Compared to previous systems,
our approach achieves higher MOS scores and better handles
language-specific phonological phenomena.
The key contribution of this work is the creation of a
reusable, studio-quality TTS dataset and the demonstration that
linguistically targeted data significantly improves TTS perfor-
mance for under-resourced languages.
7. Future Work
Future work will extend the dataset to additional Ethiopian lan-
guages, targeting at least 100 hours of high-quality speech data
per language. We also plan to evaluate cross-model generaliza-
tion by fine-tuning alternative architectures such as VITS and
FastSpeech2 using the same dataset.
In addition, expanding the coverage of linguistic phenom-
ena and increasing speaker diversity will further improve the ro-
bustness and naturalness of TTS systems for low-resource lan-
guages.
