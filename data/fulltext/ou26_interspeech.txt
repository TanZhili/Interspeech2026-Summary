Positioning Effect of Syllable Shortening on Speech Segmentation: Evidence 
from Mandarin Listeners 
Shu-chen Ou 
National Sun Yat-sen University 
sherryou@mail.nsysu.edu.tw 
 
Abstract 
 
This study examines how syllable shortening influences speech 
segmentation using an artificia l language expe riment. While 
listeners use transitional probabilities and salient prosodic cues, 
reduced prosody such as syllable shortening remains 
understudied. Native Mandarin listeners (N = 120) learned 
trisyllabic artificial words under conditions shortening either 
the initial, middle, or final syllable. Results showed that middle-
syllable shortening facilitated segmentation, final-syllable 
shortening hindered it, and initial shortening had no clear effect. 
The findings indicate that durational reduction aids 
segmentation when aligned with  predictable word internal 
structure but disrupts it when applied at word boundaries. 
 
Index Terms : speech segmentation, prosodic reduction, 
syllable shortening, artificial language learning 
1. Introduction 
A fundamental challenge in speech processing is listener’s 
ability to segment continuous speech into discrete units such as 
words. Because natural speech often lacks reliable pauses 
between words, the human auditory system must exploit 
various distributional cues to identify lexical units. Among 
these, transitional probabilities (TPs), the statistical likelihood 
that one syllable follows another, are among the most basic and 
extensively studied information sources. According to the 
statistical learning account, syllables within a word tend to have 
higher TPs than syllables spanning word boundaries. Even in 
nonsense artificial languages, listeners can successfully 
segment trisyllabic CVCVCV sequences based solely on these 
statistical regularities [1]. However, while TPs provide a 
general cognitive  mechanism for segmentation, natural 
language also contains additional phonetic cues derived from 
regular sound patterns that also play important roles in speech 
segmention [2, 3]. 
 
One extensively documented cue concerns vowel duration. 
In speech production, vowels are often lengthened at prosodic 
or word boundaries [4, 5], and listeners appear to exploit this 
durational pattern perceptually. When exposed to syllable 
sequences differing in vowel length, they tend to interpret 
longer vowels as marking unit endings, grouping preceding 
material belonging to the same unit [6]. Experimental studies 
using continuous streams of nonsen se trisyllabic items further 
show that segmentation performance improves when vowels in 
sequence final syllables are le ngthened, whereas lengthening 
initial vowels does not produce comparable benefits [7]. Cross-
linguistic evidence indicates that  listeners of many languages, 
including English, French, Dutch, German, Basque, Japanese, 
and Taiwanese Southern Min, use final vowel lengthening to 
infer word boundaries [8, 9, 10, 11], although some exceptions 
have been reported for Italian and Hungarian listeners [9, 12]. 
Together, these findings suggest that final vowel lengthening 
functions as a broadly accessible segmentation cue. 
More recent research shows that consonant duration likewise 
contributes to segmentation. Because consonants tend to be 
strengthened and lengthened at the beginnings of prosodic 
domains [13, 14, 15], listener s may interpret longer onset 
consonants as signals of wo rd initial positions. Indeed, 
segmentation accuracy improves when onset consonants in 
sequence initial syllables are lengthened in continuous streams 
of trisyllabic sequences, while lengthening consonants in final 
syllables yields no comparable advantage [16]. In this way, 
consonant and vowel duration provide complementary cues: 
longer onset consonants tend to indicate beginnings, whereas 
longer vowels tend to indicate endings. Similar effects have 
been observed across languages, including English, Hungarian, 
Italian, and Taiwanese Southern Min [10, 12], suggesting that 
consonant lengthening may also serve as a cross-linguistically 
robust segmentation cue. 
While the facilitative effects of salient cues like vowel and 
consonant lengthening as word  boundary markers are widely 
reported, much less is known about the role of prosodic 
reduction (e.g., syllable shortening), despite its systematic and 
frequent occurrence in natural speech. Prior studies suggest that 
prosodic reductions, especially in  the word internal position, 
occur regularly in language and that listeners may acquire these 
patterns through exposure. In many trisyllabic words of 
Mandarin Chinese, for example, the middle syllable is 
frequently and systematically reduced and becomes non-salient 
(e.g., huǒ chē zhàn /xwɔ ʈʂʰɤ ʈʂan/ “train station” and jiǎo tà chē 
/tɕjɑʊ̯  tʰa ʈʂʰɤ/ “bicycle”, in which the middle syllable is often 
shorter or even contracted) [1 7, 18, 19]). This systematic 
pattern may serve as a cue to holistic units, signaling to the 
listener that the reduced syllable belongs to the interior of a 
word rather than a boundary. Unlike salient cues that mark the 
edges of words (initial or fi nal), prosodic reduction might 
function as a cue to internal unit structure. 
While previous research has focused on how salient 
segement lengthening facilita tes boundary detection, the 
present study explores whether non-salient syllable shortening 
at word internal positions can also  affect speech segmentation. 
2. Method 
2.1. Design 
Following a typical AL lear ning paradigm, the present 
experiement consisted of a le arning phase and a test phase. 
During the learning phase, listeners listened to long utterrances 
composed of continuous repeti tions of articificial “words” 
without pauses. Then in the test phase, they performed a two 
forced-choice test to identify the “words”.  Identification 
accuarcy was taken as a measure of successful segmentation 
during the learning phase.

meaningless syllables: /banume/, /nebogi/, /genigo/, /mibabu/,

boundaries (e.g., /mebimo/ from the final syllable of /banume/

and “part-words” were composed of the same syllable

The component syllables of the words were recorded 
individually by a male native speaker of Taiwan Mandarin and 
then manipulated in Praat [20]. All syllables were normalized 
to a duration of 300 ms, with equal allocations of 150 ms for the

of 130 Hz to eliminate unin tended prosodic cues. Four 
experimental conditions were created by manipulating the 
duration of target syllables: 
 
 TP-only (Control): All syllables maintained the base duration 
of 300 ms 
 Initial Shortening (IS): The dur ation of the first (initial) 
syllable was reduced to 150 ms. 
 Middle Shortening (MS): The duration of the second (middle) 
syllable in each word was reduced to 150 ms. 
 Final Shortening (FS): The duration of the third (final)

2.3. Participants 
A total of 120 native Mandarin listeners (67 females and 53 
males) were recruited for the study. Their mean age was 26.78 
years old (SD = 10.65). All participants reported no history of 
hearing or speech impairment and were randomly assigned to 
one of the four experimental conditions (N = 30 per condition).   
Each participant signed a cons ent form and was compensated 
with small honorarium for their participation. 
 
2.4. Procedure 
The experiment was programmed and controlled using E-Prime 
3.0. Participants were tested individually in a sound-attenuated 
booth and instructed to listen to the continuous speech stream 
to learn the artificial language. The experiment consisted of two 
phases: 
 
 Learning Phase: Participants listened to the continuous 
speech stream (approximately 11–12 minutes) via headphones. 
They were instructed to pay attention to the sounds as they 
would be tested on what they learned later. 
 Test Phase: Immediately fo llowing the learning phase, 
participants performed a two-a lternative forced-choice test 
comprising 36 trials. In each trial, they heard one "word" and 
one "part-word" sequentially (500 ms inter-stimulus interval).  
 
Participants selected the sequence that better matched the 
“words” of the language they had learned. The order of 
presentation was counterbalanced . Crucially, “part-words” 
were not presented as units during the learning phase; they only 
occurred implicitly across word boundaries. Thus, successful 
identification of “words” over “part-words” reflects 
segmentation based on the desi gnated TP structure acquired 
during learning. In addition, test stimuli were presented in a 
neutral (non-shortened) form across all conditions to ensure that 
performance reflected segmentation during learning rather than 
acoustic differences at test. 
 
2.5. Hypotheses 
We hypothesized that while le ngthening cues help locate 
boundaries, durational reduction functions as a prosodic cue to 
internal unit structure. Based on the systematic reduction of 
middle syllables in Mandarin Chinese, we predicted that 
shortening the middle syllabl e would facilitate word 
segmentation relative to other positioning conditions. By 
investigating these effects, we aim to provide a more 
comprehensive understanding of  how listeners integrate 
statistical and prosodic inform ation to chunk the continuous 
speech stream. 
 
3. Results 
Participants’ response accuracy in the two-alternative forced-
choice test was analyzed to determine the impact of syllable 
shortening on speech segmentation. The mean accuracy rates 
across the four experimental conditions were 55.56% for the 
TP-only (control) condition, 55.46% for the Initial Shortening 
(IS) condition, 65.28% for the Middle Shortening (MS) 
condition, and 52.87% for the Final Shortening (FS) condition. 
These results are summarized in Figure 2. 
Following the analytical approach of examining condition 
level differences, a mixed-effects logistic regression model was 
fitted to the binary responses (correct vs. incorrect) using R [21, 
22]. Condition was included as the fixed factor, with the TP-
only condition serving as the refe rence level. The model also 
included a random intercept for individual words of the 
artificial language to account for group level variance.

4. Discussion 
The current study demonstrates a robust effect of the position 
of syllable shortening on speech  segmentation. Our  findings 
show that shortening the middl e syllable (MS) significantly 
facilitated segmentation compared to the TP-only baseline. This 
result supports the hypothesis that prosodic reduction at highly 
predictable, word-interna l positions enhances unit 
identification. The facilitative effect of MS can be explained by 
Mandarin listeners’ native language experience. As noted 
earlier, in natural Mandarin speech, the middle syllable of 
trisyllabic words is frequently and systematically reduced. 
Listeners appear to have internalized these patterns, interpreting 
such reduction as a cue to holistic units and used it as a strategy 
to segment speech in an unfamiliar language. 
This interpretation is furthe r supported by recent findings 
concerning pitch variability. In  terms of pitch consistency 
across syllables within and across words, Mandarin Chinese 
exhibits a pattern in which pi tch differences (i.e., smaller 
change of fundemental frequencies (F 0) are smaller within 
words (with low variablity) than across word boundaries (with 
high variability [23]). Native sp eakers likewise are reported to 
segment artificial languages more effectively under low pitch 
variability  conditions than high variability conditions [24]. Just 
as shortening middle syllable si gnals a word internal position 
through durational reduction, low pitch variability signals a 
single unit through pitch consistency, as pitch variability within 
Mandarin words tends to be smaller than between words. 
The alignment between the MS effect and the low pitch 
variability effect suggests a uni fied perceptual strategy among 
Mandarin listeners. While sali ent prosodic cues like vowel 
lengthening or onset consonant strengthening typically function 
as boundary markers that help locate word edges, our results 
indicate that both prosodic reduc tion, including shortening the 
middle syllable (MS) in this study and low pitch variabilityin 
[24] serve as segmentation cues to internal unit structure. 
Rather than relying exclusivel y on boundary signals, listeners 
utilize these internal acoustic patterns, including reduced 
duration in the middle and stable F0 across syllables of a word, 
to identify holistic units. This finding suggests that the human 
auditory system is highly flexible, employing non-salient 
reductions and stability to navigate continuous speech. 
It is also worth mentioning that shortening syllable in the 
word final position (i.e., th e FS condition) significantly 
hindered performance. This finding echos the previous study 
showing that vowel lengthening often signals word finality. 
Therefore, shortening syllable in  this position conflicts with 
listeners’ expectation of bounda ries and thus deteriorates 
segmentation performance. Thes e disruptions suggest that 
when prosodic cues conflict with the expected structure (e.g., 
shortening at a boundary or high  pitch fluctuation within a 
word), speech perception is adversely affected. 
5. Conclusion 
Taken together, the evidence from both syllable shortening of 
this study and pitch variability of [24] suggests that Mandarin 
listeners have a strong preferen ce for acoustic patterns that 
signal internal units. This st udy provides additonal evidence 
that middle syllable shortening is not merely a byproduct of 
production but a functional, lan guage-specific cue for word-
internal structure, provided it aligns with the statistical 
framework of the language. 
While the current findings highlight the significance of 
middle syllable shortening in speech segmentation, several 
issues remain for future research. A critical question concerns 
whether multiple prosodic reduc tion cues, such as syllable 
shortening and low pitch variability, operate jointly to produce 
a mutually reinforcing effect. Though our current study and 
previous research independently  suggest that both durational 
and pitch reductions serve as cues  for internal unit structure in 
Mandarin Chinese, future research should employ an 
experimental design that combin es these non-salient cues to 
determine if their co-occurrence leads to a synergistic 
improvement in segmentation accuracy or if one cue (e.g., 
duration) carries more perceptual weight than the other (e.g., 
pitch). Understanding how these cues interact will provide a 
more comprehensive model of  how listeners integrate 
multidimensional acoustic information to perceive holistic units.  
Furthermore, the extent to which the use of prosodic 
reduction is language-specific or domain-general warrants 
further investigation. The preference for middle syllable

shortening observed here is likely rooted in the specific 
phonetic patterns of Mandarin trisyllabic words. However, in 
languages like Dutch, stops in prosodically strong positions 
(e.g., word initial position) can exhibit shorter voice onset times 
and reduced aspiration. This contrasts with the patterns in 
Mandarin or Taiwanese Southe rn Min, where lengthening 
typically signals initiality. Comparative studies involving 
speakers of diverse languages, including those with phonemic 
length contrasts like Hungarian where duration cues are used 
differently or non-tonal langua ges would clarify how native-
language experience shapes the interpretation of reduction. 
Such cross-linguistic research is essential to determine whether 
the utilization of non-salient reduction cues is a universal 
cognitive strategy for identifying internal structure or a learned 
behavior dependent on specific linguistic input.
