Abstract 
This study investigates where speaker -sensitive frequency 
regions (sub-bands) are located and how much 
speaker-discriminative information each region encodes. 
Likelihood-ratio-based forensic voice comparison experiments 
were conducted using Band -Limited Cepstral Coefficient 
(BLCC) matrices derived from a systematic scan of the 0–8 kHz 
full-band range with 0.6 kHz and 1.2 kHz sub-bands. 
Approximately 30 seconds of running speech from 162 native 
male Australian English speakers were analysed. The results 
show that although all sub -bands contain useful 
speaker-specific information, frequencies above approximately 
7 kHz are the least informative, while the 0–0.6 kHz and 4–
6 kHz regions provide strong speaker-discriminative cues. The 
findings also illustrate the effectiveness of the BLCC method in 
accessing and analysing any local spectral region of interest. 
Index Terms: band-limited cepstral coefficient, forensic voice 
comparison, speaker sensitive sub-bands, male running speech 
1. Introduction 
Prior research on vowel segments has shown that 
speaker-discriminative information is unevenly distributed 
across the spectrum and tends to be more strongly encoded at 
mid-to-high frequencies (above approximately 2.5 kHz) [1-8]. 
In particular, Clermont and Ishihara [9]—using the same 
Band-Limited Cepstral Coefficient (BLCC) method adopted in 
the present work—reported strong speaker information above 
approximately 2.3 kHz and below roughly 0.3 kHz, within an 
analysis range of 0–4 kHz for Japanese vowels. 
While specific patterns depend on the dataset and analytical 
method, studies based on running speech generally indicate 
that, for male speakers, the most informative regions typically 
fall in the low (below  0.5–0.7 kHz), mid (around 2–4 kHz), and 
mid-to-high (around 3–6 kHz) bands [10-14]. These patterns 
are typically explained by linking the bands to underlying 
physiological properties and their acoustic manifestations , 
which vary with the speech segment produced. For example, 
source-related cues such as fundamental frequency (F0) and 
low-frequency harmonics contribute primarily to the lower 
band; energy distribution and spectral shaping across vowels 
and other sonorants characterise the mid band; and the 
mid-to-high band typically encapsulates individuating cues 
present in unvoiced fricatives and burst spectra. 
A detailed understanding of where in the spectrum speaker 
information is encoded, to what extent it is present, and why—
from articulatory and acoustic perspectives—is crucial for 
Forensic Voice Comparison (FVC). Such knowledge 
strengthens the interpretability of FVC outcomes and enables 
more transparent, well-substantiated explanations in forensic 
contexts. Thus, following the Likelihood-Ratio (LR)–based 
FVC paradigm, this study investigates where speaker-specific 
information is encoded across the spectrum and to what extent, 
using about 30 seconds of pseudo-interview running speech 
from 162 adult male Australian English speakers [15, 16]. 
The present study employs the BLCC method [9, 17], 
which enables flexible and precise selection of any sub-band of 
interest and extraction of its corresponding BLCCs through a 
linear transformation of the full-band CCs. This approach 
removes the need to re-analyse the original signal, unlike the 
methods used in previous studies [10-14]. A series of LR-based 
FVC experiments was conducted using BLCC vectors derived 
from each individual sub-band. In this study, BLCCs were 
extracted using sub-band widths of 0.6 kHz and 1.2 kHz, shifted 
by 0.2 kHz and 0.4 kHz, respectively, across the 0 –8 kHz 
frequency range (corresponding to the 16 kHz sampling rate). 
Sub-bands containing stronger speaker -discriminative 
information are expected to yield better FVC performance.

3. Experimental methodology  
3.1. Speech data and parametrisation 
Recordings from the pseudo-police-style interview (int) task in 
the AusEng 500+ database [15, 16] were used for this study. 
The speakers were recruited from different states and territories 
of Australia. From these int recordings, the male speakers who 
participated in both the first and second recording sessions were 
selected, yielding a total of 162 speakers.  The original 
recordings were down-sampled to 16 kHz (i.e., an analysis 
range of 0–8 kHz). Although this sampling frequency is 
relatively high for practical FVC applications—which typically 
involve mobile-phone recordings—it was purposefully chosen 
to support the scientific and investigative aims of the present 
study. Each speaker’s recording per session was split into 
“utterances”, and they were stored as separate files.  
A filter-bank analysis at Step (1) was then applied to the 
speech-only segments using 25 triangular filters spaced 
uniformly on the linear frequency scale, with 50% overlap 
between adjacent filters. Fourteen full-band LFCCs (𝐶!–𝐶!#, 
excluding log-power) were extracted at Step (2) using 25-ms 
frames with a 5-ms shift. During preprocessing, we observed 
that some recording files contained no speech. To avoid 
extracting CCs from such files, only recordings longer than two 
seconds were retained. Speech-only segments were identified 
using WebRTC VAD applied to 10-ms frames. No cepstral 
normalisation was applied.  
For each speaker and each session, the available utterances 
were first randomised. A matrix of LFCCs was then constructed 
by sequentially adding LFCC frames from the shuffled 
utterances until the matrix reached 6,000 rows, corresponding 
to approximately 30 seconds of speech. 
The full frequency range (0–8 kHz) was scanned using 
sub-bands of fixed width that were shifted across the spectrum 
at a constant rate. Two sub -band widths were examined: 
0.6 kHz and 1.2 kHz, with hop sizes of 0.2 kHz and 0.4 kHz, 
respectively. For each sub-band, a BLCC matrix was obtained 
by applying a linear transformation to the full -band LFCC 
matrix at Step (3). This procedure resulted in 38 BLCC matrices 
for the 0.6 kHz sub-bands and 18 BLCC matrices for the 
1.2 kHz sub-bands. For the 0.6 kHz sub-bands, the 𝑀𝑊 value 
was 1.05 and was rounded to 1; for the 1.2 kHz sub-bands, the 
𝑀𝑊 value was 2.1 and was rounded to 2. Consequently, each 
BLCC vector consisted of 2 coefficients for the 0.6 kHz 
sub-bands and 3 coefficients for the 1.2   kHz sub-bands, 
including the 0th-order term. The BLCC tool is available from 
the corresponding author upon request.  
3.2. Experimental descriptions 
The 162 male speakers were randomly divided into three 
batches of 54. These batches were systematically rotated so 
that, in each cycle, one batch served as the test set, another as 
the background set, and the third as the calibration set, yielding 
six folds. The average metrics of the six folds will be reported. 
Two sets of FVC experiments (Experiments 1 and 2) were 
carried out using BLCC matrices generated for each of the 38 
sub-bands (0.6 kHz width) and 18 sub-bands (1.2 kHz width). 
The experiments were conducted within the Gaussian Mixture 
Model–Universal Background Model (GMM –UBM) 
framework [18], and the resulting scores were converted to LRs 
using logistic-regression calibration [19]. Speaker models were 
derived from a diagonal -covariance GMM–UBM using 
mean-only MAP adaptation, with 256 Gaussian components. 
Owing to the nature of BLCC features, embedding -based 
systems cannot be used directly, as no suitable pre -trained 
model currently exists for BLCCs.  
System performance was assessed using the log-LR cost 
𝐶$$%, its calibration component 𝐶&’$
$$%, and the equal-error rate 
(𝐸𝐸𝑅) reported for reference only. All metrics were computed 
independently for each sub-band. Each rotation of the test set 
produced 54 same-speaker and 2,862 different -speaker 
comparisons. Experiment 1 examined how 
speaker-discriminative information is distributed across the 
spectrum of running speech by evaluating FVC performance on 
a sub-band by sub-band basis. Sub-bands containing stronger 
speaker information were expected to produce lower 𝐶$$% 
values. Experiment 2 then combined the LRs derived from 
these individual sub-bands, systematically fusing them in 
different combinations and comparing the resulting systems 
with a full band LFCC baseline. This allows identification of 
the most informative sub-band groupings.

Regardless of the sub-band widths (0.6 kHz or 1.2 kHz), all 
sub-bands yield 𝐶$$% values below one, indicating that each 
sub-band carries useful speaker-discriminative information. 
The 𝐶$$% values from the 1.2 kHz sub-bands (Figure 2b) are 
consistently lower than corresponding those from the 0.6 kHz 
sub-bands (Figure 2a), which is expected because the wider 
sub-bands capture a larger portion of the spectral content , 
resulting in better performance. The variation in 𝐶$$% values 
demonstrates that speaker -discriminative information is 
unevenly distributed across the spectrum, consistent with 
observations reported in prior studies [2, 7, 10-14, 20].  
Another consistent pattern across both sub-band widths is 
the upward trend in 𝐶$$% values as the sub-band frequencies 
approach the upper end of the spectrum (8 kHz), with this 
increase beginning at around 4.5 kHz. In both panels of 
Figure 2, the sub-bands located within the final 1 kHz display 
noticeably higher 𝐶$$% values than those in the lower-frequency 
regions. The highest values occur in the final sub -band—
0.82571 in Figure 2a and 0.67020 in Figure 2b. Although these 
are the largest values observed, they remain below one, 
indicating that even the highest-frequency sub-bands still carry 
useful speaker-discriminative information.

The different sub-band widths also show different patterns 
of speaker information in the frequency range covering

allows users to freely choose both the size and the location of 
the sub-bands. The differences observed across the various 
sub-band configurations indicate that the selection of sub-band 
parameters—including their width, position, and degree of 
overlap—must be made purposefully and with care. 
As noted above, the frequency range around 4 –5 kHz 
contains a substantial amount of speaker -discriminative 
information. This finding is consistent with previous work 
employing filter-bank-based sub-bands [10-14], which has also 
shown that the mid-to-high frequency region (approximately 3–
6 kHz) is among the most speaker-informative portions of the 
spectrum. The prominence of this region is often attributed to 
speaker-specific physiological characteristics and the acoustic 
consequences of unvoiced fricatives and burst spectra. That 
said, although fricatives have been shown to carry useful 
speaker-specific information in FVC studies [25-28], other 
segments—such as vowels and nasals [29, 30]—are generally 
considered to be more speaker-informative than fricatives and 
stops [31]. Thus, the precise reason why this spectral region 
contains substantial speaker information remains only partially 
understood. This warrants further investigation.

4.2. Experiment 2 
While Experiment 1 identified where speaker -sensitive 
sub-bands are located and how strongly they contribute to 
discrimination independently from each other, it is equally 
important to understand how these sub-bands as groups perform 
relative to the full-band CC system. Experiment 2 addressed 
this by taking the LRs produced from individual sub-bands and 
systematically fusing them. Experiment 2 is based on the 
sub-band width of 0.6 kHz. This fusion-based analysis provides 
deeper insight into how speaker-discriminative information is 
distributed across the spectrum and how overall FVC 
performance evolves as additional sub-bands are incorporated. 
The analysis considers fusion combinations involving up to 
four sub-bands; combinations beyond this were not examined 
due to computational constraints.  
Figure 4, which presents the 𝐶$$%	values of the 
best-performing fused sub-band combinations, illustrates how 
fusing LRs from multiple sub-bands enhances overall FVC 
performance relative to the full-band LFCC baseline (𝐶$$%= 
0.13643). Notable improvements are observed when four 
sub-bands are fused, yielding 𝐶$$% values that are substantially 
lower than those achieved by any individual sub-band.

otherwise, the fusion of those sub-bands would not lead to a 
performance improvement. 
5. Conclusion and future work 
Exploiting the flexibility of the BLCC method, a series of 
LR-based FVC experiments was conducted using BLCC 
matrices extracted by scanning the entire 0 –8 kHz spectral 
range with two sub-band widths: 0.6 kHz and 1.2 kHz. The aim 
was to locate speaker-sensitive frequency regions and quantify 
the amount of speaker-discriminative information encoded 
within each sub-band. Although all sub-bands carry some 
degree of speaker-specific information (𝐶$$%< 1), clear 
differences in informativeness are observed. In particular, the 
results from the 0.6 kHz sub-bands show that frequencies above 
approximately 7 kHz contain the weakest speaker information, 
whereas the 0–0.6 kHz and 4–6 kHz regions exhibit the strong 
speaker-discriminative cues. These findings suggest the 
possibility of improving system performance by training 
models with different weights assigned to different sub-bands. 
This study is based on approximately 30 seconds of running 
speech produced by native male speakers of Australian English. 
Given that phonemic inventories differ across languages, the 
distribution of speaker-specific information across the spectrum 
is also likely to vary cross-linguistically. Examining additional 
languages is therefore essential for determining the extent to 
which these patterns are language-specific and for identifying 
any potential universal tendencies.  
Moreover, the present work focuses exclusively on male 
speakers, and the duration of the speech samples was selected 
arbitrarily. Because physiological differences between sexes—
particularly those related to the size and mass of the vocal 
tract—can influence the acoustic realisation of speech, it is 
important to extend this investigation to female speakers and to 
speech samples of different lengths.
