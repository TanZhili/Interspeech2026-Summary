Abstract
Real-time speech applications demand codecs that stream with
low latency while preserving both acoustic quality and linguis-
tic content. Waveform-oriented codecs suppress fine-grained
phonetic cues at low bitrates, while semantic-aware codecs rely
on non-causal encoders or dual-branch architectures incompat-
ible with streaming. We propose LitCodec, a semantic-aware
neural speech codec that injects ASR-guided supervision di-
rectly into a fully causal encoder before quantization, embed-
ding linguistic structure into a single-codebook representation
via Finite Scalar Quantization. This avoids separate semantic
branches, enabling single-pass streaming without sacrificing se-
mantic fidelity. On LibriSpeech, LitCodec achieves the highest
PESQ and STOI among streaming codecs (PESQ 2.56, STOI
0.925) with strong semantic preservation (WER 2.8%) at 800
bps and 50 tokens per second. At 640 bps, LitCodec maintains
3.1% WER while EnCodec degrades to 29.0% at 750 bps.
Index Terms: neural speech codec, semantic supervision,
streaming, finite scalar quantization, low-latency
1. Introduction
From voice assistants to real-time translation, modern speech
applications increasingly rely on neural audio codecs to com-
press speech into compact token sequences for transmission,
storage, and language model integration. As these applications
move toward real-time interaction, there is a growing demand
forlow-latency streaming representations that preserve both
acoustic naturalness and semantic content. However, existing
neural codecs struggle to jointly satisfy these requirements.
Waveform-oriented codecs [1, 2, 3, 4, 5] achieve high per-
ceptual quality but primarily optimize for reconstruction, sup-
pressing fine-grained phonetic cues at low bitrates. Recent
semantic-aware codecs [6, 7, 8] incorporate linguistic supervi-
sion via SSL distillation or dual-branch architectures, but rely
on non-causal encoders or multi-stream synchronization incom-
patible with streaming. Meanwhile, streaming codecs such as
EnCodec [2] and TS3-Codec [9] remain waveform-oriented,
while Mimi [10] incorporates semantic distillation but requires
multiple codebook streams. This creates a fundamental divide:
no prior codec we are aware of jointly achieves semantic aware-
ness, low-latency streaming, and a single unified token stream.
We argue that this gap cannot be bridged by adding sepa-
rate semantic branches or SSL-based modules; instead, seman-
tic structure must be embeddeddirectlyinto the codec encoder
in a causal and computationally efficient manner.
We proposeLitCodec, a semantic-aware neural speech
codec designed for low-latency streaming. LitCodec in-
jects ASR-guided supervision prior to quantization, embed-
ding linguistic structure into a single-codebook representa-
tion via Finite Scalar Quantization while maintaining a fully
causal, single-path design suitable for single-pass streaming—
producing one token per input frame as audio arrives, without
requiring multi-stream synchronization.
Our contributions are threefold: (i) we show that pre-
quantization ASR supervision embeds linguistic structure more
effectively than post-quantization placement, validated through
three-way ablation; (ii) we demonstrate that FSQ produces
a unified semantic–acoustic token stream without the multi-
codebook complexity of RVQ; and (iii) we achieve state-of-the-
art PESQ, STOI, and WER among streaming codecs at both 800
and 640 bps.
Experiments on LibriSpeech demonstrate that LitCodec
achieves the highest PESQ and STOI among streaming codecs
while maintaining strong semantic preservation at just 50 to-
kens per second, validating ASR-guided supervision as a prac-
tical and effective approach for low-latency streaming speech
coding.
2. Related Work
2.1. Neural Speech Codecs
Early neural codecs such as SoundStream [1], EnCodec [2], and
DAC [3] use convolutional encoders with RVQ and adversar-
ial training, with subsequent work improving efficiency [4, 10,
5, 9]. However, these waveform-oriented codecs optimize for
acoustic reconstruction rather than linguistic structure, limiting
effectiveness for downstream speech-language modeling at low
bitrates.
2.2. Semantic-Aware Speech Coding
Several works incorporate semantic supervision into codecs,
inspired by SSL representations [11, 12, 13]. SpeechTok-
enizer [6] distills SSL features; DualCodec [7] and SAC [14]
use dual-branch architectures; and X-Codec [8] fuses semantic
and acoustic embeddings. However, these rely on dual-branch
designs or non-causal SSL encoders, making them incompati-
ble with streaming. LitCodec instead uses a single-path, fully
causal design with ASR-guided supervision before quantiza-
tion.
2.3. Streaming and Low-Latency Speech Modeling
Streaming ASR [15, 16] demonstrates that semantic reasoning
under strict latency is feasible, yet most streaming codecs [2,
9] lack semantic preservation, and those with semantic features
(e.g., Mimi [10]) require multiple codebook streams—the gap
LitCodec addresses.

3. LitCodec
As illustrated in Figure 1, LitCodec is built upon an encoder–
quantizer–decoder pipeline operating in the STFT domain.
Given input waveformx(t), we computeS in via STFT, convert
to a real-valued representation (concatenated real and imaginary
parts), and project to dimensionDvia a strided linear projection
that downsamples to the target frame rateR. A causal Con-
former [17] encoder maps this to embeddingsH∈R T ′×D,
which are discretized by FSQ into a single token streamZand
decoded back to ˜Sout by a mirrored causal Conformer decoder;
the reconstructed waveform˜x(t)is obtained via inverse STFT.
The key design principle is that linguistic structure ispreserved
through the bottleneck rather than recovered after it—avoiding
the representation fragmentation of dual-stream architectures
while eliminating the need for non-causal SSL encoders.
3.1. Pre-Quantization Semantic Supervision
Quantization acts as a compression bottleneck: structure not
explicitly present in the latent space is unlikely to survive dis-
cretization. Prior semantic-aware codecs address this via SSL
distillation or dual-branch architectures, but these rely on non-
causal encoders incompatible with streaming. We instead inject
semantic supervision before quantization using a fully causal
ASR objective. Unlike SSL-based distillation, ASR supervision
has a well-established streaming formulation—causal attention
and monotonic alignment are standard in production ASR—
allowing linguistic supervision without sacrificing causality.
The causal Conformer encoder produces frame-level em-
beddingsH=Encoder(S in)∈R T ′×D. An auxiliary
text decoder—a two-layer Conformer stack with a linear
projection—takesHas input and predicts subword tokensy.
Because this branch operates on pre-quantized features, ASR
loss gradients directly shapeHto encode linguistic content.
The text decoder is detachable at inference without reconstruc-
tion quality loss, as it only provides training gradients. To
prevent the encoder from memorizing superficial temporal pat-
terns, we apply SpecAugment-style [18] time masking to the
ASR branch input (N mask=5,p time=5%), forcing the encoder
to distribute phonetic information redundantly across frames;
masking is appliedonlyto the semantic path while the recon-
struction stream remains intact. The ASR branch is trained with
a CTC lossL sem =L CTC(ˆy,y)[19], which enforces monotonic
frame-to-token alignment without modeling output dependen-
cies, encouraging representations that support local phonetic
discrimination.
3.2. Unified Quantization with FSQ
Residual Vector Quantization (RVQ), the dominant approach in
neural codecs, introduces several complications for semantic–
acoustic unification: multiple codebook layers create implicit
hierarchies that can separate content types, commitment losses
require careful tuning, and codebook collapse remains a persis-
tent challenge [20]. Moreover, multi-codebook outputs com-
plicate integration with autoregressive language models, which
expect flat token sequences.
We instead adopt Finite Scalar Quantization (FSQ) [20],
which independently rounds each dimension of a low-
dimensional projection ofHto a small set of integer levels:
ˆHτ,k =q(H τ,k )fork=1,...,d. FSQ is fully deterministic,
requires no auxiliary losses, and is immune to codebook col-
lapse by construction—the quantization grid is fixed, so every
code is reachable. This produces asinglediscrete token stream
Z={ ˆH1,..., ˆHT ′}directly consumable by language mod-
els without interleaving multiple codebooks, preserving the uni-
fied semantic–acoustic representation that our pre-quantization
supervision establishes. The resulting vocabulary is compact
enough for efficient autoregressive modeling while rich enough
to encode both semantic and acoustic information.
3.3. Streaming Design
All components of LitCodec operate under strict causality: at-
tention in every Conformer layer is masked to attend only to

past frames, and all convolutions use causal padding. Unlike ap-
proaches that distill from non-causal teachers, our semantic su-
pervision is applied to causal encoder outputs, ensuring tokens
are produced synchronously as audio arrives. A causal archi-
tecture alone does not guarantee robust streaming performance;
models trained on full-length utterances degrade when operat-
ing on short chunks, while training only on fixed chunks sacri-
fices representational capacity. To bridge this gap, we employ
dynamic chunk training [21]: each step samples full-sequence
context with probability 0.2, otherwise drawing a chunk size
uniformly from{4,8,12,16}frames. This exposes the model
to variable context lengths, enabling a single model to serve
both streaming and offline modes.
Given a chunk sizeC(in frames) and codec frame rateR
(Hz), the algorithmic latency per chunk isτ=C/R. With
C=4frames atR=50Hz, this yields 80 ms latency, suitable
for interactive applications such as live captioning or streaming
speech-to-speech translation.
3.4. Training
While semantic supervision preserves linguistic content, it does
not guarantee perceptually natural reconstruction. We adopt the
multi-scale mel spectral lossL recon from SoundStream [1] com-
bining L1 and log-scaled L2 terms across multiple STFT res-
olutions, and employ a Multi-Period Discriminator [22] with
a Multi-Scale STFT Discriminator [2] following BigCodec [4]
and TS3-Codec [9], trained with the LSGAN objective. The
generator lossLG =λ reconLrecon +λadvLadv +λsemLsem balances
spectral fidelity, perceptual naturalness, and linguistic preserva-
tion with weightsλ recon=2,λ adv=1,λ sem=0.5. A codec opti-
mized purely for reconstruction will encode whatever features
minimize spectral error, often suppressing phonetic distinctions
that are perceptually subtle but linguistically critical; the seman-
tic term explicitly counteracts this.
4. Experiments
We evaluate LitCodec against state-of-the-art neural audio
codecs on LibriSpeech test-clean. To our knowledge, LitCodec
is theonlycodec that simultaneously supports causal stream-
ing inference, preserves semantic structure through explicit
pre-quantization supervision, and produces a single unified to-
ken stream suitable for downstream speech-language modeling.
Baseline results are from original publications; some baselines
(e.g., DualCodec, BigCodec) were trained on larger or multilin-
gual datasets, which may favor their performance.
4.1. Setup
We train LitCodec on LibriHeavy [23], a large-scale ASR
corpus containing approximately 50,000 hours of read En-
glish speech derived from LibriV ox audiobooks, resampled
to 16 kHz, and evaluate on LibriSpeech [24] test-clean. We
compare against 8 baselines spanning acoustic codecs (En-
Codec [2], BigCodec [4], WavTokenizer [5]), semantic-aware
codecs (SpeechTokenizer [6], DualCodec [7], X-Codec [8]),
and streaming codecs (TS3-Codec [9], Mimi [10]).
We train two variants: LitCodec-V1 at 800 bps (50 Hz) and
V2 at 640 bps (40 Hz). Both use 4 Conformer layers (D=1024,
FFN 4096, 8 heads, kernel 9) for encoder and decoder, with a 2-
layer text decoder projecting to vocabulary size 1024. V1 uses
STFT window/hop of 400/160 samples (100 Hz, downsampled
to 50 Hz); V2 uses 500/200 (80 Hz, downsampled to 40 Hz).
Both variants project the encoder output to 6 dimensions
with FSQ levels[8,8,8,5,5,5], yielding 64,000 codebook en-
tries (≈16 bits per token). At 50 Hz this produces 800 bps
(V1), and at 40 Hz this yields 640 bps (V2). We apply quantiza-
tion noise dropout with probability 0.5 during training follow-
ing [20]. The text decoder uses a SentencePiece tokenizer [25]
with 1024 subword units trained on LibriHeavy transcripts. We
use AdamW (β1=0.9,β 2=0.95) with OneCycleLR scheduling
(peak learning rate1×10 −5, 1% warmup) over 1M steps on 8
NVIDIA H100 (80 GB) GPUs with mixed precision (bf16) and
batch size 16 per GPU. Following TS3-Codec [9], we assess
semantic preservationvia WER using HuBERT-Large [11];
acoustic fidelityvia wide-band PESQ [26] and STOI [27]; and
naturalness and speaker identityvia UTMOS [28] and SPK-
SIM using WavLM [29] embeddings.

4.3. Ablation Study
We conduct ablation experiments to isolate the contribution of
each design choice. Table 2 presents results on LibriSpeech
test-clean.
Effect of ASR-guided supervision.Removing seman-
tic supervision causes the largest degradation: WER increases
from 2.8% to 3.5% (25% relative increase), PESQ drops from
2.56 to 2.42, and STOI from 0.925 to 0.911. In contrast, SPK-
SIM and UTMOS remain relatively stable (0.77→0.75 and
3.62→3.57, respectively), consistent with the fact that speaker
identity and naturalness are primarily governed by the recon-
struction and adversarial objectives rather than the CTC se-
mantic loss. This selective impact confirms that ASR super-
vision specifically targets linguistic preservation without inter-
fering with acoustic fidelity. Notably, the WER of 3.5% closely
matches TS3-Codec (3.6%), which uses a similar architecture
without semantic supervision—confirming that the improve-
ment stems directly from ASR-guided training rather than ar-
chitectural differences.
Effect of supervision placement.Moving ASR supervi-
sion to after quantization degrades WER from 2.8% to 3.2% and
PESQ from 2.56 to 2.45. The three-way comparison is reveal-
ing: post-quantization supervision (WER 3.2%) improves over
no supervision (3.5%) but remains far from pre-quantization
placement (2.8%). Meanwhile, the post-quantization variant’s
PESQ (2.45) is close to the no-supervision baseline (2.42), indi-
cating that supervision after the bottleneck has limited ability to
reshape encoder representations. Pre-quantization placement,
by contrast, improves both reconstruction (PESQ +0.11) and in-
telligibility (WER−0.4% absolute) over the post-quantization
variant—validating our core architectural insight that the en-
coder must be directly supervised before discretization.
Effect of SpecAugment masking.Removing time mask-
ing increases WER from 2.8% to 3.1% and reduces PESQ from
2.56 to 2.51. Without masking, the text decoder can rely on
narrow temporal windows, allowing the encoder to concentrate
phonetic information in isolated frames rather than distributing
it broadly. The masking strategy breaks this shortcut, forcing
redundant encoding across frames and improving robustness to
the temporal quantization inherent in chunk-based streaming.
5. Conclusion
We presented LitCodec, a semantic-aware neural speech codec
that bridges the gap between low-latency streaming and lin-
guistic preservation. By injecting ASR-guided supervision di-
rectly into a causal encoder prior to quantization, LitCodec em-
beds robust phonetic structure without non-causal dependen-
cies or dual-branch architectures. These results validate pre-
quantization semantic supervision as a highly effective founda-
tion for efficient, real-time semantic-acoustic modeling.
Our evaluation is limited to objective metrics on English
clean speech (LibriSpeech); subjective listening tests (e.g.,
MUSHRA) and evaluation on noisy, spontaneous, or multilin-
gual speech remain important future work, as does validation
on downstream tasks such as TTS and spoken language un-
derstanding. We also note that LitCodec’s low-latency, high-
fidelity capabilities could potentially be misused for real-time
voice manipulation; responsible deployment should incorporate
safeguards such as audio watermarking and provenance track-
ing.
