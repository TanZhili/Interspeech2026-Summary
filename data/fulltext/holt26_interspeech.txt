Talker Discrimination and Identification in 7-12-year-old Children: Effects of 
Talker Gender and Phonological Ability 
Rebecca Holt1, Parisa McGirr1, Chi Yhun Lo1, Anita Szakay1 
1 Macquarie University, Australia

Abstract 
Processing talker information is crucial for speech 
comprehension. Talker discrimination and identification 
develop over childhood but may be challenging for some 
clinical populations of children, affecting communication and 
quality of life. To support fut ure work in clinical populations, 
this study addressed three open questions about talker 
processing in typically-developing children aged 7–12, using a 
novel paradigm to assess talker identification. We asked 
whether children process pairs of female vs pairs of male voices 
similarly, to what extent discrimination and identification 
performance correlate and whether phonological skills predict 
children’s performance. Children showed similar accuracy for 
male and female talker pairs. Discrimination and identification 
were moderately-to-strongly correlated. Phonological working 
memory predicted identification accuracy. These findings 
validate our talker identification pa radigm and will facilitate 
future research in clinical populations. 
Index Terms: children, sociophonetics, talker discrimination, 
talker identification, phonological awareness, working memory 
1. Introduction 
Speech perception requires listeners to accommodate 
substantial variability across talkers. Differences in vocal tract 
anatomy, habitual pitch, speech rate, and sociolinguistic style 
introduce systematic acoustic variation, yet listeners are adept 
at both understanding linguistic content and identifying who is 
speaking. This sensitivity to both linguistic and indexical 
information is central to models of sociophonetic processing, 
which posit that talker -specific detail is encoded alongside 
phonological structure in memory, e.g., [1,2]. 
Talker processing is therefore a critical skill for children, 
facilitating, e.g., speech -in-speech perception [3] and 
integration of sociophonetic information in sentence 
comprehension [4], essential for effective communication, 
socialisation and learning. Difficulties in talker processing have 
been reported for clinical populations, including children who 
are D/deaf or Hard -of-Hearing [5] and children with 
Developmental Language Disorder [6]. Poor talker processing 
may contribute to downstream communication difficulties, with 
substantial consequences for quality of life. 
However, before examining talker processing in clinical 
populations, several open questions about the development of 
talker processing skills in typically-developing children must be 
addressed. These include (1) how well children process talker 
differences between two male talkers (compared to two female 
talkers or one male and one female talker), (2) the strength of 
the relationship between two talker processing subskills: talker 
discrimination and identification, and (3) how children’s 
developing phonological skills relate to their talker processing 
performance. This study aims to address these questions to 
provide a fuller picture of the development of talker processing 
under typical conditions. It will also validate a novel child -
friendly approach to assessing talker identification with a 
modified ABX paradigm, reducing task duration and learning 
requirements, for use in future studies with clinical populations. 
1.1. Development of talker processing skills 
Talker processing encompasses both talker discrimination and 
talker identification. Discrimination involves telling talkers 
apart, commonly assessed using an AX paradigm where 
listeners judge whether two speech tokens were produced by 
the same or different talkers. Identification refers to recognition 
of familiar talkers (“telling together”), typically assessed by 
training listeners to associate voices with talker identities and 
then assessing their ability to map novel stimuli to those talker 
identities in  a test phase. These skills differ in cognitive 
demand. Discrimination may be achieved through immediate 
acoustic comparison or use of salient perceptual cues such as 
gender. Identification  instead requires listeners to form and 
maintain stable talker representations and map variable input 
onto a consistent identity label. Identification may thus place 
greater demands on representational stability and memory [7,8]. 
This distinction between discrimination and identification 
is seen in development. Infants can discriminate their mother’s 
voice from an unfamiliar female talker prior to birth [9] and can 
discriminate pairs of unfamiliar female talkers by 4 months, 
even though these same-gender talker pairs include voices with 
overlapping acoustic characteristics [10,11]. Disc rimination 
continues to improve with age, with 7–9-year-olds, 10–12-year-
olds and adults showing progressively greater accuracy [12].  
Talker identification seems to develop relatively later. By 
16–17 months, infants can identify talkers by their voices, but 
only if they differ in gender (i.e., successful identification in 
male-female (M -F) talker pairs but not female -female (F -F) 
pairs) [7]. By 3 –6 years, ch ildren can identify talkers in M -F 
talker pairs as accurately as adults, although they are still 
substantially less accurate for F -F pairs [13]. Accuracy in 
identifying voices in F -F-F triplets continues to increase 
between ages 6 and 11 [14]. Talker processing skills thus 
gradually refine across childhood, with discrimination skill 
preceding the more cognitively demanding identification. 
However, while it is known that children can discriminate 
and identify talkers better in M-F than F-F pairs, only one study 
to date has examined talker discrimination in male-male (M-M) 
pairs, and in that study participants’ accuracy on F-F and M-M 
pairs w ere not statistically compared [6]. No studies of 
children’s talker identification have included M -M pairs. 
Children might perform differently on M-M vs F-F talker pairs 
due to potential acoustic and perceptual asymmetries. Male and 
female voices occupy pa rtially distinct acoustic spaces  and

1.2. Phonological effects on talker processing 
Talker identification may be linked to phonological processing 
skills. Adults with dyslexia, who exhibit phonological 
processing difficulties, show difficulties in talker identification, 
suggesting that phonological representations contribute to 
forming and maintaining talker identity representations [15]. 
Both phonological awareness (PA), indexed by elision (ability 
to remove individual phonemes from words), and phonological 
working memory (PWM), indexed by nonword repetition, 
predicted talker identificati on in that sample. PWM also 
predicts talker identification, but not discrimination, for non -
dyslexic adults [8]. Better PWM seems to facilitate talker 
processing when the task requires accessing stored talker 
representations but does not significantly cont ribute when the 
task can be achieved through immediate acoustic comparison. 
The relationship between PWM and talker identification is 
also observed in children aged 6 –11 [14]. Further, substantial 
improvements in children’s talker identification (specifically in 
their native language, where the phonology is familiar) occurs 
between 5 and 6 years [16,17], coinciding with improvements 
in PA at the onset of formal reading instruction. Together, these 
findings suggest a significant role for phonological skills, 
particularly PWM, in talker identification across the lifespan.  
Effects of phonological ability are particularly pertinent 
when considering future research with clinical populations. 
Populations experiencing talker processing difficulties also 
tend to lag behind typically -developing peers in phonological 
development [18,19]. Understanding the relationship between 
talker processing and phonological abilities in typically -
developing children is essential for later examining  whether 
phonological abilities contribute to talker processing 
difficulties in clinical populations. 
1.3. The present study 
To provide a strong basis for future research with clinical 
populations and to validate our experimental approach, the 
present study investigated three open questions regarding talker 
processing abilities in typical development.  First, we asked 
whether M-M talker pairs would be processed as accurately as 
F-F talker pairs. We hypothesised that M-M and F-F talker pairs 
may differ in discriminability/identifiability for children due to 
acoustic and perceptual asymmetries between male and female 
talkers. Second, we asked to what extent varianc e in 
performance would be shared between discrimination and 
identification tasks. We expected that performance on these 
tasks would be correlated, but that variability would still be 
apparent given their differing cognitive demands. Thirdly, we 
asked whether PA and/or PWM would correlate with children’s 
performance on either talker discrimination or identification. 
We hypothesised that PWM, and perhaps PA, would predict  
talker identification performance, and that this might extend to 
discrimination as well. 
These hypotheses were tested using an online AX 
discrimination task and modified ABX identification task 
administered to typically-developing 7–12-year-olds. Each task 
employed M -F, F -F and M -M talker pairings. Standardised 
assessments of elision and nonwo rd repetition quantified 
children’s PA and PWM respectively. 
By examining talker discrimination and identification in the 
same sample of children and by comparing M-F, F-F and M-M 
talker pairs, this study contributes to a more comprehensive and 
ecologically valid account of children’s developing talker 
processing skills. By examining how phonological abilities 
relate to children’s sociophonetic sensitivity, we contribute to a 
more integrated account of speech perception in development, 
where linguistic structure and talker variation are treated as 
interacting components of the same representational system. 
2. Methods 
2.1. Participants 
Twenty-nine native English -speaking 7 –12-year-olds (M age = 
10;7, SD = 1;7; 16M, 13F) participated. They had no reported 
hearing, language, cognitive or neurological disorder. One 
additional participant was excluded due to diagnosis of a 
neurodevelopmental disorder. Institutional ethical approval and 
parental written informed consent were obtained. 
2.2. Materials 
For the discrimination task, 86 sentences were created, divided 
into 43 pairs (3 practice, 40 test). All sentences were 7 –9 
syllables long. For the identification task, 27 sentences were 
created (3 practice, 24 test). All were 9–11 syllables long. 
Sixteen native Australian English-speaking adults (8M, 8F) 
were recorded producing subsets of these sentences using 
Audacity [20]. Two additional talkers (1M, 1F) recorded 
sentences to be used in practice trials. Talkers were instructed 
to speak in a natural, child -directed manner. Each talker was 
also assigned a gender-neutral name and produced three ‘talker 
introductions’ for the identification task (e.g. “Hi, I’m Riley”). 
Audio recordings were segmented in Praat [21] and the mean 
intensity of each sentence was normalised to 70 dB. 
A subset of the auditory stimuli was evaluated by 20 adult 
listeners, who rated each possible pairing of same -gender 
talkers for similarity. Talker pairings used in the study were 
selected based on these ratings to avoid highly confusable or 
highly distinctive pairings. The same talker pairings were used 
for the discrimination and identification tasks. 
2.3. Procedure 
Participants completed the task remotely. Parents completed an 
online consent form and language background questionnaire. 
Then child participants completed talker discrimination and 
identification tasks via Gorilla Experiment Builder [22]. 
Participants wer e asked to participate in a quiet location and  
adjust their device volume so the stimuli could be heard clearly 
and comfortably. To maintain engagement, a character was 
introduced who had lost their belongings. After every 8 trials 
participants ‘found’ one of the missing belongings as a reward. 
Standardised assessments of phonological skills were 
subsequently administered via a Zoom video call [23]. 
Participants received an online gift voucher for participation.

2.3.1. Talker discrimination task 
The discrimination task used an AX paradigm. Participants 
heard pairs of sentences and were asked to press a key (Q or P) 
to indicate whether the two sentences were produced by the 
same talker or by different talkers. Three practice trials 
preceded 40 test trials. These included 16 same -talker trials (8 
male, 8 female) and 24 different-talker trials (8 M-F pairs, 8 F-F 
pairs, 8 M-M pairs), presented in randomised order. 
2.3.2. Talker identification task 
The identification task used a modified ABX paradigm. In each 
trial, participants heard ‘talker introductions’ produced by two 
different talkers (e.g., “Hi, I’m Riley”, “Hey, I’m Taylor”) 
followed by a sentence produced by one of the two talkers (“I 
like to have eggs on toast for breakfast”). Participants were then 
prompted to select which talker (Riley or Taylor) produced the 
statement by answering a simple comprehension question (in 
this case, “Who likes eggs on toast?”), pressing a key (Q or P) 
to indicate their answer. Three practice trials preceded 24 test 
trials. The test trials included 8 M-F talker pairs, 8 F-F pairs and 
8 M-M pairs, presented in randomised order. 
2.3.3. Phonological skills assessment 
Following [15], participants’ PA and PWM were assessed using 
the Elision and Nonword Repetition subtests of the 
Comprehensive Test of Phonological Processing 2 nd Edition 
(CTOPP-2) [24] respectively. Responses were coded on-line by 
an experimenter following standard scoring procedures. 
2.4. Analysis 
To assess sensitivity in the discrimination task, d′ was 
computed for each participant. Hit rates reflected the proportion 
of ‘different’ trials correctly identified, and false alarm rates the 
proportion of ‘same’ trials incorrectly judged as ‘different’. 
Four children showed no evidence of discrimination sensitivity 
(d′ ≈ 0 ; a strong bias toward ‘different’ responses and an 
absence of correct rejections). These participants were excluded 
from the discrimination analysis. For identification, all  children 
performed above chance and were retained in the analysis. 
All analyses were conducted in R [25] using mixed -effects 
logistic regression models implemented in the lme4 package 
[26]. Accuracy (correct vs  incorrect) was analysed at the trial 
level using a binomial link function. 
Two models were fitted: one for discrimination and one for 
identification. In both, talker pair type, elision and nonword 
repetition were entered simultaneously as fixed effects to assess 
their independent contributions. Age was included as a 
covariate. Continuous predictors were z -scored to facilitate 
interpretation and model convergence, and random intercepts 
were included for participant. Target talker was also included 
as random effect in the identification model to account for 
talker-specific differences in identifiability. 
Finally, to examine the relationship between performance 
in the two tasks, we computed a Pearson correlation between  
identification and discrimination accuracy. As performance on 
cross-gender (M-F) trials was near ceiling, the correlation was 
tested on same-gender (F-F and M-M) trials only. 
3. Results 
3.1. Talker discrimination 
For talker discrimination there was a strong main effect of talker 
pair (Fig. 1, Panel A). Accuracy was significantly higher for 
M-F trials than both F -F trials (β = −2.483, SE = 0.469, z = 
−5.298, p < .001) and M-M trials (β = −2.643, SE = 0.468, z = 
−5.651, p < .001). Pairwise comparisons revealed the two same-
gender conditions did not significantly differ (p = .616). Neither 
nonword repetition (β = 0.055, SE = 0.128, z = 0.430, p = .667), 
elision (β = −0.091, SE = 0.146, z = −0.623, p = .533), nor age 
(β = 0.117, SE = 0.136, z = 0.854, p = .393)  significantly 
predicted discrimination performance.  
3.2. Talker identification 
For talker identification there was a robust main effect of talker 
pair (Fig. 1, Panel B). Accuracy was significantly higher for 
M-F trials than for both F-F trials (β = −2.050, SE = 0.343, z = 
−5.971, p < .001) and M-M trials (β = −2.215, SE = 0.342, z = 
−6.484, p < .001). F -F and M -M trials did not significantly 
differ from one another (p = .702).  
Nonword repetition significantly predicted identification 
accuracy (β = 0.274, SE = 0.102, z = 2.689, p = .007; Fig. 1, 
Panel C). Children with higher nonword repetition scores were

contrast, elision did not predict identification performance (β = 
0.002, SE = 0.109, z = 0.017, p = .986), nor did age (β = −0.034, 
SE = 0.105, z = −0.330, p = .741).

4. Discussion 
This study addressed three open questions about talker 
processing in typically -developing children, with a view to 
future research with clinical populations. We asked whether 
M-M talker pairs would be processed differently to F -F talker 
pairs, to what extent variance would be shared between talker 
discrimination and identification performance and whether 
phonological skills would predict discrimination and/or 
identification accur acy. This study also served to validate a 
novel method to assess talker identification in a child -friendly 
manner with reduced time and memory demands. 
4.1. Effects of talker gender 
Talker pairing significant ly impacted children’s performance. 
Both discrimination and identification were significantly more 
accurate for M-F pairs than for either same -gender pair type, 
with no reliable difference between F -F and M-M trials. As in 
prior research, differences between male and female voices 
seem to function as a salient cue facilitating both discrimination 
and identification.  
In contrast, distinguishing between same -gender talkers 
requires finer-grained acoustic differentiation and proves more 
challenging. F -F and M -M talker pairs tend to differ in their 
degree of acoustic similarity, but do not differ significantly in 
discriminability or identifiability in our study. Therefore, for 
this age range and stimulus set, gender may function primarily 
as a categorical cue for talker processing, rather than supporting 
finer-grained asymmetries between female and male voices. 
4.2. Discrimination-identification relationship  
As hypothesised, children’s talker discrimination and 
identification performance were significantly correlated. This 
relationship was moderate to strong, supporting the notion that 
these tasks tap into cognitive processes with both shared and 
unique components. To our knowledge, this is the first study to 
empirically demonstrate that children with strong talker 
discrimination skills also tend to have strong talker 
identification skills. This aligns with the characterisation of 
talker discrimination and iden tification as related but distinct 
processes that are acquired sequentially, with discrimination 
potentially acting as a prerequisite for identification. 
4.3. Phonological abilities and talker processing 
We found that PWM significantly predicted talker 
identification performance, but PA did not. Neither measure 
predicted discrimination performance. This dissociation 
suggests that phonological abilities are selectively involved in 
children’s talker identification rather than in talker processing 
more generally, aligning with prior findings from adults [8]. 
Identification requires listeners to map variable acoustic 
input onto a stable talker representation and maintain that 
mapping over time. Discrimination, on the other hand, can often 
be solved through immediate acoustic comparison. The 
selective association between nonword repetition and 
identification suggests that PWM may support the formation, 
maintenance, or retrieval of stable talker representations. 
Elision did not predict performance in either task. As 
nonword repetition and elision were included in the same 
models, this suggests that the relationship was not driven by PA 
in a broad sense. Moreover, the lack of correlation indicated that 
the two measures indexed largely independent variance.  
From a representational perspective, talker identity is 
encoded through systematic variation in phonetic detail. 
Exemplar-based accounts propose that linguistic and indexical 
information are stored together in detailed memory traces, e.g., 
[1,2]. Under suc h accounts, successful identification depends 
on the stability and accessibility of phonological 
representations capable of supporting fine -grained acoustic 
distinctions across tokens. Children with stronger PWM may be 
better able to encode and retain talk er-specific phonetic 
patterns, facilitating more accurate identity mapping. This 
aligns with developmental models of speech perception that 
emphasise the interaction between linguistic structure and 
variability across talkers. 
The differing relationships of discrimination and 
identification with PWM help to validate our novel method for 
assessing talker identification. Rather than training participants 
to recognise voices to be recalled in a later test phase, we used 
a less time - and memory -intensive ABX paradigm to assess 
more immediate talker identification. The finding that PWM is 
associated with identification only, and not discrimination, 
suggests that our AB discrimination and ABX identification 
tasks are tapping into different cognitive abilities, despite their 
superficial similarity. Prior research using a training -and-
delayed-test method has found a relationship between PWM
