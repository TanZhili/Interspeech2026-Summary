Abstract
We proposeU-Codec, anUltra low frame-rate neural speech
Codecthat achieves high-fidelity reconstruction and fast speech
generation at a frame-rate of 5Hz (5 frames per second). Ex-
treme temporal compression typically leads to severe intelligi-
bility and spectral detail loss. We overcome this by introducing
a Transformer-based inter-frame long-term dependency mod-
ule and systematically exploring residual vector quantization
(RVQ) depth and codebook size to identify optimal configura-
tions. Moreover, we apply U-Codec to a large language model
(LLM)-based auto-regressive TTS model, which leverages global
and local hierarchical architecture to effectively capture depen-
dencies across multi-layer tokens. Experiments demonstrate that
U-Codec improves LLM-based TTS inference speed by 3× over
high-frame-rate codecs while maintaining similarity and natural-
ness. These results validate the feasibility of using 5Hz discrete
tokens for fast and high-fidelity speech synthesis.
Index Terms: neural speech codec, speech LLMs, speech gener-
ation, low frame rate
1. Introduction
Neural speech codecs (NSCs) compress speech into discrete rep-
resentations and reconstruct audio from them. Traditionally used
in communication [1, 2, 3, 4], NSCs [5, 6, 7] have advanced zero-
shot text-to-speech synthesis (TTS) [ 8, 9, 10, 11, 12, 13] and
have recently emerged as essential tokenizers for large language
models (LLM)-based speech generation. These discrete tokens
allow LLMs to process acoustic and textual information within a
unified framework, establishing the foundation for cross-modal
alignment and generation. Current NSCs [ 14, 15, 16] achieve
high fidelity at low bitrates using residual vector quantization
(RVQ). However, state-of-the-art (SOTA) systems such as Sound-
Stream, EnCodec, and DAC operate at high frame rates with
50-75 frames per second (FPS), which is inefficient for auto-
regressive LLM-based TTS since each frame requires a forward
pass. For example, the DAC produces audio at 75 FPS, and TTS
needs to make at least 75 forward passes to predict one second
of audio. This leads to long token sequences and slow inference.
Reducing the frame rate shortens token sequences and ac-
celerates LLM-based TTS. However, prior work [17, 18, 19, 20]
shows that even 12.5Hz causes significant quality degradation

2. Methodology
A schematic diagram of U-Codec is shown in Figure 2. Refer-
ring to the mainstream neural speech codecs [1, 2, 24, 17], we
carefully optimized every component of U-Codec, including the
encoder, decoder, quantizer, and a HiFi-GAN-based discrimina-
tor, to achieve high-quality speech compression at 5Hz.
2.1. Structure of U-Codec
Encoder.The encoder transforms a 16kHz waveform into com-
pact latent representations through five residual convolutional
blocks with dilated and strided convolutions, along with ELU
non-linearities and weight normalization. To achieve 5Hz (i.e.,
one token per 200 ms) temporal resolution, each residual block
is followed by a strided 1D convolution layer with strides of
[8,5,5,4,4] , corresponding to 16000÷3200 = 5 Hz. The
12.5Hz version uses strides (5,4,4,4,4) for higher temporal
resolution. The encoder starts with 64 channels and doubles the
width after the final downsampling stage.
Long-term Dependency Module.At an ultra-low frame
rate of 5Hz, each token covers a long speech span, which may
degrade intelligibility and spectral fidelity. To address this, we in-
troduce a contextual Transformer bottleneck directly after down-
sampling, which consists of 8 layers, each with 8 attention heads,
RoPE position encodings, GELU activations, hidden size 512,
and MLP dimension 2048. The difference lies in the frame
rate of the input to the Transformer: MimiCodec uses a frame

Decoder.The decoder mirrors the encoder’s downsampling

RVQ Module and Quantization Rate.To improve code-
book utilization and achieve stable reconstruction, we adopt fac-
torized residual vector quantization (FRVQ). This design results
in more efficient codebook utilization and higher-quality speech
compression. Building on this foundation, we systematically
explore RVQ under extreme temporal compression conditions,
where the bitrate remains around 1kbps. Guided by the entropy
formulation S×N×log 2 C, S is the frame rate, N the number
of quantization layers and C denotes codebook size. We explore
a wide range of configurations from shallow (8 layers, large
codebooks up to 16,384) to deep (100 layers, small codebooks
down to 4). This analysis provides valuable insights into the
trade-off between reconstruction quality, token compactness, and
bitrate constraints. Finally, our design balances sequence length
reduction with sufficient acoustic detail for high-fidelity TTS.
Discriminator.To stabilize adversarial training, we adopt
discriminators from BigCodec [ 25]. Specifically, we employ
the HiFiGAN Multi-Period Discriminator (MPD) [26] and the
multi-scale short-time Fourier transform (MS-STFT) with FFT
sizes{78,126,206,334,542,876,1418,2296}[13, 27].
Training Objective.U-Codec adopts a standard loss setup
from prior neural codec frameworks, combining multi-scale mel-
spectrogram L1 reconstruction loss [2], LSGAN adversarial loss
with multi-period discriminators [ 26], feature matching loss,
and vector quantization loss with commitment term, using fixed
weights (15, 1, 1, 0.25) respectively.

3. Experiments
3.1. Speech Codec Experiments
3.1.1. Experimental Details
Dataset.Our U-Codec is trained on a large-scale multilingual
corpus containing approximately 115k hours of speech, includ-
ing LibriLight (60k hours) [29], GigaSpeech (10k hours) [30],
and Multilingual LibriSpeech (45k hours) [ 31]. All speech is
resampled to 16kHz. We evaluate reconstruction performance
on the LibriSpeech test-clean set, containing 2620 utterances,
which serves as a standard benchmark for codec assessment.
Training Setup.The codec model is trained for 600k steps
with a learning rate of 1×10 −4 and a 1000-step warmup sched-
ule. We use different codebook sizes and employ a projection
dimension of 8 in our vector quantization (VQ) module. We
employ 16 H20 GPUs with a batch size of 16 for training.

3.1.2. Results and Discussion.
Reconstruction.We evaluate U-Codec using WER 2, STOI,
PESQ, SPK-SIM 3, and UTMOS 4, comparing with SOTA
codecs including DAC, SpeechTokenizer, BigCodec, EnCodec,
WavTokenizer, Mimi, and SemanticCodec. As shown in Table 1,
the U-Codec with 32 RVQ layers at 5Hz achieves WER 3.44,
PESQ 3.20, and STOI 0.93, surpassing prior low frame-rate
codecs and matching the intelligibility of BigCodec at 80Hz
(0.93). Increasing RVQ depth from 16 to 32 layers improves
PESQ (3.02→3.20) and SPK-SIM (0.83→0.87), confirming the
effectiveness of deeper residual quantization when operating at
5Hz. Although U-Codec does not yet match the PESQ 4.15 and
SPK-SIM 0.95 reported for DAC, our method narrows the gap
and maintains strong trade-offs of quality and inference speed.
Ablation Study.We conduct ablation studies to analyze
the contributions of key components. Table 2 summarizes re-
sults under varying configurations, focusing on the impact of
RVQ depth, codebook size, and Transformer architecture. The
systematic increase in RVQ layers demonstrates clear perfor-
mance improvements. This validates our hypothesis that deeper
quantization compensates for the loss of temporal resolution at
ultra-low frame rates. Within the 8-layer configurations, increas-
ing codebook size from 8192 to 16384 improves most metrics
(WER: 5.41→5.04, PESQ: 1.95→2.07) but with diminishing
returns and a slight bitrate increase (0.52→0.56kbps). These
results highlight a clear trade-off between representational ca-
pacity and compression efficiency. Further scaling to 100 layers
provides only marginal additional benefits. Crucially, replacing
the Transformer with a convolution module leads to consistent
degradation (WER: 3.44→5.40, PESQ: 2.59→2.55, SPK-SIM:
0.87→0.84). While the drops appear moderate, they reveal a lim-

3.2. TTS Experiments
3.2.1. Experiments Details
Dataset.For TTS model training, we use Libriheavy [ 41], a
large-scale English speech dataset containing 50k hours of high-
quality audio data. TTS systems are evaluated on a subset of
LibriSpeech test-clean including 4-10 seconds utterance, con-
taining approximately 4 hours of speech data.
Training Setup.TTS models are trained for 4 epochs with a
batch size of 8k maximum tokens and a maximum learning rate
of 6×10 −4. We employ a cosine learning rate schedule with
warmup covering 3% of the max steps of 30k. During training,
text and speech tokens are concatenated and cropped to a max-
imum sequence length of 15k tokens. The global Transformer
comprises 24 layers, with an attention dimension of 1536, 12
attention heads, and a feed-forward dimension of 6144. The
local Transformer follows a similar design but with 8 layers.
For comparison, we use the SoundStream-3RVQ-c1024 code
provided by UniAudio and retrain a Codecformer TTS system
[23]. During inference, we follow the strategy of the multinomial
Top-k sampling (k=5, temperature=1.0).

3.3. Complexity Analysis
Complexity Analysis.We further use thop to compute mul-
tiply–accumulate operations (MACs) for 1-second speech seg-
ments and analyze real-time factor (RTF). RTF values are mea-
sured on a single NVIDIA H20 GPU. As shown in Table 5,
U-Codec-8RVQ-c16384 achieves an RTF of 0.52, corresponding
to a 2–3× speedup compared with SoundStream-3RVQ-c1024
(1.40) adopted in UniAudio. Importantly, U-Codec-32RVQ-
c256 achieves comparable TTS quality with only 0.89G MACs,
a reduction of over 50×. The 8RVQ and 16RVQ configurations
primarily target inference efficiency, whereas the 32RVQ con-
figuration prioritizes reconstruction quality at the expense of
additional local autoregressive decoding. Across all 5Hz con-
figurations, the global MAC cost (MAC-G) remains between
0.163–0.277G, much lower than SoundStream’s 0.906G, con-
firming that ultra low frame-rate design substantially alleviates
the computational bottleneck. Note that extremely deep RVQ
stacks (e.g., 100 layers) keep low total MACs (1.45G) but result
in larger RTFs due to increased sequential local decoding.
4. Conclusion
We introduce U-Codec, an ultra low frame rate speech codec
designed to reduce the computational complexity in LLM-based
TTS. It achieves high-quality speech compression at five frames
per second. We incorporate a Transformer-based mechanism to
handle long-term dependencies across frames. By introducing
the Codecformer network, we achieve a 3× speedup in LLM-
based TTS inference while maintaining high speech quality.
These results demonstrate the feasibility of using discrete tokens
at a 5Hz frame rate for fast, high-quality speech generation.
