The Effect of Neck Skin Vibration on the Periauricular Acoustic Receiver
Ruoyan Li1, Yuhao Sun1, Fan Fan1,∗∗
1 Central Media Technology Institute, Huawei, China
liruoyan1@huawei.com, sunyuhao4@huawei.com, fanfan1@huawei.com
Abstract
During self-voicing, vocal cord vibrations propagate through
the vocal tract and surrounding tissues, generating air-borne
acoustic components radiated from the oral cavity as well as
structural vibrations in the neck and its surface. This study in-
vestigates the impact of neck skin vibrations on periauricular
acoustic receivers during self-voicing. The recordings were ac-
quired under self-speech conditions using an array of micro-
phones positioned at various locations on the head. Compara-
tive analyses were conducted using experiments with a dummy
head, real human speech tests and simulations. The results
showed that periauricular acoustic receivers located near the
neck detected significant air-borne acoustic components asso-
ciated with neck skin vibrations. This finding holds signif-
icant implications for refining self-speech simulation models
that treat mouth radiation as the sole acoustic source.
Index Terms: self-voicing, neck skin vibration, periauricular
microphones
1. Introduction
Human speech production begins with vocalization, where air-
flow from the lungs drives the vibration of the vocal folds, cre-
ating a quasi-periodic sound source. The generated sound prop-
agates through the vocal tract, where its spectrum is shaped by
resonances determined by articulatory configuration [1]. While
the radiated speech is commonly treated as an airborne acoustic
signal emitted primarily from the mouth and to a lesser extent
the nasal cavity, speech production also induces structural vi-
bration in the surrounding biological tissues. The vocal tract
walls, laryngeal framework, and adjacent soft tissues can be ex-
cited by periodic glottal source and pressure fluctuations within
the tract.
Because the larynx and lower vocal tract are anatomically
close to the surface of neck, vibrations of internal tissues can
be transmitted to the skin, resulting in measurable vibrations
on the surface of neck during vocalization. Previous research
in speech science and biomedical sensing has shown that ac-
celerometers or contact sensors mounted on the neck can cap-
ture vibrations associated with vocalization with high signal-to-
noise ratios, which reflect the fundamental frequency compo-
nent and its harmonics [2, 3, 4, 5]. These skin vibrations provide
an additional pathway for speech-related energy to reach wear-
able devices that are in contact with or mechanically coupled to
the head and neck.
Periauricular acoustic receivers—i.e., microphones or
acoustic sensors located on or around the ear such as in-ear,
behind-the-ear, or ear-hook devices—are widely used in hear-

ing aids, audio devices, and speech acquisition systems. During
vocalization, air-borne sound waves caused by neck skin vibra-
tions may modulate the response of microphones, introducing
low-frequency artifacts and distorting spectral features. The air-
borne components caused by skin vibrations must be combined
with air-borne speech signals, which complicates tasks such as
acoustic modeling of self-generated speech.
Although some studies have investigated the relation of
neck vibrations with the sound pressure levels of speech mea-
sured by contact microphones or accelerometers [2, 3, 4, 5],
the specific impact of neck vibrations on periauricular acoustic
receivers during vocalization has not been fully explored. Re-
vealing this coupling relationship is crucial for improving the
design of wearable audio devices and for developing self-voice
separation algorithms that can distinguish self-generated speech
from others’ speech.
This study investigates the effects of neck skin vibrations
on periauricular acoustic receivers during vocalization. We (i)
compared results from dummy head vocalization experiments
with BEM simulations, (ii) contrasted the experimental results
of real human vocalization with simulation results, and (iii) dis-
cussed the findings in terms of their implications for the influ-
ence of neck vibrations on the sound pickup by periauricular
microphones. Thess results demonstrate the vibro-acoustic cou-
pling pathways affecting speech recording around the ear.
2. Method
2.1. Experimental setup and measurements
2.1.1. Dummy-head measurement with mouth playback
A GRAS KEMAR Head & Torso equipped with a mouth sim-
ulator was used to obtain an ideal radiation condition that em-
ulates self-voicing without inter-trial variability. All playback
and recordings were made using the RME Fireface 802 audio
interface with a sampling rate of 48 kHz. The Fireface 802
output drove the mouth simulator with a linear sweep excita-
tion spanning 20 Hz–20 kHz. Four microphones were recorded
simultaneously: two periauricular microphones placed around
the ear region (see Figure 1 for the geometric layout), one mi-
crophone on the forehead, and one microphone in front of the
mouth simulator acting as a near-mouth acoustic reference. The
microphones on the periauricular area and forehead were placed
very close to the surface.
2.1.2. Human-subject recording with neck vibration sensing
Two participants (S1–S2) were recorded during stable voiced
speech. Each subject was instructed to continuously read a short
passage for at least 30 s while maintaining a steady vocal effort.

2.2. Boundary Element Method (BEM) simulation
To model the exterior acoustic field of self-voicing in free
space, simulations were carried out using the frequency-domain
boundary element method (BEM). The head geometries were
obtained from 3D scanning and retained pinna details. The
surface was discretized using triangular constant elements. A
hard-wall boundary condition was imposed on the head sur-
face. Self-voicing excitation was modeled via a prescribed uni-
form pressure boundary condition of 1Paapplied over a de-
fined mouth region, providing a normalized source for transfer-
function evaluation. Under linear time-invariant assumptions,
this response is equivalent to the Fourier transform of an im-
pulse response.
The self-developed GPU-accelerated BEM solver was val-
idated against the COMSOL BEM solver using a sphere-
scattering benchmark with an analytical solution. A rigid (hard-
wall) sphere of radius 0.1 m was excited by an incident plane
wave, and the sound pressure was evaluated at a field point lo-
cated 0.1 m from the sphere surface. Figure 3 shows that the
self-devoloped fast BEM solver achieves comparable accuracy
while being significantly faster than COMSOL.
Due to computational cost and mesh-resolution constraints,
the frequency sweep for the human-head simulations was lim-
ited to 0–8 kHz with a step size of 125 Hz. For meshes with
fewer than 20,000 elements, the sweep required approximately
30 minutes per head model.

3. Results and Discussion
3.1. Dummy-head measurement vs. BEM simulation
In the dummy-head experiment, the loudspeaker was placed in-
side the mouth cavity, so the generated sound field is domi-
nated by a single controlled acoustic source radiating through
the mouth opening. This configuration matches the BEM simu-
lation that the mouth is the only acoustic source. Therefore, the
dummy-head dataset provides a direct validation case for the
BEM model under conditions where structural vibration of the
neck skin is absent.
Figure 4 plots the measured and BEM-simulated complex
RTFs for the multi-channel microphones. Overall, the simula-
tion reproduces the main spectral characteristics observed in the
measurement. In particular, the forehead microphone shows a
close agreement with the BEM prediction across the analyzed
frequency range in both magnitude and phase, indicating that
the source modeling and the global head-related propagation are
captured reliably at this location. For the microphones located
adjacent to the ear canal and beneath the pinna, the measured re-
sponses follow the same overall frequency-dependent trends as
the simulation, but exhibit noticeably larger deviations relative
to the forehead channel. The agreement is generally better at
low frequencies, whereas discrepancies increase toward higher
frequencies.
This pattern is consistent with the fact that the periauricular
region is dominated by fine, highly local scattering and diffrac-
tion effects that are more difficult than forehead to model and
to reproduce experimentally. At low frequencies, the acoustic
wavelength is large compared with the geometric details of the
pinna; consequently, the sound field varies more smoothly in
space and is less sensitive to small geometric perturbations. Un-
der these conditions, both the BEM model and the experimen-
tal setup are more likely to yield similar responses. At higher

3.2. Human measurement vs. BEM simulation
Unlike simulated sources or dummy heads capable of generat-
ing full-bandwidth audio from 20 Hz to 20 kHz, human speech
exhibits a naturally constrained frequency range. Therefore, a
spectral analysis was performed on the stable speech recordings
collected from the participants. Figure 5(a) indicates a favorable
signal-to-noise ratio above 200 Hz, negligible energy beyond
4000 Hz, and salient components below 1 kHz.
Figure 6 plots the magnitude and phase of the measured
and simulated RTFs across the 0–4 kHz range. The micro-
phone on forehead exhibits strong agreement with simulation
in both magnitude and phase, which is consistent with the re-
sults obtained from the dummy-head experiment. For periau-
ricular microphones, phase alignment is comparatively better
at low frequencies, and the overall frequency-dependent phase
trend remains consistent with simulation at higher frequen-
cies. However, a noticeable magnitude discrepancy is observed.

The measured periauricular signals exhibit additional en-
ergy not captured by the BEM simulation. This discrepancy
aligns with expectations regarding neck skin vibration contribu-
tions. Receiver locations nearer the neck display the most pro-
nounced elevation compared to simulation, whereas positions
farther away show smaller deviations and better agreement with
oral radiation only models. This spatial trend strongly suggests
the presence of a neck-related source mechanism absent in the
current acoustic model.
Specifically, the excess energy appears predominantly at
low frequencies. Prior work indicates that skin vibration are
most pronounced below approximately 2 kHz [6, 7, 8]. Given
these physical limitations and our experimental findings, we de-
duce that neck vibration plays a critical role in the frequency
band below 1 kHz.
4. Conclusion and Future Work
This paper examined the of neck-skin vibration on periauric-
ular acoustic reception by comparing measurements acquired
on an dummy head and on human subjects. The results show
that vibration of the neck surface can generate secondary air-
borne sound that contributes measurably to the signals captured
by near-ear microphones. The effect is strongly dependent on
receiver location: microphones positioned closer to the neck
exhibit larger vibration-induced contributions. This coupling is
most pronounced at low frequencies, where the radiated acous-
tic component driven by neck vibration is relatively stronger.
Overall, the findings indicate that neck vibration constitutes
a non-negligible excitation pathway for self-produced speech
recordings around the ear and should be accounted for when
analyzing, modeling, or synthesizing periauricular signals.
Several factors limit the generality of the present conclu-
sions. First, the number of human subjects is limited, and inter-
subject variability in neck geometry, tissue properties, and ar-
ticulation can affect the magnitude of vibro-acoustic coupling.
Second, the sensors used were not calibrated, and its inherent
noise floor may affect the accuracy of the measurements, par-
ticularly for measuring normal speech from a real person, which
is not very steady and loud. Finally, the current study focuses on

Future work will focus on enabling quantitatively accurate
vibro-acoustic simulations and on generating high-fidelity, re-
producible self-speech transfer functions for wearable audio de-
vices. A primary next step is to calibrate the vibration sensor
on neck to obtain absolute neck-surface acceleration or equiva-
lent normal velocity as a function of frequency. This calibration
will allow the measured vibration to be used as a physically
grounded boundary condition in BEM-based acoustic simula-
tions. In addition, the effects of skin impedance and radiation at
the nasal opening will also be further considered.
Finally, by providing precise near-ear self-speech simula-
tions under acoustically plausible conditions, these studies can
support applications such as the efficient generation of realistic
training data, thereby accelerating the development of AI-based
directional voice pickup and speech enhancement algorithms
that can be widely used in various types of wearable acoustic
devices.
