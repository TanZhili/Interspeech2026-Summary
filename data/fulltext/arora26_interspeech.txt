Abstract
What happens when a text-to-audio (T2A) model is asked
to generate silence or exclude a specific sound? Existing mod-
els often generate the very events they are instructed to omit.
Negation comprehension is critical for faithful audio genera-
tion, yet no benchmark has systematically measured it. We
introduceAudio Negation Benchmark, comprising one million
negated prompts spanning four negation types and three nega-
tion scopes, derived from AudioCaps captions. Human an-
notation of a representative subset confirms that the intended
negation is correctly introduced in 99.6% of prompts. To
evaluate negation understanding at scale, we propose an Au-
dio Question Answering (AQA) protocol that probes the pres-
ence or absence of negated events using multiple-choice ques-
tions, scored against the ground-truth absence label. Evaluat-
ing three state-of-the-art models, AudioGen, AudioLDM2, and
TangoFlux, we find that AQA recall for negated audio falls
below 0.05 across all negation types and all models: negated
and affirmative prompts produce acoustically near-identical out-
puts. Re-captioning the generated audio independently con-
firms that negative prompts consistently default to affirmative
soundscapes. These results establish negation handling as a fun-
damental open problem in multimodal generative AI and moti-
vate negation-aware training objectives for semantically faithful
audio generation.

1. Introduction
Negation is fundamental to how humans communicate con-
straints. “There are no gunshots.” “The crowd is silent.” “No
sirens are heard.” These are natural, everyday descriptions.
Yet text-to-audio (T2A) generation models cannot honor them.
When given a negated prompt, current systems produce the very
sounds they are told to exclude - generating gunshots when ex-
plicitly told there are none, and sirens when told the area is
quiet. Figure 1 illustrates this failure: across multiple prompts
and models, negative constraints such as “no,” “quiet,” and
“without” are systematically ignored, while the negated sound
events are faithfully generated.
This is not an isolated failure, rather it is a systematic bias.
At the deployment scale, T2A models generate thousands of au-
dio clips automatically. Even a small negation failure rate prop-
agates erroneous outputs across pipelines, making manual veri-
fication infeasible. The consequences are significant: A training
simulation that generates gunfire in a scene specified as silent

What makes negation in audio uniquely challenging is the
nature of the task. In natural language processing, negation
benchmarks test logical contradiction between sentences [1].
In vision, negation typically means removing a discrete object
from a scene [2]. Audio is different. Negating a sound event re-
quires suppressing a specific acoustic pattern within a continu-
ous, ambient soundscape, while leaving the rest of the scene in-
tact. Existing NLP benchmarks cannot be directly repurposed;
a linguistically valid negation pair like “The company’s stock
value increased” versus “The company’s stock value decreased”
is acoustically meaningless. No existing T2A benchmark ad-
dresses this gap.
The root cause runs deeper than benchmarks. Standard
audio-caption datasets like AudioCaps [3], Clotho [4], and Au-
dioSet [5] describe what is present in a scene. They contain
little to no representation of absent or negated sound events.
Models trained on such data develop a strong affirmation bias:
they learn to generate sounds associated with the words in a
prompt, without effectively processing negation operators. A
prompt like ”no dog barking” activates the acoustic features of
dog barking, because the model attends to the entity rather than
its exclusion. These observations motivate a systematic study
of negation in T2A generation, supported by a benchmark and
evaluation protocols designed specifically for the audio setting.

•Audio Negation Benchmark. We introduce a large-scale
dataset of one million systematically constructed negated
prompts, built from AudioCaps captions. The dataset spans
four types of negation (lexical, syntactic, semantic, and

2. Related Work
To contextualize the challenges of negation-aware audio gener-
ation, this section reviews three critical areas. First, we examine
how negation is handled across broader multimodal generative
models to establish a theoretical baseline. Second, we analyze
the architectural paradigms of current T2A generation models
to understand how their underlying encoding mechanisms pro-
cess (or fail to process) semantic constraints. Finally, we dis-
cuss the inherent biases in audio training datasets that funda-
mentally limit a model’s ability to learn logical exclusion.
Negation in Multimodal Generative Models.Negation
has traditionally been a difficult challenge to overcome in nat-
ural language understanding tasks. Prior work has shown that
large language models often underestimate the impact of nega-
tion in language understanding [6, 7]. To address this limita-
tion, the CANNOT dataset [1] was introduced, consisting of en-
tailing and contradicting pairs specifically designed to capture
the negation phenomenon. This dataset was used to fine-tune
the BLEURT evaluation metric [8], with the goal of improving
the negation awareness of large language models. This phe-
nomenon is also identified in multimodal settings. Zhang et al.
[9] introduced the NegVQA benchmark, comprising two-choice
questions designed to evaluate negation comprehension in Vi-
sion Language Models (VLMs). By using large language mod-
els to systematically negate existing Visual Question Answering
(VQA) questions and inverting the truth values of their corre-
sponding answer choices, the authors demonstrated a significant
degradation in VLM performance. Their findings reveal that
state-of-the-art models consistently struggle to process linguis-
tic negation accurately within visual contexts. The challenge of
negation is also a well-investigated problem within the domain
of text-to-image generation [10]. To systematically evaluate and
mitigate these failure modes, researchers have developed spe-
cialized benchmarks comprising diverse image-caption pairs.
Datasets such as CC-Neg [2] and T2I-CompBench [11] have
been introduced to explicitly test models against a wide array of
negation types, spanning morphological, syntactic, and seman-
tic constraints, embedded within complex textual prompts.
T2A Generation Models.State-of-the-art T2A generation
models that generate audio by conditioning on textual prompts
can be broadly categorized into two dominant architectural
paradigms: autoregressive modeling and latent diffusion. Au-
toregressive frameworks, such as AudioGen [12], treat audio
generation as a sequence modeling task over discrete tokens.
On the other hand, latent diffusion frameworks like AudioLDM
[13] and Make-An-Audio [14] rely on iterative denoising of
continuous latent spaces. More recently, hybrid and control-
lable frameworks like TangoFlux [15] have introduced auxiliary
conditioning for fine-grained style and acoustic properties.
Despite their architectural differences, these models share
a common reliance on joint audio-text encoders, such as CLAP
[16], to align semantic information across modalities. While
cross-modal representations are highly effective at capturing
global semantic similarity, they are not inherently designed to
enforce fine-grained, discrete logical constraints. This limita-
tion closely mirrors well-documented compositional failures in
visual generative models, where the inability to handle logical
composition is recognized as a fundamental architectural flaw.
Consequently, models learn to rely on strong co-occurrence pri-
ors, optimizing for the presence of identifying features rather
than their exclusion.
Data Scarcity and Affirmation Bias.This architectural
weakness is compounded by a fundamental flaw in the training
corpus: data scarcity and affirmation bias. Acoustic datasets
exhibit a near-total absence of explicit negations. Datasets pre-
dominantly describe present sound events while ignoring their
absence. Datasets such as AudioCaps [3], Clotho [4], and Au-
dioSet [5] primarily describe events and sounds that are present
in an acoustic scene. They contain little to no representation
of negated or absent concepts. As a result, models learn anaf-
firmation bias, mapping affirmative and negated descriptions to
similar representations.
This structural weakness is further exacerbated by the lack
of explicit negative supervision and the inherent difficulty of
constructing realistic negation examples in the training data of
T2A generation models. Research in other multimodal domains
has attempted to mitigate this via techniques like rule-based
negation [1], hard negative mining [17], or synthetic genera-
tion via LLMs [18]. Although these methods are effective in
learning robustness to semantic changes, they are only appli-
cable to text or vision-language tasks and do not fully address
the complexity of negation in audio. Hard negative mining is
particularly challenging in the audio domain because the notion
of a “hard negative” is not clearly defined. Current T2A gener-
ation techniques enhance the fidelity of valid sounds, but they
fail to encode logical exclusion, leaving the problem of negation
largely unaddressed in audio synthesis.
To bridge this gap, our work addresses the architectural in-
ability of current models to enforce logical constraints and af-
firmation bias in acoustic training data. We introduce an Audio
Negation Benchmark designed to systematically quantify and
expose the limitations of state-of-the-art T2A generation mod-
els in comprehending negation awareness.

3.2. Negation Types
To evaluate T2A generation models across varied linguistic sur-
face forms [19], we define four negation types. Covering mul-
tiple types allows us to test whether model failures are consis-
4026
tent across different ways of expressing the same semantic con-
straint, or whether certain linguistic structures are more effec-
tive at conveying negation. A brief description of each type is
as follows:
•Lexical Negation:Negation expressed through negative lex-
ical items that modify the event description directly, such as
”no”,”without”,”absence of”. The event remains explicitly
referenced, but its presence is negated at the word level.
•Syntactic Negation:Applied through grammatical structure,
e.g., auxiliary verbs or negative constructions (does not oc-
cur,is absent). Tests the model’s sensitivity to syntactic cues.
•Semantic Negation:Involves substituting a sound event
with a conceptually incompatible alternative (e.g., replacing
applausewithsilence), testing understanding of meaning-
level inversion beyond surface text.
•Mixed Negation:A combination of lexical, syntactic, and
semantic negation across multiple events in the same caption,
reflecting complex real-world scenarios.
3.3. Negation Scope
Negation scope defines which sound events within a caption are
negated. The number of possible negation combinations fore i
sound events is2 ei. For captions with more than two sound
events, the combinatorial space grows exponentially. Thus, we
define threenegation scopes:S={all,partial,mixed}. They
can be briefly described as follows:
•Full scope:All sound events are negated.
•Partial scope:Only a subset of sound events are negated.
•Mixed scope:Negation applies asymmetrically or heteroge-
neously across events.

4. Experimental Setup
As mentioned in Figure 4, we structure our experimental
pipeline into four sequential phases to systematically evaluate
negation handling in Text-to-Audio (T2A) generation models:
(1) Negative Audio Synthesis, focusing on generating audio
from negative prompts; (2) Negative Re-caption Generation,
which processes the synthesised negative audio back into text
to analyze its context within the text modality; (3) Affirmative
Baseline Analysis, establishing a control baseline by generating
audio from original captions (positive audio) and captions from
original audio (positive captions) to isolate negation failures and
observe our evaluation protocols in the absence of negation; and
(4) Acoustic Quality Assessment, validating the structural and
perceptual integrity of the synthesised audio to ensure failures
are not due to poor generation quality.
To execute this pipeline, we employ a diverse set of SOTA
models. For audio generation, we utilize AudioGen [12], Au-
dioLDM2 [13], and TangoFlux [15]. We specifically selected
these three models because they represent distinct, leading syn-
thesis paradigms in the field: AudioGen represents widely
adopted autoregressive architectures, AudioLDM2 represents
state-of-the-art latent diffusion models, and TangoFlux offers
high-fidelity generation utilizing a flow-matching approach. By
testing across fundamentally different architectures, we ensure
that our observations regarding negation failures are general-
izable to current T2A paradigms broadly, rather than being an
isolated artifact of a single model’s design.
For the audio captioning phase, we employ four architec-
turally diverse models with varying exposure to the AudioCaps
dataset: Whisper-Large [20], Qwen2Audio [21], MERaLiON-
2 [22], and V oxtral-Small [23]. Whisper-Large (pre-trained on
AudioCaps) and Qwen2Audio (evaluated on AudioCaps [24])
serve as dataset-aligned captioning models. MERaLiON-2 and
V oxtral-Small have no prior exposure to the dataset. Includ-
ing these unseen models ensures that the re-captions are driven
purely by the acoustic content in a zero-shot manner, confirm-
ing that the observed content stems from negative audio only.
