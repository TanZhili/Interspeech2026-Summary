SEA-MDD: Self-adapting Mispronunciation Detection and Diagnosis Models
via Test-Time Training
Minglin Wu1, Helen Meng 1
1 The Chinese University of Hong Kong, HKSAR
minglinwu@link.cuhk.edu.hk, hmmeng@se.cuhk.edu.hk
Abstract
Mispronunciation detection and diagnosis (MDD) assists sec-
ond language (L2) learners in improving pronunciation. How-
ever, the substantial variability in learner proficiency and the di-
verse mispronunciation types hinder the development of reliable
MDD systems. Large-scale annotation to cover all mispronun-
ciations is prohibitively expensive. To address this data scarcity,
we propose SEA-MDD, a SElf-Adapting MDD method that
leverages Test-Time Training (TTT) to effectively adapt trained
models to new speech samples. Specifically, we integrate mul-
tilayer perceptron (MLP)-based TTT modules into Transformer
blocks of wav2vec 2.0. The parameters of TTT modules are
updated during both training and test time via self-supervised
learning. This design enables trained models to adjust dynami-
cally to novel inputs, mitigating performance degradation com-
monly observed in prior MDD models when exposed to diverse
speech distributions. Experiments validate the effectiveness of
the proposed method.
Index Terms: mispronunciation detection and diagnosis, test-
time training, self-adapting, L2 English
1. Introduction
Mispronunciation detection and diagnosis (MDD) systems are
developed to identify pronunciation errors and provide diag-
nosing information about these errors to second language (L2)
learners. Figure 1 illustrates a typical MDD workflow in
the context of L2 English learning. First, the MDD system
prompts learners to articulate a given sentence, accompanied
by its canonical pronunciation. The system then recognizes
the phonemes produced by the learners and aligns the result-
ing phoneme sequence with the canonical reference. Through
this alignment, the system detects deviations from the expected
pronunciation and generates targeted feedback to assist learners
in improving their pronunciation.
Existing approaches to MDD in L2 English can be catego-
rized into phoneme scoring and phoneme recognition. Phoneme
scoring aims to assess the correctness of each phoneme within
a given utterance. Studies [1–5] have proposed various con-
fidence measures derived from acoustic models to detect mis-
pronunciations with low confidence scores. However, phoneme
scoring methods are inherently limited, as they do not pro-
vide detailed diagnostic information about mispronunciations.
To overcome this limitation, researchers introduced phoneme
recognition method focusing on recognizing all phonemes artic-
ulated by L2 learners. MDD is performed through comparison
between recognized and canonical phoneme sequences. Some
researchers [6–9] combine acoustic models and other auxiliary
modules, such as canonical pronunciation encoder and language
encoder, to achieve end-to-end phoneme recognition capability

However, L2 learners exhibit diverse proficiency levels and
produce a wide range of mispronunciation types. Consequently,
models trained on a fixed dataset often experience performance
degradation when confronted with speech samples whose data
distribution diverges from that of the training set. The collection
and annotation of large-scale L2 English speech data that cap-
tures all possible mispronunciations is both highly challenging
and costly. This data scarcity issue presents significant obsta-
cles to the development of robust MDD systems. To address
this issue, Wanet al.[15] proposes to use model-agnostic meta-
learning (MAML) [16] to enable the MDD model to rapidly
adapt to previously unseen speakers. However, this method
still necessitates a substantial amount of speech data from tar-
get speakers to attain satisfactory performance. The process
of collecting and annotating sufficient data for target speakers
remains costly and labor-intensive, thereby introducing a sec-
ondary data scarcity challenge.
In this paper, we address the challenge of L2 data scarcity
by proposing SEA-MDD, a SElf-Adapting MDD model that
leverages Test-Time Training (TTT) to effectively and effi-
ciently adapt the trained models to new speech samples. Origi-
nally introduced in [17–20], TTT is designed to maintain long-
range contextual coherence and to manage tasks involving ex-
ceptionally extended input sequences. TTT updates the model
weights not only during training but also at test time for ev-
ery input. The inherent adapting capability makes TTT particu-
larly well-suited for rapidly adapting trained models to new in-
puts, forming the foundation for our self-adapting MDD frame-
work. Specifically, we instantiate TTT as a multilayer percep-
tron (MLP) based neural network and insert it into the Trans-
former blocks of wav2vec 2.0 [21]. The parameters of the
MLP inside TTT module are optimized during both training and
test time through learning an auxiliary self-supervised learn-
ing task. This optimization strategy enables SEA-MDD models

2. Methods
2.1. Overall architecture
The overall architecture of the proposed SEA-MDD model is
illustrated in Figure 2 (a). It comprises a feature encoder, Trans-
former blocks integrating TTT module, and linear layers. The
feature encoder consists of temporal convolutional layers that
convert raw L2 English speech into latent speech representa-
tions, denoted asZ. The Transformer blocks integrating TTT
module further processZto generate contextualized speech
representationsC. Finally, the linear layers projectCinto
phoneme probabilities, which are used to compute the training
loss during training and derive the predicted phoneme sequence
at inference.
2.2. Transformer block integrating TTT module
Figure 2(b) depicts the architecture of the Transformer block
integrating TTT module. In this design, the TTT module
is inserted before the feed-forward sub-layer of the standard
Transformer block [22]. Each TTT-integrated Transformer
block comprises three sequential sub-layers: a multi-head self-
attention mechanism, a TTT module, and a feed-forward net-
work. Following each sub-layer, an ”Add&Norm” operation
is applied. The ”Add&Norm” operation following the TTT
module is employed to enhance training stability.

2.3.1. Architecture of TTT module
The TTT module can be instantiated with regular neural net-
works. As illustrated in Figure 2 (c), we implement the TTT
module as a two-layer MLP (two linear layers with a GELU ac-
tivation [23] in between, denoted asf M LP), followed sequen-
tially by layer normalization, a residual connection, and a gating
mechanism. Formally, letX= (x 1,...,x T )∈R d×T denote an
input sequence to TTT module, where eachx t∈R d represents

TTT(X) =Gate(X+LayerNorm(f M LP (X)))(1)
Here,Gate(x) =tanh(α)⊙x, whereα∈R d is a learn-

TTT-MLP, which serves as the default configuration through-

variant, TTT-Linear, in which the two-layer MLP in Figure 2(c)
is replaced with a single Linear layer.

3. Experiments
3.1. Datasets
The L2 dataset employed is CU-CHLOE [24], comprising 34.6
hours of English speech produced by 100 Cantonese and 110
Mandarin speakers. Each participant read a set of 86 sentences
carefully designed to capture a broad range of English pho-
netic characteristics. The sentences are organized into four cat-
egories: (a) The AESOP’s fable “The North Wind and the Sun”,
(b) Confusable words, (c) Minimal pairs, and (d) Phonemic sen-
tences. Linguists annotated this dataset at the phoneme level
using English phonemes. The data split is presented in Table 1.
3.2. Experimental setups
The feature encoder and Transformer blocks used to integrate
TTT modules adopt the same architectural specifications as
the wav2vec 2.0 base model. Specifically, the feature encoder
comprises seven temporal convolutional layers, each configured
with 512 channels. The respective strides and kernel sizes for
these layers are set to (5, 2, 2, 2, 2, 2, 2) and (10, 3, 3, 3, 3, 2,
2). There are 12 Transform blocks in our model, each equipped
with 8 attention heads, a model dimension of 768, and a feed-
forward dimension of 3072. In the TTT-MLP module, two lin-
ear layers of size 768×768 are employed, whereas the TTT-
Linear variant utilizes a single 768×768 linear layer. Each of
the projection matricesθ K,θ V , andθ Q has an input dimension
of 768 and an output dimension of 768. The learnableαfor
gating has a dimension of 768. The mini-batch sizebis set to
32. As described in Section 2.3.2, we refer to the process of
updating W within each TTT module as the inner loop, and the
optimization of the remaining network parameters as the outer
loop.W t>0 is learned in the inner loop during both training and
testing. In contrast, the initialW 0 and all other learnable pa-
rameters are learned in the outer loop during training. Models
incorporating the TTT-MLP and TTT-Linear modules are de-
noted as SEA-MDD-MLP and SEA-MDD-Linear, respectively.
The learning rateηin Equation (2) is set to 1e-3.
We adopt CTC loss and follow the fine-tuning protocol
for wav2vec 2.0 on 10-hour-level data [25] to perform the
outer-loop optimization for all SEA-MDD models. Specifically,
outer-loop training is conducted using the Adam optimizer [26]
in conjunction with thetri stagelearning rate schedule [25],
with a peak learning rate of 5e-4. Training is carried out for
20,000 updates on two NVIDIA H100.
3.3. Evaluation metrics
We evaluate each model’s phoneme recognition performance
in L2 English speech using phoneme error rate (PER). For the

evaluation of MDD, we adopt the framework from [27], which
aligns canonical, labeled, and recognized phoneme sequences
to obtain true acceptance (TA), false acceptance (FA), false re-
jection (FR), and true rejection (TR). With the alignment, TA
occurs when both labeled and recognized phonemes match the
canonical phoneme; FA when only the recognized phoneme
matches the canonical phoneme; FR when only the labeled
phoneme matches the canonical phoneme; and TR when neither
the labeled nor the recognized phoneme matches the canonical
phoneme. Within TR, correct diagnosis (CD) and diagnosis er-
ror (DE) indicate whether the recognized phoneme matches the
labeled one or not, respectively. Based on these categories, we
compute false rejection rate (FRR, FR/(TA+FR)), false accep-
tance rate (FAR, FA/(FA+TR)), Precision (PR, TR/(TR+FR)),
Recall (RE, TR/(TR+FA)), F1 score, and Diagnosis Accuracy
(DIAA, CD/(CD+DE)) to assess MDD performance.
3.4. Results
3.4.1. Main results
Table 2 presents the performance of various approaches on the
adopted test set. We adopt wav2vec2-CTC [10] and wav2vec2-
MAML [15] as baseline models. wav2vec2-MAML is the only
existing adaptable MDD approach, designed for rapid speaker
adaptation. However, it requires additional data from target
speakers for adaptation. Following [15], we randomly sam-
ple one-third of the test data to serve as adaptation data for
wav2vec2-MAML, while the remaining two-thirds are used as
the test set for evaluating all methods. Notably, only wav2vec2-
MAML uses these extra data for adaptation. The wav2vec 2.0
base model consists of 12 Transformer blocks, and our method
allows for flexible insertion of the TTT module into any subset
of these blocks. We report results for four representative con-
figurations that vary both the insertion location (first blockvs.
all blocks) and the type of TTT module (Linearvs.MLP).
As shown in Table 2, all SEA-MDD variants consistently
outperform the baseline models across all evaluation metrics.
Specifically, SEA-MDD-MLP (1 st block), SEA-MDD-MLP
(All blocks), SEA-MDD-Linear (1 st block), and SEA-MDD-
Linear (All blocks) achieve PERs of 8.18%, 8.03%, 8.26%,
and 8.13%, with corresponding F1 scores of 81.18%, 81.54%,
81.03%, and 81.28%. In comparison, wav2vec2-CTC and
wav2vec2-MAML yield PERs of 8.53% and 8.46%, and F1
scores of 80.40% and 80.67%, respectively. The observed per-
formance improvements can be attributed to the test-time pa-
rameter updates via learning the self-supervised reconstruction
task. This mechanism eliminates the model’s ”surprise” to
unexpected variations in speech representations, enabling the
model to assimilate new data distributions and adapt effectively
to novel speech samples.
Moreover, the SEA-MDD-MLP variants slightly outper-
form their SEA-MDD-Linear counterparts. For instance, the F1
scores of SEA-MDD-MLP (All blocks) and SEA-MDD-MLP
(1st block) are 81.54% and 81.18%, respectively, surpassing
those of SEA-MDD-Linear (All blocks) and SEA-MDD-Linear
(1st block), which are 81.28% and 81.03%, respectively. A
plausible explanation for this performance gap is that the deeper
architecture of the two-layer MLP offers greater capacity for
model adaptation. Additionally, the ”All blocks” configuration

3.4.2. Ablation studies
Table 3 presents the additional computational overhead incurred
by different adaptation strategies. The comparison spans three
dimensions: number of parameters, latency, and data require-
ments. The original inference latency of wav2vec 2.0 is 8ms.
As shown, wav2vec2-MAML demands substantially higher re-
sources, requiring 94.4 million parameters, approximately 30
minutes of latency, and two hours of adaptation data, to perform
adaptation. Notably, even when supplied with target-speaker
data from the test set, wav2vec2-MAML yields only marginal
improvements over the wav2vec2-CTC baseline. This outcome
reflects the considerable intra-speaker distributional variability
and highlights the inherent difficulty of the adopted L2 English
dataset. In contrast, the proposed SEA-MDD variants exhibit
markedly lower computational costs. SEA-MDD (1 st block)
variants reduce the number of parameter for adaptation to 2.4
million and 3.0 million, respectively. Although SEA-MDD (All
blocks) variants require up to 35.5 million parameters, they
remain substantially more parameter-efficient than wav2vec2-
MAML. For SEA-MDD-Linear configurations, Triton [28] ker-
nels enable efficient implementation, reducing adaptation la-
tency to 3 ms for SEA-MDD-Linear (1 st block) and 33 ms for
SEA-MDD-Linear (All blocks). For SEA-MDD-MLP config-
urations, following [17], we adopt data loading–computation
asynchrony [29] and gradient checkpointing along the sequence
dimension [18] to reduce the adaptation latency to 5 ms for
SEA-MDD-MLP (1 st block) and 52 ms for SEA-MDD-MLP
(All blocks). Moreover, all SEA-MDD configurations achieve
adaptation using only a single sentence, highlighting their effi-
ciency in low-resource scenarios. Overall, these results demon-
strate that SEA-MDD models achieve parameter efficiency,
time efficiency, and data efficiency, satisfying the real-time per-
formance requirements of MDD systems.
Notably, the ”1 st block” configuration achieves efficiency
while maintaining superior performance relative to baseline
models, thereby demonstrating a balance between model com-
pactness and adaptive capability. Under this ”single block” set-
ting, we only insert TTT module into one of the 12 Transformer

blocks of wav2vec 2.0. Figure 4 investigates the influence of
the inserting positions of the TTT modules on system perfor-

4. Conclusions
In this paper, we propose SEA-MDD, a SElf-Adapting MDD
approach that utilizes Test-Time Training (TTT) to effectively
adapt trained models to new speech samples. Specifically, we
instantiate TTT as MLP-based modules and insert them into
the Transformer blocks of wav2vec 2.0. The parameters of
the MLP within the TTT module are optimized during both
the training and test time by solving a self-supervised recon-
struction task. This optimization strategy enables the model
to effectively assimilate and adapt to unseen speech samples.
Experiments demonstrate that the proposed SEA-MDD mod-
els outperform both the non-adaptable wav2vec2-CTC and the
adaptable wav2vec2-MAML with enhanced parameter, time,
and data efficiency. These results highlight that SEA-MDD of-
fers a promising and efficient framework for constructing adapt-
able models in the MDD domain. Future work will aim to ex-
tend SEA-MDD to broader contexts, including a wider range of
L1 backgrounds and more varied recording conditions.
