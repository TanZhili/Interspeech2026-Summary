Adaptive A VSR: Integrating Speaker and Environmental Embeddings for
Robust Audio-Visual Speech Recognition
Christopher Simic1, Korbinian Riedhammer1, Tobias Bocklet1
1Technische Hochschule Nuernberg, Germany, Germany
christopher.simic, korbinian.riedhammer, tobias.bocklet@th-nuernberg.de
Abstract
This work introduces adaptive mechanisms that integrate
speaker and environmental noise embeddings into Audio-Visual
Speech Recognition (A VSR) systems. Our approach extends a
Whisper ASR model by an A V fusion module and incorporates
speaker and noise scenario specific embeddings, which are ex-
tracted from the audio input samples. While we develop a cus-
tom attention-based model for noise embedding generation, we
rely on X-Vector embeddings for speaker adaptation and evalu-
ate three integration strategies: prefix concatenation, additional
cross-attention layers and gated weighting. Noise embeddings
are injected into the audio-visual fusion module while speaker
embeddings are incorporated at the pre-decoder level. Eval-
uation on LRS3 across various noise conditions demonstrate
gains of 3.5% for noise and 3.2% for speaker adaptation over
our baseline. Compared to SOTA approach A V-HuBERT our
models achieve relative WER reductions of up to 13.4%.
Index Terms: Audio-visual speech recognition, speaker adap-
tation, noise adaptation, attention mechanisms, embeddings
1. Introduction
Automatic Speech Recognition (ASR) models aim to gener-
ate transcripts from audio-only input speech sequences. By
training on huge amounts of speech data, models like Whis-
per [1], achieve transcription quality that match human perfor-
mance [2].
Audio-Visual Speech Recognition (A VSR) approaches use
additional visual information, usually from lip movements, to
improve transcription quality in scenarios with increased noise
levels that are still challenging for traditional ASR models.
ASR and A VSR models are usually trained on a large number
of different speakers and a wide range of environmental con-
ditions, including different noise categories and noise levels,
resulting in good generalization abilities. While good gener-
alizability is important, we still see potential for A VSR perfor-
mance improvements by providing additional information about
speaker characteristics and environmental noise scenarios. To
extract speaker characteristics, we rely on established speaker
embedding models, but we develop a custom model for noise
information extraction.
In this work we build on a pre-trained Whisper model, like
earlier approaches like [3, 4], which has demonstrated state-
of-the-art performance. For our work we chose an early fu-
sion strategy, based on concatenation and self-attention.This
work’s objective is to systematically analyze the impact of
speaker and noise adaptation on A VSR performance by
evaluating three adaptation strategies: prefix concatena-
tion, the insertion of additional cross-attention layers and
gated weighting.
2. Data
2.1. Audio-Visual Training Data
For training and evaluation, we rely on two commonly used
audio-visual datasets that provide complementary characteris-
tics. LRS3 [5] is a standard benchmark for A VSR models,
containing approximately 430 hours of aligned and transcribed
speech video sequences with over 9k speakers from YouTube
Ted Talks. It is divided into Pretrain (407h), Trainval (30h),
and Test (1h). In our setup, Pretrain and Trainval are com-
bined for model training, while reserving 2048 samples from
Trainval for validation and final model selection. The test parti-
tion, which contains a completely independent set of speakers,
is used exclusively for final evaluation. Babble noise examples
for final testing are generated by mixing randomly 30 utterances
from different speakers from the Test partition. Also utterances,
for sidespeaker noise are taken from the Test set to prevent any
speaker overlap between training and testing.
The V oxCeleb2 dataset [6] consists of roughly 2k hours of
speech videos from more than 6k speakers. Since it does not
provide transcriptions, we employ it only for self-supervised
pre-training using pseudo labeling.
Data preparation and augmentation follow the established
pipeline from [7]. Specifically, during training, clean speech is
mixed with noise samples using signal-to-noise ratios (SNRs)
uniformly sampled between -15dB and 30dB.
2.2. Noise Data
To simulate realistic and diverse acoustic conditions, we incor-
porate several noise datasets. The MUSAN corpus [8] provides
audio-only samples from three categories: speech (60h), music
(42.5h), and natural noise (6h). We create babble noise sam-
ples from the speech samples following the same strategy as for
LRS3. Each noise category is split 80/10/10 into training, val-
idation and test sets, with strict separation to ensure no overlap
between training, validation and testing.
In addition, we prepare an alternative noise augmentation
set covering four categories: babble, natural, music and sides-
peaker noise. Sidespeaker samples are extracted from V ox-
Celeb2. Babble noise is synthetically generated by mixing 27
to 33 speech samples from LibriSpeech [9]. Natural noise is
taken from ESC50 [10], which provides 2k samples covering
50 different urban and natural environments. Music samples are
obtained from a database [11], containing excerpts from 1886
songs across 9 genres.

4. Method
4.1. Model Architecture and Adaptation Strategies
Figure 1 illustrates the overall architecture of our proposed sys-
tem. The baseline builds on a pre-trained Whisper ASR model
and extends it by an upstream audio-visual (A V) fusion module.
For audio and visual feature extraction, we rely on the method
proposed in [7], which has demonstrated strong performance
in A VSR. While approaches like [3, 7, 4] implement a cross-
attention-based A V fusion module, this work builds on self-
attention layers with upstream audio-visual feature concatena-
tion. This decision is justified by comparative experiments with
both attention-types, leading to 1.3% better results with the self-
attention-based fusion module, that is consequently chosen for
this work. To emphasize local interactions, the concatenated
features are further divided into chunks before attention layer
processing.
The noise and speaker embeddings, that are generated from
audio inputs need to be prepared for subsequent processing
within the A VSR model. For both types of embeddings we ap-
ply the same preparation strategies, that are illustrated in Fig-
ure 1 atEmbedding Preparation. In the first variant, embed-
dings are projected to the processing dimension of the A VSR
model through a two-layered multilayer perceptron (MLP),
while the second variant passes embeddings through a two-layer
MLP with a final sigmoid activation to produce gating vectors,
which are then used for feature channel modulation.
The adaptation is performed at two different levels of the
model. Noise embeddings are integrated into the A V fusion
module to support the fusion of audio and visual modalities. In
contrast, speaker embeddings are incorporated into the decoder,
in order to directly support the decoding process by providing
additional speaker information.
To incorporate the additional information into the A VSR
models, we investigate three different adaptation strategies, as
illustrated on the right side of Figure 1 belowProposed Adap-
tation Strategies.
ForPrefix Adaptation, the mapped embeddings are concate-
nated as prefixes to the feature sequences before being passed to
subsequent attention layers. For speaker adaptation, the Whis-
per encoder embeddings are extended with the speaker embed-
dings, while for noise adaptation, the embeddings are added to
each feature chunk.
ForCross-Attention Adaptation, additional cross-attention
layers are introduced to separate adaptation from the baseline
model’s layers. For speaker adaptation, one cross-attention
layer is inserted prior to the Whisper decoder, ensuring that the
decoder’s architecture remains unchanged while still incorpo-
rating speaker information into the decoder representation. For
noise adaptation, a cross-attention layer is placed before each
self-attention block within the A V fusion module.
InGated Weighting Adaptation, gating vectors, produced
through sigmoid activation are used to weight individual fea-
ture channels. These learned vectors are applied channel-wise
to the embeddings, thereby modulating the influence of spe-
cific features based on the speaker or noise information. For
speaker adaptation, the gating vector is multiplied with the em-
beddings after the encoder, whereas for noise adaptation it is
applied to the concatenated audio-visual feature vectors. In par-
ticular, weighting audio and visual features according to noise
information appears to be a promising strategy.
To provide additional speaker information, we employ X-
Vector and ECAPA-TDNN embeddings, which have demon-
strated strong performance in speaker recognition tasks.
For noise modeling, we introduce a dedicated module that
predicts noise level and noise category from speech samples.
The module consists of four encoder layers with eight self-
attention heads and a hidden dimension of 256, followed by
two prediction heads, each implemented as two fully connected
layers: one for noise level regression (signal-to-noise ratio pre-

diction with a single output neuron) and one for noise category
classification with four output units corresponding to babble,
music, natural, and sidespeaker noise. Training is performed
jointly with a mean squared error (MSE) loss for the noise level
prediction head and a cross-entropy (CE) loss for the noise cat-
egory classification head. The embedding extracted after the
final encoder layer, but before the prediction heads, is supposed
to capture all relevant noise information. This representation is
then provided as input to the A VSR model.
4.2. Training Details
Our models build on a Whisper base model, with 74M parame-
ters extended by the A V fusion module with approximately 13M
parameters. Prefix adaptation does not require additional pa-
rameter. The MLP used for noise adaptation adds 14k parame-
ters, while the MLP for speaker adaptation adds 263k parame-
ters, reflecting the different target dimensions (80 within the A V
fusion module vs. 512 at the Whisper embedding level). The
added cross-attention layers result in additional 3.6M parameter
for noise adaptation and 4.2M parameter for speaker adaptation.
As a starting point for all following experiments, the base-
line model is pre-trained without adaptation over three epochs
on a combination of V oxCeleb2 and LRS3. During pre-
training only A V fusion module and encoder are tuned in a
self-supervised manner using a MSE loss, in a simmilar man-
ner to [7]. All models are fine-tuned with a batch size of 512,
starting with an initial fine-tuning epoch with a constant learn-
ing rate of10 −4 which is subsequently linearly reduced over 4
epochs. Until full convergence each model is repeatedly trained
for single epochs, with a decayed learning rate starting from
2×10 −5. The same augmentation strategy, as described in Sec-
tion 2, is applied consistently across all models, including the
baseline. Each model is trained until three consecutive epochs
do not result in a new best performance on the validation set
but with a maximum of 11 fine-tuning epochs. For fine-tuning,
the MSE loss at encoder embedding level is combined with a
Cross-Entropy (CE) loss at decoder logit level. Each model is
trained on four A40 Nvidia GPUs. Pre-training takes about 50
hours, while each fine-tuning epoch takes about 2.5 hours. For
model testing, we used a test batch size of 8. On a NVIDIA A40
GPU, processing one batch required 0.58s without adaptation,
0.63s with noise adaptation and 0.72s with speaker adaptation.
Running the same test on A100 GPUs reduced the processing
time by approximately 27%.

5.3. Similarity between Noise and Speaker Adaptation
Contrary to our expectations, it is notable that speaker adap-
tation mainly improves performance at higher noise levels (-5
dB), showing some similarity to noise adaptation. To inves-
tigate this behavior, we trained a noise classification model on
prepared X-Vector embeddings to determine whether X-Vectors
encode noise-related information. The evaluation reveals that
this model achieves an accuracy of 90%, compared to 94% for
our dedicated noise embedding model. The strong performance
of the X-Vector–based model confirms the assumption that X-
Vector embeddings contain explicit information about noise
scenarios, which explains the similarity to the noise-adapted
model’s results. In contrast, performing the same experiment
with ECAPA embeddings yielded a much lower prediction ac-
curacy of only 64%, indicating that ECAPA embeddings retain
far less noise information. We expect that this finding also ac-
counts for the weaker performance observed when ECAPA em-
beddings were used for A VSR adaptation.
We further investigate a combination of noise and speaker
adaptation for A VSR. Therefore, our best noise and speaker
adaptation strategies from Table 1,Noise Gated Weight Adapt
andSpeaker Prefix Adapt, are combined for joint processing
within one model. This combined adaptation strategy didn’t re-
sult in any further improvements and revealed results that are
almost identical to those ofNoise Gated Weight Adapt, with a
maximum absolute WER difference atAll Mix10dB of 0.03%
(1.93% vs. 1.96%). This further confirms the hypothesis that
noise and X-vector embeddings contain redundant information
and, therefore, combining both embedding types does not lead
to any further improvement.
