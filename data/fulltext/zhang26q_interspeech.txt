Abstract
Aspirated fricatives are realized through multiple interacting
acoustic parameters rather than a single defining parameter.
This study investigates the realization of aspirated fricatives in
Shaxi Bai, focusing on three acoustic parameters: initial dura-
tion ratio, spectral center of gravity, and H1*–H2*. Acoustic
data from 30 speakers across three age groups were analyzed
using linear mixed-effects models. Results show that the rela-
tive magnitude of acoustic effects varies across fricative types
and age groups. Temporal and spectral measures exhibit robust
acoustic differences, whereas phonatory measure shows weaker
and less consistent effects. Young speakers show a different co-
variation pattern among acoustic parameters, consistent with an
ongoing reorganization of the aspiration contrast. These find-
ings support a multidimensional account of aspirated fricatives
and demonstrate intergenerational change in the relative contri-
bution of temporal, spectral, and phonatory dimensions.

1. Introduction
Although research on aspirated fricatives remains relatively
limited, previous studies have examined their phonological sta-
tus, historical development, and acoustic realization across sev-
eral languages [1, 2]. Experimental evidence suggests that as-
pirated fricatives exhibit a composite acoustic structure involv-
ing two temporally adjacent mechanisms of noise generation: a
supraglottal frication source and an aspiration component asso-
ciated with glottal turbulence [1, 3, 4]. Spectrographic analyses
consistently reveal a two stage noise pattern in which the as-
pirated portion typically shows reduced intensity and a more
diffuse spectral energy distribution relative to the frication por-
tion [4].
More generally studies of fricatives have shown that spec-
tral structure, particularly the center of gravity, reliably differ-
entiates place of articulation and the spectral energy distribu-
tion of frication noise [5, 6]. Temporal properties such as frica-
tion duration and the rise time of the frication amplitude en-
velope contribute to fricative and affricate contrasts [7, 8]. In
addition, phonation measures such as H1*–H2* which are typ-
ically assessed in the following vowel serve as acoustic corre-
lates of breathy voice quality and glottal configuration [9, 10].
Taken together, these findings indicate that aspiration contrasts
in fricatives are implemented through coordinated variation
across temporal, spectral, and phonation dimensions rather than
through a single defining parameter.

Some acoustic work on aspirated fricatives suggest that the
aspiration contrast is primarily encoded within the consonant
interval rather than in vowel quality or tone [11, 12]. In terms
of acoustic realization, the contrast is multidimensional, involv-
ing coordinated variation in temporal structure, spectral distri-
bution, and phonation [4,13,14].These dimensions differ in sta-
bility: temporal measures tend to be relatively robust, whereas
spectral and phonation-related cues may weaken or reorganize
across speakers and generations [14,15]. Evidence from Bai di-
alects further suggests that shifts in the relative contribution of
acoustic dimensions are closely related to patterns of variation
and potential sound change [14, 16].
Despite these findings, few studies have systematically
modeled the relative acoustic contributions of different dimen-
sions within a unified statistical framework, nor have they quan-
tified age-related variation using mixed-effects approaches.
This paper aims to enhance our understanding of the sound
change mechanisms of aspirated fricatives in the Bai language,
using the Shaxi variety as an example. Shaxi Bai is a dialect
spoken by approximately 24,171 people in Jianchuan County,
Yunnan Province. This paper provides a detailed acoustic anal-
ysis of aspirated fricatives in Shaxi Bai and attempts to answer
the following questions: how are multiple acoustic measures
distributed across speakers, and whether the effects of these
acoustic dimensions as estimated by the model exhibit system-
atic age-related differences.
2. Methods
2.1. Speakers and materials
30 native Bai speakers from Shaxi Town, Jianchuan County,
Yunnan Province, China participated in this study. They were
divided into three age groups: older speakers aged 56 and above
(n = 14), middle-aged speakers aged 36–55 (n = 13), and young
speakers aged 18–35 (n = 3). The sample was nearly balanced
for gender, with 14 female and 16 male speakers.
All participants were lifelong residents of Shaxi and re-
ported no history of long-term residence or employment out-
side the region. They were all fluent in Bai and used it as their
primary language in daily communication. The word list was
mainly compiled based on the actual pronunciation of Shaxi Bai
speakers and previously published materials used in research on
aspirated fricatives in Sino-Tibetan languages [4, 14]. The en-
tire word list is shown in Table 1. Each speaker produced the 16
target words twice, yielding a total of 960 tokens for acoustic
analysis.

2.2. Instruments and data acquisition
All recordings were completed in Lijiang, China. The signals
were recorded in Adobe Audition 2021 on a laptop computer
with a sampling rate of 44.1kHz and 16-bit quantization. The
Sony ECM-77B microphone and the Eggforsingers 7050A were
used for recording signals which passed through an external
mixer XENYX 302USB and sound card XONAR U7 before
entering the computer.
2.3. Acoustic measures
All acoustic analyses were conducted within a unified annota-
tion framework. Speech materials were segmented and anno-
tated in Praat. Annotation tiers included the fricative interval,
the aspiration interval, the following vowel, and the entire syl-
lable. Acoustic parameters were extracted along three dimen-
sions: the temporal dimension, the spectral dimension, and the
phonation dimension, following the measurement framework
in [14].
2.3.1. Temporal measure
Temporal realization was quantified using the initial duration
ratio, defined as the proportion of the onset consonant duration
to the total syllable duration [14]. For aspirated fricatives, the
onset interval comprised both the fricative portion and the sub-
sequent aspiration portion. Normalizing onset duration by syl-
lable duration facilitates comparison across tokens while con-
trolling for variation in overall syllable length and speech rate.

2.3.2. Spectral measure
Spectral centroid (center of gravity, CoG) was used as the pri-
mary descriptor of fricative spectral structure, with higher val-
ues indicating greater concentration of energy at higher frequen-
cies [14]. To capture dynamic spectral variation within the frica-
tive interval, spectral centroid values were measured at 20% and
80% of the time normalized fricative duration. The difference
between these two measurement points was computed to quan-
tify within segment spectral change.
Spectral Centroid Difference=SC 20% −SC 80%
This measure reflects both the direction and magnitude of
spectral change over the course of the onset interval.
2.3.3. Phonation measure
Phonation related properties associated with aspiration were
characterized using H1*–H2*, the formant-corrected amplitude
difference between the first and second harmonics, extracted via
V oiceSauce [17] over the stable voiced portion of the following
vowel, from after the onset of regular periodic voicing to a point
where F0, amplitude, and F1/F2 trajectories were stable.
2.4. Statistical analysis
Before modeling, all acoustic measures were standardized
within speaker to reduce interspeaker variability. For each pa-
rameter, a separate linear mixed-effects model was fit with as-
piration condition as the primary fixed effect. Other predictors,
including fricative category and its interaction with aspiration
condition, were included when applicable. Random intercepts
for speaker and lexical item were specified to account for re-
peated observations.
Inference was based on estimated marginal means and pair-
wise contrasts between aspiration conditions. Results were vi-
sualized using EMM plots and speaker-level paired compar-
isons. All analyses were performed in R (v4.4.2) using lme4 for
model fitting and emmeans for marginal means and contrasts.
3. Results
3.1. Temporal dimension: initial duration ratio
The initial duration ratio revealed clear age-related differences
in the temporal realization of the aspiration contrast (Fig.1). In
the elderly and middle-aged groups, aspirated fricatives exhib-
ited significantly higher initial duration ratios than unaspirated
fricatives. Across fricative types, /C/ and /s/ showed higher ini-
tial duration ratios than /f/ and /x/.
In the young group, the aspiration-related effect in initial
duration ratio was largely absent. Except for /s/, no signifi-
cant difference was observed between aspirated and unaspirated
fricatives. For /f/, the unaspirated category showed a slightly
higher initial duration ratio.
These results suggest a reduction in temporal contrast in the
young group. Within-group variability was relatively low in the
elderly and middle-aged groups but substantially higher in the
young group. Given that the young group included only three
speakers, this increased dispersion should be interpreted with
caution.

3.3. Phonation dimension: H1*–H2*
As shown in Figure 3, significant aspiration related effects in
H1*–H2* were largely restricted to the fricative /C/. Aspirated
/C/ shows significantly higher H1*–H2* than its unaspirated
counterpart for both the elderly and middle-aged speakers, con-
sistent with increased breathy phonation of the following vowel.
Beyond /C/, evidence for an effect is limited. /f/ reaches signif-
icance only among the elderly speakers, while /s/ and /x/ show
no significant difference. Effects are generally small and non-
significant for young speakers across fricative types. Notably,
/C/ shows a reversal in effect direction for the young speakers
relative to the older groups, and this pattern requires further ver-
ification.
H1*–H2* was limited in scope and varied substantially
across fricative types and age groups. Effect magnitudes were
smaller in the middle-aged and young groups than in the elderly
group, with minimal separation between aspiration conditions
in the young group.
Given the substantial dispersion of H1*–H2* across age
groups, additional speaker level paired analyses were conducted
as shown in Figure 4. Although group level means differed,
considerable interspeaker variability was observed, including
some speakers exhibiting reversed patterns. This variability
likely contributes to the relative instability of the H1*–H2* ef-
fect compared to temporal and spectral dimensions.
More specifically, H1*–H2* showed statistically significant
differences only in limited age groups and fricative categories.

Speaker profiles (Fig.6) show that temporal, spectral, and
phonatory effects do not scale together. Many speakers exhibit
a spectrally weighted profile, as in F14, M8, F11, M13, and F12,
where the spectral cells are consistently darker than the tempo-
ral and phonatory cells. Others show a temporally weighted
profile, as in F7, with a stronger temporal effect and moderate
spectral and phonatory effects. A smaller subset shows rela-
tively prominent phonatory effects alongside moderate to strong
spectral effects, as in F4 and F8. Some speakers show generally
weak effects across all three measures, as in F9. This configu-
ration diversity indicates that speakers distribute the aspiration
effect differently across acoustic dimensions, which contributes
to the dispersion seen in group summaries.
Previous study on Jinshan Bai suggests that aspirated frica-
tives can develop along two directions. When temporal proper-
ties such as fricative duration are enhanced, aspirated fricatives
tend to converge with the corresponding unaspirated fricatives.
When spectral properties such as center of gravity and intensity
patterning are enhanced, they tend to develop toward aspirated
affricates at the same place of articulation [14]. The present
speaker heatmap aligns with this account because temporally
weighted and spectrally weighted speaker profiles provide dif-
ferent acoustic bases that could support different historical out-
comes.
4. Discussion and conclusion
The results indicate that aspirated fricatives in Shaxi Bai are not
realized through a single acoustic parameter, but through coor-
dinated effects across multiple acoustic dimensions. The rela-
tive magnitude and stability of these effects differ across frica-
tive types, age groups, and individual speakers.
Systematic differences are observed across fricative types.
Alveolar and palatal fricatives such as /s/ and /C/ exhibit robust
aspiration related effects in both temporal and spectral dimen-
sions, while labiodental and velar fricatives such as /f/ and /x/
show weaker acoustic contrasts and greater variability.
Clear age-related differences are also observed. Young
speakers show reduced and less stable temporal contrasts,
while spectral effects remain evident in certain fricative types.
Middle-aged and older speakers show more stable and more
broadly distributed temporal and spectral effects. The reduction
of temporal contrasts among young speakers is accompanied by
smaller spectral effects, indicating an overall weakening of the
acoustic contrast in this group.
Speaker level analysis shows continuous variation in how
the aspiration effect is distributed across acoustic dimensions,
plausibly providing a basis for different diachronic directions
of aspirated fricatives. For speakers relying predominantly on
temporal measures, reduced temporal effects could lead to con-
vergence toward the corresponding unaspirated fricatives and
merger in production; for speakers with larger spectral effects,
reduced temporal effects could instead yield a pattern aligning
more closely with the corresponding aspirated affricates.
Despite variability across fricative types, age groups, and
speakers, a stable dimension ranking emerges: temporal and
spectral measures show larger and more consistent effects than
the phonation measure at both the group and speaker levels, and
phonation related effects remain modest even when statistically
significant.
Taken together, speaker level variation reflects structured
differences in the acoustic realization of a multidimensional
contrast. This pattern indicates that the aspiration contrast in
Shaxi Bai is primarily implemented in temporal and spectral
domains, with phonation contributing a secondary component.
The present study extends a multidimensional perspective
to aspirated fricatives in Shaxi Bai and demonstrates system-
atic differences in the relative magnitude and stability of tem-
poral, spectral, and phonation-related effects across fricative
types, age groups, and individual speakers. Future work should
include perception experiments to examine perceptual separa-
bility of the observed acoustic contrasts, and complementary
articulatory evidence would further clarify the mechanisms un-
derlying the temporal, spectral, and phonation related patterns
and their role in contrast maintenance.
