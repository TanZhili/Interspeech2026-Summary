Abstract
This paper investigates adapting Audio Large Language Models
(ALLMs) for speaker verification (SV). We reformulate SV as
an audio question answering task and conduct zero-shot eval-
uations on public benchmarks, showing that current ALLMs
have limited zero-shot SV capability and struggle in diverse
acoustic conditions. To address this challenge, we perform su-
pervised fine-tuning on SV data. A rule-based hard pair sam-
pling strategy is proposed to construct more challenging training
pairs. Lightweight fine-tuning substantially improves perfor-
mance, though a gap remains between ALLMs and conventional
models. We then extend the approach to text-dependent SV by
jointly querying ALLMs to verify speaker identity and spoken
content, yielding results competitive with cascaded ASR–SV
systems. Our findings demonstrate that, with proper adaptation,
ALLMs have strong potential as a unified framework for ro-
bust SV systems while maintaining general audio understanding
capabilities.
Index Terms: Audio Large Language Model, Speaker Verifica-
tion, Zero-Shot Evaluation, Hard Pair Sampling
1. Introduction
Speaker Verification (SV) is a critical technology for authenti-
cating an individual’s identity by analyzing biometric features
within their voice signal. SV plays a pivotal role in fields such
as smart homes and personalized voice assistants. With the
rapid development of deep neural networks (DNN), popular so-
lutions for SV typically consist of a DNN-based embedding
extractor [1, 2, 3], followed by a scoring backend to determine
speaker similarity. These approaches have been highly effective
under relatively simple testing scenarios [ 4]. However, under
challenging real-world scenarios, such as noisy environments
and device mismatch, the robustness of SV systems remains a
challenge [5, 6].
Motivated by the success of text-based Large Language
Models (LLMs) [7], Audio LLMs (ALLMs) [8, 9] have recently
witnessed rapid development. Compared with conventional mod-
els, where specific architectures are designed for different tasks,
ALLMs achieve strong performance on diverse audio understand-
ing tasks, spanning areas such as automatic speech recognition
(ASR), audio captioning, and music question answering. In
addition to understanding tasks that involve processing audio
inputs to produce textual outputs, recent ALLMs [ 10, 11, 12]
can generate both text and audio, supporting spoken dialogue
between humans and ALLMs.
Due to their large parameter size and the diversity of training
tasks and data, ALLMs exhibit superior generalization capabili-

ties compared to conventional task-specific smaller models. For
instance, they show some ability to recognize speaker-related
attributes such as gender, age, and accent, suggesting their po-
tential for SV . Nevertheless, current ALLM-based interactive
systems remain largely insensitive to speaker identity in dialogue.
This naturally raises the question: to what extent can ALLMs
effectively handle SV tasks? To explore the potential of ALLMs
for SV , we pioneer a comprehensive study addressing three key
questions: (1) Can ALLMs perform zero-shot speaker verifica-
tion? (2) How can we effectively adapt ALLMs for SV tasks? (3)
Can ALLMs handle both text-independent and text-dependent
SV scenarios?
First, we extensively evaluate the performance of ALLMs
on SV under challenging conditions where speaker pairs share
similar attributes, such as gender, age, and recording devices.
Evaluation pairs under these specified conditions are constructed
from public benchmark datasets, including V oxCeleb [1], CN-
Celeb [6], and 3D-Speaker [5].
To adapt ALLMs to SV , the task is reformulated as an audio
question answering (QA) task where two utterances, includ-
ing enrollment and testing utterances, are fed to ALLMs. We
investigate four enrollment–test prompting strategies and find
that concatenation with silence achieves the best performance.
However, regardless of the evaluation condition or prompting
strategy, ALLMs exhibit poor performance on SV . This moti-
vates our exploration of supervised fine-tuning approaches. To
expose ALLMs to more challenging pairs during training, we
adopt a hard pair sampling strategy based on the aforementioned
conditions (e.g., same gender or same age), rather than relying
on random sampling. Results show that lightweight fine-tuning
substantially improves SV performance while preserving the
core audio understanding capabilities. Remarkably, under the
short-duration condition, fine-tuned ALLMs even surpass the
strong conventional baseline.
Finally, we extend our study to text-dependent SV , which
requires simultaneous verification of speaker identity and spo-
ken content. We reformulate text-dependent SV as an audio
QA task, where the model is required to determine whether two
utterances are spoken by the same speaker and whether their
spoken content matches. The reformulation helps exploit the
inherent speech understanding capabilities of ALLMs. Exper-
imental results demonstrate that ALLMs achieve competitive
performance compared with cascaded approaches based on con-
ventional models.

2. Zero-Shot Evaluation of ALLMs on
Speaker Verification
2.1. Audio Large Language Model
Although ALLMs can recognize speaker attributes such as gen-
der, their ability to distinguish individual speakers remains under-
explored. Therefore, we evaluate their zero-shot SV performance
using three representative models. All three models follow a com-
mon Audio Encoder–LLM paradigm but differ in their front-end
design: Qwen2-Audio [9] employs continuous acoustic features
extracted by Whisper [13], MiMo-Audio [11] adopts discrete au-
dio tokens, and Kimi-Audio [10] integrates both. Regardless of
their encoding strategies, these models rely on an autoregressive
LLM backend, which requires framing downstream tasks as text
generation.
2.2. Speech Pair Prompting Strategy
To align SV with this generative paradigm, we transform it into
an audio-based QA task: given two utterances and a query, the
model is expected to generate an answer determining whether the
two utterances are from the same speaker. Since both enrollment
and test utterances are provided, different prompting strategies
can be designed to organize the input pair:
1. Separate: The two segments are input as independent utter-
ances: “Audio 1: [audio1], Audio 2: [audio2]”. The
textual prompt is “Please determine whether the above two
audio segments are from the same speaker or different speak-
ers.”
2. Concat: The two segments are concatenated into a single
utterance: “[audio1]∥[audio2] ”. The textual prompt
is “Please determine how many speakers are present in this
audio segment.”.
3. Concat + Silence: The two segments are concatenated with
a 1-second silence interval inserted in between: “[audio1]
∥sil∥[audio2] ”. The textual prompt is the same as
Concat.
4. Mix: The two segments are mixed and overlaid to form a sin-
gle utterance: “[audio1⊕audio2]”. The textual prompt
is “This audio segment is composed of two audio tracks mixed
together. Please determine whether these two audio tracks are
from the same speaker or different speakers.”
2.3. Evaluation Settings
We evaluate the Instruct variants of the three models intro-
duced in Section 2.1:Qwen2-Audio-7B-Instruct,Kimi-Audio-
7B-Instruct, andMiMo-Audio-7B-Instruct.
Although benchmarks such as V oxCeleb exist, we aim to
evaluate model performance along specific challenging dimen-
sions. To this end, we construct a comprehensive test set by
selectively sampling from existing benchmarks, focusing on di-
versity across multiple dimensions. For each dimension, positive
and negative pairs are constructed based on whether the attributes
correspond or differ. For example, positive pairs regarding age
are drawn from different speakers with an age gap of over 10
years, while negative pairs regarding gender consist of different
speakers who have the same gender. Detailed conditions are
shown in Table 2. A 1:1 ratio of positive and negative pairs is
maintained across all dimensions, ensuring approximate cover-
age of the speakers in the source data. Since ALLMs directly
output text without similarity scores, we use accuracy for evalu-
ation instead of the commonly used Equal Error Rate (EER).

2.4. Zero-Shot Performance
Zero-shot performance of ALLMs on SV is shown in Table 1.
The results indicate that the choice of prompting strategy is crit-
ical:Concat + Silenceachieves the best performance across
most evaluation dimensions, whereasMixperforms close to
chance level, suggesting limitations in the model’s ability to
process mixed speech. Furthermore, robustness remains a pri-
mary challenge: although an accuracy of ∼70% is achieved
on long-duration utterances, performance degrades significantly
under complex scenarios such as cross-device and cross-dialect
conditions. Based on these findings, we select Kimi-Audio and
employ theConcat + Silencestrategy for the subsequent fine-
tuning stage.
3. Method
3.1. Model Architecture
As shown in Figure 1, we follow the architecture of Kimi-Audio,
with a hybrid audio encoding module and an LLM backbone.
The audio encoding module combines the discrete tokens [15]
and the continuous Whisper features to form audio embeddings.
The text prompt and encoded audio embeddings serve as the
input to the LLM backbone.

3.2.2. Rule-based Hard Pair Sampling Strategy
ALLMs are fine-tuned to determine whether speech pairs share
the same speaker identity or textual content. Consequently, the
pair sampling strategy is vital to the model’s discriminative abil-
ity. Inspired by the large zero-shot performance variations across
different dimensions, we propose a rule-based hard pair sampling
strategy, where challenging pairs are constructed with an explicit
focus on different dimensions, as illustrated in Section 2.3.
4. Experimental Setup
4.1. Datasets
For text-independent SV , the ALLM is fine-tuned on the V ox-
Celeb2, CN-Celeb, and 3D-Speaker development or training sets
(with 9 million training pairs in total), and its performance is
evaluated on the corresponding test sets described in Section 2.3.
For text-dependent tasks, we fine-tune and evaluate the model on
the LibriSpeech [16] dataset, where positive and negative pairs
are randomly constructed in equal proportions, yielding 280k
training pairs and 5,560 evaluation pairs.
4.2. Evaluation Metrics
To compare fine-tuned ALLMs with conventional models, we
compute the EER based on the outputs of ALLMs. Since the
model is trained to directly output “One” or “Two”, we derive
scores from the normalized probability of the token “One”:
p(One) = exp(sOne)

ing to the tokens “ One” and “Two” in the LLM’s vocabulary,
respectively.
4.3. Implementation Details
We adopt Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA) [17] with rankr= 16
and scaling factor α= 32 , applying it to all attention projec-
tion layers (excluding biases). The model has 18M trainable
parameters. The AdamW optimizer is used, with a learning rate
following a cosine schedule and an initial value of10 −5.

5. Results
5.1. Fine-tuning Effects
As shown in Table 3, fine-tuning greatly reduces Kimi-Audio’s
SV error rates: with only 18M trainable parameters, the EERs
under gender and language conditions drop to 4.87% and 2.93%,
respectively. The ablation confirms the importance of hard pair
sampling, as replacing it with random sampling worsens the
dialect EER from 10.93% to 15.40%, consistent with prior find-
ings that hard samples improve representation robustness [18].
Although the fine-tuned ALLM still lags behind conventional
models overall, it is competitive in challenging scenarios and
excels under short-duration conditions, indicating promising ro-
bustness in real-world acoustic environments with limited audio
context.
We further explore the performance of other ALLM back-
bones. As presented in Table 4, fine-tuning consistently enhances
SV performance, regardless of the backbone. This reveals that
mainstream ALLM models tend to underemphasize speaker-
discriminative information. Although these models demonstrate
strong semantic understanding performance, they largely over-
look paralinguistic cues such as speaker identity.

5.2. Extension to Text-Dependent SV
Results of applying ALLMs to text-dependent SV are shown in
Table 5. We compare ALLMs with a cascaded approach using
Whisper for text recognition and ECAPA-TDNN for SV . The
results indicate that under zero-shot conditions, ALLMs perform
poorly on SV while maintaining high text recognition accuracy,
as expected given that ASR is included in pre-training. After fine-
tuning, ALLMs achieve slightly higher overall accuracy than
the cascaded approach and comparable SV performance. These
results highlight the potential of ALLMs as a unified system that
integrates speaker identity with semantic content.

5.3. Performance on Other Tasks
To explore how fine-tuning on SV affects performance on other
tasks, we evaluate the fine-tuned model on general audio un-
derstanding tasks. As shown in Table 6, the performance re-
mains largely stable before and after fine-tuning, indicating that
the model effectively retains its original semantic understand-
ing capabilities while acquiring speaker discriminativeness. In-
terestingly, on AAC, the performance improves substantially,
which may be attributed to the speaker-related elements in au-
dio captions (e.g., the speaker number described in the caption).
This suggests that SV facilitates capturing acoustic features, and
ALLMs may benefit from integrating SV into pre-training.
6. Conclusion
This study is the first to explore ALLMs for speaker verifica-
tion. Zero-shot evaluation shows near-chance performance un-
der challenging scenarios, while lightweight LoRA fine-tuning
with rule-based hard pair sampling substantially improves re-
sults. Extending ALLMs to text-dependent verification leverages
their intrinsic speech understanding and achieves performance
comparable to cascaded conventional ASR-SV systems. The
results show that fine-tuning enhances speaker awareness while
preserving general audio understanding, suggesting that speaker-
discriminative objectives in pre-training could further improve
ALLMs. Future work will narrow the gap between ALLMs and
specialized models, fully realizing the potential of generative
paradigms in speaker recognition.
