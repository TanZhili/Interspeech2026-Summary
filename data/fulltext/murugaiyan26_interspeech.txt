Abstract
Sentence stress refers to the relative prominence of certain
words within a sentence. Automatic sentence stress detec-
tion aims to identify these prominent words from spoken utter-
ances and is important for effective Human–Computer Interac-
tion (HCI) systems. Since sentence stress is context-dependent
and relative in nature, modeling sequential dependencies across
words is essential. Existing approaches use fixed-layer repre-
sentations from large-scale pre-trained models like Whisper for
detection. However, we observe that directly using representa-
tions from a single layer does not optimally capture prosodic
cues, as different layers encode different levels of acoustic and
contextual information. We propose a weighted layer fusion
framework within Whisper that learns an optimal combination
of encoder and decoder representations. Experiments on natu-
rally spoken and synthetic datasets show an improvement of up
to 4.48% and 6.09%, respectively, for sentence stress detection.
Index Terms: sentence stress detection, human-computer inter-
action, computational paralinguistics
1. Introduction
In spoken language, speakers do not distribute emphasis uni-
formly across all words. Instead, certain words are produced
with greater prominence to highlight important information or
convey communicative intent; this relatively greater promi-
nence is known as sentence stress [1]. The placement of stress
within an utterance can influence interpretation and alter mean-
ing [2], making prominence an important component of spo-
ken language understanding and Human–Computer Interaction
(HCI) systems [3]. Automatic sentence stress detection aims to
identify these prominent words directly from spoken utterances.
Sentence stress is inherently relative and context-dependent;
therefore, we must capture both local acoustic cues and rela-
tionships between words within a sentence for effective model-
ing [4].
Acoustically, stressed words are typically associated with
increased intensity, longer duration, and pitch variation [5].
Early computational approaches therefore relied on handcrafted
prosodic features such as fundamental frequency (F0), energy,
duration, and spectral tilt combined with conventional machine
learning models [6, 7, 8]. Later systems incorporated sequential
modeling techniques, including HMM-based frameworks and
bidirectional Recurrent neural networks (RNNs), to better cap-
ture contextual dependencies across words [9, 10]. Although
these approaches improved performance, they depend heavily
on manually engineered features and large annotated datasets,

limiting scalability and robustness across speakers and domains.
Recent advances in large-scale pretrained speech models have
substantially reduced this dependency on handcrafted represen-
tations. Self-supervised models such as wav2vec 2.0 [11] and
HuBERT [12], along with encoder–decoder architectures such
as Whisper [13], learn hierarchical speech representations from
massive unlabeled corpora that transfer effectively to down-
stream tasks.
From a linguistic perspective, sentence stress cannot be de-
termined solely from local acoustic realization. Perceptual stud-
ies show that stress judgements depend jointly on acoustic cues
and higher-level linguistic structure [14]. It is further shown
that both acoustic and lexical cues provide complementary in-
formation for sentence prominence detection, with models in-
tegrating both sources achieving improved prediction perfor-
mance [4]. Recent work such as SupraDoRAL [15] operational-
izes this view, showing that stress modeling benefits from cap-
turing suprasegmental dependencies. Together, these findings
highlight the need for models capable of jointly representing
acoustic realization and sentence-level contextual structure at
the word level.
Encoder–decoder speech architectures provide a natural
mechanism for integrating such complementary information.
In models such as Whisper, the encoder primarily represents
acoustic–phonetic content extracted from speech, while the au-
toregressive decoder models longer-range linguistic and contex-
tual dependencies through cross-attention. Motivated by these
properties, recent studies have applied Whisper-based mod-
els to sentence stress detection, demonstrating that pretrained
encoder–decoder representations contain useful prosodic infor-
mation for stress prediction [16, 17]. These approaches rely
on representations extracted from a fixed encoder and decoder
layer. This implicitly assumes that stress-related information
can be captured at a single representation level. However,
layer-wise analyses of self-supervised speech models show
that acoustic, phonetic, and word-level linguistic information
emerge progressively across layers rather than being localized
within one layer [18, 19, 20], making fixed-layer embedding
extraction insufficient for sentence stress prediction.
Proposed Approach:Addressing these limitations, we
propose a weighted layer fusion framework within Whisper for
automatic sentence stress detection. WhiSSDapt jointly learns
an optimal combination of weights across both encoder and de-
coder layers, allowing the model to adaptively combine repre-
sentations associated with acoustic and contextual information.
We also perform layer-wise analysis of the learned weights to
examine how different representation layers contribute to stress
detection and conduct ablation studies.

2. Dataset
We conduct experiments using three different datasets, cover-
ing both naturally recorded non-native speech and synthetically
generated speech with controlled emphasis patterns. This com-
bination enables us to evaluate stress detection under realistic
pronunciation variability as well as large-scale controlled su-
pervision.
ISLE corpus:We use the ISLE corpus [21], which con-
tains speech utterances from 23 non-native English speak-
ers each from Italian (ITA) and German (GER) backgrounds.
The dataset is manually annotated with word-level promi-
nence markings. Word boundaries are obtained from phoneme
transcriptions through forced alignment using the Kaldi ASR
toolkit [22], ensuring precise word-level timestamps aligned
with binary prominence labels (0 for unstressed and 1 for
stressed words). The data are partitioned into training and test
splits while maintaining a balanced distribution across speaker-
related attributes such as nativity, age, gender, and language
proficiency, following the methodology described in the orig-
inal corpus documentation. This dataset enables evaluation on
real non-native speech with natural stress variability.
TinyStress-15k:We utilize TinyStress-15k [16], a syn-
thetic dataset for sentence-level stress detection comprising ap-
proximately 15 hours of speech. Stress labels are assigned us-
ing GPT-4o-mini to select semantically significant words, and
speech is synthesised via the Google Text-to-Speech API using
SSML markup that directly adjusts amplitude, pitch and speak-
ing rate on a per-word basis. This explicit acoustic manipu-
lation ensures consistent alignment between stress annotations
and synthesised audio, enabling large-scale supervised training
with accurate stress labels.
EmphAsses:We incorporate EmphAsses [23], developed
by Meta Research. This dataset consists of synthetically gener-
ated speech in which each utterance contains at least one em-
phasized word, synthesised using four open-source Expresso
TTS voices. It includes 3,652 speech samples with 4 speaker
configurations each with 913 unique transcripts, with empha-
sis introduced through controlled voice rendering rather than
acoustic signal manipulation. The multi-voice strategy in-
creases speaker variability while maintaining controlled empha-
sis placement, making it suitable for evaluating emphasis detec-
tion and cross-speaker scalability. Model is trained on speakers
ex03, ex04 mentioned in the Emphasess corpus and test it on
ex01, ex02, similar to the baseline paper

4. Experimental Setup
We evaluate all models using Whisper-small (English-only) as
the frozen backbone, with dimensiondmodel = 768. The base ar-
chitecture consists of an additional Whisper decoder block that
attends to encoder representations via cross-attention, followed
by a two-layer feed-forward classification head (768→1536
with ReLU,1536→2logits) for token-level binary stress clas-
sification. All models are trained with weighted cross-entropy
loss to account for class imbalance, assigning class weights
[1.0,2.33]to unstressed and stressed classes respectively, op-
timized using AdamW [24] with a learning rate of5×10 −4,
weight decay of0.01, and a batch size of24, with early stop-
ping based on validation loss.
Our primary baseline follows WhiStress [16], using a fixed
encoder and decoder Layer 9 as context for the additional de-
coder block. WhiSSDapt extends this by applying learnable
scalar weights over all encoder and decoder hidden states in-
dependently, normalized via temperature-scaled softmax as de-
scribed in Section 3, withτ= 0.1found to be optimal for GER,
ITA and Emphasses datasets, andτ= 0.01for TinyStress-15k
which is substantially larger.Only the fused weights, additional
decoder block, and classification head are updated; the Whisper
backbone remains frozen throughout.

We then conduct a layer weight analysis to interpret which en-
coder and decoder layers are most informative for stress detec-
tion. Finally, we evaluate a set of fixed-layer ablation configu-
rations motivated by the layer analysis findings.

5.1. Comparison with Baselines
Table 2 reports results across all four datasets. WhiSSDapt
consistently outperforms WhiStress across both naturally spo-
ken and synthetic datasets, with relative improvements of up
to 4.48% on naturally spoken and 6.09% on synthetic speech.
WhiStress [16] conducts a prosodic information analysis to un-
derstand how prosodic cues are distributed across Whisper lay-
ers, yet does not leverage these findings in its architecture. In-
stead they evaluate a fixed set of individual layers (3, 6, 9, and
12) on TinyStress-15k and selecting Layer 9 as it yields the
highest F1 score, without exploring combinations or learning
the selection. WhiSSDapt addresses this gap directly: rather
than selecting a single fixed layer by trial, it learns an optimal
weighted combination across all encoder and decoder layers si-
multaneously, effectively automating and generalising the layer
selection that WhiStress tries to achieve manually.
On the naturally spoken ISLE subsets, WhiSSDapt addi-
tionally outperforms SupraDoRAL [15] by a substantial mar-
gin, with relative improvements of up to 9.12% on GER and
3.16% on ITA. SupraDoRAL models suprasegmental depen-
dencies at the syllable level using wav2vec 2.0 embeddings to
capture inter-syllable context for stress detection. While this
approach captures fine-grained sequential structure, it operates
within a single aggregated representation and does not benefit
from the hierarchical acoustic and contextual information avail-
able in an encoder–decoder architecture. WhiSSDapt, by con-
trast, leverages the complementary strengths of Whisper’s en-
coder and decoder layers through adaptive layer fusion, provid-
ing a richer representational basis for stress detection on non-
native speech.
5.2. Layer Weight Analysis
To understand which layers WhiSSDapt relies on, we analyse
the learned weight distributions from the trained model.
Decoder weights.As shown in the top row of Figure 2,
Layer 9 emerges as the most preferred decoder layer across all
datasets, indicating that mid-depth decoder representations are
the most useful for stress modeling. The deepest decoder layer,
despite carrying the strongest autoregressive context, appears
too specialised toward next-token prediction to support rela-
tive prominence judgments. On TinyStress-15k, this preference
is far more extreme; the weight collapses almost entirely onto
Layer 9, with all remaining layers receiving negligible weight.
We attribute this to the nature of TinyStress-15k, where stress
is introduced through direct SSML manipulation of amplitude,
pitch, and speaking rate, making prominence unambiguously
encoded in the acoustic signal. A single mid-depth representa-
tion is therefore sufficient, unlike EmphAsses, GER, and ITA,
where weight is more broadly distributed across decoder layers.

5.3. Fusion Configurations
The following configurations are motivated by the layer weight
analysis above and are evaluated to validate the layer pref-
erences identified therein. For fixed dual-encoder configura-
tions, hidden states from two encoder layers are concatenated,
yielding dimension2×d model = 1536, and projected back to
dmodel via a linear projection with GELU activation before being
passed as context to the additional decoder block.
To understand whether fixed layer combinations are suffi-
cient for sentence stress detection, we evaluate five configura-
tions, denotedE(layers)–Dn, where the encoder entry lists the
specific layers used andnis the decoder layer index.
We evaluate three encoder pairings with decoder Layer 9

the effect of deeper decoder representations, we additionally

6. Conclusion
We presented WhiSSDapt, a weighted layer fusion framework
for automatic sentence stress detection built upon a frozen
Whisper encoder–decoder backbone. By learning an adap-
tive combination of encoder and decoder layer representations,
WhiSSDapt eliminates the need for manual layer selection and
captures complementary acoustic and contextual information
more effectively than fixed-layer approaches. Experiments on
naturally spoken and synthetic datasets demonstrate consistent
improvements over WhiStress and SupraDoRAL, with relative
gains of up to 4.48% on naturally spoken and 6.09% on syn-
thetic speech. Layer weight analysis reveals two consistent
anchor points — decoder Layer 9 and encoder Layer 12 —
and ablation experiments confirm that these preferences are in-
terpretable and directly predictive of fixed-layer performance.
Future work will explore extending this framework to other
prosody modeling tasks.
