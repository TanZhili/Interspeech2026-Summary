Ada-Mic: Orientation-Adaptive and Robust Close-to-Mic Speech Detection on
Smartphone Using Generalized Cross-Correlation Features

Abstract
We introduceAda-Mic, a method for adaptive close-to-mic
speech detection that allows for flexible orientation ranges
of the smartphone. Our method uses Generalized Cross-
Correlation features as an auxiliary spatial signal that implicitly
encodes the Direction-of-Arrival of speech signals to the smart-
phone, disentangling features for distance from those relating
to orientation. It is a lightweight module that can be easily
integrated into existing close-to-mic speech detectors, adding
little to negligible amounts of extra computation. Our results
show up to 24% improvement in accuracy over prior works for
challenging distances and orientations, and demonstrates strong
robustness to background noise.Ada-Micadvances the goal
of No-Hot-Word wakening, enabling more natural interactions
with users.
Index Terms: voice smart assistants, no-hot-word wakening,
close-to-mic speech detection, phone orientation, generalized
cross-correlation
1. Introduction
No-Hot-Word wakening of smart assistants aims to provide
more natural user interaction by forgoing the hot-words em-
ployed in traditional hot-word wakening methods [1, 2, 3, 4].
Though, by doing so, it often forces the user to hold the phone
at specific postures, introducing new pain points during interac-
tions. For instance, close-to-mic speech detection is one method
of No-Hot-Word wakening which detects if the user is speaking
close to the microphone of the smart device. ProxiTalk [5] and
ProxiMic [6] are prominent works in this area, however nei-
ther the voice energy features of ProxiTalk nor the single-mic
ProxiMic solution with pop-noise and Mel spectral features can
ensure strong robustness with respect to phone orientation due
to signal attenuation with non-axial Direction of Arrival (DoA).
This imposes restrictions on the angular ranges for the phone
placement, and thus interferes with natural phone manipulation
(see Figure 1).
Other methods employ a variety of sensors for the wakening
of smart assistants. Gaze-based wakening [7, 8] is only practi-
cal for static devices (TVs, speakers, etc.), and cannot be read-
ily used on smartphones. Gesture-based methods [9, 10, 11]
utilize gesture activations (hand or face/lips) with sensors like
earphones, cameras/infra-red cameras, or microphones in ultra-
sonic ranges. Some of these sensors can be problematic when

We propose a novel framework for close-to-mic speech
detection called Adaptive-Mic(Ada-Mic)that directly utilizes
information inside Generalized Cross-Correlation (GCC) fea-
tures [13]. These features implicitly encode relevant spatial in-
formation, including DoA [14, 15, 16, 17, 18] and multi-path

mation on the phone orientation. Having this information is
beneficial since the signal energy in each channel, as well as
their difference, both depend on the incoming speech DoA.
Incorporating GCC features helps to better disentangle signal
components related to distance from those related to phone ori-
entation, which improves the detector’s ability to classify close
from far speech in a setup with varying phone orientation. Par-
ticularly, our goal is to offer a pluggable module that can be eas-
ily included in arbitrary existent architectures for close-to-mic
speech detection, by simple finetuning of the classifier and op-
tional adapter layers, while keeping baseline architectures and
their inputs unaltered, to fully preserve the knowledge in pre-

methodology consists of two streams: processing the speech in-
put and extracting DoA information from the GCC. The outputs
from these two components are fused for classification of close
vs. far distance speech.

3. Experiments
3.1. Dataset
We collected a total of 38 hours of pure speech, on Huawei
phones: Mate30, P30, Mate30Pro, Mate40E, P40Pro+, P40Pro,
P50E, Mate50Pro, P50Pro, and Mate60 (with inter-mic distance
of 14-16cm). A total of 66 volunteers (18+ of age) took part
in the study after providing a written consent. They were in-
structed to read a set of voice assistant commands to a phone
held at various distances (2cm, 5cm, 15cm, and 30cm) from the
mouth. Our dataset consisted of 1600 unique commands cov-
ering various categories including reminders, navigation, and
messaging. Participants were instructed to speak at a normal
talking volume as if they were speaking to someone directly
in front of them. A set of two-minute audio clips were col-
lected from each volunteer. On average, each volunteer went
through 580 randomly selected commands across all of their
sessions. The volunteers recorded multiple audio clips for each
of the following phone orientation categories, with diverse an-
gular ranges as shown in Figure 1:
1. Spoken into the bottom microphone. (a) Direct: Laid flat
with pitch angleα≈−20to20 ◦. (b) Indirect: Raised up with
pitch angleα≈30to70 ◦ and/or with yaw angleψ≈−45to
45◦.
2. Spoken into the top microphone. (a) Direct: Laid flat
with pitch angleα≈150to180 ◦. (b) Indirect: Lowered with
pitch angleα≈90to140 ◦ and/or with yaw angleψ≈−45to
45◦.
We note that the operational ranges for the close-to-mic
speech detection method proposed in [6] correspond to pitch
angle variations of up to an absolute elevation of∼45 ◦ only.
Collected audio samples were subsampled to 16 kHz and
intervals containing speech were identified using Silero V AD
[20]. Data were split into training, validation, and test sets as a
90-5-5 split, with no overlap between samples or participants.
The test split contained only the samples from Mate60 speech
recordings. Additionally, we collected a total of 6.52 hours of
noise samples from various sources on Mate60 phones. Noise
sources included: (a) Music noise (pure music, or music played
in meeting rooms and offices), and (b) Office noise (meeting
room and office background speech/noise). Approximately 4-

3.2. Implementation Details
We establish two baselines for method comparison: (a) A
lightweight Convolutional Neural Network (CNN) backbone
that we build following the model in the SoTA close-to-mic
speech detection framework, ProxiMic [6], and (b) A large fine-
tuned foundational speech model, HuBERT [21], with the back-
bone based on the transformer architecture. Since the GCC
requires dual-channel data, we extend the ProxiMic backbone
models with an additional input channel to handle dual-channel
inputs for fair comparison and train them from scratch. For
HuBERT, we duplicate the pre-trained weights of the first pro-
jection convolution.
Our speech data were preprocessed according to the back-
bone model used. For the ProxiMic-based [6] model, we ex-
tract an80×200two-dimensional time-frequency spectro-
gram for each audio channel, from the 1s input signal (at 8 kHz
sampling rate) using the Short-Time Fourier Transform (STFT).
The STFT window size is set to 20 ms and the hop length is set
to 5 ms. We then filter by triangular filter bank, defined as a mix
of linear and Mel-scale filters as in [6]. After preprocessing,
the final input feature is a Mel-spectrum STFT 40×200 (fre-
quency×time) matrix, where the first dimension is obtained
by concatenating the two audio channels along the frequency
dimension. For the HuBERT-based model, the input is a 1s
dual-channel audio waveform sampled at 16 kHz, which was
preprocessed following [21].
The ProxiMic backbone is defined with audio data only,
which is a CNN processing dual-channel spectrogram data in
this implementation. It consists of two 1D convolutions with
kernel size=3 and output channels 50 and 100 respectively, in-
terleaved with a max pooling layer with kernel size=3, stride=3,
and padding=1. The final global max pooling layer yields a
100-dimensional feature vector.
For HuBERT, we attach a classifier to the end of the encoder
and fine-tune the model using Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA)
[22] withr= 8andα= 1. The classifier is constructed as
follows: we first apply a trainable weighted average to combine
the encoder outputs. We then use three 1D point-wise convolu-
tions with 256 output channels and ReLU activations, resulting
in a 256-dimensional feature vector.
The GCC vectors were estimated using the GCC-PHAT
method on the input dual-channel audio intervals. We over-
sample the IFFT in the GCC-PHAT calculation to increase the
precision in peak position interpolation, and regard the sam-
ples that correspond to[−2τ,2τ], whereτis the maximum
possible absolute delay between two microphones. This al-
lows us to also capture the information on the multi-path re-
flections, assumed to likewise implicitly encode the phone ori-
entation, further contributing to disentanglement of phone ori-
entation from its distance, on top of the mechanisms behind the
primary GCC-PHAT signals, leading to additional boost in clas-
sification accuracy. Finally, we subsample the GCC vectors to
100 points. The addition of the GCC feature encoder results
in solely 7.6% of extra FLOPs for ProxiMic, and<0.01%
for HuBERT, which is negligible. Normalization implements
Batchnorm, and activation- ReLU. We adopt a residual archi-
tecture for the GCC encoder, where the sum of the main and
residual branches are used as input to the following block. The
two feature vectors that result from the backbone and GCC-
branch are concatenated and are fed through a dense layer with
sigmoid activation, which outputs a single probability for close
vs. far distance from the phone. A summary of the architecture
is shown in Figure 3.
The binary cross-entropy loss function was used during
training and the Adam optimizer was used with default parame-
ters. The batch size was set to 64 and the training was done for
200 epochs with a learning rate of4×10 −6.

In more detail, the boost in accuracy is particularly promi-
nent for the transition distances of 5 and 15 cm, where we ob-
serve consistent, substantial improvements of∼5%over the
baselines. As an example, for the 5 cm bottom indirect orienta-
tion, our framework is able to disentangle the signal confusion
and improves∼10%compared to the baselines. For the top mi-
crophone orientations, the transition distances appear somewhat
more challenging, which we speculate is due to the smaller mi-
crophone gain of the top microphone on Mate60 smartphones.
As the GCC is recorded relative to the bottom microphone, the
Ada-Micmodels are able to distinguish between the bottom
and top microphone orientations, resulting in accuracy improve-
ments of up to24%.
Our experimental results demonstrate the benefit of intro-
ducing the GCC features to the system, and suggests the usage
of our methodology as a potential plugin to CNN-based dual-
channel close-to-mic solutions. The shown improvements on
test data that includes a large range of feasible phone orienta-
tions (Section 3.1) suggest that our methodology can signifi-
cantly extend the feasible ranges of phone orientations, and al-
low for more natural phone manipulation by the user.
4. Conclusions
We proposeAda-Mic, a robust close-to-mic speech detection
system inside a novel framework of no-hot-word-wakening on
mobile phones. This task is inherently challenging because the
received audio mixes components related to both the speaker’s
distance and their angle relative to the device. To address
this,Ada-Micleverages the angular dependency of radiation
patterns—implemented using GCC-PHAT vectors—to effec-
tively disentangle these two components. We integrate this as a
lightweight, pluggable module that reuses the main model’s in-
put data. With minimal computational overhead, this approach
isolates close-to-mic speech and resolves prior challenges re-
garding robustness to varying phone orientations.
Our experimental results suggest considerable benefits of
the proposed method, particularly in the close-to-far transition
distances. Moreover, the method can be applied to any de-
vice with two microphones, which includes most modern smart-
phones. We hope that this work inspires further research into
strengthening the robustness of close-to-mic speech detection
and improving the interactions with smart assistants.
