Abstract
Textual captions can unlock the reasoning capabilities of Large
Language Models (LLMs) for Audio-Visual Question Answer
(A VQA), yet our investigation reveals that naive audio captions
often degrade performance. They lack task-relevant detail and
are inconsistent with the visual scene. We propose VividAC, a
simple training-free framework in which a Visual Agent gen-
erates query-relevant video descriptions that guide an Audial
Agent to produce visually contextualized audio captions. The
entire pipeline requires no audio-visual joint training and op-
erates solely through natural-language communication between
off-the-shelf models. On MUSIC-A VQA, VividAC surpasses
the strongest end-to-end Audio-Visual LLM by 7.0%p and de-
livers consistent gains across 11 of the 12 tested LLM-VLM
combinations, with improvements up to 11.01%p, confirming
that structured cross-modal communication between off-the-
shelf agents offer a practical path toward stronger audio-visual
reasoning.
Index Terms: audio captioning, audio-visual question answer-
ing, zero-shot learning, inter-agent communication
1. Introduction
A central desideratum in the multimodal community is to build
agents that can jointly look and listen to understand the world,
reason about the interplay between modalities, and tell their
understanding through language. Vision-Language (VL) re-
search has achieved significant advancements in recent years,
focusing on understanding and responding to visual informa-
tion. Notably, the performance of Visual Question Answering
(VQA)[1] and Video Question Answering (VideoQA) [2] has
been enhanced by leveraging image or video captions [3, 4, 5].
These studies suggest that textual captions can effectively aid
the visual reasoning capabilities of large models [6, 7]. Fur-
thermore, Vision-Language Models (VLMs) have emerged as
powerful tools, enabling Large Language Models (LLMs) to
leverage their strong reasoning and world knowledge for visual
understanding tasks [8, 9, 10]. However, Audio-Visual Ques-
tion Answering (A VQA)[11] differs from this research focus,
as it inherently requires more complex and demanding simulta-
neous understanding and joint reasoning across visual and au-
ditory modalities [12, 13]. Despite its importance, developing
robust methods to integrate salient auditory information with
visual cues for comprehensive A VQA reasoning remains a sig-
nificant challenge [14, 15]. This difficulty often leads models to
underutilize or misinterpret the audio modality [16, 17].
Dedicated Audio-Visual Large Language Models (A V-
LLMs) [18, 19] have been developed to enhance complex rea-

information for joint reasoning has proven a persistent hurdle,
often hindering a full capture of their nuanced interplay. One
might hope that audio captions could serve as an effective tex-
tual interface, unlocking robust audio-visual reasoning capa-
bilities in large models for A VQA. However, our initial inves-
tigation reveals that this direction is fraught with challenges.
Specifically, using naive audio captions may yield counterpro-
ductive outputs. Our experimental evidence in Figure 1 (a)
highlights the critical concern: in certain cases, the use of naive
audio captions can lead to a notable performance drop com-
pared to relying solely on visual information.
This failure stems from the inherent limitations of naive
audio captioning methods when applied to A VQA reasoning.
Figure 1 (b) quantitatively demonstrates the visual context in-
consistencies of naive audio captions. Specifically, their entity
overlap score, calculated using Jaccard similarity on MUSIC-
A VQA instrument nouns [20], is significantly lower than ours.
This disparity indicates substantial content mismatch for naive
captions. Moreover, the naive captions not only struggle with
content-level consistency but also frequently fail to provide
query-specific information and can even misrepresent the vi-
sual scene. This failure is clearly demonstrated in Figure 1 (c).
For instance, when the visual context and question refer to a
“guzheng,” a naive audio caption might mention a “harp,” high-
lighting a visual inconsistency and failing to provide contextu-
ally crucial details for A VQA.
To overcome these limitations, we propose VividAC
(Visually Informed and Visually Interacted Audio Captioning),
a simple cascaded framework centered on structured interac-
tion between a Visual Agent and an Audial Agent, realized by
VLMs and Audio Language Models (ALMs) respectively. Cru-
cially, VividAC requires no joint audio-visual training or pa-
rameter modification — the entire framework operates through
natural-language communication between existing models. The
core of VividAC is a cascaded information flow that generates
enhanced, visually contextualized audio captions.
VividAC begins with the Visual Agent generating a query-
relevant video caption by utilizing noun phrases from the
A VQA question. This yields a concise textual representation
of the visual scene that is directly pertinent to the query. This
structured, question-aware video caption then provides the con-
textual foundation for the Audial Agent to produce a visually
contextualized audio caption, ensuring alignment with both the
visual narrative and the specific question. While the cascaded
communication in VividAC inherently risks error propagation
from the initial video caption, we mitigate this by refining the
visual context provided to the Audial Agent. Specifically, we
extract nouns from the video caption that exhibit high textual

2. Method
While recent work has explored cascaded prompting and multi-
agent orchestration for complex reasoning [21, 22, 23], these
approaches operate within a single modality. We show that a
minimal cross-modal variant—requiring no training and only
off-the-shelf models—suffices to substantially improve Audio-
Visual Question Answering (A VQA). To this end, we propose
VividAC (Visually I nformed and V isually I nteracted Audio
Captioning), which implements a structured, cascaded commu-
nication flow: a Visual Agent processes visual information rele-
vant to the query, and its output directly guides an Audial Agent.

2.1. Inter-Agent Communication
The core of VividAC is a natural-language-based information
flow that facilitates structured interaction between the Visual
and Audial Agents, enabling the generation of visually in-
formed audio captions (C a). This specific flow, as illustrated
in Figure 2, begins with the Visual Agent generating a query-
relevant video caption,C v. Specifically given the videoVand
nouns NP(Q) 1 extracted from the questionQ, the Visual Agent
producesC v based onVand NP(Q).
This query-relevantC v then provides crucial textual con-
text to the Audial Agent. The Audial Agent generates the final
audio captionC a by conditioning on the audio inputA, the vi-
sual contextC v, and specific instructions designed to foster this
visual grounding, as shown in Table 1. This sequential condi-
tioning is a cornerstone of VividAC. It ensures thatC a is not
merely a standalone audio description but is closely linked to
the visual description. We posit that such tightly coupled, cross-
modal conditioning is vital for resolving the ambiguities and
fostering coherent audio-visual reasoning. The efficacy of this
interactive approach in enhancing A VQA performance across
various settings is empirically demonstrated in Section 3.
2.2. Mitigating Error Propagation
Our framework employs a cascaded information flow from a
Visual Agent to an Audial Agent to generate visually informed
audio captions,C a. The Visual Agent initially produces a video
descriptionC v that, while providing visual context, risks propa-
gating errors if it contains query-irrelevant or inaccurate details.
To mitigate this, we introduce a query-guided video caption
refinement step. This step aims to filter out query-irrelevant
noise or potentially misleading details withinC v by distilling
its most query-relevant elements.
The refinement process operates as follows: first, we ex-
tract all nouns fromC v as candidate terms. Then, to ensure
these terms are directly aligned with the questionQ, we cal-
culate their semantic similarity toQusing the text encoder of
CLIP[24]. We compute CLIP-based similarity between each
question noun and each candidate visual noun, and retain can-
didate nouns if their maximum similarity to any question noun
exceeds (τ= 0.8). This yields a distilled set of query-focused
visual nouns, which are then provided as contextual guidance to
the Audial Agent. The refined set of nouns is then incorporated
into the prompt for the Audial Agent. For instance, we add the
⟨keywords⟩in the Audial Agent’s input template shown in Ta-
ble 1, to guide the generation of query-relevant audio captions.

3. Experiments
3.1. Experimental Details
We evaluate our framework using multiple model combinations.
For the visual agent, we employ Qwen2.5-VL (7B, 3B) [25],
VideoChat-R1-7B [26] and InternVL2.5-8B [27]. The audial
agent uses Qwen2-Audio-7B-Instruct [28] to generate contex-
tualized audio captions. For the reasoning agent, we primarily
report results with Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct [29], Llama-3.1-8B-
Instruct [30], and Mistral-7B-Instruct [31].
3.2. Datasets
We evaluate the VividAC framework on the MUSIC-A VQA
[20] benchmark. MUSIC-A VQA features videos of instrumen-
tal performances and includes questions that are categorized
into nine types, testing various reasoning skills. For our experi-
ments, we used the official test split, which comprises 9192 QA
pairs corresponding to 6399 unique video and audio samples.
3.3. Evaluation Metric
To assess our method’s performance, we employ an accuracy
metric consistent with prior work [32]. Before any comparison,
both the generated answers and the ground-truth texts undergo
standard normalization. Accuracy is subsequently assessed by
determining whether the normalized predicted response con-
tains the keywords in the normalized ground truth answer. A
response is deemed correct if these essential ground truth words
are present in the normalized prediction.
3.4. Overall Result
Table 2 presents zero-shot results on MUSIC-A VQA. Vivi-
dAC consistently outperforms the Naive baseline, with gains
up to 11.01%p (Llama-3.1 + Qwen2.5-3B). The improvement
is model-agnostic: it holds across all three LLM backbones and
four VLMs, suggesting the benefit stems from inter-agent inter-
action. Gains are most pronounced for weaker baseline config-
urations, indicating that VividAC effectively compensates for
limited audio-visual reasoning capacity. The sole exception is
Llama-3.1 + InternVL2.5 (−0.63%p). However, even in this
case, A-Avg improves by 2.37%p, suggesting that VividAC still
enhances audio-centric reasoning.

3.6. Impact of Interaction Directionality
We ablate the direction of inter-agent communication by
comparing our proposed Vision-to-Audio (V→A) interaction
with an Audio-to-Vision (A→V) flow and a “No Interaction”
baseline, as shown in Table 4. With Qwen2.5-VL-7B as
the Visual Agent and Mistral as the reasoning LLM, V→A
achieves 58.18%, outperforming “No Interaction” by 2.63%p
and “A→V” by 5.64%p. The consistent superiority of “V→A”
suggests that visual context, which carries richer scene-level
cues, is more effective as apromptfor refining audio captions
than audio context is for refining audio captions.
3.7. Integration with Existing A V-LLMs
We further investigate whether VividAC-generated captions can
enhance existing end-to-end A V-LLMs when provided as sup-
plementary text input. The results are shown in Figure 3. The
consistent advantage over naive captions suggests that incorpo-
rating visual context during caption generation yields higher-
quality audio captions that better support downstream A VQA
reasoning. This provides empirical evidence that audio-visual
inter-agent communication is an effective mechanism for pro-
ducing captions that are more useful for A V-LLMs.

4. Conclusion
We introduce VividAC, a simple cascaded framework in which
a Visual Agent generates query-relevant descriptions that guide
an Audial Agent to produce visually contextualized audio cap-
tions for A VQA. Without any audio-visual joint training, Vivi-
dAC surpasses Video-SALMONN on MUSIC-A VQA and de-
livers consistent gains across 11 of the 12 LLM-VLM combina-
tions, confirming that the improvement stems from inter-agent
communication rather than a specific model pairing. These
results suggest that structured cross-modal dialogue between
off-the-shelf agents offers a practical and scalable path toward
stronger audio-visual reasoning, complementary to end-to-end
approaches.
