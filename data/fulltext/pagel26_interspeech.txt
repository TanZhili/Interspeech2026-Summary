What happens when we speak together?
Multidimensional convergence in face-to-face interaction

Abstract
Convergence is a pervasive phenomenon in human commu-
nication, in which speakers adapt their behaviour to their in-
terlocutor. It is assumed to reflect both automatic and so-
cially motivated processes. However, existing research has pre-
dominantly examined individual parameters in isolation, lead-
ing to a fragmented understanding. Moreover, the interplay
between patterns of convergence and modulations related to
prosodic structure remains largely unexplored. This study ad-
dresses these issues by investigating convergence as a multi-
dimensional and multimodal phenomenon, with particular at-
tention to its relationship with prosodic prominence. Drawing
on an information-structurally controlled dataset of interactive
speech from 15 dyads, we analyse several acoustic–prosodic,
articulatory, and co-speech kinematic parameters, providing a
more comprehensive account of convergence.
Index Terms: accommodation, convergence, face-to-face con-
versation, prosody, multimodality
1. Introduction
Human interaction is characterised by the tendency of individ-
uals to adjust their behaviours to those of others. Suchconver-
gence, also referred to as accommodation, entrainment, or in-
teractive alignment, has been investigated across a wide range
of disciplines, including sociology, psychology, neuroscience,
computer science, and linguistics [1].
1.1. Convergence in interaction
In conversation, convergence can be defined as the “similar-
ity of communicative behaviors between conversing interlocu-
tors”, spanning multiple linguistic dimensions [2]. Beyond
word choice or syntactic structuring [3, 4], interlocutors report-
edly converge to each other in acoustic–prosodic parameters of
speech, such as fundamental frequency (F0) and temporal char-
acteristics [5, 6]. There is preliminary evidence that they also
mutually adapt in the supra-laryngeal articulatory movements
underlying sound production, e.g., in jaw, lip, and tongue kine-
matics [7]. Since communication extends beyond the auditory
to the visual modality, convergence has likewise been examined
in speech-accompanying body movements, where it surfaces in
manual gestures, head movements, or facial expression [8, 9].
Convergence between interlocutors has been shown to have pos-
itive interactional and social correlates: It has been described as
a “social glue” that facilitates effective communication, fosters
relationships, and helps building trust [10].

1.2. The role of prosodic prominence
The way a speaker produces an utterance is not only influenced
by their interlocutor but also closely tied to information struc-
ture. Words considered important for the listener are in focus,
while others remain in the background [11, 12]. Focus is ex-
pressed through enhancedprosodic prominenceacross the au-
ditory and visual modalities. Given that prosodic prominence
recruits subsystems of speech production that partially overlap
with those involved in inter-speaker convergence, it is crucial to
consider its role in convergence processes.
In many languages, a salient correlate of prosodic promi-
nence is the placement of a pitch accent [13]. Additionally, the
phonological type and phonetic realisation of the accent may
vary with degrees of prominence [14, 15, 16]. Prominent units
typically exhibit longer durations [17, 18, 19] and enhanced ar-
ticulatory movements of the jaw, lips, and tongue, referred to
as prosodic strengthening [20, 21, 22, 23]. More recently, stud-
ies have shown that prominent words are also frequently ac-
companied by salient body movements in the visual modality
[24, 25, 26, 27].
1.3. The present study
The present study aims to address two challenges arising from
previous convergence research, namely tied to parameter choice
and prosodic prominence. Regarding parameter choice, it be-
comes evident that convergence has been investigated exten-
sively in the acoustic–prosodic domain, but remains relatively
underexplored in supra-laryngeal articulation and co-speech
body movements. Moreover, studies typically examine conver-
gence in isolated parameters instead of including several pa-
rameters simultaneously. However, this leaves the relations be-
tween parameters in convergence processes unclear, and draw-
ing broader conclusions about the presence or absence of con-
vergence based on a single parameter may contribute to the het-
erogeneity of previous findings. The present study addresses
this challenge by adopting a multidimensional approach, exam-
ining convergence across four parameters, to characterise their
relative weighting within multidimensional convergence pro-
files across dyads.
Regarding the role of prosodic structure in modulating con-
vergence processes, it becomes evident that previous conver-
gence analyses rarely control for the prosodic organisation of
the speech material. However, this risks confounding con-
vergence between speakers with prominence-driven variation
within speakers. The present study therefore draws on an
information-structurally controlled dataset, enabling a system-
atic investigation of whether and how interlocutors converge to
each other in their cues to prosodic prominence.

2. Methods
2.1. Data collection
The study was conducted under ethics approval granted to the
IfL phonetics laboratory, University of Cologne, Germany, by
the ethics committee of the DGS (2020-04-200327). Each par-
ticipant gave written informed consent and the research was
conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. For
details on the data collection, see [28] and https://osf.io/9fmqh.
2.1.1. Participants
The study involved 30 native speakers of German between 18
and 36 years of age (mean=24.67, SD=4.51), including 17
female, one non-binary, and 12 male participants. They were
unaware of the experimental aim and reported no speech or
voice disorders, nor any reading, writing, vision, or hearing im-
pairments. For each session, two previously unacquainted par-
ticipants were paired together, resulting in a total of 15 dyads.
2.1.2. Speech material
The produced speech material was controlled both lexically
and information-structurally, to permit fine-grained multidi-
mensional analyses. For the subset of the corpus reported here,
four trisyllabic proper names were used as target words: Medina
(/me"di:na/), Manila (/ma"ni:la/), Benali (/be"na:li/), and Milano
(/mi"la:no/). The penultimate syllable was lexically stressed and
served as the target syllable. Target words were embedded in
consistent German carrier sentences as part of question–answer
pairs. Each target utterance was preceded by a question de-
signed to elicit the target word either in corrective focus (CF) or
in the background (BG), as illustrated in Table 1. Only tokens
produced in corrective focus entered the convergence analyses
reported here.
2.1.3. Task and communicative modes
The question–answer sequences were elicited through a
purpose-built card game,DiCE(Dialogic Collecting Expedi-
tion, [28]), which was implemented in two communicative
modes: solo and dialogue (see Figure 1). Within the game, par-
ticipants assumed the role of collectors sorting valuable objects
from different cities into piles, according to their value and ori-
gin — the latter corresponding to the target words. Task instruc-
tions ensured adherence to the predetermined lexical structure
of the utterances. In the dialogue mode, the two participants
played cooperatively, alternating between posing questions and
providing responses. In the preceding solo condition, each par-
ticipant completed a simplified version of the task individually,
responding to questions presented on a screen. This solo condi-
tion served as a baseline for assessing individual speaking pat-
terns prior to the interaction.
2.1.4. Recording technique
Data were collected using two electromagnetic articulographs
(EMAs) and two head-mounted microphones. Sixteen EMA

torso, to capture movements of supra-laryngeal articulation as

three video cameras, but results are reported elsewhere.
2.2. Data analysis
The data and code for the present data analysis are provided in
a repository: https://osf.io/3uebk.
2.2.1. Post-processing
Audio recordings were annotated for target words and their con-
stituent segments using OpenAI Whisper [29] and the Mon-
treal Forced Aligner [30], with intermediate manual correc-
tions performed in Praat [31]. Kinematic EMA data were pro-
cessed with Carstens NormPos and theema2wavconverter
[32], extracting data on tongue-body and head movements.
F0 was derived through a multi-step procedure implemented
inparselmouth[33], which involved estimating speaker-
specific pitch floors, converting values to semitones, and ap-
plying a low-pass filter. In total, 2,230 target-word productions
in corrective focus were included in the analysis, comprising
1,110 from the solo mode and 1,120 from the dialogue mode.
2.2.2. Analysed parameters
The analysis included four parameters spanning the acoustic–
prosodic, supra-laryngeal articulatory, and co-speech visual do-
mains (see Figure 2). Parameters were analysed with respect to
the target word or, where appropriate, a narrower target window
encompassing the target syllable±one adjacent segment.
•Duration: acoustic time interval of the target word (ms)
•F0 excursion: difference between the maximum and mini-
mum F0 within the target window (st)
•Tongue displacement: absolute tongue-body displacement
from the preceding vowel to the stressed target vowel, based
on vertical/high–low motion (mm; restricted to target words
‘Manila’ and ‘Milano’)
•Head velocity: maximum tangential head speed during
the target word, based on combined vertical/high–low and
horizontal/front–back motion (mm/s)
These parameters were selected based on prior research on
focus encoding, as they have been established as signatures of
prosodic prominence, capturing temporal and spatial enhance-
ments across auditory and visual modalities. Additionally, the
parameter choice aimed to span both well-researched and rela-
tively unexplored dimensions of inter-speaker convergence.

2.2.3. Validity analysis of prosodic prominence
The suitability of the data set and the selected parameters was
evaluated through an analysis of focus-encoding patterns across
speakers. Word productions in corrective focus were compared
with those in the background. This confirmed that speakers reli-
ably produced prosodically prominent target words, as reflected
in the four parameters. Focused target words showed longer
durations, larger F0 excursions, greater tongue displacements,
and higher head velocities. Accordingly, only target words in
corrective focus were retained for the convergence analysis.
2.2.4. Modelling
Convergence was operationalised as a reduction in between-
speaker distance in the dialogue relative to the solo mode.
This was assessed separately for each combination of param-
eter×dyad, fitting one Bayesian hierarchical linear model per
combination. The modelling was implemented inbrms[34] in
R [35], yielding a total of 60 models. Parameter values were
modelled as a function of speaker within the dyad (A vs. B),
communicative mode (solo vs. dialogue), and their interaction,
including a random intercept and mode slope by target word:
landmark_value∼speaker * mode +
(1 + mode | target_word)
Weakly informative priors were specified, with prior pre-
dictive checks confirming their suitability. Models were sam-
pled using four MCMC chains (9,000 iterations each, 3,000
warmup) and employed Gaussian families for duration and
tongue displacement, and lognormal families for F0 excursion
and head velocity. Diagnostics indicated model robustness
( ˆR≤1.00, ESS>100), with few divergent transitions. Pos-
terior predictive checks confirmed good model fit, and HMC
diagnostics revealed no irregularities.

the model’s conditional means (by speaker and mode) and the
posterior distribution of the difference in between-speaker dis-
tance from solo to dialogue (βd−s), including its mean and cred-
ible intervals. The more negative this difference, the more simi-
lar speakers became when shifting from the solo to the dialogue
mode. Additionally, the posterior probability that this differ-

3. Results
Out of the 15 dyads, evidence for convergence is compelling
in nine dyads for duration, eight dyads for F0 excursion, two
dyads for vertical tongue-body displacement, and four dyads for
head velocity. The correspondingβ d−s values andP(β d−s <0)
are reported in Table 2. Comparison with pseudo-dyads shows
that pseudo-distributions are centred on zero, with posterior
probabilities near 0.5, whereas the distributions for real dyads
are concentrated away from zero, with posterior probabilities
≥0.90. These results indicate that the observed convergence
in real dyads reflects genuine interlocutor-specific adaptation
rather than a general artefact of the change in mode.
The proportions of dyads showing convergence, diver-
gence, and no change are visualised in Figure 3. Duration ex-
hibits the highest number of converging dyads, while only few
dyads show divergence or no change. F0 excursion displays a
similar pattern, albeit with slightly fewer converging dyads but
also fewer diverging dyads. Head velocity shows a more mixed
distribution, including both converging dyads and a noticeable
proportion showing divergence or stability. Tongue-body dis-
placement exhibits the fewest converging dyads and a compar-
atively high proportion of dyads showing no change.
The posterior probability values underlying this classifica-
tion are visualised for all 15 dyads in Figure 4, offering a more
fine-grained view of effects beyond threshold-based classifica-
tions. This representation also facilitates the examination of
dyad-specific patterns. The results reveal substantial variabil-
ity across dyads: One dyad shows no convergence in any of
the examined measures, others converge in up to three param-
eters, and the majority fall between these extremes. The spe-
cific parameters that dyads converge in vary across dyads but
generally reflect differences in parameter-specific convergence
strength reported above. Convergence in tongue displacement
or head velocity is rarely present in isolation but typically co-
occurs with convergence in duration and/or F0. In contrast, du-
ration and F0 may constitute the only parameters in which con-
vergence is observed.

4. Discussion
The results demonstrate that convergence occurs across all four
examined parameters, indicating that interlocutors converge in
cues to prosodic prominence. Notably, however, the parame-
ters differ substantially in their susceptibility to convergence:
Acoustic–prosodic dimensions are most strongly converged in,
followed by co-speech motion, and lastly supra-laryngeal artic-
ulation. While we hypothesise that convergence effects might
be even more pronounced in less controlled interactive settings,
this remains subject to future investigation.
Earlier work has often interpreted parameter-specific differ-
ences in convergence as evidence of weak or unreliable effects.
In contrast, we argue that such variation should not be dismissed
as a limitation but rather be understood as an informative feature
of convergence. Convergence appears to be inherently multidi-
mensional, with the strength of convergence differing systemat-
ically across parameters. This selectivity is plausibly shaped by
multiple interacting factors, among whichperceptual saliency,
constraints on flexibility, and the extent of interlocutors’expo-
sureto a given parameter may play particularly important roles.
Perceptual saliency refers to the degree to which a cue is
noticeable or accessible to listeners [37, 38]. Phonetic cues
vary in saliency, often reflecting their communicative relevance
and functional load. We understand saliency to shape con-
vergence in two ways: Speakers can align only in percepti-
ble features, and alignment in salient cues is more likely to be
noticed by the interlocutor, amplifying its communicative im-
pact (see [39, 40, 41, 42, 43] for related accounts). Acoustic–
prosodic cues such as duration and F0 are highly salient due
to their communicative relevance, influencing listeners’ percep-
tion of emotion, prosodic structure, intelligibility, and accent
[44, 45, 46, 47, 48]. Tongue movements, by contrast, are pri-
marily tied to sound production and are perceived only indi-
rectly via formant cues. Their perception relies more heavily
on categorical phonological contrasts, rendering subtle gradient
modulations less salient to listeners [49, 50, 51]. Head move-
ments occupy an intermediate position: They serve a range of
communicative functions and are integrated into speech pro-
cessing; yet their perceptual saliency appears comparatively
lower, functioning primarily as a visual complement to audi-
tory information and becoming more relevant particularly when
auditory cues are ambiguous or degraded [52, 53, 54, 55].
A second relevant factor is the degree of flexibility within
a parameter. Parameters with weaker constraints on variabil-
ity may allow for greater convergence, both because individual
speakers are more adaptable and because larger baseline differ-
ences between speakers enable more pronounced adjustments
(see [56] and [57] for related accounts). Acoustic–prosodic
features such as duration and F0 typically exhibit substantial
variability [58, 59], whereas supra-laryngeal articulation is sub-
ject to strong constraints arising from phonological require-
ments, physiological limits, and highly routinised motor pat-
terns [60, 61, 62]. Head movements are relatively flexible but
remain limited due to demands of postural stability, biomechan-
ical coupling with speech, and coordination with gaze and facial
expressions [63, 64, 65], covering a middle ground.
A third important factor influencing parameter susceptibil-
ity to convergence is exposure. Both in the present experiment
and in everyday interaction, exposure to specific multimodal
speech cues is unevenly distributed across parameters. Yet suf-
ficient exposure constitutes a prerequisite for convergence (see
[66] for a related account). For instance, to perceive how an in-
terlocutor articulates a given vowel (and thereby to infer supra-
laryngeal configurations), a listener must encounter that vowel
in the relevant segmental and prosodic context, as this informa-
tion cannot easily be derived from other parts of speech. By
contrast, parameters such as F0 and temporal cues are present
throughout larger stretches of the interlocutor’s speech, allow-
ing listeners to extract general patterns across entire utterances
rather than isolated segments. While head velocity is similarly
broadly available across the speech stream, its only moderate
convergence suggests that exposure alone is insufficient to ex-
plain convergence strength, pointing to the relevant influences
of other factors, such as saliency and flexibility.
Taken together, the high perceptual saliency of duration and
F0, along with their flexibility and frequent exposure, likely ac-
counts for their strong convergence. Head motion occupies an
intermediate position, while the weaker convergence observed
in supra-laryngeal articulation may reflect its lower saliency,
greater constraints, and more limited exposure. Convergence
is not a uniform process, as some dimensions appear to provide
a natural substrate for interpersonal convergence, whereas oth-
ers entail higher costs and offer lower communicative gain. The
emerging selective multidimensional convergence profiles can
thus be interpreted as functionally relevant and strategic, bal-
ancing stability, adaptability, and communicative efficiency.
5. Conclusion
The present study explores the multidimensional nature of con-
vergence in dyadic conversation. Controlled interactive speech
was analysed across four parameters spanning the auditory and
visual modalities. Results reveal substantial inter-parameter dif-
ferences in susceptibility to convergence: Acoustic–prosodic
dimensions are most strongly converged, followed by co-speech
body movements, and lastly supra-laryngeal articulation. We
argue that convergence strength is systematically shaped by
parameter-inherent perceptual saliency, constraints on flexibil-
ity, and extent of exposure. This selective multidimensionality
appears to be an inherent property of convergence, supporting
both stability and adaptability, while reflecting a strategic bal-
ance between production effort and communicative benefit.
