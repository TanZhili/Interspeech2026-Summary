Abstract
Alzheimer’s disease and related dementia (ADRD) remain
largely undiagnosed, as early cognitive symptoms are rarely
captured by structured clinical data. Natural speech offers a
sensitive, non-invasive window into cognitive decline that struc-
tured clinical data cannot provide. This study validates conver-
sational speech from phone calls and participant verbal com-
munication as a biomarker for early cognitive impairment de-
tection. Using an attention-based fusion model across 175
participants, structured clinical data alone achieved AUC =
0.74; adding phone calls improved performance (AUC = 0.76),
and participant verbal communication yielded the largest gains
(AUC = 0.90). The best configuration achieved AUC = 0.92
and F1 = 83.08, establishing naturalistic speech as a scalable,
non-invasive biomarker in clinical settings.
Index Terms: Speech biomarkers, Multimodal fusion,
Alzheimer’s disease detection, Naturalistic Speech
1. Introduction
Alzheimer’s disease and related dementia (ADRD) represent a
growing health burden [1], with 11–16 million projected cases
by 2050 [2, 3]. Despite high healthcare costs, more than half of
cases remain undiagnosed, often due to limited biomarkers and
the subtlety of early symptoms [4, 5]. In response, the National
Institute on Aging prioritizes AI–based screening methods for
earlier detection.
Electronic Health Record (EHR) algorithms show limited
success, as early cognitive signs are sparsely documented. Zol-
noori et al. [6] reported F1 = 63% and AUC = 0.76 using
Word2Vec on nursing notes plus structured data. Mao et al.
[7] fine-tuned BioClinicalBERT [8] on notes from patients with
prior MCI, achieving AUC of 0.88 and F1 = 68%.
Recent studies [9, 10, 11] show that speech and language
changes (e.g., phonetic errors, disfluencies, word-finding dif-
ficulties) are reliable markers of severe cognitive impairment,
particularly Alzheimer’s disease. On public lab-recorded cor-
pora such as DementiaBank and the ADReSSo-2021 Cookie
Theft task [12], transformer-based and multimodal models
achieve strong performance in separating Alzheimer’s cases,
typically reflecting late-stage impairment, from controls [13,
14, 15]. These datasets rely on brief, structured tasks that are
resource-intensive and often miss the subtle signs of early de-
cline present in natural conversation. While late-stage detec-
tion is easier, its clinical value is limited by the lack of effec-
tive treatments; the more impactful goal is early identification

reverse progression [16]. Motivated by these limitations, we
look beyond laboratory settings to real-world clinical care, a
fast-growing U.S. sector delivering medical, rehabilitative, and
supportive services [17]. Patient–nurse conversations offer a
rich but underused source for cognitive impairment detection,
with predictive value still unexplored. These interactions pro-
vide two complementary streams: an acoustic–prosodic chan-
nel (e.g., speech rate, pauses) reflecting subtle neurophysiologic
change, and a linguistic channel (e.g., lexical content) often
missing from EHRs. Together, they integrate content and deliv-
ery, offering a multimodal signal that complements structured
EHR data and enhances cognitive decline detection.
This study validates naturalistic conversational speech,
drawn from check-in phone calls and patient–nurse communi-
cations in real-world care settings, as a biomarker for cognitive
impairment detection. Rather than relying on controlled labo-
ratory tasks, we leverage speech captured in ecologically valid,
real-world care settings, where subtle cognitive signals mani-
fest naturally in spontaneous communication. We hypothesize
that naturalistic speech encodes clinically meaningful markers
of cognitive decline that are not captured by structured EHR
data alone, and that integrating these complementary modali-
ties will substantially improve detection performance. To test
this hypothesis, we develop an attention-based multimodal fu-
sion framework to systematically quantify the incremental di-
agnostic value of each speech source (check-in phone calls and
patient–nurse verbal communications) over EHR baselines.
2. Method
As shown in Figure 1, the framework integrates EHR data with
speech data from check-in phone calls and patient–nurse verbal
communications using a fusion model.
2.1. Dataset
The study’s dataset is a multimodal corpus of audio-recorded
patient–clinician interactions linked to EHR data. To the best
of our knowledge, this represents the only dataset for cognitive
impairment detection that utilizes both naturalistic patient-nurse
verbal communication and check-in phone calls. [10].

were aged 29-50, 53.1% aged 51-75, and 39.8% aged 76-99; in
the cognitive impairment group, 11.1% were aged 29-50, 55.6%

The EHR data included two types of information. The first
was structured clinical variables, consisting of approximately
100 features (e.g., “risk of fall,” “activities of daily living,”
“age”). The second was free-text nursing notes, which captured
narrative assessments of patient conditions, care-team commu-
nications, and administrative tasks.
The speech data comprised two sources: The first was
check-in phone calls, conducted by research assistants using
standardized questions such as “What changes have you no-
ticed in your health over the past few days?” and “What symp-
toms have been bothering you lately?”. These calls averaged 9
minutes (SD = 5); all patients completed the first call, and 114
also completed a second. The second source was patient–nurse
verbal communications, which included both clinical content
(e.g., symptom review and functional status) and interactive di-
alogue (e.g., social exchanges, clarifications, storytelling) that
reflected patients’ cognitive and communicative functioning in
real-world care. These interactions were substantially longer,
averaging 29 minutes (SD = 11), with 113 patients contributing
one recording and 68 contributing two.
2.2. Data preprocessing
To prepare the EHR data, constant features were removed, and
categorical variables were one-hot encoded for model compati-
bility. Free-text nursing notes that were empty or lacked mean-
ingful clinical content were also excluded.
For speech data, preprocessing included transcription,
speaker diarization, and imputation. Transcripts were gener-
ated with Amazon Web Services General Transcribe (AWS-
GT) [18]. AWS-GT also provided word-level timestamps and
diarization labels (e.g., Speaker 0 and Speaker 1). We used
GPT-4o to map diarized speakers to patient or clinician roles,
ensuring that cognitive decline detection relied only on patient
speech.
Finally, missing speech modalities were addressed using
a mean imputation strategy. While no missing values were
present in the EHR or first check-in call, some patients lacked
either the second check-in call or patient-nurse verbal commu-
nications. To ensure the integrity of the evaluation, we calcu-
lated the mean value for each missing feature exclusively from
the training set. These training-derived means were then used to
fill missing values across the training, validation, and test sets.
2.3. Feature extraction
To capture acoustic and temporal signs of cognitive decline, we
leveraged SpeechDETECT, a tool developed for speech anal-
ysis [19]. This system extracted features across six domains:
(1) frequency, including fundamental frequency (F0) and for-
mants (F1–F4); (2) spectral features and cepstral coefficients
(e.g., MFCCs, spectral centroid); (3) voice quality metrics (e.g.,
noise-to-harmonic ratio, shimmer); (4) loudness and intensity
(e.g., sound pressure level, short-time energy); (5) signal com-
plexity (e.g., entropy, fractal dimension); and (6) fluency (e.g.,
pause rate, disfluencies, fillers, voice breaks), which are indica-
tive of lexical retrieval difficulties and cognitive disorganiza-
tion.
To capture linguistic patterns of cognitive decline, we used
pre-trained transformer-based language models (e.g., BERT
[20], ClinicalBERT [21]) to generate semantic and syntactic
embeddings from speech transcripts and nursing notes. These
models leverage self-attention mechanisms to capture contex-
tual dependencies, word-level relationships, and sentence struc-
ture, enabling the detection of anomalies such as disfluencies
and semantic errors that signal early impairment. Among the
evaluated models, BERT performed best for speech transcripts
and BioMedBERT [22] for nursing notes.

2.5. Model training and hyperparameter tuning
The dataset was partitioned into training (55%), validation
(20%), and test (25%) sets, stratified by gender, cognitive out-
come, and imputation status (observed vs. imputed) to maintain
balanced splits.
The bottleneck fusion model was implemented with a
streamlined architecture to focus attention on the most infor-
mative multimodal cues. The model utilized 8 attention heads
and a single transformer layer. To act as the information bot-
tleneck, we introduced 8 trainable fusion tokens (m= 8). The
feed-forward network within each attention block employed a
multiplier of 2. Training was conducted using class-weighted
cross-entropy to address dataset imbalance, AdamW optimiza-
tion, and early stopping based on the validation F1-score.
To systematically assess the incremental value of each
modality, we adopted a stepwise training strategy: (1) base-
line model with EHR data, (2) inclusion of check-in phone
calls-derived acoustic and linguistic features, and (3) integra-
tion of patient-nurse verbal communication-derived speech and
linguistic features. This approach isolated the added predictive
value of each data source.
Finally, due to the inherent randomness in training, each
configuration was run with five random seeds. We report the
mean and standard deviation of the F1-score for cognitive im-
pairment, macro F1-score, and AUC-ROC across runs.

3. RESULTS
3.1. Model performance
The largest gains were observed with patient-nurse conversa-
tions. Adding the first patient-nurse verbal communication to
the EHR baseline raised performance to AUC= 0.87±0.03
and F1= 72.45±1.19, with further improvements when
two conversations were included (AUC= 0.90±0.02; F1
= 78.22±2.12). See Table 1 and Figure 2c for more details.
The best configuration combined EHR, the first check-
in phone call, and both patient-nurse verbal communications,
achieving AUC= 0.92±0.03(Figure 2d) and balanced per-
formance across metrics (F1= 83.08±3.59; Macro F1=
88.31±2.68). These findings underscore the complementary
value of structured EHR data, check-in phone calls, and natu-
ralistic patient-nurse interactions for early cognitive decline de-
tection.

Within EHR features (Figure 3b), physiological and func-

When comparing acoustic versus linguistic modalities, con-
tributions were nearly balanced (linguistic: 47.40%, acoustic:
52.60%). Within the acoustic modality (Figure 3c and Figure
3d), spectral and cepstral features accounted for more than 80%
of the attribution mass across both check-in calls and patient-
nurse verbal communications, reflecting sensitivity to articula-
tory imprecision, monotonic prosody, and reduced clarity, char-
acteristics consistently identified as markers of cognitive im-
pairment [24, 25, 26]. V oice quality and loudness or intensity
contributed at secondary levels, reflecting the impact of neu-
romuscular changes on vocal stability and amplitude control
[27, 28]. In contrast, frequency cues, fluency, and signal com-
plexity contributed only marginally.
Taken together, the explainability analysis shows that the
model relies heavily on low-level acoustic biomarkers from
speech and core physiological/functional data from EHR, while
other categories provide complementary but weaker contribu-
tions. This reinforces the value of routine conversational speech
as a sensitive, non-invasive marker of cognitive decline.
3.3. Robustness and ablation analyses
To assess the impact of missing data imputation, we conducted
a sensitivity analysis restricted to the 113 participants with com-
plete data across all modalities. This subset achieved F1 = 81.85
±3.39, comparable to the full-dataset result (F1 = 83.08±
3.59), confirming that mean imputation did not meaningfully
inflate performance.
To isolate the relative contribution of acoustic versus lin-
guistic information within the speech modalities, we trained
single-modality variants of the model. Acoustic features alone
achieved F1 = 79.61±3.48 and linguistic features alone
achieved F1 = 76.21±3.80, both below the combined speech
representation (F1 = 83.08±3.59). This performance gap
confirms that acoustic and linguistic streams carry complemen-
tary information, consistent with the near-balanced attribution
scores reported in Section 3.2 (acoustic: 52.60%, linguistic:
47.40%), and justifies the multimodal fusion design.
Performance was additionally stable across gender (female:
F1 = 81.29±4.53; male: F1 = 85.74±5.30) and older age
groups (50-74: F1 = 79.02±6.13; 75-99: F1 = 84.00±8.94),
suggesting the model does not disproportionately rely on demo-
graphic proxies.
4. Discussion and conclusion
This study demonstrates that natural speech from check-in
phone calls and patient-nurse verbal communications enhances
cognitive impairment detection in real-world clinical care. EHR
data alone yielded modest results, but adding the first check-in
phone call increased discrimination (AUC = 0.76, F1 = 61.95),
and incorporating patient-nurse verbal communications raised
performance further (AUC = 0.90, F1 = 78.22). The strongest
configuration, EHR with the first check-in phone call and both
patient-nurse verbal communications, achieved an AUC of 0.92
and F1 of 83.08, reflecting the complementary value of struc-
tured records and conversational speech. These findings high-
light the potential of natural speech as a non-invasive, scalable
biomarker of cognitive decline in real-world clinical care.
These findings also position speech analysis alongside
emerging biomarker advances. While FDA-approved plasma
assays (e.g., pTau217/β-amyloid) capture pathology, they do
not reflect functional impact. Speech instead provides a func-
tional marker, quantifying real-world communication changes
such as reduced pitch range, prosodic flattening, and semantic
drift. Combining speech with blood tests enables earlier detec-
tion and personalized interventions.
Future work will focus on expanding the dataset to improve
generalizability, with special attention to diverse linguistic pop-
ulations, such as Spanish speakers. Larger longitudinal studies
are needed to test whether the identified speech features predict
progressive decline rather than short-term fluctuations.
