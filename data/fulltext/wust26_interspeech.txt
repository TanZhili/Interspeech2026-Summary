Applying the Simplified Vocal Profile Analysis to Swiss German Dialects 
Alessia Wüst1, Adrian Leemann1 
1 University of Berne, Switzerland 
alessia.wuest@unibe.ch, adrian.leemann@unibe.ch 
 
Abstract 
This work presents the first perceptual investigation of vocal 
profiles in Swiss German, utilizing data from 127 speakers 
(aged 18-40) from the Swiss Dialäktatlas. Using Simplified 
Vocal Profile Analysis, four coders assessed vocal profiles to 
map geographic and social trends. While inter-rater reliability 
proved challenging (κ=0.09–0.39), consensus coding revealed 
clear stratification. Regarding gender, men typically exhibited 
a relaxed vocal tract ('hypoarticulation'), whereas women 
showed a tendency toward pharyngeal expansion ('hy -
perarticulation'). Geographically, Northeast Switzerland is 
characterized by nasality, while Central Switzerland is marked 
by pharyngeal expansion – a finding likely confounded by the 
gender distribution in the sample. Notably, creaky voice 
remains rare in Swiss German. These results suggest that while 
vocal profiles function as salient markers of regional and social 
identity, methodological complexities call for refined protocols 
in future research.  
Index Terms: Vocal Profile Analysis, Voice quality, Socio-
phonetics, Swiss German. 
1. Introduction 
While Swiss German segmental phonetics are extensively 
documented (e.g., [1, 2]), the suprasegmental domain of vocal 
profiles remains a significant research gap. Laver [3] and 
Abercrombie [4] define vocal profiles as the cumulative au-
ditory coloring of the habitual vocal tract configuration. Despite 
flourishing research in Anglophone (e.g., [5, 6]) and Romance 
linguistics (e.g., [7, 8]), studies on German varieties remain 
sparse – and for Swiss German, virtually nonexistent beyond 
peripheral mentions in older dialect grammars. These historical 
accounts remained impressionistic, lacking a systematic coding 
framework to ensure reliability. They offer isolated 
observations rather than rigorous data on how voice quality is 
distributed across the population. This absence of evidence is 
critical, as in a dialect landscape as diverse as Switzerland’s, a 
specific vocal setting likely acts as a salient 'badge' of identity 
– a function that has never been empirically tested. To do so, 
we present the first perceptual analysis of Swiss German vocal 
profiles using the Simplified Vocal Profile Analysis (SVPA) 
protocol [9]. Drawing on spontaneous speech from 127 
speakers in the Dialäktatlas corpus [1], this paper investigates 
the inter-rater reliability (IRR) of perceptual coding, explores 
gender-specific articulatory patterns, and creates the first 
geographic maps of vocal profile settings for German-speaking 
Switzerland. 
1.1.  Sociophonetic perspectives on voice quality 
Pioneering work by Laver [3] established the Vocal Profile 
Analysis (VPA), distinguishing between anatomical constraints 
('organic features') and culturally learned, habitual 'phonetic 
setting features' or long-term muscular biases. Research shows 
these settings are socially structured. Stuart-Smith’s [5] work 
on Glasgow English revealed strong correlations with class and 
gender, identifying working-class markers like open jaws, 
while associating men with creaky voice and women with whis-
pery phonation. Beck and Schaeffler [10] found that among 
Scottish adolescents, variation was driven primarily by gender 
and biological maturation, with males actively lowering vocal 
tract resonances to project masculinity. Recently, White et al. 
[6] noted a rapid rise in creaky voice among young Australian 
women, contrasting with historical data where creak served as 
a 'marker of masculinity'. Cross-linguistic research reinforces 
these gendered norms. Pépiot and Arnold [7] demonstrated that 
female bilinguals exhibited higher breathiness than males 
across French and English, a pattern Mendoza et al. [11] simi-
larly confirmed in Spanish. 
1.2. Voice quality in German varieties 
Research in the German-speaking context remains limited. 
Peters [12] investigated regional variation in the Bersenbrücker 
Land, finding that northern speakers exhibited 'harder' voices 
compared to 'softer, breathier' southern voices. He also noted 
that women generally displayed higher breathiness than men, 
ultimately concluding these qualities were markers of the 
speech community rather than the specific variety. While 
modern systematic studies of Swiss German are lacking, 
historical dialect grammars provide some impressionistic 
descriptions of vocal profiles, suggesting two primary zones: 
Northeast and Central Switzerland. I n the Northeast, 
descriptions frequently center on nasality: Vetsch [13] 
described strong nasalization in Appenzell, and Wiget [14] 
observed a 'relaxed velum' in Toggenburg. However, this 
feature was not uniform; Berger [15] noted tense articulation in 
the St. Gallen Rheintal, while Streiff [16] and Weber [17] 
explicitly noted an absence of nasality in Glarus and Zürich, 
respectively. Central Swiss descriptions, by contrast, focus on 
specific lip and jaw settings. Schmid [18] highlighted strong 
labial activity and a small mouth opening in Entlebuch, whereas 
Clauß [19] noted weak lip activity and a small jaw angle in Uri. 
1.3.  Objectives 
To move beyond impressionistic observations, this study sub-
jects Swiss German vocal profiles to empirical scrutiny using 
the SVPA protocol developed by San Segundo and Mompean 
[9]. Applying this method to the Dialäktatlas corpus [1] rep-
resents a novel methodological endeavor. This investigation is 
guided by three primary research questions: 
1. Reliability: To what extent can the SVPA yield high IRR 
when applied to Swiss German data, and which specific 
settings (e.g., lip rounding) elicit the highest agreement 
among coders? 
2. Gender: What specific vocal profile patterns – such as 
vocal tract tension or phonation type – can be identified 
as distinct gendered markers within the sample?

3. Geography: Are there empirically verifiable regional

Based on previous literature and historical observations, we 
hypothesize that IRR will generally remain low (Fleiss’ Kappa 
<0.5). Regarding social variables, we anticipate that female 
speakers will exhibit greater breathiness, while males will tend 
toward creaky voice. Geographically, we expect to confirm the 
historical reports of a 'nasality belt' in Eastern Switzerland. 
2. Methods 
2.1.  Speakers 
Participants were selected from the Swiss Dialäktatlas corpus, 
a large-scale project documenting contemporary Swiss German 
of 1016 speakers across 127 survey sites. The full corpus 
features a stratified design with eight speakers per location: four 
older (60-80 years) and four younger (18-40 years), with two 
males and two females per age group. For the current perceptual 
investigation, a subsample of 127 speakers was established, 
representing one individual per analyzed location. To minimize 
age-related physiological changes – such as the natural 
reduction of vocal tract tension [20] – the sample was restricted 
to the younger cohort, excluding the older generation to avoid 
confounding variables. The resulting sample is gender -
balanced, comprising 64 male and 63 female subjects. Due to 
the selection of a single speaker per survey point, the 
geographic distribution of gender is randomized, with each 
location represented by either a male or a female voice. 
2.2.  Materials 
To ensure high-fidelity audio despite pandemic restrictions, the 
Dialäktatlas employed a hybrid remote strategy: While 
researchers supervised via Zoom, participants recorded speech 
locally using a smartphone application to bypass standard audio 
compression (recording quality of the Dialäktatlas corpus: 
44.1kHz, 16 bit, cf. [21]). The current analysis exclusively used 
spontaneous speech derived from a ten-minute conversation 
conducted at the end of each of the interviews. 
2.3.  Data coding 
For the perceptual analysis, we used the SVPA developed by 
San Segundo and Mompean [9]. Designed as an adaptation of 
Laver’s [3] comprehensive scheme, the SVPA mitigates cog-
nitive load and enhances replicability by reducing fine-grained 
scalar judgments to simplified categorical distinctions. Rather 
than rating degrees of deviation, raters coded for the presence 
of specific non-neutral settings (e.g., tense or lax) against a 
neutral baseline across ten vocal profile parameters. We 
adapted the protocol specifically for Swiss German phonology; 
for instance, the neutral baseline was defined to exclude vocalic 
nasalization. The coding team consisted of four raters with 
phonetic experience ranging from six months to over twenty 
years. Prior to data collection, the team underwent a rigorous 
calibration phase, initiated by a training session with San 
Segundo and followed by meetings in September 2025 to 
establish a reference sheet with auditory and visual anchors. 
Following the calibration phase, 127 spontaneous speech sam-
ples of about one minute each were assessed between October 
and November 2025. These individual judgments served as the 
basis for the calculations of IRR (IRR, see 2.4). Final decisions 
for the 127 profiles were determined during five synchronous 
consensus review meetings where discrepancies were resolved 
through group re-evaluation. 
2.4.  Statistics 
To evaluate the consistency of perceptual coding across the four 
independent raters, IRR was assessed using Fleiss’ Kappa (κ). 
An unweighted Kappa coefficient was calculated individually 
for each of the ten vocal profile settings using the 
kappam.fleiss() function from the irr package in R [22]. 
Interpretation followed benchmarks established by Landis and 
Koch [23] and Kassambara [24]. According to these guidelines, 
κ<0 indicates agreement below chance, while positive values 
are categorized as: 0.00–0.20 slight agreement, 0.21–0.40 fair 
agreement, and 0.41–0.60 moderate agreement. Statistical 
significance was assessed with a thres hold of p<0.05 to 
determine if the observed agreement exceeded random chance. 
3. Results 
3.1.  Reliability 
Fleiss’ Kappa analysis revealed generally slight to fair relia-
bility across the ten settings (κ=0.09–0.386), though consensus 
significantly exceeded chance in all settings (p<0.05). A 
distinct hierarchy emerged: four settings achieved fair agree-
ment, led by velopharyngeal (κ=0.386) and voice type 
(κ=0.302). Conversely, five settings demonstrated only slight 
agreement (κ<0.20), including laryngeal tension, laryngeal 
height, labial, mandibular, and pharyngeal, see Table 1.

3.2.  Gender 
The analysis of the 127 speakers (64 male, 63 female) revealed 
two distinct supralaryngeal patterns. While most speakers 
(n=77) showed neutral vocal tract tension, a clear gender 
pattern emerged in this setting: men exhibited a relaxed vocal 
tract (n=24) significantly more often than women (n=10) (χ2 (2, 
N=127) = 7.67, p = .022), see Figure 1.

Regarding voice type, modal voice dominated (n=112); non-

only 12 speakers (~9%; 7 men, 5 women). Other types were 
marginal (2 men harsh, 1 man breathy) or absent (whispered). 
3.3.  Geography 
The perceptual analysis of the 127 speakers revealed clear 
geographic clusterings for specific voice quality settings, af-
firmatively answering the research question regarding the ex-
istence of regional voice quality markers. The mapping of the 
data was conducted using QGIS-LTR [25] with scalar inter-
polation (k nearest neighbor smoothing, k=5; cf. [26]). We 
found two primary regional phenomena: widespread nasality in 
the Northeast and pharyngeal expansion in Central Switzerland. 
3.3.1. Velopharyngeal Setting (Nasality) 
The most distinct regional pattern observed is the prevalence of 
nasality (velopharyngeal setting) in Eastern and Northeastern 
Switzerland. The data show a strong concentration of nasal 
profiles in the cantons of Appenzell, St. Gallen, Schaffhausen 
and Thurgau. While isolated instances of nasality were recorded 
in Central Switzerland and the Valais, the Northeast forms a 
coherent 'nasality belt', see Figure 2.

In section 3.2 we reported that pharyngeal expansion varies

Keeping in mind that the gender distribution in our sample is 
geographically uneven (see 2.1), the question then emerges, 
whether the hotspots in Figure 3 may be an artifact of the gender

that females are slightly overrepresented in our sample in 
Central Swiss localities. Consequently, this gender bias 
coincides with – and may drive – the high degree of pharyngeal 
expansion observed in this Central Swiss region.  
4. Discussion 
4.1.  Inter-Rater Reliability 
IRR proved generally challenging, with Fleiss’ Kappa values 
ranging from κ=0.09 to 0.39. While falling within the fair to 
marginal range [23, 24] and trailing San Segundo and 
Mompean’s [9] pilot results, these figures parallel Webb et al. 
[27], who reported similar ranges (0.01–0.32) using the original 
VPA. These results suggest that simplifying the protocol to 
binary decisions does not entirely eliminate  the inherent 
subjectivity of auditory analysis. This perceptual instability 
underscores the inherent difficulty of applying discrete 
categories to a continuous acoustic signal. The fact that even 
experienced coders struggled with vocal tract settings suggests 
that these features may function as 'gestalts' – holistic 
impressions – rather than isolated articulatory maneuvers. 
However, agreement levels were not random; rather, they 
appear to be structured by a combination of acoustic clarity and 
social salience. The highest reliability was observed for the 
velopharyngeal setting (κ=0.386, i.e., 'nasality'), likely because 
this feature is a highly recognizable marker of Eastern Swiss 
dialects. This supports the argument by Llamas et al. [28, p. 4] 
that certain linguistic forms are "key carriers of social 
meaning." In this case, because nasality is so socially prominent 
and 'marked' in the Swiss context – unlike in French, where it

is a standard part of the grammar [29] – it serves as a clear 
mental category for raters, leading to more consistent coding. 
Conversely, settings reliant on visual cues yielded the lowest 
agreement (mandibular κ=0.09; labial κ=0.12). Similarly, poor 
reliability in pharyngeal expansion (κ=0.09) highlights the 
ambiguity of impressionistic definitions like Beck’s [30] 
'strangulated' quality, which is compounded by the acoustic 
difficulty of isolating pharyngeal constriction from laryngeal 
height [31]. 
4.2.  Gender Distibution 
The study found clear differences in men’s and women’s vocal 
profiles. Men were more likely to speak with a relaxed vocal 
tract ('hypoarticulation'; n=24 vs. n=10 women), while women 
significantly more often used pharyngeal expansion 
('hyperarticulation'; n=16 vs. n=3 men). However, this finding 
must be interpreted with caution: the IRR for the pharyngeal 
setting was notably low (κ=0.094), indicating substantial 
disagreement among coders regarding the presence of this 
feature. Despite this methodological limitation, the pattern 
– where identified – supports Fishman’s [32] concept of 
'interactional work,' suggesting that women invest more 
articulatory effort to ensure communicative success. Bluntly 
put, while men can 'afford' to be relaxed without losing a u-
thority, women may prioritize clarity and exactness [33] to 
guarantee they are understood. Furthermore, the prevalence of 
relaxed settings in males may reflect a 'covert prestige' 
associated with linguistic nonchalance in Swiss German mas-
culine identity. This 'relaxed' posture may serve as a local 
phonetic marker of authenticity and effortless belonging within 
the male speech community. 
Regarding voice type, results diverge significantly from re-
cent sociophonetic trends. While Anglophone research (e.g., [6, 
34]) highlights prevalent creaky voice, particularly among 
young women, this study found non-modal phonation was 
marginal. Contrary to hypotheses derived from these studies 
and Peters’ [12] findings on German creaky voice appeared in 
only 12 speakers (<10%) with no gender imbalance (7 men, 5 
women), while breathy (n=1) and whispered voices were 
virtually absent. This implies a strong Swiss German preference 
for modal voice. Resistance may stem from negative 
evaluations; Anderson et al. [35] link vocal fry to sounding less 
competent, and Gobl and Chasaide [36] associate it with 
boredom, contradicting the Swiss imperative of 'friendliness' 
described by Rash [37]. Although lexical anglicisms are 
common [38], this phonetic feature has not permeated. 
4.3.  Geographic Distribution 
Geographic analysis confirms that vocal profiles are spatially 
structured, largely validating the third hypothesis regarding 
Northeastern nasality. This finding likely results from the dorsal 
/r/ prevalent in the Northeast. As Graf [39] notes, uvular 
articulation mechanically lowers the velum to facilitate vi -
bration or constriction against the tongue root; this creates a 
velopharyngeal opening that adds a 'nasal coat' to surrounding 
vowels. Consequently, the zone of nasality identified in this 
study overlaps almost perfectly with the distribution of the 
uvular /r/ documented in the Dialäktatlas [1]. This suggests that 
the nasal quality of Northeastern dialects may not merely be a 
habitual phonation choice, but an articulatory consequence of 
the regional consonant inventory. 
Linguistically, the pharyngeal expansion observed in Central 
Switzerland may correspond to the morphological preservation 
of full vowels in unstressed syllables – a trait distinguishing 
these dialects from High Alemannic schwa-reducing varieties 
[1]. The articulation of such open vowels (e.g., [tanæ] vs. [tanə] 
for 'fir tree'; [v̥iæɾ] vs. [v̥iəɾ] 'four') necessitates a wider 
pharyngeal cavity, reinforcing the perceptual impression of 
'hyperarticulation' or 'speaking to an audience.' However, this 
interpretation demands significant caution due to intertwined 
demographic and methodological constraints. First, the 
geographic pattern is likely skewed by demographics. Because 
pharyngeal expansion is already more common in women (see 
Section 3.2), the fact that our Central Swiss sample has slightly 
more female speakers naturally leads to higher rates of 
expansion in this region. Second, and perhaps more critically, 
the IRR for the pharyngeal setting was notably low (κ=0.094). 
This substantial disagreement indicates that while the 
physiological link between open vowels and pharyngeal space 
is theoretically plausible, its perceptual verification in this study 
remains tentative. 
5. Conclusion 
This study analyzed spontaneous speech from 127 speakers to 
address the lack of research on Swiss German vocal profiles. 
Results indicated slight to fair IRR (κ<0.40), though salient 
features like nasality achieved fair agreement. Socially, a gen-
der dichotomy emerged: women exhibited pharyngeal expan-
sion ('hyperarticulation'), while men tended toward a relaxed 
vocal tract ('hypoarticulation'). Non-modal phonation like 
creaky voice was marginal. The dominance of modal voice 
contrasts with the frequent borrowing of English vocabulary. 
This implies that while the lexicon is open to change, the vocal 
profile remains conservative, rejecting foreign ph onetic 
features that may not align with local communicative norms. 
Geographically, distinct regional qualities were confirmed, 
notably a Northeastern 'nasality belt' linked to the dorsal /r/. 
However, the hyperarticulation observed in Central Switzerland 
requires cautious interpretation, as it is likely confounded by the 
regional overrepresentation of female speakers and 
compromised by the low reliability of the pharyngeal setting. 
Beyond these specific interpretative challenges, the validity 
of the maps shown is defined by the structural constraints of the 
dataset. First, the sampling protocol – restricted to a single 
speaker per survey site – constrains local representativeness, 
necessitating the use of scalar interpolation to visualize broader 
regional trends. Second, the reliance on audio -only data 
impeded the assessment of visual settings, resulting in lower 
reliability for mandibular and labial configurations. Third, the 
perceptual baseline was inevitably filtered through the coders’ 
own linguistic backgrounds (High Alemannic). Finally, the 
interview context itself may have induced speech 
accommodation [40], potentially influencing speakers to adapt 
features [41] to the interviewer. 
Future research should validate these findings by expanding 
the analysis to include the full cohort of four younger speakers 
per location in the Dialäktatlas corpus. To mitigate the 
subjectivity of perceptual coding, future studies should trian-
gulate these results with acoustic measurements, such as for-
mant analysis for vocal tract settings [42] or automated algo-
rithms for detecting creak [43]. Finally, perception experiments 
could investigate whether specific profiles contribute to the 
known social stigmatization of these dialects.
