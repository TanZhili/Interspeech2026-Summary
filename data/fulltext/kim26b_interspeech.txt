The Role of Laryngeal Position in the Articulation of American English Velar 
Stop Consonants 
Daejin Kim 
Department of Linguistics, University of New Mexico 
daejin.kim.hyu@gmail.com 
 
Abstract 
This study re-examines the articulation of voiceless /k/ and 
voiced /ɡ/ velar stop consonants in American English (AE) by 
modeling movements of the hyoid bone (HY) and the tongue 
dorsum (TD) as articulatory gestures using dynamic analysis of 
ultrasound tongue recordings and multivariate statistical 
inference methods. HY was found to raise and advance for /k/ 
and lower and retract for /ɡ/ when TD reaches its target, both of 
which correlate with the high f0 peak for /k/ and the low f0 peak 
for /ɡ/ during their following and preceding vowels. /ɡ/ was 
produced with longer, larger, and faster TD raising toward the 
velar surface, indicating that /ɡ/ is conditioned by the full 
spatiotemporal expansion of the tongue with the maximal 
linguovelar contact. This study suggests that varying degrees of 
expansion of the tongue, linguovelar contact, and laryngeal 
positioning simultaneously condition the articulation of AE 
velar stops, all of which relate to their acoustic correlates.  
Index Terms: speech production, consonantal contrast, 
ultrasound tongue imaging, American English, multivariate 
statistical modeling, virtual target hypothesis  
1. Introduction 
This study examines the linguolaryngeal articulation of voiced-
voiceless distinction in American English (AE) velar stops (/k/ 
vs. /ɡ/) by examining the kinematics of the tongue dorsum 
(TD), where the tongue surface faces the velopharyngeal port 
and contacts the velum when it raises for velar stops, and the 
position of the hyoid bone (HY) relative to the mandible (MD), 
based on the frame-by-frame dynamic analysis of ultrasound 
recordings [1]. TD has been argued to be the anatomical origin 
and developmental source of linguolaryngeal articulation for 
vowels [2] and consonants across languages.   
1.1. Articulation of voiced-voiceless AE velar stops 
Consonantal contrast in AE has been of interest due to the 
unclear phonetic status involved in its production [3]. AE stop 
consonants (stops) in the syllable onset (C1) are less likely to 
be produced with voicing prior to the release (i.e., vocal fold 
vibration) [4], as is the case in many other languages with 
phonological voicing contrast, and it is, instead, further 
contrasted by closure-to-release duration (CD) and voice onset 
time (VOT) and acoustic properties of adjacent segments, such 
as fundamental frequency (f0) and duration (VD) of the 
following vowel: /ɡ/ is produced with longer CD, shorter VOT, 
lower f0, and longer VD than /k/. In articulation, [5] reported 
that the tongue body raises longer with a higher peak velocity 
for non-word intervocalic /ɡ/. However, the understanding of 
precise and underlying mechanisms of AE velar stops in 
relation to its contrast using acoustic correlates is fairly limited 
as few studies have provided comprehensive and simultaneous 
measurements of lingual and laryngeal articulations.  
 To develop a better understanding of their underlying 
articulatory mechanisms and acoustic-articulatory relations, 
this study explores the kinematics of TD measured in relation 
to HY, considering it as a major laryngeal articulator that 
controls fundamental frequency (f0) [6] and reflecting the 
biomechanical structure of the tongue [7], to examine the true 
lingual mechanism of linguovelar contact and laryngeal 
coordination that should implicate consonantal contrast.   
 TD is one major articulatory component of velar 
stops and their qualitative contrasts occurring during adjacent 
segments for the following reasons. First, TD is one of the 
linguolaryngeal anatomical links affecting tongue-pulling and 
increased f0 during the vowel. The tongue pulling associated 
with TD raising may increase the stiffness and tension of the 
vocal folds and thus relates to the f0 difference that high vowels 
have a higher f0 than low vowels [8]. If the vowel-intrinsic f0 
contrast exists due to the tongue pulling, the degree of tongue 
pulling simultaneously shifting the laryngeal structure, such as 
HY, forward and upward should also exist for the velar stops 
along with the increased rate of vocal fold vibration, as velar 
stops are primarily produced with TD raising. If this is the case, 
/k/ is expected to be produced with longer and larger TD raising 
along with the more raised HY position than /ɡ/.  
 Second, another possible involvement of TD and HY 
in these stops is grounded in the aerodynamic voicing constraint 
(AVC) [9], where vocal fold vibration may not be fully 
maintained, and airflow is reduced as air pressure increases 
during the closure of voiced stops . This is because of 
aerodynamic constraints that likely happen in vocal fold 
vibration during the closure for voiced stops, even though AE 
voiced stops do not produce pre-release voicing (i.e., vocal fold 
vibration). AE /ɡ/ is phonetically voiceless yet categorized as a 
phonologically voiced stop. To avoid AVC, speakers were 
found to expand the supraglottal space by lowering TD for 
voiced stops. This, however, occurs regardless of different 
phonetic status of phonologically voiced stops. [10] found that 
TD lowering occurs both in voiced stops in American English, 
which is phonetically voiceless, as well as in voiced stops in 
Brazilian Portuguese that fully produces voicing during the 
closure. This finding is in line with [11], [12], which reported 
that speakers lowered the tongue as well as the larynx for voiced 
stops in varieties of French. It is, therefore, likely that HY 
positioning is highly involved in voiced-voiceless distinction 
achieved by the lingual articulation though the precise 
mechanism has been unknown. If /ɡ/ in AE is constrained 
aerodynamically as found in previous studies, speakers should 
produce /ɡ/ with the decreased TD raising and the increased HY 
lowering to bypass AVC, resulting in an opposite outcome of 
TD and HY movement for /k/, supported by the tongue-pull 
hypothesis that conditions f0 contrast. 
1.2. Virtual target hypothesis in AE velar stops  
Speakers, in addition, were found to directly control the degree 
of linguovelar contact using the tongue and sometimes push the 
tongue beyond the limit of the contact surface  to indicate 
consonantal contrast. This observation has led to the virtual 
target hypothesis, evidenced from articulatory studies of 
various languages [13], [14], [15]. A basic premise of this 
hypothesis is that the target is set up virtually beyond the 
physiological limits of the articulators. This articulatory 
behavior can lead to substantial variation in linguovelar contact, 
which should result in varying movement distance and duration, 
velocity during the closing phase, and any other kinematic 
characteristics between major gestural landmarks during the

 However, since the lingual articulation is highly 
variable across speakers, and previous findings were based on 
a limited number of speakers, this study takes a different 
approach by estimating the lingual articulation and the HY 
position separately but also by modeling the TD movement 
from HY, as HY is one of many origins of extrinsic tongue

the movement from one muscular origin of the tongue part 
should provide its true kinematic characteristics of the tongue 
because the tongue is able to press the velar surface as well as

muscular forces connected to the laryngeal structure (i.e., HY); 
this measure should reveal the exact estimates of tongue raising 
and indirect estimate of linguovelar contact and test the 
virtuality of the linguovelar target potentially induced by 
consonantal contrast. 
1.3. Linguolaryngeal activities as articulatory gestures 
  More importantly, this study aims to understand the 
lingual and laryngeal components as basic units of phonological 
representation in speech production following the framework of 
Articulatory Phonology [15]. From this perspective, the lingual 
gesture for AE velar stops  should have distinctive 
spatiotemporal characteristics, which manifest as phonological 
contrast, concomitant with their phonetic (i.e., acoustic and 
articulatory) components, such as the duration between closure 
and release (i.e., CD) and between release and the beginning of 
vocal fold vibration (i.e., VOT). Again, since AE speakers have 
been known not to fully exhibit the pre-release voicing [4], the 
articulatory characteristics of TD and HY at major articulatory 
landmarks, such as the beginning, the target (spatially maximal 
and minimal), and the end of the movement, should be a key 
articulatory attribute of consonantal contrast, all of which reveal 
the key articulatory attributes /k/ and /ɡ/ in articulation.  
Relating possible correlations between the acoustic and 
articulatory components of these stops, this study formulates 
two competing hypotheses regarding the TD articulation and 
HY positioning for two velar stops at the syllable onset in AE:   
• Hypothesis 1: /k/ is produced with longer and larger 
TD raising and more raised and fronted HY position 
due to their higher f0 during the following vowel than 
/ɡ/, which accompanies with increased tongue 
pulling. 
• Hypothesis 2: /ɡ/ is produced with the longer, larger, 
and faster TD raising with lowered HY , 
corresponding to its lower f0 during the following 
vowel and bypassing AVC.  
2. Methods 
This study used the Telemed EchoBlaster 128 ultrasound 
system to record audio-ultrasound synchronized videos. Ten 
native speakers of AE (three females and seven males; mean 
age at the time of recording = 20.3) participated in the 
recording. All speakers were born and raised in the states of 
Colorado, New Mexico, and Texas in the US and reported no 
history of speech- and language-related difficulties or disorders. 
The recording was conducted in the sound-attenuated booth in 
the Linguistics Graduate Lab at the University of New Mexico 
campus. During the recording, speakers wore the head stabilizer 
unit, made of aluminum, to synchronize the probe position and 
the head, which also rotates the lingual and laryngeal parts. 
Recorded videos include the tongue surface (a red line), a 
darker edge below white and bright reflections, which spans 
from the root (TR) to the front (TF) (from left to right), a 
shadow at the left corner below the lowest part of the tongue, 
which indicates HY, another shadow at the right corner of the 
image, which indicates MD, and an additional white surface 
above the tongue, indicating the velum, when the tongue 
contacts the velar surface ((a) in Figure 1). If a tongue contour 
or two shadows indicating HY and MD are completely invisible 
and unable to estimate due to low imaging quality, tracking 
errors, or some other reasons, it is excluded from the analysis.

2.1. Target words and sentences 
Speakers produced target words (keep /kip/, geek /ɡik/, coop 
/kup/, goop /ɡup/, cod /kɑd/, god /ɡɑd/) in the target sentences 
designed to elicit these words as a general answer ("I wrote a 
cod on the paper." after listening to "What did you do today (or 
yesterday)?"). The target segment follows /ə/, so the closing and 
opening movement of the tongue for two velar stops should be 
seen clearly with TD raising with little variability. Speakers 
read six randomly ordered target sentences in one block and 
repeated a block twelve times. This means that 72 recorded 
tokens were collected from one speaker; in total, 720 tokens 
were collected.  
2.2. Tongue contour tracking and estimation 
Tongue contours and the TD and HY positions ((b) in Figure 1) 
were tracked consecutively over time as constantly moving 
parts using DeepLabCut [16] implemented in the Articulate 
Assistant Advanced (AAA) software [17], [18], which tracks 
the tongue surface indicated by the darker edges below the 
whiter and brighter reflections and the position of HY signaled 
by the shadow below the tongue root. Tracked tongue 
coordinates, shown as a spline (T0-T10), and positions of HY 
and MD, shown as a vector, were then quantified with Cartesian 
[x, y] coordinates, calculated from a fiducial line drawn below 
two shadows ((b) in Figure 1). Tongue coordinates were then 
normalized by rotating according to the TD-HY vectors and 
equally scaling it to the length of TD-HY vector at each frame. 
This study identifies the TD movement as the Euclid ean 
distance between HY and the fifth coordinates (T4) of tongue 
contours ((c) in Figure 1). Tokens for which these coordinates 
were completely invisible in recordings and could not be 
estimated were excluded from the analysis (174 productions).  
2.3. Acoustic and articulatory annotations 
After ultrasound recording and post -recording processing, 
major acoustic properties of velar stops were annotated using 
Praat [19] Acoustic correlates include acoustic stop closure and 
release and the beginning and end of the vocal fold vibration, 
all of which turn into the closure-to-release duration (CD), 
voice onset time (VOT) and vowel duration (VD). Also, the f0 
peak was annotated during VD (at the top of Figure 2).

2.4. Statistical analyses 
2.4.1. Linear mixed-effects models 
The effect of independent variables on the univariate response 
variables was estimated by fitting the linear mixed-effects 
models (LMEMs), using the lmerTest [20] packages in R. 
Response variables are acoustic and articulatory properties of 
vowels and consonants including movement distance and 
duration of TD and TR between gestural landmarks. Predictor 
variables are consonant (C1 = /k, ɡ/) and vowel (V = /i, u, ɑ/) 
types, and all levels of (categorical) variables were dummy-
coded. Reference levels are underlined. Only statistically 
significant variables (p < .05 between levels of a variable and 
between models with and without the testing variable) are 
included in the linear mixed-effects models presented in the 
study.  
2.4.2. Vector generalized linear models 
This study employed the vector generalized linear models 
(VGLMs) to evaluate the statistical significance of the effect of 
velar C1 types on the position of HY in relation to or away from 
MD shown as a vector between gestural landmarks, using the 
vgam package [21] in R. VGLMs allow the estimation of 
multivariate responses, including two-dimensional vectors, 
such as horizontal and vertical displacements [dx, dy], as a 
function of various categorical and continuous predictor 
variables. The current VGLM method is designed only for 
estimating fixed effects and supports for neither random 
intercepts and slopes nor repeated measures; therefore, it is 
prone to the underestimation of speaker-related variations. To 
overcome this limitation, two additional LMEMs were 
conducted for estimating the effect of velar stops available 
between gestural events in horizontal and vertical displacement 
with same independent variables. After fitting two separate 
LMEMs, post-hoc pairwise comparison analys es were 
conducted using the emmeans package [22] to estimate 
between-level differences.  
2.4.3. Generalized additive mixed models 
The tongue contour shape differences of velar C1s at ONSET, 
TARGET, and OFFSET positions were estimated by fitting 
Generalized Additive Mixed Models (GAMMs) using the mgcv 
package [23], and smooths of levels of variables and between-
level differences were plotted using the tidygam package [24] 
in R. All predictor variables (C1, V) are coded as tensor 
interaction (ti) terms as a result of changes in y -axis as a 
function of tongue measurement points (TP), and their levels 
are dummy-coded, which enables the model to compare means 
between levels of a variable against the baseline conditions 
(e.g., C1 = /k/; V = /i/). Also, speaker-related variation 
(SpeakerID) was added as a factor smooth (bs = "fs") 
interaction over trajectories to minimize any random effects 
from speakers, such as size, length, and orientation of tongues.  
3. Results 
3.1. Acoustic properties of AE velar stops 
The LMEMs estimated that /ɡ/ was produced with a longer CD 
(β (a difference estimate between levels: /k/ and /ɡ/) = -11.8, SE 
(standard error) = 2.04, p < .001), a shorter VOT (β = 62.8, SE 
= 2.81, p < .001), and a longer duration of the following vowel 
(β = -26.0, SE = 1.75, p < .001) than /k/. Vowels following /ɡ/ 
were articulated with a lower f0 peak than those with /k/ (β 
= .69, SE = .07, p < .001). Model results regarding the acoustic 
correlates of voicing are similar to those from previous studies. 
3.2. Differences in tongue contour shapes 
The GAMMs (Figure 3) estimated that no statistically 
significant differences (a')-(c') between /k/ and /ɡ/ in tongue 
contour shapes (a)-(c) were found at ONSET (edf. (estimated

(edf. = 1.01, F = 1.91, p = .93) (c') positions. /ɡ/ was produced 
with a slightly lower and more retracted tongue at TARGET than 
/k/ (edf. = 2.92, F = 3.42, p < .001) (b'). Even though the effect 
was estimated statistically significant, the actual difference in 
tongue contour shapes between /k/ and /ɡ/ in C1 is interpreted

4. Discussion 
This study argues that TD displacement and HY positioning are 
the primary articulatory components of velar stops in AE. /ɡ/ 
was produced with increased distance and duration of TD away 
from HY with a higher PV (i.e., faster and more stiff movement) 
from ONSET to TARGET than /k/, similar to a previous finding 
[5]. /ɡ/ is thus produced by the full spatiotemporal expansion of 
TD. There are, however, various aspects newly reported in this 
study. 
 At first glance, the lingual articulation does not 
directly relate to the consonantal contrast using f0 if this claim 
is relied on the tongue contour estimation , which rejects 
Hypothesis 1, a possible tongue -pulling mechanism for 
consonantal contrast. Instead, the finding that /k/ is produced 
with more raised and fronted HY at TARGET and OFFSET than /ɡ/ 
is concomitant with the higher f0 during vowels following /k/. 
In other words, HY positioning, which indirectly indicates the 
shifted position of the larynx, might have conditioned or been
