Privacy vs. Performance: Assessing Communication Utility of Anonymized
Voice Features

Abstract
V oice anonymization is essential for protecting user privacy, yet
it may distort acoustic cues that are critical for communication
assessment. In this work, we study the impact of phase vocoder
time-scale modification (PV-TSM), a lightweight anonymiza-
tion technique selected for operational efficiency, on acous-
tic and prosodic feature preservation. We integrate PV-TSM
into an end-to-end speech analysis pipeline and evaluate its ef-
fects on acoustic features as well as automatic speech recog-
nition (ASR) performance. Our results show that anonymiza-
tion can largely preserve these features while effectively mask-
ing speaker identity. Further, fine-tuning the ASR model with
anonymized speech mitigates recognition errors and restores
downstream feature accuracy. These findings demonstrate that
privacy protection and prosodic fidelity can coexist, enabling re-
liable communication assessment in privacy-sensitive contexts.
Index Terms: voice anonymization, automatic speech recogni-
tion, acoustic fidelity, communication assessment
1. Introduction
Spoken response plays a key role in assessing effective commu-
nication, revealing insights on how individuals express ideas,
manage pacing, and convey tone or confidence [1] [2] [3]. How-
ever, speech also carries personally identifiable voice traits,
such as pitch range and vocal tract resonance patterns, which
can expose a speaker’s identity [4] and raise privacy concerns.
Regulations like the EU’s General Data Protection Regulation
assert that safeguarding personal data is not just good practice, it
is a legal obligation [5]. Failing to protect speech data can result
in privacy breaches, biased results, and potential data misuse.
V oice anonymization aims to obscure identifiable traits
while retaining the information needed for analysis. Yet, the
acoustic transformations involved can risk distorting cues that
are central to assessing communication quality. These acoustic
and prosodic cues underpin measures such as fluency and af-
fect; altering them may impact scoring accuracy. Most previous
work has evaluated anonymization in terms of ASR accuracy
(WER) or speaker re-identification resistance (EER) [6], with
less attention to how these manipulations reshape the features.
This study investigates how voice anonymization impacts
feature fidelity using phase vocoder time-scale modification
(PV-TSM), a lightweight method that alters pitch and timing to
mask speaker identity [7]. Unlike prior work that focuses pri-
marily on WER or EER, we directly quantify how anonymiza-
tion affects specific acoustic and prosodic features. We further
examine how ASR fine-tuning with anonymized data can miti-
gate these effects. Given the growing importance of balancing
analytical precision with ethical responsibility, this work aims
to quantify that trade-off and explore strategies to minimize it.
2. Related work
2.1. Voice anonymization
With the increasing use of voice-based technologies, sharing
speech data raises significant privacy concerns, as adversaries
may exploit recordings for speaker identity or biometric de-
tails [8]. V oice anonymization addresses this risk by modify-
ing speech signals to obscure speaker identity while retaining
the linguistic content [9]. This can be achieved with signal
processing–based methods such as McAdams coefficient mod-
ification [10] or PV-TSM [7], which transform acoustic prop-
erties to mask speaker-specific traits. Other deep learning ap-
proaches include phonetic intermediate representations [11], x-
vector-based [12] and neural audio codec–based anonymization
[13], which leverage neural networks for more advanced levels
of anonymization. V oice conversion-based techniques aim to
map a speaker’s voice to a different target identity [14] [15].
2.2. Prosody in assessment
Prosody plays a central role in speaking and communication
evaluation, as aspects such as duration, rhythm, or pauses can
carry information about fluency, emphasis, and social cues be-
yond the literal words. Previous studies have utilized prosodic
attributes (e.g. speech rate, pause/silence structure, rhythm
measures) as input features to automated speaking assessment
models [16] [17], and as a basis for deriving fluency and ef-
fective communication scores [18]. Prosodic features can also
be used to infer higher-level constructs such as confidence or
emotional tone [19] [20]. Despite the recognized importance
of prosody in this context, there has been little research inves-
tigating how these features and the constructs they inform are
affected when speech is anonymized, leaving a gap in under-
standing the impact on automated communication assessment.
2.3. Privacy vs. utility
V oice anonymization research has largely focused on the pri-
vacy–utility trade-off in terms of speaker verification to quan-
tify the privacy, typically measured in equal error rate (EER),
and ASR performance to quantify the utility, typically measured
by word error rate (WER) [6]. However, far less attention has
been given to how anonymization affects acoustic and prosodic
features that are essential for communication assessment. These
features can have just as much of an influence on the utility of
an application. Recent studies have analyzed and aimed to pre-
serve prosodic features in the context of voice conversion, by
extracting and treating them separately [21] [22], or modifying
the loss function [23]. In this work, we examine these features
in the context of a simple, lightweight signal processing method
used for downstream communication assessment.

3. Privacy-preserving communication
assessment
Effective communication assessment is a key component of the
feedback given to users of our application that assesses work-
place readiness and durable skills. These users are employees
or candidates that employers are interested in evaluating, and
the results can potentially be used to inform meaningful deci-
sions. In such a context as the workplace, it is especially impor-
tant to safeguard identity not only for privacy and legal reasons,
but also for avoiding bias and discrimination. In our feedback
pipeline (Figure 1), anonymization is applied as the foremost
preprocessing step. Raw speech recordings are anonymized
prior to ASR and feature extraction, after which the original
audio is permanently discarded. All subsequent stages, includ-
ing the feature extraction and model prediction (e.g. models in
Table 1) steps, operate exclusively on the anonymized audio,
ensuring privacy compliance.
Given the efficiency requirements for implementation and
integration within a wider application, we utilized the PV-TSM
technique for anonymization due to its low computational cost,
minimal latency (∼0.4s for a 2-minute utterance), robustness to
noisy and varied data, as well as the absence of any extensive
model-training requirements. Previous studies have shown that
PV-TSM provides a favorable privacy–utility balance, yield-
ing minimal degradation in ASR word error rate while signif-
icantly reducing speaker re-identification accuracy [7]. In prior
work comparing several lightweight anonymization methods
[24], PV-TSM achieved an EER of 30.54% in a job-interview
setting. It outperformed McAdams coefficient modification in
privacy and neural audio codec–based anonymization in ASR
utility. While this study focuses on PV-TSM as the anonymiza-
tion method, we note that alternative techniques could also be
explored. Our choice of PV-TSM reflects practical considera-
tions around computational efficiency and deployment within
real-world assessment platforms, and we leave evaluation of
other methods to future work.
4. Experimental setup
4.1. Dataset
This study utilizes a dataset of recorded online interviews con-
ducted across the United States [25]. The corpus consists of
monologue recordings of∼2 minutes each, from participants
of diverse profiles. As the data were collected from a real-
world online platform, audio quality varies from relatively clear
to moderately noisy. The interviews consist of spontaneous re-
sponses to prompts targeting constructs such as communication,
interpersonal skills, leadership, etc. often in a workplace con-
text. A randomly selected subset of 1000 recordings was used
for efficiency purposes (254 speakers; 54% female/46% male;
ages 18-65; 90% American accent). All audio signals were con-
verted from stereo to mono and resampled to 16 kHz.
4.2. Feature set
The features analyzed in this study are selected from a wider
feature set used to generate user feedback on effective com-
munication within our assessment platform 1. This feature set
was selected by learning scientists according to informative-
ness, coverage, and user goals. The subset of features we are ex-
amining include the higher-level constructs of perceived confi-
dence, persuasiveness, formality, and language proficiency. The
first three are predicted by machine learning models trained on
acoustic features as given in Table 1. More complex deep learn-
ing models were not employed for these constructs due to com-
putational efficiency considerations, as noted in Section 3. The
language proficiency model is the “BERT large MTL model”
detailed in [26]. Lower-level features analyzed in this study
consist of: speaking rate, speech fluency metrics including
counts of repetitions, hesitation markers, and pauses, and two
ratio-based measures: filler ratio (proportion of disfluencies to
content words) and pause ratio (proportion of pause duration
to speaking time). Collectively, these features capture prosodic
and behavioral aspects of communication, contributing to feed-
back generation and serves as a good basis for evaluating the
effects of anonymization.

4.3. ASR models
The baseline ASR model used for prototyping our platform is an
internal model trained on a large proprietary corpus of English
speech. This model has been widely used and successfully de-
ployed across many applications within our organization, thus
serving as a natural starting point. The system is implemented
using the Kaldi framework [27] employing a modified nnet3-
chain recipe, trained with∼1,600 hours of speech recorded un-
der fairly clean conditions. At training, the model had a 19.5%
WER on its evaluation set. Importantly, this model provides
phone-level features (e.g. phone duration, pauses), as well as
hesitation markers (e.g. “uh”, “um”) and repetitions (e.g. “I, I
had...”) which are used to compute downstream feedback.
However, when applied to more varied and noisy data rep-
resentative of the type of recordings from our new assessment
platform, the internal model showed a notable drop in per-
formance (see Table 2). To improve robustness under these

As our initial dataset [25] lacks human transcriptions, we sought
an additional data source to adapt the model. We identified the
AMI Corpus [31] which consists of 100 hours of conversational,
spontaneous speech (meeting recordings) with human transcrip-

recordings, 36 hours were anonymized with PV-TSM to use as
training data, with a further 26 hours of original speech that
contains ‘uh’ or ‘um’ tokens to maximize disfluency coverage.

4.4. Evaluation protocol
Firstly, we assess speech recognition accuracy by computing the
WER on both original and anonymized audio using the internal
baseline ASR model, the Azure baseline model, and the Azure
custom fine-tuned model. This evaluation quantifies the impact
of anonymization on transcription accuracy, which directly in-
fluences our downstream communication assessment feedback.
Additionally, to evaluate the impact of anonymization on
communication-relevant features, we conduct a comparative
analysis between feature values extracted from original and
anonymized audio samples. For each feature, we compute pair-
wise correlations and descriptive statistics to quantify the de-
gree of consistency across the two conditions. Specifically,
Pearson’s correlation (r) and mean absolute error (MAE) mea-
sure distributional similarity and distortion, respectively. This
comparison allows us to analyze how closely the anonymized
speech preserves each of the features related to communication
assessment detailed in Section 4.2. By examining deviations
in the feature values and predicted scores, we can determine
whether anonymization affects signal fidelity and to what extent
these differences influence downstream scoring performance.

5.2. Impact of anonymization on speech features
Evaluation was conducted with the dataset described in Sec-
tion 4.1 [25], which reflects our target use case in terms of
speaker diversity, recording conditions, and spontaneous con-
versational content. The dataset is independent of any ASR
model’s training corpus, allowing for an impartial comparison
of model performance across anonymization conditions.
5.2.1. Low-level speech features
Results for low-level linguistic and prosodic features are pre-
sented in Table 3. In the 1-1 comparisons (a–c), we observe
consistent trends across models. The MAEs for all features ex-
cept number of pauses decrease from the internal baseline to the
Azure baseline, and further with the Azure custom model. Sim-
ilarly, most correlations increase across this same progression,
with the Azure custom model typically achieving the strongest
correlations. Notably, features that depend heavily on ASR
transcripts (e.g. repetitions, hesitation markers, fillers) show the
clearest improvements, reflecting the benefit of fine-tuning.
An interesting exception is observed for pause features,
where the Azure baseline shows slightly better correlations than
the custom model. This may be due to fine-tuning altering the
model’s voice activity detection or frame segmentation thresh-
olds, introducing additional variation in the resulting pauses.
It is also possible that prior to fine-tuning, the baseline model
may have misinterpreted some disfluencies or brief hesitations
as background noise or other non-linguistic input. After tuning
the model to recognize these as utterances, this could result in
more individual, shorter silences which can bring some variabil-
ity to the overall number of pauses. Despite this, the correlation
and MAE for these features remain strong. These results indi-
cate that the fine-tuned model achieves greater consistency not
only in ASR accuracy, but also across derived linguistic and
prosodic features going from original to anonymized audio.

model’s original audio results are compared to the Azure mod-

features. The slightly reduced pauses correlation is consistent
with the explanation above, though the MAE is an improve-
ment. Conversely, the speaking rate has better correlation but
a higher MAE. The numbers are quite close, so this could just
be due to minor timestamp-level variations. Importantly, these

5.2.2. High-level communication constructs
High-level construct results are presented in Table 4. In the
1-1 comparisons (f–h), all MAEs either decrease or are main-
tained from the internal model to the Azure baseline, and fur-
ther to the Azure custom model. Correlations similarly increase
or are maintained across models for all constructs but formal-
ity, which shows a slight (1%) decrease each time. This is
still competitive and highly comparable; the small difference
is likely due to sensitivity to timing-based features (e.g. dura-
tions) that may vary slightly across ASR outputs, though the
effect is minor and the MAE still shows improvement. Persua-
sion, which relies solely on MFCC and formant-based acous-
tic features, remains unaffected by ASR variation and thus ex-
hibits constant performance across all models. Its low MAE
of 0.04 suggests that anonymization introduces minimal degra-
dation, though the correlation drops to 0.23. A paired t-test
(t(999) = 1.13,p= 0.26) confirms that this difference is not
statistically significant (α= 0.05), however it is insightful to
note this relationship and consider whether further iteration may
be needed to improve the estimation of this construct. Overall,
the Azure custom model continues to demonstrate the most con-
sistency when going from original to anonymized audio across
high-level, aggregated communication constructs.
In the cross-model comparisons (i–j), the Azure custom
model outperforms the Azure baseline model for confidence
and formality, while proficiency shows a slight degradation.
Since the proficiency model depends entirely on the ASR tran-
script as input, this likely reflects differences in transcription
accuracy. As shown in Table 2, the internal model exhibits high
WER even on original speech, with the Azure base model on
anonymized speech achieving a WER more comparable to that
case than the Azure custom model. Thus, the proficiency results
appear consistent with the underlying WER patterns. In this
case, it is again probable that the Azure custom model is more
objectively “correct”. Nonetheless, the confidence and formal-
ity results confirm that fine-tuning the Azure model restores
some performance in relation to the original internal baseline.
6. Conclusion
This work demonstrates that when using PV-TSM to anonymize
speech, it can sufficiently preserve the acoustic and linguis-
tic information essential for communication assessment. With
targeted ASR adaptation, discrepancies between original and
anonymized WERs are reduced to near parity, and robustness is
improved across both low-level and high-level speech features,
narrowing the performance gap introduced by anonymization.
