Beyond Pitch: Multidimensional Cue Reweighting of Two High-Falling Tones
in Pingdingshan Mandarin
Zhuo Chen1,2, Bingliang Zhao ID 1,2, Xiyu Wu ID 1,2,∗∗
1 Laboratory of Language Sciences, Peking University, China
2 Department of Chinese Language and Literature, Peking University, China

Abstract
Tonogenesis typically describes the process by which conso-
nant voicing develops into phonation and subsequently evolves
into pitch distinctions. The opposite process, where degraded
pitch allows other cues to be enhanced, is less documented.
This study investigates the cue reweighting of two high-falling
tones in Pingdingshan Mandarin. We analyzed speech and
EGG data from 32 speakers of different ages, focusing on mul-
tiple cues including pitch, phonation, duration, and energy.
Mixed-effects modeling and random forest analyses indicate
that older speakers primarily distinguish these tones through
pitch. For younger speakers, as pitch differences narrow, the
relative weights of phonation (creaky voice) as well as dura-
tion have significantly increased. It shows a reweighting pro-
cess from a pitch-dominant to a multidimensional cue system
in a Mandarin variety, reflecting dynamic competition among
multiple cues in tonal evolution.
Index Terms: Pingdingshan Mandarin, tonal contrast, falling
tone, creaky voice, sound change
1. Introduction
Tone, traditionally defined as a pattern of pitch variation with
a lexical contrastive function [1], is increasingly accepted as
a multidimensional phenomenon in which phonation and du-
ration also serve as crucial acoustic cues [2, 3, 4, 5, 6]. Syn-
chronically, the interaction between pitch and phonation forms
a continuum across languages. In certain languages, phonation
plays a decisive role in phonemic contrast, resulting in distinct
registers that are independent of tone, as observed in Black
Miao [2], White Hmong [7], and Southern Yi [8]. In most
tone languages, pitch dominates while phonation may func-
tion as a secondary cue, such as the creaky voice associated
with Mandarin Tone 3 [9, 10, 11] and the Cantonese low-falling
tone [12]. In some cases, phonation even outweighs pitch in
specific tonal contrasts, such as the two dipping tones in North-
ern Vietnamese [13].
Beyond synchronic co-occurrence, phonation and pitch are
closely linked in sound change due to shared articulatory mech-
anisms [11]. Traditional tonogenesis theory suggests that the
loss of consonant voicing phonologizes into phonation distinc-
tions, which subsequently shift into pitch contrasts (e.g., Wu
Chinese [14] and Vietnamese [15]). Reverse trajectories, where
contrasts erode and phonation becomes more distinctive, have
been documented in tonoexodus research. For instance, in the
Nghe An dialect of northern Vietnam, the six historical tone cat-
egories have largely merged into a few falling contours, with the
remaining contrasts sustained by minimal pitch differences and

phonation [16, 17]. However, apparent-time evidence of such
a pitch-to-phonation reweighting process within a Sinitic tonal
system remains rare.
Theoretically, cue reweighting may occur when pitch con-
tours converge, prompting speakers to reinforce secondary cues
to maintain phonemic contrast. The phenomenon of phonation
playing an increasing role when pitch differences weaken has
been observed in phrase-final positions in both Mandarin and
Thai [18]. When pitch information is masked by noise or re-
moved, listeners compensate by utilizing secondary cues such
as phonation and duration to facilitate tone recognition [19, 20].
Although synchronic cue compensation is well documented,
diachronic cue reweighting from pitch to phonation remains
rarely reported.
The Mandarin dialects of Henan Province and adjacent re-
gions in China provide an ideal context for investigating this
reverse trajectory. Preliminary studies of speech production
and perception indicate that certain dialects in this area employ
creaky voice to distinguish two acoustically similar high-falling
tones [21, 22, 23, 24]. However, prior research has generally
lacked sociolinguistic data and systematic physiological anal-
yses, leaving it uncertain whether the role of creaky voice is
undergoing a diachronic shift. To clarify the contribution of
phonation to lexical tone, systematic comparison and analy-
sis of its physiological and acoustic properties across different
phonetic environments, such as isolated forms and connected
speech, are required [25]. Such research is both necessary and
urgent for Mandarin dialects in these regions.
This study examines Pingdingshan Mandarin in Henan
Province, where Tone 2 (T2) and Tone 4 (T4) are described as
nearly identical high-falling tones [26]. A cross-generational
speech production experiment was conducted, integrating f0
analysis, electroglottography (EGG), and automatic creaky
voice detection. By analyzing multidimensional cues across
three generations of speakers, the study attempts to reveal the
contrastive mechanisms between T2 and T4 and to determine
whether the acoustic cue weights in this two-high-falling-tone
system are currently undergoing diachronic change.
2. Methods
2.1. Participants and Materials
A total of 32 native speakers of Pingdingshan Mandarin partic-
ipated in the recordings. All speakers had no history of hear-
ing or speech disorders, and had not received prior professional
training in linguistics or psychology. Most participants are also
fluent in Standard Mandarin. All participants signed informed
consent forms authorizing the use of their data for academic re-
search and received monetary compensation.

2.2. Recording Equipment and Data Collection
Recordings were conducted in a quiet environment in Pingding-
shan. Acoustic and glottal impedance (EGG) signals were cap-
tured synchronously using a Sony ECM-44B microphone and
a Laryngograph EGG-D200, respectively. Both signals were
routed through a Behringer XENYX 302 mixer and an ASUS
XONAR U5 sound card, then digitized into Adobe Audition
2024 at 44.1 kHz/16-bit resolution.
Materials were presented to the participants in Chinese
characters, either in isolated forms or in a carrier sentence “I
say [X] for you to hear”(/uo55 ùuo24 [X] kW24 ni55 thiN24/). All
speech stimuli were presented randomly using PsychoPy [27].
Each target item was repeated three times in both conditions.
2.3. Data Annotation and Extraction
Acoustic signals were annotated using Praat (v.6.3.17), with
vowels segmented from the second glottal pulse to the point of
significant formant decay [28]. V oiceSauce [29] and KayPentax
CSL (3700) were employed to extract acoustic (f0, RMS, dura-
tion) and electroglottograph parameters, respectively. The Open
Quotient (OQ) and Speed Quotient (SQ) (OQ = open phase / pe-
riod, and SQ = opening phase / closing phase) were determined
using a 25% amplitude threshold on the EGG signal [30, 31].
Additionally, a MATLAB programme was employed to auto-
matically detect creaky voice segments in speech signals [32],
with the detection threshold set to 0.04 [33]. All vowel seg-
ments were divided into 18 equal time intervals for mean value
calculation; creaky voice was binarized (1 if > 50% duration
per interval, otherwise 0).
2.4. Data Analysis
Analyses were performed in R (v.4.4.3). To mitigate coarticula-
tion, only the middle 16 intervals were analyzed.F0 and Energy
were log-transformed [34], followed by within-subject z-score
normalization for all continuous parameters. Outliers beyond±
3 S.D. were removed, leaving 4,252 valid samples (T1: 1016;
T2: 1160; T3: 1072; T4: 1004).
Functional principal component analysis (fPCA) and logis-
tic fPCA [35, 36] were applied to time-course data ( f0, OQ,
SQ, Energy, and binary Creak). Optimal B-spline basis (k) and
smoothing penalties (λ) were determined via grid search and the
generalized cross-validation (GCV) criterion [37]. PCs explain-
ing> 70% cumulative variance were used for cue weighting.
Linear mixed models (LMMs via lme4) examined fixed
effects (Tone, Age, and Condition) on PCs and duration. A
top-down strategy determined the maximal convergent random
effects structure. The initial model included the maximal ran-
dom effects structure, which was then stepwise refined based on
model convergence and goodness-of-fit. Detailed model formu-
las will be presented in the results.
Conditional random forests ( party package) [38] quanti-
fied cue contributions for T2/T4 contrasts at group and individ-
ual levels. Eleven predictors were used to predict Tone. The
predictors include duration, PC scores for f0, OQ, SQ, Energy,
Creak, and a null baseline generated from randomized F0 PC1.
Model hyperparameters were set to 300 trees, a minimum split
of 10, and a minimum bucket of 4. To handle collinearity, con-
ditional permutation importance [39] was calculated over 100
iterations. Relative weights were derived from raw importance
proportions [40], with differences assessed via beta regression
and model performance via AUC.
Statistical significance was set at α = 0 .05. For post-
hoc comparisons, we used estimated marginal means (EMMs)
from the emmeans package and p-values corrected for multiple
comparisons using the Bonferroni correction.
3. Results
3.1. Temporal Dynamics of Phonetic Cues
The GAMM curves (f0, OQ, SQ, and Energy) in Figure 1 show
that T2 and T4 are both falling tones with high onsets. However,
subtle differences exist in their f0 contours. The f0 of T2 is
fairly flat in the first half of the syllable. In contrast, the f0 of
T4 begins to decline significantly earlier. This results in a lower
fundamental frequency at the offset. The OQ, SQ, and Energy
curves of the two tones also diverge significantly in the second
half of the syllable. This suggests their phonological contrast
relies on multidimensional acoustic cues.
The creak heatmap in Figure 2a visualizes the results of
automatic creaky-voice detection. This not only further corrob-
orates the divergence in phonation but also reveals a notable
generational evolution: from the older to the younger genera-
tion, the probability and extent of creaky voice at the end of
T2 continuously shrink, while the distribution in T4 expands
substantially. Furthermore, although continuous speech (carrier
sentences) generally suppresses creaky production, this trend of
generational evolution remains robust.
Subsequently, this study employed fPCA to extract time-
course principal component scores forf0, OQ, SQ, and Energy
(a principal component represents variation around the mean;
PC1 represents a relatively constant vertical shift in the mean,
and PC2 represents a linear contrast between the first and sec-
ond half with a single crossing of zero [37]). Logistic fPCA was
used to extract the trajectory features of the binarized creaky
voice data (where PC1 represents the probability of creakiness
in the second half of the syllable). The cumulative variance
explained by the principal components for each parameter all
exceeded 70%: f0 (84.8%), OQ (73.9%), SQ (76.7%), Energy
(89.2%), and Creak (90.2%).

3.3. Random Forest-Based Cue Weighting Analysis
The preceding LMMs suggest the younger group has begun us-
ing duration and phonation cues to distinguish the two falling
tones. To quantify how much each cue shapes this contrast, this
section uses Random Forest models to systematically assess the
cue weights speakers use in differentiating T2 and T4. For all
models, the average AUC exceeded 0.97. This indicates robust
model performance and high classification accuracy. Figure 4
shows how each parameter’s relative weight differs across the
groups.
Intergenerational comparison reveals a clear reorganiza-
tion in cue weight distribution. In the older group, F0 PC1
and F0 PC2 are dominant. They serve as the main and sec-
ondary cues for T2-T4 contrast. Duration has a minor role,
and phonation does not distinguish tones. In the middle-aged
group, F0 PC2’s weight drops. The system then relies mostly
on F0 PC1. In the younger group, F0 PC1 keeps a high weight,
but its dominance lessens. Meanwhile, duration and phonation
(Creak PC1) gain significant weight and emerge as two major
cues. These generational changes align with earlier LMM re-
sults: as the T2-T4 pitch difference narrows, the younger group
maintains tonal contrast through duration and phonation cues.

4. Discussion and Conclusion
The present study, conducted from a speech-production per-
spective and using an apparent-time paradigm, reveals the in-
tergenerational difference of tonal-contrast mechanisms in the
Pingdingshan Mandarin. The core finding is that the contrastive
cues for the high-falling tones, T2 and T4, are transitioning
from being pitch-dominant to a coordinated mechanism involv-
ing pitch, phonation, and duration.
To compensate for the reduced pitch contour difference be-
tween T2 and T4, younger speakers have increased the relative
weights of phonation and duration, thereby helping to main-
tain the acoustic distance between the two tones. Specifically,
changes in T4 have led to increased differences in voice quality
and duration compared to T2. This finding differs from strate-
gies observed in previous studies. For example, in the Huantai
Mandarin of Shandong [41] and the Chaoyang dialect of east-
ern Guangdong [42], tonal contrast is maintained by expanding
pitch contour differences. This reflects a unique pattern of cue
reweighting in the Pingdingshan Mandarin.
More importantly, our findings supplement cross-linguistic
theories of tonal evolution. Previous work has emphasized a
unidirectional path from phonation to pitch [14, 15, 43]. Re-
verse trajectories, where pitch erosion makes phonation more
distinctive, are documented in tonoexodus research (e.g., Nghe
An Vietnamese [16, 17]). However, apparent-time evidence has
remained limited. The present study shows that the weakening
of pitch differences can trigger the phonologization of phona-
tion and duration as enhancing cues. This strongly suggests
that the evolution of distinctive cues in tonal systems is not al-
ways a linear, unidirectional path. Instead, it can be an organic
process of multidimensional cue competition and dynamic self-
adaptation aimed at contrast maintenance.
Duration can serve as a vital cue in tonal contrasts, but
typically in languages with existing or historical checked-
unchecked syllable contrasts, where duration differences are
coda-driven (e.g., Southern Chinese dialects [28, 44] and
Thai [45]). However, T2 and T4 in the Pingdingshan Mandarin
lack coda contrasts. The differences in duration are likely due
to the T4 contour’s physiological characteristics. Falling tones
are generally easier to produce and require shorter durations due
to the physiological properties of the laryngeal muscles (partic-
ularly the cricothyroid) [46, 47]. Furthermore, creaky voice is
often associated with lowf0 [48]. The realisation of the creaky
target in T4 coincides with a rapid f0 descent. Compared to
the more gradual f0 decline in T2, the shorter duration of T4,
originally a non-primary feature, has been reinforced.
Overall, the Pingdingshan Mandarin case offers an
example of a direction opposite to the well-documented
phonation-to-pitch shift, providing typological value. Although
phonation weight remains lower than pitch at the group level,
five individual speakers (3 young, 2 middle-aged) show phona-
tion surpassing pitch. This variation reflects the gradual nature
of sound change and individual strategies. Diachronic language
change is not a sudden systemic mutation but a gradual accumu-
lation of micro-variations within a speech community [49]. The
pitch-to-phonation shift is a possible but not yet realized out-
come of ongoing cue competition in Pingdingshan Mandarin.
This suggests that rare cross-linguistic sound change patterns
are not impossible. For example, young Tamang speakers reuti-
lise ancient features to reconstruct voicing contrasts [50]. The
direction of sound change is not predetermined but emerges
from interpersonal interactions.
The conclusions of this study are currently drawn from
speech production data. Future validation through perception
experiments is necessary to assess how listeners perceive and
distinguish these sounds. Such experiments will clarify whether
production and perception are aligned and will contribute to a
more comprehensive understanding of the mechanisms under-
lying sound change.
