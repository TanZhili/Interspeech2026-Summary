Extreme Few-Shot Phoneme Discovery for Indigenous Australian and Pacific
Languages via Typological Transfer Learning
Prasanth Yadla1
1 Independent Researcher, Seattle, W A, USA
pyadla2@alumni.ncsu.edu
Abstract
Indigenous Australian and Pacific languages face extinc-
tion, and many have fewer than ten hours of transcribed speech.
Conventional acoustic unit discovery often assumes over 100
hours of unlabeled audio—far beyond what is available for en-
dangered languages. We propose an extreme few-shot phoneme
discovery framework that operates on under one hour of audio,
leveraging typological transfer from high-resource Austrone-
sian languages through a Vector-Quantized Variational Autoen-
coder (VQ-V AE). We introduce Typological Anchor Selection
(TAS), which selects optimal source languages based on pho-
netic inventory overlap. Evaluated on three endangered lan-
guage datasets, our approach improves Normalized Mutual In-
formation by 18.8% and cluster purity by 15.6% over multilin-
gual self-supervised baselines. This provides a scalable path for
rapid language documentation and revitalization.
Index Terms: endangered language documentation, acoustic
unit discovery, typological transfer learning, vector-quantized
variational autoencoder, low-resource speech processing
1. Introduction
As of 2026, more than half of the world’s approximately 7,000
languages face the threat of extinction within the next century
[1], with Indigenous Australian and Pacific islander communi-
ties representing a substantial portion of this endangered lin-
guistic heritage. This underscores the urgency of developing in-
clusive speech technologies that accommodate the remarkable
diversity of human phonetic systems, particularly those of his-
torically marginalized communities.
The primary obstacle to digital preservation of endangered
languages is what we term the “transcription gap”—the vast dis-
crepancy between available audio recordings and orthographic
transcription. For many Indigenous languages, linguistic doc-
umentation exists primarily as analog field recordings from the
1960s through 1990s that have been digitized but remain untran-
scribed. Traditional manual transcription requires specialized
phonetic expertise and proves prohibitively time-consuming,
with estimates suggesting a 100:1 hour ratio for endangered lan-
guages exhibiting complex phonological systems [2].
Recent advances in Self-Supervised Learning (SSL) have
demonstrated promise for automatic speech recognition in low-
resource scenarios. Models such as Wav2Vec 2.0 [3] and XLS-
R [4] can learn robust acoustic representations from unlabeled
speech data. However, these approaches exhibit inherent biases
toward Indo-European phonological systems due to the compo-
sition of their training data. In extreme low-resource (ELR) set-
tings, where recorded material comprises less than 60 minutes
of speech, state-of-the-art models fail to capture subtle phone-
mic contrasts characteristic of Australian and Austronesian lan-
guages, including retroflexion, vowel length distinctions, and
complex tone systems.
This work investigates whether typological similarity be-
tween languages can serve as an effective bridge for phoneme
induction in undocumented languages. We hypothesize that
pre-training on linguistically related high-resource languages
provides a more robust latent space for discovering acoustic
units in target languages than generic multilingual pre-training.
This hypothesis derives from fundamental principles of linguis-
tic typology: languages within the same family or areal group-
ing exhibit systematic phonological patterns that can be lever-
aged for cross-lingual transfer [5].
Our contributions are threefold. First, we introduce Typo-
logical Anchor Selection (TAS), a principled method for iden-
tifying optimal source languages based on quantified phonetic
inventory overlap using PHOIBLE [6]. Second, we present an
adapted VQ-V AE [7] architecture optimized for noisy heritage
recordings through commitment loss stabilization. Third, we
establish a rigorous evaluation protocol for extreme few-shot
learning scenarios and validate our approach on three endan-
gered languages from publicly available datasets.
2. Related Work
2.1. Self-Supervised Speech Representation Learning
Self-supervised learning has transformed speech processing by
enabling models to learn meaningful representations from unla-
beled data. Wav2Vec 2.0 [3] employs contrastive learning over
masked speech inputs, achieving competitive performance on
English automatic speech recognition benchmarks. XLS-R [4]
extends this framework to 128 languages using 436,000 hours
of publicly available speech data, demonstrating cross-lingual
transfer capabilities. HuBERT [8] iteratively refines cluster tar-
gets through offlinek-means clustering, yielding strong spo-
ken language understanding representations. Despite these ad-
vances, such models exhibit three fundamental limitations for
endangered language documentation: substantial data require-
ments (typically exceeding 100 hours per language), phonetic
bias toward well-represented language families, and computa-
tional overhead that precludes deployment in field settings.
2.2. Unsupervised Acoustic Unit Discovery
Unsupervised methods for discovering discrete acoustic units
have evolved from classical approaches based on Hidden
Markov Models and Gaussian Mixture Models [9] to modern
neural architectures [7, 10]. The Zero Resource Speech Chal-
lenge [11] has catalyzed progress in this domain, establishing
standardized evaluation protocols for acoustic unit discovery
without labeled data. However, most competitive systems re-

2.3. Cross-Lingual Transfer for Low-Resource Speech Pro-
cessing
Cross-lingual transfer learning has been extensively explored
for low-resource automatic speech recognition [5, 12]. Key
findings from this literature indicate that typologically similar
source languages consistently yield superior transfer compared
to geographically proximate but linguistically distant languages
[12], multi-source transfer generally outperforms single-source
approaches [13], and phoneme-level alignment improves upon
purely acoustic transfer methods [14]. However, prior work typ-
ically assumes availability of at least 10 hours of target language
data. Our work extends these findings to the extreme few-shot
regime where traditional fine-tuning approaches fail due to se-
vere overfitting.

3.4. Two-Stage Training Protocol
Training proceeds in two stages. Stage one involves pre-training
the VQ-V AE on selected high-resource source languages for
100,000 steps with batch size 8 (64 seconds total per batch). We
employ the Adam optimizer [17] with learning rate3×10 −4
and cosine annealing schedule. Codebook sizeKis set to the
average phoneme inventory size of source languages (typically
K= 40for Austronesian languages). Codebook vectors are
updated via exponential moving average with decay factor 0.99.
Stage two adapts the pre-trained model to target language
data. Critical modifications include retaining the source lan-
guage codebook rather than random re-initialization, reducing
the learning rate to1×10 −4 to prevent catastrophic forgetting
[18], applying SpecAugment [19] data augmentation with time

warping and frequency masking, and implementing early stop-
ping based on codebook utilization (training halts if fewer than
50% of codes are actively used). Target language training pro-
ceeds for 5,000 to 10,000 steps depending on data quantity.
Following training, we extract discrete codes for all tar-
get language utterances and apply post-processing to map
codes to phoneme-like units. This involves analyzing code co-
occurrence patterns to identify allophonic variants, modeling
duration distributions with Gaussian mixture models, hierar-
chically clustering codebook vectors by acoustic similarity, and
validating discovered units against known minimal pairs when
lexical documentation exists.

4. Experimental Setup
4.1. Datasets
We utilize exclusively public datasets to ensure reproducibil-
ity. Target languages include Te Reo M ¯aori (Polynesian lan-
guage of New Zealand, from the M ¯aori Speech dataset [20]),
Pitjantjatjara (Pama-Nyungan language of Central Australia,
from endangered language archives [21]), and Nauruan (Mi-
cronesian language with fewer than 10,000 speakers, from pub-
licly available linguistic documentation archives1). Source lan-
guages comprise Javanese (127 hours validated from Mozilla
Common V oice [15]) and Tagalog (163 hours validated). All
audio is resampled to 16 kHz mono, with target language train-
ing strictly limited to 60 minutes and remaining data held out
for validation. Target language audio was randomly split 60/40
for training and validation, stratified by speaker where metadata
was available.
4.2. Baseline Methods
We compare against five methods: Wav2Vec 2.0 [3] pre-
trained on English LibriSpeech [22] and quantized to 100 codes
viak-means clustering; XLS-R [4] (300M parameters) with
layer 8 representations quantized viak-means; HuBERT [8]
with hierarchical clustering-based quantization (English vari-
ant); VQ-V AE with random initialization (no source language
pre-training) as an ablation baseline; and our full typological
transfer method.

4.3. Evaluation Metrics
Automatic metrics include Normalized Mutual Information
(NMI) [23] measuring self-consistency of cluster assignments
across two independent noisy forward passes, and phoneme pu-
rity quantifying the proportion of clusters dominated by a single
assignment under input perturbation. We emphasize that these
metrics measurecluster stability under perturbationrather than
direct phoneme-level accuracy against ground-truth transcrip-
tions. High self-consistency NMI indicates that the model dis-
covers robust, reproducible acoustic units, which serves as an
upper bound on phoneme discovery quality in the absence of
labeled reference data.
4.4. Implementation Details
The encoder and decoder each employ five convolutional lay-
ers with 512 hidden dimensions. Codebook sizes vary from
30 to 60 codes in ablation studies, with embedding dimension
D= 64and frame rate 100 Hz. Data augmentation includes
SpecAugment [19] with maximum 20-frame time masking and
8 mel-bin frequency masking, pitch shifting within±2semi-
tones, and background noise mixing from the MUSAN corpus
[24] at 10–20 dB SNR to simulate heritage recording condi-
tions. All experiments were conducted on a single NVIDIA
DGX Spark, with source language pre-training requiring ap-
proximately 12 hours and target language adaptation requiring
2–4 hours per language. Figure 1 shows the spectrogram audio
for the original and reconstructed audio.

6. Discussion
Our results demonstrate that typologically informed transfer
learning provides superior initialization compared to generic
multilingual models. We hypothesize three mechanisms un-
derlying this improvement. First, acoustic template matching
occurs when related languages share formant patterns for cor-
responding phonemes, enabling codebook vectors learned on
source language phonemes to align well with target language
realizations through minor adaptation. Second, phonotactic pri-
ors transfer when languages exhibit similar syllable structures,
allowing the encoder to learn appropriate segmentation bound-
aries. Third, typological knowledge provides implicit regular-
ization against overfitting to idiosyncratic acoustic patterns in
limited target data.
Several limitations merit discussion. Our current architec-
ture does not explicitly model pitch, potentially causing diffi-
culties for tone languages common in Papuan language fami-
lies. When no high-resource typological relatives exist (as for
language isolates), our method degrades to random initializa-
tion performance. Pitjantjatjara, while not Austronesian but
Pama-Nyungan, benefits from typological transfer due to shared
phonetic characteristics with Austronesian languages: simple
five-vowel systems, absence of lexical tone, and predominantly

7. Conclusions
This work demonstrates that typological transfer learning pro-
vides an effective pathway for phoneme discovery in extreme
low-resource scenarios using less than one hour of audio. By
leveraging high-resource relatives within language families,
we achieve 15.6% average improvement in cluster purity and
18.8% improvement in NMI over the state-of-the-art XLS-R
multilingual self-supervised learning baseline. Our Typolog-
ical Anchor Selection method provides a principled approach
to source language selection validated through experiments on
three endangered Austronesian and Australian languages. The
adapted VQ-V AE architecture with commitment stabilization
proves robust to acoustic challenges characteristic of heritage
recordings. Beyond academic contributions, this work sug-
gests potential pathways toward practical language documenta-
tion applications, including preliminary lexicographic hypothe-
ses, semi-supervised ASR development, and early-stage writ-
ing system design, though validation by community linguists
remains essential future work.
