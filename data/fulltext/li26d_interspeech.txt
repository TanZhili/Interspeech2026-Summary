Abstract
With the rapid advances of audio generation models and
audio processing techniques, new types of distortions and au-
dio domains are introduced, posing challenges to static audio
quality assessment (AQA) models. Traditional AQA models
cannot keep pace with the dynamic and diverse nature of real-
world audio data. To address this issue, we propose CAQA-
Net, a systematic continual learning framework for AQA, in
which the model continuously acquires new knowledge from
a sequence of tasks while retaining previously learned knowl-
edge. The framework adopts a dual-branch and multi-head ar-
chitecture that combines a trainable waveform encoder with a
spectrogram-based semantic anchor. A knowledge-distillation
regularizer preserves old knowledge, while a prototype-based
gating mechanism supports task-agnostic inference. Results
demonstrate that the proposed method successfully balances
plasticity and stability, achieving performance within 0.036
SRCC (4.6%) of the joint-learning upper bound.
Index Terms: speech quality assessment, music quality assess-
ment, continual learning, catastrophic forgetting
1. Introduction
Numerous audio quality assessment methods [1, 2, 3] have been
developed to automate subjective evaluation. Early work pri-
marily focused on speech quality assessment [4] and achieved
substantial progress on task-specific problems. In recent years,
the rapid advancement of deep generative models for speech
synthesis, voice conversion, and music generation has intro-
duced new distortion types and evaluation criteria [5]. As a
result, AQA is no longer limited to speech but has expanded
to music [3, 6] and more complex audio domains, creating a
need for cross-task AQA. As new tasks and training data contin-
uously emerge, the conventional once-for-all learning strategy
that trains a static model on all available data at once becomes
impractical for dynamic AQA scenarios. The most straightfor-
ward solution is joint learning, which retrains a static model on
both new and previously collected data. Clearly, this strategy
requires substantial storage and computation overhead. A more
cost-effective alternative is to fine-tune the model using only
the new data. Unfortunately, fine-tuning is prone to catastrophic
forgetting of previously learned knowledge.
Continual learning (CL) [7, 8] aims to enable mod-
els to learn new tasks from a sequential data stream with-
out catastrophic forgetting. Its key challenge is the stabil-
ity–plasticity dilemma, which requires balancing adaptation to
acquire knowledge from emerging domains with retention of
previously learned knowledge. Although CL has been exten-
sively studied for image processing problems [9, 10, 11], its
application to audio processing tasks remains largely unex-
plored. Compared with conventional image classification, con-
tinual audio quality assessment faces several unique and com-
plex challenges. First, the task evolution from speech to music
involves large acoustic feature domain shifts. Second, as a re-
gression task the model must learn and preserve a continuous,
fine-grained mapping to perceptual scores rather than simple
class boundaries. Finally, inconsistent scoring standards across
datasets and label noise further increase the difficulty of achiev-
ing stable convergence when learning across AQA tasks.
Saget et al. [12] offered an initial feasibility study on ap-
plying CL to speech quality assessment, emphasizing the neces-
sity and importance of considering task sequences and domain
shifts. However, they did not propose a unified CL framework
to mitigate catastrophic forgetting, nor did they consider emerg-
ing music quality assessment tasks or the speech-to-music do-
main shift. In contrast, we present a dedicated CL pipeline
that is applicable to both speech and music, integrating archi-
tectural design, training strategies, and adaptive inference. To
our knowledge, this is the first AQA study to systematically for-
mulate and address CL challenges with concrete methods and
comprehensive evaluation.
The proposed framework adopts a dual-branch, multi-head
architecture that fuses raw waveform and frame-level spectro-
gram features. During training, a knowledge-distillation based
regularization term is introduced to mitigate catastrophic for-
getting, and at inference time a gating mechanism is used to
adaptively weight the outputs of different task heads. We con-
duct a comprehensive evaluation on a continual task sequence
composed of five speech and music datasets. Experimental re-
sults demonstrate that the proposed method significantly out-
performs baselines on multiple metrics, while its performance
approaches the joint-learning upper bound.
2. Method
2.1. Framework Design and Model Architectures
We propose an architecture that integrates raw waveform and
frame-level spectrogram features to learn complementary audio
representations. To address the stability–plasticity dilemma, we
freeze the spectrogram branch to maintain stability, while fine-
tuning the waveform branch to maintain plasticity.
Specifically, we adopt M2D [13] as the trainable waveform
branchF w, which operates on raw waveforms, while BEATS
[14] serves as the frozen spectrogram branchF s, operating
on log-mel spectrograms. Built upon masked-prediction self-
supervised learning, the M2D branch is well suited for online
fine-tuning and capturing task-specific distribution shifts, en-
suring strong plasticity and rapid adaptation. In contrast, the

3. Experimental Settings
3.1. Datasets
We evaluate our method on five datasets reflecting the evo-
lution of AQA tasks across speech and music domains (see
Table 1). We begin with TCD–VOIP [23], an early small-
scale speech communication dataset with synthetic degrada-
tions. Then NISQA–SIM is adopted, which is the simulated
subset of the NISQA corpus [24] and covers diverse synthetic
distortions. The third task, Tencent Corpus [25], combines real-
istic and simulated degradations from online-meetings scenar-
ios. SingMOS [26] focuses on singing voice quality assess-
ment, while MusicEval [27] provides instrumental music sam-
ples. Collectively, these datasets span substantial shifts in con-
tent and distortions, forming a challenging CL benchmark.

3.2. Implementation
Each dataset is divided into training, validation, and test sets
in a ratio of 80%/10%/10%. We use the Adam optimizer with
a learning rate of 2×10 −4. For each task, the model is trained
for 10 epochs: the first 3 epochs are used to warm up the pre-
diction head only, and the remaining 7 epochs jointly train the
prediction head and the feature extractor with early stopping.
The batch size is set to 16. During inference, we useN= 64
for the number of prototypes and we find the performance is in-
sensitive to the choice ofN.αin Eq. 7 is set to 16 based on the
validation sets.

3.4. Comparison Methods
•SL (Separate Learning): For each task, we train a separate
model. The results of this approach can serve as an upper
bound for evaluating task performance in AQA.
•JL (Joint Learning): This approach trains the model on the
union of all task datasets simultaneously. It is typically re-
garded as the performance upper bound for CL.
•FT (Fine-Tune): Using a single prediction head model, after
training on one task, the trained model is directly fine-tuned
on a new task without employing methods to prevent forget-
ting. The results can serve as a baseline for CL methods.
•MH-FT (Multi-Head-Fine-Tune): Building on FT, MH-FT
adds a prediction head for each new task. MH-FT serves as
the baseline for all regularization-based CL methods.
•CAQA-Net (EWC): Our proposed CAQA-Net variant with
Elastic Weight Consolidation (EWC) [21]. EWC pre-
serves important parameters from previous tasks by adding
a quadratic penalty term to the loss. In our experiments, the
regularization weight is set toλ= 1000.
•CAQA-Net (MAS): Our CAQA-Net variant with Memory
Aware Synapses (MAS) [22]. MAS constrains large updates
on parameters that are important to the model output. The
regularization weight is set toλ= 100.
•CAQA-Net (LwF): Our proposed full implementation,
which uses a knowledge-distillation-based regularizer within
the CAQA-Net architecture. It optimizes the model using
Eq. 5 withλ= 100.
4. Results
4.1. Main Results
We draw several observations from Table 2. Although SL
achieves the highest mSRCC, it is trained on a single dataset
and therefore generalizes poorly to unseen datasets. Compared
with SL, the JL approach leads to a drop in accuracy on single
task but still provides an upper bound for all CL methods. How-
ever, this comes at a high computational and storage cost. With-
out mitigation, FT suffers from severe forgetting, while MH-FT
improves all metrics. Compared with MH-FT without regular-
ization, the three regularization-based CL methods all mitigate
catastrophic forgetting and lead to better average performance,
validating the framework’s generality across different regular-
izers. Among them, LwF outperforms the others on all four
metrics and achieves a better plasticity–stability trade-off. Its
average performance is close to the upper bound provided by
JL, which supports our hypothesis that, for audio MOS predic-
tion, constraining the model’s outputs is more effective than im-
posing rigid constraints directly on the parameters.

4.2. Ablation Study
4.2.1. Inference Strategies
Table 3 compares three inference strategies: using only the
last prediction head (Last Head), the prototype-based gating
mechanism (Gating Mechanism), and the ideal task-specific
head (Task-Specific Head). The Last Head strategy empha-
sizes adaptation to new tasks but ignores specialized heads for
previous tasks, resulting in the worst performance across all
methods. The Task-Specific Head can be regarded as an up-
per bound, since it assumes that the task identity of each test
sample is known and always selects the most appropriate head,
thus achieving the highest mSRCC and mPSI. In contrast, our
proposed gating mechanism operates in a realistic setting where
task labels are unavailable and automatically assigns weights
to different prediction heads. It significantly outperforms Last
Head and approaches the performance of Task-Specific Head
in most cases. This shows prototype-based gating effectively
bridges the gap to the upper bound while balancing plasticity
and stability. This demonstrates that a gating mechanism built
on frozen feature anchors can achieve near-optimal head selec-
tion in task-agnostic inference scenarios.

4.2.2. Feature Fusion
Table 4 highlights the complementary roles of M2D and BEATS
in CL. The M2D-only model shows strong plasticity with good
stability, whereas training BEATS alone yields very high sta-
bility, with mSI reaching 0.961, but almost loses plasticity as
mPI drops to 0.587. When the two branches are fused, differ-
ent freezing strategies reveal a clear trade-off. Keeping M2D
trainable and BEATS frozen gives the best balance, with mPSI
reaching 0.903 and mSRCC improving to 0.788. In contrast,
when BEATS is trainable and M2D is frozen, stability further
increases to mSI 0.976 but plasticity decreases to mPI 0.788,
leading to a worse overall balance. This is not because M2D is
more stable, but because BEATS adapts poorly to new tasks: its
limited parameter updates hinder learning new knowledge while
preserving old representations. These results indicate that using
M2D as the plasticity branch and frozen BEATS as a stability
anchor is the most effective design for CL.
Because single-branch architectures do not provide a
frozen, task-invariant feature extractor, prototype drift occurs
across tasks and renders the gating mechanism ineffective—this
is an inherent limitation of single-branch designs. Accordingly,
for a fair comparison, all methods in Table 4 are evaluated using
the Task-Specific Head inference strategy.

5. Conclusions
In this paper, we propose the first continual learning framework
for audio quality assessment that can handle a stream of AQA
tasks without suffering from catastrophic forgetting. Extensive
experiments on diverse speech and music datasets with various
distortion types demonstrate the effectiveness and robustness of
the proposed method. In future work, we plan to further inves-
tigate how to better balance the large distribution gap between
speech and music, and how to reduce instability under domain
shifts, especially in scenarios with frequent task switching, in
order to improve overall prediction performance.
