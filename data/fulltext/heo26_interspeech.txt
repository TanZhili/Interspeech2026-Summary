Leveraging Diarization Labels for Robust Score Calibration in T arget Speaker T agging
via Gaussian Mixture Modeling
Hee-Soo Heo, Minjae Lee, Y oungki Kwon, Han-Gyu Kim, Bong-Jin Lee
NA VER Cloud Corporation, South Korea
heesoo.heo@navercorp.com
Abstract
T arget speaker tagging (TST) diarizes multi-speaker audio and assigns
enrolled speaker identities to the resulting segments. However,
the short duration of many diarized segments leads to unreliable
verification scores with high variance. While aggregating scores
across co-labeled segments within a session offers a potential remedy,
naive averaging is inherently fragile under erroneous diarization
labels. W e propose a calibration method based on a two-component
Gaussian mixture model (GMM) fitted to the score distribution within
each diarization cluster. One component captures correctly clustered
segments and the other absorbs misclustered ones. Each segment
is assigned the mean of its most probable component, enabling robust
aggregation that is resilient to diarization errors. Experiments on
TST -Bench and the ICSI Meeting Corpus demonstrate consistent
improvements over uncalibrated scoring and conventional baselines
across multiple operating points and embedding architectures.

1. Introduction
Speaker recognition encompasses a family of closely related
tasks, including speaker verification, speaker identification, and
speaker diarization [1, 2]. Although these tasks share fundamental
technologies such as speaker embeddings [3, 4, 5, 6, 7], each
addresses a distinct objective. Speaker verification determines
whether two utterances originate from the same individual, speaker
identification classifies an utterance into one of a closed set of
known speakers, and speaker diarization partitions a recording into
homogeneous segments labeled with anonymous cluster indices.
Recently,target speaker tagging(TST) was formally introduced
as a composite task that integrates speaker diarization and open-set
speaker identification into a unified pipeline [8]. In TST , the system
processes a long, multi-speaker audio recording, segments it into
single-speaker regions, and assigns actual speaker identities to
segments spoken by pre-enrolled target speakers, while labeling the
rest as “non-target. ” This task directly supports real-world applications
such as meeting transcription and annotation, where knowingwho
saidwhatandwhenis essential.
Importantly, TST is not a mere concatenation of independently
developed diarization and identification modules; combining
these tasks introduces unique characteristics that must be carefully
addressed. First, unlike conventional speaker recognition, which
typically operates on pre-segmented utterances of controlled duration,
TST must handle real conversational speech where speaker turns vary
widely in length—from brief interjections of barely one second to
extended monologues spanning tens of seconds. This variability in
segment duration directly translates to variability in embedding quality
and verification score reliability [9, 10], making the identification
stage inherently less stable than in traditional pre-segmented
settings. Second, the session-level nature of TST provides a unique
compensatory opportunity absent in standard speaker recognition:
since diarization assigns a shared label to all segments believed to
originate from the same speaker, information can be aggregated across
co-labeled segments within a session to mitigate the unreliability of
any individual segment’s score. These two characteristics—unstable
scores arising from uncontrolled segmentation and the availability
of session-level diarization structure for compensation—together
motivate the score calibration approach proposed in this work.
While straightforward aggregation strategies—such as averaging
scores across co-labeled segments—can leverage this structure,
they are inherently vulnerable to diarization errors that mix scores
from different speakers within the same label. T o address this, we
propose a score calibration method based on a two-component
Gaussian mixture model (GMM). For a given segment, we collect the
verification scores of all segments sharing its diarization label and fit
a 2-component GMM to this score distribution. The key insight is that
one component captures the scores of segments genuinely belonging
to the same speaker, while the other absorbs the scores of segments
that were misclustered by the diarization system. By assigning each
segment the mean of its most probable component, we achieve robust
score calibration that effectively utilizes session-level information
while remaining resilient to diarization errors.
The main contributions of this paper are:
• W e analyze the challenge of unreliable segment-level verification
scores in target speaker tagging and demonstrate that naive score
aggregation methods are insufficient.
• W e propose a novel GMM-based score calibration method that lever-
ages diarization labels to aggregate session-level evidence while ex-
plicitly accounting for diarization errors through mixture modeling.
• W e conduct comprehensive experiments showing that the proposed
method significantly outperforms uncalibrated scoring and
conventional aggregation baselines, validating its effectiveness
for real-world target speaker tagging.
2. T arget Speaker T agging
T arget speaker tagging (TST) was recently formalized as a distinct
speaker recognition task that integrates speaker diarization and
open-set speaker identification into a unified pipeline [8]. Unlike
standard diarization, which only assigns anonymous labels within a
single session, TST requires assigning globally meaningful identities
drawn from a set of enrolled speakers across multiple sessions. While
speaker diarization [7, 11] and speaker identification [2, 12, 13] have
been extensively studied as independent problems, the formalization
of their joint operation as TST highlights challenges that neither task
addresses in isolation.
In [8], several unique challenges of TST are identified. Diarization
errors have asymmetric effects on downstream tagging: under-
clustering, where different speakers are merged into a single cluster,

3. Framework
Following the framework defined in [8], target speaker tagging oper-
ates in two phases: enrollment and tagging. During enrollment, each
target speaker provides one or more reference utterances. Speaker em-
beddings are extracted from each utterance and averaged to produce a
single representative embedding per speaker, forming a gallery setG=
{(ek,idk)}N

identity of thek-th enrolled speaker. The tagging phase consists of two
sequential steps: speaker diarization, which partitions the input record-

identification, which assigns identities to the resulting segments.
3.1. Speaker Diarization
The tagging phase begins with speaker diarization. The input audio is
first processed by a voice activity detector to identify speech regions.
Speaker embeddings are then extracted from short sliding windows
within these regions and clustered to group segments belonging to
the same speaker. Each resulting segmentdi is assigned a diarization
labelLi∈{1,...,M}, whereM is the estimated number of speakers.

4.1. Motivation
The setSLi contains verification scores of segments that the diariza-
tion system believes belong to the same speaker. If the diarization were
perfect, all scores inSLi would reflect the same underlying speaker
identity, and simple averaging would be optimal. In practice, however,
diarization errors—particularly under-clustering—introduce scores
from different speakers intoSLi. This creates aheterogeneousscore
distribution that cannot be faithfully summarized by a single statistic.
Our key observation is that when diarization errors are present,
SLi typically exhibits a bimodal structure: a cluster of higher scores
from segments that are genuinely from the target speaker, and a
cluster of lower (or differently distributed) scores from misclustered
segments. A two-component GMM is a natural and parsimonious
model for capturing this structure.
Figure 1 illustrates this phenomenon on a representative diarization
label from TST -Bench, where an under-clustering error has merged
segments from two different speakers into a single label. The
correctly clustered segments (blue) concentrate at high scores, while
the misclustered segments (red) form a separate low-score cluster.
The fitted two-component GMM cleanly recovers this bimodal
structure without access to ground-truth labels, enabling the proposed
calibration to avoid the score distortion caused by naive averaging.

4.5. Interpretation and Advantages
The two-component GMM provides a principled mechanism for
separating correctly clustered segments from misclustered ones within
SLi. Its advantages over the baseline aggregation strategies can be
summarized as follows:
• Robustness to diarization errors.When under-clustering mixes
segments from different speakers intoS Li, the GMM isolates
the correct group in one component, so thatˆsi =µ c∗ aggregates
reliable evidence while discarding misclustered scores—avoiding
the distortion that cripples naive label-level averaging.
•V ariance reduction.Replacing each unreliable segment-level
score with its component mean suppresses the high variance of
short-segment scores while still exploiting session-level evidence.
• Graceful degradation.Absent diarization errors, the components
converge or one dominates, soµc∗ approximates the overall mean
and the method reduces smoothly to simple averaging.
5. Experimental Setup
5.1. Dataset
W e use two datasets for evaluation: TST -Bench [8] as the primary
benchmark and the ICSI Meeting Corpus [16] for supplementary
validation on real data.
TST -Benchis a large-scale synthetic benchmark specifically
designed for TST evaluation [8]. It comprises 300 multi-speaker
sessions (20–60 minutes each,∼200 hours total) synthesized from
single-speaker recordings in the Multilingual LibriSpeech (MLS)
corpus [17]. The speaker pool consists of approximately 350 speakers,
partitioned into 150 enrolled and 200 unknown speakers. Each session
contains 8–30 speakers, with each enrolled speaker appearing in 10–30
sessions. Perfect ground-truth annotations with global speaker labels
are available by construction. Its evaluation set contains over 204k
segments (average duration 2.80 s, range 1–23 s), providing a large
and controlled testbed for systematic evaluation of score calibration.
ICSI Meeting Corpusprovides real meeting recordings with
consistent speaker identities across sessions. Following the protocol
in [8], speakers appearing in at least two sessions are designated
as target speakers, with enrollment segments selected from one
session. Evaluation segments are extracted excluding enrollment
utterances and overlapping speech. The resulting evaluation set
comprises approximately 52k segments from 15 enrolled speakers
(average duration 3.07 s, range 1–100 s), complementing TST -Bench
by validating our findings on real conversational data.
5.2. System Configuration
Speaker diarization.W e employ a clustering-based diarization
pipeline built on a high-resolution embedding extractor (HEE) [18]
trained on V oxCeleb1 and V oxCeleb2 [19, 20, 21]. The extracted
embeddings are enhanced with dimensionality reduction and
attention-based aggregation, and then clustered using spectral
clustering [22, 23, 24, 25] to produce diarization labels.
Speaker embedding extractor .For open-set speaker identification,
we primarily use an ECAP A-TDNN model [26, 27, 28] trained on
the V oxCeleb1 and V oxCeleb2 corpora [19, 20]. T o evaluate gen-
eralization across architectures, we additionally employ ResNet34 and
ResNet293 models [29, 30] publicly available from the W eSpeaker
toolkit [31], also trained on V oxCeleb1 and V oxCeleb2. Similarity
scores are computed using cosine similarity followed by adaptive sym-
metric score normalization (AS-Norm) [32]. The cohort set for score
normalization consists of utterances from 2,000 speakers randomly
selected from V oxBlink2 [33, 34], with an adaptation size of 20.
GMM fitting.The 2-component GMM is fitted per diarization
label per session using the EM algorithm. A minimum of 5 segments
per label is required for GMM fitting; for labels with fewer segments,
the original scores are retained without calibration.
5.3. Evaluation Metrics
Following the evaluation protocol in [8, 34, 35], we employ two
metrics:
• False Alarm Rate (F AR):The proportion of non-target segments
incorrectly accepted as one of the enrolled speakers.
• Detection and Identification Rate (DIR):The proportion of target
speaker segments correctly identified with the correct identity.
DIR and F AR exist in an inherent trade-off controlled by the thresh-
oldθ. W e report DIR at a fixed F AR operating point (DIR@F AR),
which enables fair comparison across methods [34]. W e evaluate
at four operating points—DIR@F AR=0.5%, 1%, 5%, and 10%—to
characterize performance from strict to relaxed operating regimes.

Effect of the number of GMM components.T able 3 reports the
effect of varying the number of mixture components on TST -Bench.
With a single component (C=1), no bimodal separation occurs and
the calibration effect is minimal. Increasing to two components
yields a substantial jump at F AR=0.5% and F AR=1%, confirming
that the bimodal separation of correctly clustered and misclustered
scores is the primary driver of the improvement. With three or
four components, marginal gains appear at relaxed operating points
(F AR=5% and 10%), but performance at strict F AR does not
improve further and can even slightly degrade. Given that additional
components increase the number of EM parameters to estimate from
a limited number of per-label scores, two components offer the best
trade-off between modeling capacity and estimation reliability.
Channel- and device-induced multimodality .Beyond diarization
errors, multimodality in the per-label score distribution may also arise
from channel or device variation—for instance, when a target speaker
is recorded with different microphones across or within sessions. In
such cases, the fitted components may capture channel-induced modes
rather than purely speaker-induced ones. Even so, the calibrated
score corresponds to the dominant component containing the segment,
which remains more stable than the raw score of a short segment, so
the mixture formulation degrades gracefully rather than failing outright.
A more explicit treatment of channel-induced multimodality—e.g.,
conditioning the mixture on channel factors—is left for future work.
