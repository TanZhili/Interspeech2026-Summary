Steps toward a wearable-informed model of real-world listening effort and 
fatigue among adults with hearing loss

1 National Acoustic Laboratories, Sydney, New South Wales, Australia

Abstract 
Listening-associated fatigue is common in hearing loss, even 
with advanced hearing aids, and likely arises from cumulative 
listening effort. Current hearing -aid algorithms adapt to 
acoustics but not to users' moment -to-moment experience. We 
tested whether consumer wearables can capture listening effort 
and fatigue in daily life. Forty -six adults with hearing loss (23 
hearing-aid users, 23 non -users) completed a 7 -10 day field 
study using Apple Watch -based ecological momentary 
assessments (EMAs), passive heart-rate and sound monitoring, 
and daily phone surveys. Participants engaged well: without 
prompts, they logged check -ins comparable to prior phone -
based EMA studies. Using wearable -informed features, we 
classified high listening effort and high fatigue with accuracies 
of 68% and 76%. Wearable -informed measures of effort and 
fatigue appear feasible in everyday contexts and may support 
future real-time adaptive hearing technology and longitudinal 
monitoring. 
Index Terms : ecological momentary assessment, listening 
effort, fatigue, wearable sensing, hearing loss 
1. Introduction 
Beyond speech perception and spoken -language 
comprehension, listening effort and listening -related fatigue 
are critical concerns for individuals with hearing loss because 
they affect daily communication and overall quality of life. 
Despite advances in hear ing technology, hearing -aid users 
often report greater listening effort, particularly in noisy 
environments, and mental fatigue after prolonged listening. 
Identifying a robust, non -invasive measure of listening effort 
and cumulative fatigue in real-world settings could enable user-
state aware sound processing in hearing aids, improving 
outcomes without requiring manual adjustments. 
Listening effort is multifaceted and can involve cognitive, 
physical, and emotional components. Prior studies suggest that 
increased listening effort in hearing impairment is associated 
with elevated cognitive load [1], [2] . To compensate for 
degraded auditory input, listeners may allocate additional 
cognitive resources to speech understanding, leaving fewer 
resources for other tasks and contributing to fatigue over time 
[3]. Physiological measures such as heart rate variability (HRV) 
and electrodermal activity (EDA) have been investigated as 
potential indicators of listening effort in both normal -hearing 
and hearing -impaired listeners. HRV has been linked to 
cognitive load a nd stress, whereas EDA reflects sympathetic 
nervous system activity that often increases with mental effort 
[4], [5]. 
The acoustic environment also plays a major role in 
listening effort. Background noise, reverberation, and complex 
auditory scenes can substantially increase the demands of 
speech understanding for people with hearing loss, motivating 
advanced hearing -aid processing strategies [6]. Directional 
processing and beamforming, which aim to enhance target 
signals while attenuating competing sounds, can reduce 
listening effort in some conditions [7]. However, real -time 
adaptation of such processing based not only on acoustics but 
also on a user’s moment -to-moment cognitive or fatigue state 
remains an open challenge. 
Despite substantial prior work, relatively few studies 
integrate intrinsic physiological signals with objective acoustic 
context to derive a comprehensive, ecologically valid measure 
of listening effort and related fatigue. Many existing approaches 
rely on subjective reports alone or laboratory -based measures 
that may not capture the variability and complexity of everyday 
listening. Here, we address this gap by combining wearable 
sensing with ecological momentary assessment (EMA) to 
collect continuous physiological measures, contextual acoustic 
information, and self-reported effort and fatigue in daily life. 
2. Methods 
2.1. Participants 
Forty-six adults with clinically diagnosed sensorineural 
hearing loss participated. The sample comprised 23 bilateral 
hearing-aid users (>= 6 months of experience; 24 -76 years; 
mean 61; 14 female) and 23 non -users of hearing devices (35-
83 years; mean 62; 10 female), recruited from our participant 
databases. Bilateral four -frequency average hearing loss 
(4FAHL; 0.5, 1, 2, and 4 kHz) ranged from mild to moderate 
in both groups (hearing-aid users: 32-59 dB HL; non-users: 26-
49 dB HL). A Wilcoxon rank -sum test indicated significantly 
higher 4FAHL in hearing-aid users than non-users (W = 78.5, 
p < .001, r = 0.603). Hearing loss was relatively symmetrical, 
with interaural 4FA differences <= 9 dB HL. Hearing-aid users 
wore their own devices with their everyday settings; non-users 
did not use hearing devices. Individual and group -averaged 
audiograms (left and right ears) for hearing-aid users and non-
users are shown in Figure 1. All participants provided written 
informed consent under approval from the Human Research 
Ethics Committee and were remunerated for their time. 
2.2. Experimental procedure 
At an onsite visit, participants completed the Revised Hearing 
Handicap Inventory - Screening Version (RHHI -S) [8]. All 
participants scored > 8, indicating self -perceived hearing 
difficulty, and proceeded to a 7 -10 day field study. Before the

2.3. Data collection 
A custom Apple Watch -based ecological momentary 
assessment (EMA) app was used to collect self -reports via 
rapid on -screen check -ins while simultaneously logging 
acoustic descriptors of the current listening environment. As 
illustrated in Figure 2, each check-in required only a few taps 
on the watch screen. 
 
Figure 2: Screenshots of the watch -based EMA interface in 
chronological order. Check -in questions were completed via a 
small number of screen taps. 
At the start of each check -in, the system captured a 30 s 
window of acoustic features (sampling rate: 22,050 Hz; frame 
length: 23 ms). To protect participant privacy, no raw audio 
recordings were stored. Extracted acoustic features included 
sound level (A -weighted and unweighted), zero -crossing rate, 
power spectrum measures, pitch-related features during voiced 
speech (cepstrum -based), spectral flatness, entropy, and 
reverberation-related metrics. 
Morning and evening surveys were delivered via another 
iPhone app that scheduled prompts by time of day. The morning 
survey captured sleep -related self -report measures (e.g., 
perceived sleep quality, sleep duration, and nocturnal 
awakenings). The evening s urvey assessed retrospective 
listening experiences across the day, including the number of 
difficult listening situations, time spent in those situations, the 
most difficult situation encountered, hearing -aid use (if 
applicable), and overall fatigue. 
3. Results 
3.1. Acceptability and effectiveness of the watch-based 
EMA 
We hypothesised that an ultra -brief EMA delivered on a 
consumer wearable would be acceptable to participants and 
would capture effortful listening situations in daily life at least 
as well as conventional phone -based EMA. In our previous 
study, participants received three scheduled prompts per day on 
an iPhone and were encouraged to self -initiate additional 
check-ins when they encountered difficult listening situations. 
In contrast, the present study imposed no minimum number of 
Apple Watch check -ins; parti cipants were instructed only to 
log a check -in whenever they experienced hearing challenges 
in their everyday environment.

participants with higher completion rates contribute more to the 
density estimate). The mean number of entries per day is also 
shown for each study. On average, participants completed 
approximately three check-ins/surveys per day in both studies.

Despite the absence of scheduled prompts or minimum 
requirements, the watch -based EMA achieved a comparable 
number of daily entries to the prompted phone -based protocol. 
The watch-based entries also clustered around midday and early 
afternoon, consistent with participants logging check-ins during 
naturally occurring listening demands rather than in response to 
fixed-time prompts. 
3.2. Predict listening effort and fatigue 
3.2.1. Predict listening effort 
Listening effort was assessed from the watch check -in using a 
5-point Likert scale (Very Low, Low, Moderate, High, Very 
High). Nearly  half of the ratings were Moderate (46%), 
whereas the extremes were less frequent (Very Low: 6%; Very 
High: 10%). Across check -ins, the acoustic context indicated 
that 50% of entries occurred in an A -weighted sound -level 
range of approximately 57-67 dB(A), with a mean of 61 dB(A). 
Based on  prior work, we pre -selected a set of predictor 
variables including: (i) heart rate (HR; bpm), (ii) loudness at 1 
kHz (dB above threshold) [11], (iii) heart-rate variability (HRV; 
ms), and (iv) engineered terms (HR -to-loudness ratio, squared

HRV, and interaction terms among HR, HRV, and loudness). 
Linear and non -linear models showed limited performance 
when predicting the full 5 -point effort rating. We therefore 
reformulated the task as a binary classification problem: High  
effort (High or Very High) versus Not  high effort (Very Low, 
Low, or Moderate). 
Under a 70/30 train/test split , a Random Under -Sampling 
and Boosting (RUSBoost) classifier  [12], using HR, HRV, 
loudness, and engineered terms (ratio, squared, and interaction 
features), performed substantially above chance. The model 
achieved high recall (92.6%), indicating strong sensitivity to 
moments of elevated listening effort, and precision of 77.9%, 
resulting in an F1 score of 84.6%. Receiver operating 
characteristic area under the curve (ROC AUC) improved after 
probability calibration, suggesting improved ranking and 
reliability of the predicted probabilities. 
Generalisability was evaluated using leave-one-participant-
out cross-validation (LOPOCV). Participants with insufficient 
within-participant variability in reported effort (e.g., repeatedly 
selecting the same effort level across contexts) were excluded 
from this analysis. Across individuals, F1 scores ranged from 
33.33% to 95.24%; 13 of 25 qualified participants achieved F1 
> 70%. Recall reached 100% for 15 participants, indicating that 
the model frequently detected high -effort moments, although 
this was som etimes accompanied by reduced precision due to 
false positives. Overall, the model tended to favour sensitivity, 
detecting most high -effort instances at the expense of 
occasionally misclassifying not-high instances. For all but two 
participants, performance exceeded the chance baseline. 
3.2.2. Predict fatigue 
Daily fatigue was assessed in the evening survey using the 
item: “Overall, how fatigued did you feel today?”, rated on an 
11-point scale from 0 (not fatigued) to 10 (very fatigued). Self-
reported sleep was collected in the morning survey with the 
question “How well did you sleep last night?”, rated from 0 
(very poorly) to 10 (very well). Self-reported sleep scores were 
entirely missing for one participant. For fatigue, four 
participants showed little within-participant variation, whereas 
others exhibited su bstantial variability. Mean fatigue levels 
also varied widely across participants, ranging from 
approximately 0 to 8. In contrast, most self -reported sleep 
scores were above 6, with only two participants reporting low 
mean sleep scores around 2.5. 
An objective sleep measure was derived from Apple Watch 
data as the total duration of rapid eye movement (REM) sleep 
plus deep sleep. For three participants, this objective measure 
diverged from their self -reported sleep scores (e.g., relatively 
short REM+deep duration but high self-reported sleep quality). 
As with listening effort, acceptable predictive performance 
was achieved only after binarising fatigue ratings for 
classification: not fatigued (< 6) versus fatigued (>= 6). This 
yielded 72 and 73 observations per class (approximately 
balanced). Unlike effort prediction, fatigue was modelled using 
day-level predictors aggregated over each day (06:00 –22:00) 
and across all watch-based EMA check-ins that day, including 
sleep (subjective sleep score; REM+deep duration), physiology 
(max heart rate; mean HRV), ac oustic exposure (max daytime 
sound level; mean EMA -derived acoustic descriptors), daily 
self-reports of difficult listening, and baseline questionnaires 
(RHHI-S, SSQ, VFS). Days without any watch -based EMA 
check-ins or phone-based surveys were excluded fro m fatigue 
modelling. Model performance was evaluated using overall 
accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, confusion matrices, and ROC 
AUC. Under a 70/30 split, Random Forest [13] discriminated 
well between fatigued and not-fatigued days, achieving 76.2% 
accuracy, with sensitivity approximately 76.2% and specificity 
at a similar level. Accuracy was significantly above chance (p 
= 0.00047), supporting the utility of wearable-informed fatigue 
classification. Feature importance (mean decrease in accuracy) 
indicated that the five most influential pre dictors were: (1) 
mean A -weighted sound level during watch check -ins, (2) 
maximum daytime heart rate, (3) prior -night REM+deep sleep 
duration from Apple Watch, (4) mean rate of change in spectral-
band measures, and (5) the self -reported number of difficult  
listening situations experienced during the day. 
To assess generalisability at the individual level, we applied 
LOPOCV using the same Random Forest model. The classifier 
achieved mean accuracy 63.5%, with mean sensitivity 63.2% 
and mean specificity 69.1%. Median accuracy was 67%, 
indicating that performance was reasonably robust for many 
participants. Some participants were excluded from sensitivity 
(n = 8) and specificity (n = 7) summa ries because the held-out 
test set contained only one class label, preventing those metrics 
from being computed. 
3.3. Comparisons between aided and unaided group 
We examined potential differences between hearing -aid (HA) 
users and non-users. An independent-samples t-test compared 
self-reported daily fatigue between non-users (M = 4.41, SD = 
2.34) and HA users (M = 5.55, SD = 1.76). The difference did 
not reach statistical significance (t(43) = -1.85, p = 0.071). 
Additional between-group comparisons were conducted for 
self-reported sleep quality, Apple Watch –derived sleep 
duration, heart-rate variability, and acoustic features recorded 
during watch -based check -ins (A -weighted sound level, 
entropy, spectral flatness, cepstral features, zero -crossing rate, 
and reverb eration). No significant group differences were 
observed for these measures. The difference in A -weighted 
sound level during check-ins approached significance (Welch’s 
t(45.3) = 1.86, p = 0.069), motivating an exploratory analysis 
of sound level as a function of rated listening effort (Figure 4).

4. Discussion 
Listening effort and listening -related fatigue are critical 
concerns for individuals with hearing loss because they directly 
affect daily communication and quality of life. Even with 
advanced hearing aids, many users report elevated effort in 
noisy environ ments and mental fatigue after prolonged 
listening. Building on prior work, this study combined intrinsic 
physiological signals with objective acoustic descriptors to 
investigate wearable -informed metrics of listening effort and 
fatigue in everyday life. U nlike laboratory paradigms, our 
approach focused on naturally occurring listening experiences 
during a ~10-day field study. We used an Apple Watch –based 
EMA system to collect rapid self -initiated check -ins with 
concurrent acoustic feature extraction, along side passive 
physiological monitoring. In parallel, time -of-day–scheduled 
phone surveys captured morning sleep reports and end -of-day 
fatigue ratings.  
A primary aim was to evaluate whether watch-based, ultra-
brief EMA supports engagement and captures effortful listening 
episodes without fixed prompting. Compared with an earlier 
phone-based study in which participants received three 
scheduled prompts per day, the present protocol did not impose 
minimum check -in requirements and relied entirely on self -
initiation. Despite this, participants completed a comparable 
number of daily entries on average. The fact that all entries in 
the current study were self -initiated suggests that the watch 
interface was sufficiently accessible and convenient to support 
engagement without external reminders. Moreover, the time-of-
day distribution differed between protocols: watch -based 
check-ins clustered around early afternoon , whereas phone -
based entries aligned more closely with scheduled prompt times 
and extended later into the day. This pattern is consistent with 
the wearable system capturing listening challenges as they 
occur, rather than reflecting the timing of notificat ions. 
However, because many participants had prior research 
participation experience, some may have inferred an 
expectation to complete multiple check -ins per day, which 
could partially explain the uniform daily counts observed for 
some participants (e.g., exactly three entries per day). 
Predicting moment-to-moment perceived listening effort in 
daily life remains challenging, particularly when using 
consumer-grade devices and without controlled manipulation 
of acoustic scenes. Models performed poorly when attempting 
to predict the full 5 -point effort scale, even with targeted 
physiological and acoustic predictors and engineered features. 
However, performance improved substantially when effort 
ratings were binarised (High/Very High vs. other ratings). 
Under this formulation, the classifier a chieved high sensitivity 
to elevated effort, supported by features including heart rate, 
heart-rate variability, and loudness -related measures. Leave -
one-participant-out evaluation suggested that the model 
generalised reasonably well for many participants,  although 
performance varied widely. In particular, precision was reduced 
for some individuals, indicating that false positives were 
common when high -effort states were rare or inconsistently 
reported. 
We hypothesised that daily fatigue might be more 
predictable than momentary effort because fatigue reflects 
longer-term accumulation and may be better indexed by 
aggregated physiological and contextual measures. In practice, 
fatigue modelling was constrain ed by the data structure: only 
one fatigue rating was collected per day, yielding 145 labelled 
observations with corresponding sleep measures. Predictive 
performance for the continuous fatigue rating was limited, but 
binarising fatigue status (fatigued vs.  not fatigued) yielded a 
Random Forest classifier with balanced sensitivity and 
specificity (~76.2%) and accuracy of 76.2%, significantly 
above chance, with ROC AUC close to 0.8. Cross -validated 
performance at the participant level was more modest but 
suggested that fatigue classification can benefit from combining 
environmental and physiological signals. The most informative 
predictors included acoustic exposure during watch check -ins 
(mean A -weighted sound level and spectral variability 
measures), maximum daytime heart rate, and prior -night sleep 
(REM+deep duration). These findings align with the view that 
fatigue is influenced by both daily activity/physiological load 
and the listening environments encountered, with sleep 
providing an important baseline for next-day fatigue. 
Several limitations should be considered. Missingness 
reduced the usable dataset (approximately 32% of expected 
data were missing), which increased modelling difficulty and 
contributed to variability in participant -level estimates. This 
missingness largely reflected days with incomplete daily inputs 
(missing morning/evening surveys, no watch check -ins, or 
missing Apple Watch sleep data), because fatigue features 
required all components to form a complete day -level record. 
In addition, some cross -validation folds contained only one 
class label for a given participant, preventing calculation of 
sensitivity or specificity and limiting interpretability of 
individual-level performance. More broadly, consumer 
wearables impose signal constraints relative to laborat ory 
systems. The Apple Watch SE (2nd generation) provides 
limited control over sampling and access to certain signals, 
including coarse environmental sound -level sampling, lack of 
continuous ECG -grade cardiac measures, limited HRV 
sampling control, lack of  blood oxygen saturation data access, 
and wrist-level (rather than ear-level) acoustic measurement. 
Finally, group comparisons between hearing -aid users and 
non-users revealed no statistically significant differences in 
self-reported fatigue or acoustic measures during difficult -
listening check -ins. Nevertheless, we observed a consistent 
trend whereby hearing-aid users tended to report similar effort
