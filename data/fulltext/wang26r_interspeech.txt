Abstract
Speech conveys not only linguistic information but also critical
cues to speaker identity. Previous studies have identified
bilateral superior temporal cortex (STG/STS) as central to
human voice perception. However, speaker identity, comprising
multiple-layer information, has largely been treated as a unitary
construct. The present fMRI study investigated whether distinct
speaker traits, such as gender, age, and accent, are processed
by shared or dissociable neural mechanisms. Results revealed a
common voice-processing core centered on bilateral STG/STS,
which was engaged across all conditions. However, neural
responses within this core were not uniform, with accent
processing eliciting stronger activation than gender and age.
Beyond this shared region, each trait recruited partially distinct
cortical regions. These findings revealed a “core-plus-extension”
model of speaker identification, in which shared auditory
mechanisms are complemented by trait-specific cortical regions.
Index Terms: speaker identification, speaker traits, voice
perception, fMRI
1. Introduction
Speech signals convey rich information, encompassing not only
linguistic content but also paralinguistic features such as speaker
identity [1]. Accurate perception of these speaker-specific cues
is critical for successful social interaction, enabling individuals
to recognize others, infer social attributes, and adapt their
own speech and behavior accordingly. Previous functional
magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) studies using passive
listening paradigms that contrasted vocal and non-vocal sounds
have demonstrated that the bilateral upper bank of the superior
temporal cortex, particularly the mid and anterior portions of
the superior temporal cortex (STG/STS), responds selectively to
human voices [2, 3]. These regions, collectively known as the
“temporal voice areas” (TV As), play a central role in processing
vocal sounds and supporting speaker recognition.
One important research gap is that most prior work has
treated speaker identity as a unitary construct, assuming it is
processed homogeneously within the TV As [4, 5]. However,
speaker identity is inherently multidimensional, comprising
separable attributes such as gender, age, and regional accent.
These attributes provide partially independent sources of
information that differentiate individuals. As a result, the neural
mechanisms supporting the processing of specific speaker traits
remain largely under-explored. This limitation is important
because the perception of individual speaker characteristics not
only contributes to identity recognition but also shapes language
comprehension processes. Mismatches between sentence

Given their importance in communication, the present
study aims to reveal how distinct speaker traits are encoded
in the brain. Since these traits are conveyed through partially
dissociable acoustic and phonological cues, it is less likely
that different speaker traits are processed by the identical
mechanisms. For instance, gender is strongly associated with
fundamental frequency (F0) and formant frequencies, with males
generally exhibiting lower F0 and more closely spaced formants
than females [10, 11, 12]. Age is linked to changes in temporal
and spectral features reflecting vocal tract maturation, with
durations, F0, and formant frequencies gradually decreasing
throughout development [ 13]. In contrast, accent is defined
by systematic phonological deviations and prosodic patterns
[14], including differences in vowel quality, consonant
articulation, stress placement, and intonation [ 15]. While
acoustic features such as F0 and formants are predominantly
processed by bilateral STG/STS [16], phonological and prosodic
cues engage widespread fronto-temporal areas, including
inferior frontal gyrus (IFG), middle and superior frontal gyri
(MFG/SFG), sylvian-parietal-temporal region (area Spt) and
superior parietal lobule (SPL) [ 17]. The partial dissociation
of these vocal attributes implies that the neural representation
of speaker identity is unlikely uniform, with different traits
potentially engaging both a shared auditory processing network
and trait-specific cortical regions specialized for particular
features. Thus, previous studies which treat the speaker
identity as a monolithic entity may overlook the complex and
multidimensional nature of neural encoding of individual speaker
traits.
Understanding how the brain encodes these distinct speaker
traits is critical not only for developing a comprehensive model
of voice perception, but also for revealing how brain efficiently
extracts paralinguistic information in daily communication. To
address this question, the present study employed fMRI to record
the brain activities while participants listened to pseudowords
produced by speakers varying in gender, age, and accent. By
analyzing neural responses to each speaker trait, we aimed to
identify brain regions that support the encoding of these distinct
speaker characteristics, thereby providing a detailed map of how

2. Materials and methods
2.1. Participants
Thirty-four native Mandarin adults (Age: 24.09 ± 2.45 years
old, 18 females) were recruited for this study. All participants
are right-handed, as evaluated by the Edinburgh handedness
inventory [18]. All subjects have self-reported normal hearing,
normal or corrected-to-normal vision, and no neurological
impairment or speech disorders. All participants signed written
informed consent forms before the experiment. The study was
approved by the Human Subjects Ethics Sub-Committee of The
Hong Kong Polytechnic University.
2.2. Stimuli
To minimize the interference from the automatic linguistic
processing, we chose pseudo-English words as stimuli. A total
of 96 pseudo-English words (e.g., sparnick) were generated
using ChatGPT-4. These words were evenly distributed across
three speaker traits (32 words each). Auditory stimuli were
synthesized usingText to Speech OpenAI.
The gender condition employed two adult male and two
adult female speakers, with each speaker producing eight
pseudo-words. The age condition included voices from two
children (one boy and one girl) and two adults (one male and one
female), with each producing eight pseudo-words. The accent
condition featured two varieties of English: British English and
Indian English, each with one male and one female speaker.
Additionally, two pure-tone stimuli, one level and one rising
(mean F0 = 196 Hz; 570 ms) were synthesized in Praat [ 19]
for the tone identification task (the baseline task). All auditory
stimuli were normalized to 70 dB to ensure consistent loudness
across trials.
2.3. Experimental design
The experiment followed a blocked design controlled by
E-Prime, consisting of four task conditions: gender, age, accent,
and tone. Four conditions were randomized across subjects and
divided into two functional runs, with 16 trials per condition
in each run. Prior to each block, participants were presented
with an instruction indicating the specific task for that block.
Task blocks were separated by four to six silent trials, during
which only a visual asterisk was displayed on the screen and no
auditory stimuli were presented.
Figure 1:The illustration of experimental procedure.
The trial procedure is illustrated in Figure 1. Each active trial
consisted of a 500-ms fixation, followed by a 3000 ms period
during which the auditory stimulus was presented binaurally via
MRI-compatible headphones and a visual prompt appeared on
the screen showing two response options (e.g., “Male / Female”
for the gender condition). Participants need to indicate their
choice by pressing a left or right button within this 3000 ms
response window.
2.4. MRI Data Acquisition
MRI data were acquired on a 3T Siemens Magnetom Prisma
scanner using a 32-channel head coil. High-resolution
anatomical T1-weighted (T1w) MPRAGE images were collected
first (voxel size = 1 × 1.15 × 1.15 mm 3, TR = 2500 ms,
TE = 2.16 ms, FOV = 220 × 220 mm 2). Subsequently, two
runs of functional images were acquired using a multiband
T2*-weighted echo-planar imaging sequence (TR = 1000 ms,
TE = 30 ms, voxel size = 3 × 3 × 3.5 mm 3, FOV = 240 × 240
mm2; multiband acceleration factor = 4).
2.5. MRI Data Preprocessing
Structural and functional MRI data were preprocessed using
DeepPrep (v25.1.0) [ 20], a BIDS-compliant standardized
pipeline. Anatomical T1w images underwent motion correction
and bias field correction, followed by skull stripping and
tissue segmentation into gray matter, white matter, and
cerebrospinal fluid. Cortical surfaces were subsequently
reconstructed and aligned to the fsaverage6 standard surface
template, and anatomical images were spatially normalized
to Montreal Neurological Institute (MNI) space using the
MNI152NLin2009cAsym:res-2 template.
Functional MRI data from each blood-oxygen-level-
dependent (BOLD) run were corrected for head motion
and slice-timing differences. Nonsteady-state volumes were
identified and flagged for subsequent processing, while
no susceptibility distortion correction was applied. The
BOLD reference image was then co-registered to the
T1w anatomical image using FreeSurfer’s boundary-based
registration (bbregister) with six degrees of freedom. Following
co-registration, the preprocessed BOLD time series were
resampled into both volumetric and surface-based templates in a
single interpolation step, incorporating head motion corrections
and anatomical co-registration transforms.
2.6. Univariate analyses of MRI data
To identify brain regions activated under each task condition,
first-level general linear model (GLM) analyses were conducted
separately for each participant on the surface-based fMRI data.
For each run, regressors corresponding to the experimental
conditions of interest were constructed by convolving event
onsets with the canonical hemodynamic response function and
subsequently standardized across time points. Six head-motion
parameters were also included as nuisance regressors in the
design matrix. At each cortical vertex, the BOLD signal was
standardized across time points and fitted with a linear regression
model using the design matrix. This procedure yielded beta
coefficients for each regressor of interest, representing the
estimated neural response to the corresponding condition, which
were then extracted for subsequent group-level analyses.
For each participant, first-level beta maps were averaged
across the two functional runs. Condition-specific contrast maps
were then computed by contrasting each speaker trait condition
(i.e., gender, age, and accent) against the tone baseline, thereby

isolating neural activity associated with speaker trait processing
beyond basic acoustic features. At the group level, these contrast
maps were analyzed using cluster-based one-sample, one-tailed
permutation tests against zero across participants [ 21], with
10,000 permutations. Vertex adjacency was defined according
to the fsaverage6 surface mesh. Statistical significance was
determined at a cluster-level threshold of p <0.05 , and only
clusters spanning at least 20 vertices were included in the
analysis.
2.7. Statistical analysis
Behavioral data: One-way repeated-measures ANOV As were
conducted on participants’ accuracy rates and response times
(RTs) to assess performance differences across the three speaker
traits. Post-hoc analyses were performed using paired-samples
t-tests, with Bonferroni correction for controlling multiple
comparisons. Effect sizes were calculated using Cohen’sd.
fMRI data: To examine shared neural substrates across
all speaker traits, a conjunction analysis was performed
by identifying overlapping clusters significant across the
three trait-specific contrasts. This intersection defined a
conjunction region of interest (ROI) representing the core areas
engaged in processing all speaker traits. Within this ROI,
a one-way repeated-measures ANOV A was conducted with
speaker trait as the within-subject factor to examine differences
in activation magnitude. Post-hoc analyses were carried out
using paired-samples t-tests with 10,000 permutations to correct
for multiple comparisons.
3. Results
3.1. Behavioral Performance
Participants demonstrated an overall high accuracy rate in
identifying all three speaker traits (Figure 2a). The ANOV A
revealed a significant main effect of speaker trait on accuracy
(F(2,66) = 51.96 , p <0.001 ). Post-hoc analyses indicated
that accent identification (M ± SD: 86.76 ± 7.10%) was the most
difficult feature, yielding significantly lower accuracy than both
gender (97.70 ± 3.71%; t = 9.25, p <0.001 , Cohen’s d = 1.59)
and age (95.40 ± 3.29%; t = 6.58, p <0.001 , Cohen’s d =
1.13). In contrast, gender identification was the easiest feature to
identify, with significantly higher accuracy than age (t = 2.71,
p= 0.01, Cohen’sd= 0.46) and accent.

Participants’ RTs were generally fast across conditions
(Figure 2b). A significant main effect of speaker trait on RTs was
observed (F(2,66) = 73.80 , p <0.001 ). Post-hoc analyses
revealed that accent processing (1087.33 ± 156.43 ms) elicited
longer RTs than both gender (853.58 ± 134.74 ms; t = 10.21,
p <0.001 , Cohen’s d = 1.75) and age (917.79 ± 144.42 ms; t =
8.55, p <0.001 , Cohen’s d = 1.47), while gender identification
was faster than age ( t = 3.92, p <0.001 , Cohen’s d = 0.67),
indicating increasing cognitive demands from gender through
age to accent processing.
3.2. Brain Activation
The univariate GLM revealed robust bilateral activation within
the superior temporal cortex for all three traits (shown in

processing human vocalizations [3]. A conjunction analysis
across all conditions defined a shared “voice core” centered on
the bilateral STG/STS and Heschl’s gyrus (HG) (Figure 3). This
conjunction-defined ROI represents the common neural substrate
for initial extraction of vocal information, supporting previous
findings that the superior temporal cortex serves as a core hub
for speaker perception [22].

4. Discussion
By decomposing speaker identity into its constituent dimensions,
our study demonstrates that the neural processing of speaker
identity is not unitary, but rather distributed and functionally
differentiated. Behavioral performance and cortical activation
patterns converged to indicate that accent processing imposed
the greatest neural demands, followed by age and gender. While
all speaker traits engaged a shared superior temporal auditory
cortex (bilateral HG and STG/STS), each dimension additionally
recruited distinct cortical systems. These findings revealed
a “core-plus-extension” pattern of voice perception, in which
shared “voice core” primarily identify general vocal information,
whereas other higher-order cortical areas selectively process
trait-specific information.
4.1. A shared auditory “voice core” for speaker trait
perception
By a conjunction analysis, we identified a fundamental “voice
core” in bilateral HG and STG/STS, revealing overlapping
activation across all three speaker traits. This convergence aligns
with the established role of TV As in recognizing human voice
[2]. Extending previous work, we further analyzed the activation
within these shared areas and revealed that neural activity within
this core, specifically STG/STS, was not uniform but varied
systematically across speaker traits.
In bilateral posterior and middle STG/STS, accent
processing, behaviorally the most demanding condition, elicited
stronger activation than age or gender judgments. Although
STG fundamentally encodes acoustic-phonetic features, recent
studies revealed that it is responsible for word-level structure
analysis and modulated by linguistic experience and cognitive
demands [ 23]. Accent processing requires not only simple
acoustic-phonetic decoding but also higher-order linguistic
information, including phonological rules governing segment
patterns, as well as prosodic structure such as stress and
intonation patterns that shape rhythmic and prominence cues
[24]. However, age and gender mainly rely on specific
acoustic cues but not phonological or prosodic information.
Besides, the behavioral results suggested that accent task is more
cognitive-demanding than gender and age. Thus, enhanced STG
activation was observed for accent processing. The finding is
also consistent with the hierarchical organization of the temporal
lobe, downstream areas in bilateral anterior and posterior STS
are particularly responsive to speech intelligibility [25]. Gender
and age judgments rely on partially overlapping acoustic cues,
and thus they resulted in similar overall activation within these
regions. Together, this trait-specific modulation indicates that
STG/STS regions do not merely passively analyze acoustic
features, but actively participate in decoding different speaker
traits [26].
Within the shared voice-core brain region, HG showed
comparable activation across tasks with minimal task-dependent
modulation, probably because HG supports basic acoustic
spectro-temporal encoding [ 27], which is shared across all
conditions.
4.2. Trait-specific regions beyond the voice core
Beyond the shared “voice core”, we observed a clear dissociation
in higher-order recruitment across traits. Age identification
engaged the left sensorimotor and premotor cortices, suggesting
that listeners rely on sensorimotor integration mechanisms when
parsing age-related vocal characteristics. This supports auditory
and motor coupling, where motor regions facilitate speech
perception in complex acoustic tasks by simulating the speaker’s
articulatory constraints [28]. Given that vocal aging inherently
stems from physiological changes of the vocal tract and motor
control [29], accurate age judgments require more than acoustic
analysis and may involve embodied simulation mechanisms
to map vocal features onto the listener’s internal articulatory
representations [30].
Processing accented pseudo-words recruits widespread
frontal areas, including bilateral IFG, MFG and SFG, reflecting
substantial top-down cognitive involvement. Frontal regions
are thought to modulate auditory cortical activity and support
the tracking of speech rhythm as well as comprehension
under challenging listening conditions [ 31]. Moreover, the
concurrent recruitment of frontal and temporal regions likely
reflects increased demands on phonological working memory,
prosodic parsing, and executive control [32], as listeners need to
accommodate systematic deviations from familiar phonological
and prosodic patterns (e.g., stress) by mapping unfamiliar
input onto accent-specific templates maintained in phonological
working memory, a process supported by IFG-posterior STG
mediated rehearsal mechanisms [33].
In contrast, gender identification relied exclusively on
superior temporal auditory cortex, probably because it is
primarily grounded in low-level and relatively static acoustic
features such as F0 and formant patterns [34]. Behavioral data
for the gender identification, showing the highest accuracy and
shortest RT, further highlight the ease with which listeners extract
these salient cues. Together, these neural and behavioral findings
indicate that gender is the most salient speaker trait that requires
the least brain effort to process.
In summary, while classic voice perception models treat
the speaker perception as a unitary entity, the neural activation
results in the present study revealed a complex pattern for
speaker perception, which follows a “core-plus-extension”
model. Specifically, a central auditory area, consisting of
HG and STG/STS, provides foundational representations of
acoustic-phonetic features that are essential for human speech
and voice perception. Beyond this core region, trait-specific
cortical areas are dynamically recruited depending on the
perceptual complexity and cognitive demands of particular
vocal attributes. These areas range from sensorimotor and
premotor cortices, which support age-related vocal processing
via articulatory and embodied simulation mechanisms, to frontal
regions, which are primarily involved in working memory and
executive control processes that support the discrimination of
complex vocal attributes such as accent. This hierarchical
organization enables efficient processing of both salient identity
cues and more nuanced socially relevant traits.
Findings of the present study suggest that speaker traits
are meaningfully encoded in the brain. Considering the brain’s
hierarchical and trait-sensitive processing of vocal attributes,
future speech recognition models could leverage these features
to improve speaker adaptation performance.
