Abstract
We present GADVOX, the first openly accessible German-
language speech dataset designed for the automated detection
and severity estimation of depression and anxiety. Record-
ings from 1,004 adults were collected via a crowdsourcing plat-
form using ten structured free-speech prompts presented in ran-
domised order, yielding an average of 18.4 minutes of sponta-
neous speech per participant. Each session is annotated with
validated PHQ-9 and GAD-7 severity scores (Cronbach’sα=
.85and.86, respectively), enabling regression-based modelling
beyond binary classification. A multi-stage quality assurance
pipeline comprising attention checks, automated voice activity
detection, and human annotation ensured data integrity from an
initial pool of 1,420 candidates (29.3% exclusion rate). Meta-
data including demographics, psychosocial context items, and
per-session speech quality estimates (ITU-T P.SAMD / P.566)
are released openly; full audio is available upon reasonable re-
quest.
Index Terms: depression detection, anxiety detection, speech
dataset, crowdsourcing, mental health, PHQ-9, GAD-7
1. Introduction
The automated analysis of vocal biomarkers for mental health
conditions has attracted considerable research interest over the
past decade. Studies have demonstrated that depression and
anxiety manifest in measurable acoustic features such as funda-
mental frequency, speech rate, pause patterns, and voice qual-
ity [1, 2, 3]. Modern deep-learning approaches, particularly
transformer-based models, have achieved prediction accuracies
of approximately 80 % on established benchmarks [4, 5]. How-
ever, these results rest on a critically thin empirical foundation:
nearly all published work relies on the DAIC-WOZ corpus [6],
which contains 189 interview sessions with 107 female and 82
male English-speaking participants recorded in a clinical inter-
view context. This represents a discrepancy of orders of mag-
nitude compared to the training data used for state-of-the-art
speech quality models of similar architecture [7].
Depression and anxiety are conceptually distinct condi-
tions, yet their co-occurrence is the rule rather than the excep-
tion in clinical practice: comorbidity rates exceed 50 % [8],
and practitioners screening for one condition might also need
to assess the other. This motivates the development of voice-
based tools that can jointly characterise both—a goal that re-

Three limitations of the existing landscape stand out. First, the
dominance of English-language data prevents adaptation of AI-
based diagnostic tools to other languages and health systems;
for the German-speaking context, no freely accessible dataset of
this kind exists. Second, most approaches reduce the problem
to binary classification (depressed vs. not depressed), whereas
the clinical instruments underlying data collection—the Patient
Health Questionnaire (PHQ-9) [9, 10] and the Generalized Anx-
iety Disorder scale (GAD-7) [11]—provide ordinal severity rat-
ings that support richer regression-based and multi-target mod-
elling. Third, the scarcity of data makes it difficult to disen-
tangle the acoustic correlates specific to depression from those
specific to anxiety in the presence of high comorbidity. Re-
cent work has begun to address some of these challenges: Hi-
dalgo Julia et al. [12] demonstrated multimodal biomarker iden-
tification in large-scale remote recordings, and Maji et al. [13]
highlighted the impact of foundation models on depression de-
tection, while both groups underscored the bottleneck imposed
by data scarcity.
We address this gap with GADVOX, a German-language
speech corpus annotated with both PHQ-9 and GAD-7 severity
scores. Crucially, both the voice recordings and the question-
naire responses were produced by the same individuals: each
participant recorded their own speech and completed the clini-
cal scales as a self-assessment, so symptom labels and acoustic
data are paired without any external or third-party rating step.
The dataset features naturalistic free speech elicited by struc-
tured prompts, collected via crowdsourcing to maximise eco-
logical validity. Our quality assurance pipeline and the inclu-
sion of per-session ITU-T P.566 quality scores support trans-
parent downstream use. Metadata are released openly under
CC BY 4.0; audio is available upon reasonable request, see Sec-
tion 3.
2. Data Collection
Data collection proceeded in two stages: a laboratory pre-study
(n= 25) to develop and validate the speech elicitation material,
followed by the main crowdsourcing experiment in which par-
ticipants both recorded their speech responses and completed
the clinical questionnaires.

2.1. Pre-Study and Prompt Development
An initial exploratory phase (n= 5) revealed that open-ended
instructions to speak freely consistently produced short, hesi-
tant responses, motivating a shift to structured prompts. Over
the remaining 20 sessions, prompts were iteratively refined un-
der progressively reduced experimenter involvement; the final
group of five participants received only written instructions,
mirroring the unattended crowdsourcing context. Of 15 can-
didate prompts, ten were retained based on their ability to elicit
fluent, on-topic responses of approximately 20 seconds without
prompting disclosure of personally identifying or health-related
information. Pre-study samples are not included in the final
dataset.
2.2. Crowdsourcing Procedure
Participants were recruited via the German-language crowd-
sourcing platform Crowdee. A general job was posted without
pre-screening by symptom severity; all participants who passed
quality control were included. The following instruction was
displayed above each prompt:“Bitte sprich ausf ¨uhrlich und frei
¨uber die folgende Frage. Eine gute Antwort ist etwa 20 Sekun-
den lang. Sprich frei und mit deiner nat ¨urlichen Stimme. ”
(“Please speak in detail and freely about the following ques-
tion. A good answer is about 20 seconds long. Speak freely
and in your natural voice.”) The ten prompts were presented
in randomised order; each was recorded as a separate audio
file. Prompt topics were deliberately emotionally neutral and
universally accessible (see Table 1). Participants additionally
completed the PHQ-9 and GAD-7 and provided basic demo-
graphic information, and were encouraged to use an external
microphone or headset where available.

2.3. Ethical Approval and Informed Consent
The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Fac-
ulty IV of TU Berlin (ref no.: 412). All participants provided
informed consent via a dedicated, detailed form prior to par-
ticipation. The form disclosed the nature of the data collected,
the possibility of sharing pseudonymised data with researchers
from recognised institutions after individual vetting, and par-
ticipants’ rights to withdraw consent and request deletion at
any time. Each participant received a unique pseudonymous
code enabling independent deletion requests; researchers who
receive access are contractually required to propagate subse-
quent deletion requests to their local copies.
2.4. Audio Preprocessing
The ten prompt responses per participant were delivered as sep-
arate audio files by the crowdsourcing platform and concate-
nated into a single session-level file (W A V , mono, 48 kHz).
First, leading and trailing silence was removed from each in-
dividual recording. Second, to produce a naturally paced con-
tinuous file, the mean within-speaker pause duration between
words and sentences was estimated per participant via voice
activity detection and inserted as the inter-recording gap dur-
ing concatenation, preserving individual speaking rhythm. Raw
per-prompt files are available alongside the concatenated ses-
sions at the OSF repository (see Section 3).
2.5. Quality Assurance
From 1,420 recruited participants, 1,004 sessions were retained
after a four-stage pipeline aligned with ITU-T Recommenda-
tions P.808 [14] and E.812 [15], which provide guidance for
crowdsourced data collection and quality control. Table 2 sum-
marises the exclusion counts at each stage.
Attention checks.Four trap items were embedded
throughout the questionnaire, each containing an explicit in-
struction in the question text (e.g.,“W ¨ahle unbedingt diese
Checkbox aus”/ “Please check this box”). Two used check-
box responses; two used the Likert-format response scales of
the PHQ-9 and GAD-7 respectively. Only participants who an-
swered all four correctly were retained.
Automated audio screening.All recordings were algorith-
mically screened for absence of a human voice signal. Speech
quality was estimated as a post-hoc test, not as a selection crite-
rion.
Human annotation.A trained research assistant listened
to the opening seconds of each recording and labelled it as ei-
ther likely genuine human speech or non-genuine (TTS output,
broadcast audio, ambient sounds). Only recordings labelled as
likely human were retained.
Speech quality estimation.All retained recordings were
processed with SQ-AST, the reference implementation of ITU-
T P.566 [7, 16, 17]. Since the model operates on 10-second seg-

3. Dataset Description
The final dataset comprises 1,004 sessions. All statistics below
refer to this quality-filtered subset. The metadata file is released
openly at https://osf.io/k4z2v/. Full audio is provided upon re-
quest; access is granted after individual review of institutional
affiliation and research purpose. Researchers interested in ob-
taining the audio data should visit the OSF repository, where
detailed instructions for submitting a request are provided.
3.1. Demographics
Participants ranged in age from 18 to 77 years (M= 37.2,
SD= 11.9). The sample comprised 511 male (50.9 %), 483 fe-
male (48.1 %), 7 diverse (0.7 %), and 3 unspecified (0.3 %) par-
ticipants. Educational background spanned from lower sec-
ondary certificate (Hauptschule,n= 49) to doctoral degree
(n= 23), with intermediate secondary (Realschule,n= 219)
and bachelor’s degree (n= 213) most frequent. The major-
ity were employed (n= 788); students and trainees (n=
24), the self-employed (n= 8), and unemployed individuals
(n= 8) were also represented. The most prevalent job sectors
were IT / Software / Data (n= 97), Business / Administration
(n= 87), and Healthcare (n= 63). As is typical of crowd-
sourcing panels, the sample skews toward higher educational
attainment and digital-literacy-compatible occupations relative
to the general German population; researchers should account
for this when generalising results. Regarding recording equip-
ment, 806 participants (80.3 %) used a built-in microphone; 198
(19.7 %) used an external device.
3.2. Recording Duration
Figure 1 shows the distribution of recording lengths after con-
catenation alongside the total crowdsourcing session durations.
Median session duration was 14.7 minutes (M= 18.4min,
SD= 13.6min), with a range of 3.8 to 114.6 minutes. The
long right tail reflects a small number of particularly verbose
participants rather than recording errors; all sessions passed the
quality assurance pipeline described in Section 2.5.
3.3. Depression and Anxiety Scores
PHQ-9 and GAD-7 items demonstrated good internal consis-
tency in this sample (PHQ-9:α=.85, 95 % CI[.836,.864];
GAD-7:α=.86, 95 % CI[.848,.874]), consistent with values
reported in the original validation studies [10, 11] and suggest-
ing that crowdsourcing did not substantially degrade measure-
ment quality.
PHQ-9 total scores ranged from 0 to 26 (M= 7.6,SD=
5.1, skewness= 0.88, excess kurtosis= 0.47). Using estab-

the minimal range (0–4), 405 (40.3 %) mild (5–9), 180 (17.9 %)
moderate (10–14), 85 (8.5 %) moderately severe (15–19), and
27 (2.7 %) severe (≥20). The share scoring at or above the
moderate threshold (PHQ-9≥10) was 29.2 %. For context,
RKI GBE data report a 14.4 % prevalence of depressive symp-
toms in Germany [18]; however, that figure reflects clinically
assessed or administratively recorded diagnoses, whereas PHQ-
9 is a self-report screening instrument designed to measure

3.4. Psychosocial Context Items

ing (17.8 %), compulsive thoughts or behaviours (13.7 %), sex-
ual difficulties (13.1 %), chronic pain (11.6 %), and loss of con-
trol over substance use (9.0 %). A total of 19.8 % of participants
(n= 199) endorsed none of the listed items. These items are

3.5. Speech Quality
Figure 3 shows the distributions of the five SQ-AST quality di-
mensions. Overall MOS was moderate (M= 2.84,SD=
0.56), expected for in-the-wild recordings on consumer devices
in uncontrolled environments. Noise (M= 3.78), discontinu-
ity (M= 3.68), and loudness (M= 3.54) were rated consis-
tently higher, while coloration showed the greatest variability
(M= 3.16,SD= 0.43), reflecting the diversity of record-
ing hardware. Crucially, neither PHQ-9 nor GAD-7 scores cor-
related meaningfully with overall MOS (PHQ-9:r=.008,
p=.800; GAD-7:r=.015,p=.627), indicating that symp-
tom severity does not co-vary with recording quality.
4. Conclusion
We presented GADVOX, the first openly available German-
language speech dataset annotated with validated continuous
depression and anxiety severity scores from 1,004 participants.
By combining structured free-speech prompts, crowdsourcing-
based collection, and a rigorous multi-stage quality assurance
pipeline with a 29.3 % exclusion rate, GADVOX addresses a
critical gap in the mental health speech research community.
The strong internal consistency of PHQ-9 and GAD-7 scores in
the crowdsourced sample, the absence of a quality–severity con-
found, and the inclusion of per-session ITU-T P.566 estimates
make GADVOX particularly suited for the development of ro-
bust, clinically informed models. The dataset supports multi-
target regression over both scales simultaneously and enables
research into the shared and distinct vocal correlates of depres-
sion and anxiety.
Several limitations should be noted. PHQ-9 and GAD-7 are
self-report screening instruments, not clinician-administered di-
agnostic assessments; labels should be interpreted as proxies
for symptom severity rather than clinical ground truth. Ac-
cordingly, GADVOX should be viewed as a resource for mod-
elling symptom severity and mental health screening indicators
rather than clinical diagnoses. The crowdsourcing recruitment
strategy introduces known sampling biases toward higher ed-
ucational attainment and digital literacy relative to the general
German population, and the prevalence of moderate-to-severe
symptoms (≈30 %) exceeds population-level estimates, re-
flecting the general composition of active crowdsourcing pan-
els. Dialectal variation, non-native speech, and code-switching
were not systematically controlled; nor was room acoustics or
precise distance to microphone, though ITU-T P.566 estimates
provide a partial acoustic characterisation. Human annotation
covered only the opening seconds of each recording, and a
single rater was employed. The concatenation preprocessing
may alter temporal pause statistics relevant for clinical analy-
sis; raw per-prompt files are available on request for researchers
for whom this is a concern.
Future work will establish acoustic feature extraction
pipelines and baseline modelling experiments to provide ref-
erence benchmarks for the community. Additionally, we plan
to release ASR-derived transcripts of all sessions via the same
OSF repository, enabling text-based and multimodal analyses
without requiring researchers to run their own speech recogni-
tion pipelines.
