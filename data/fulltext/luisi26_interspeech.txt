Smooth Formant Tracking with Differentiable Linear Prediction
Bryn Luisi ID 1, Lauri Juvela ID 1
1 Department of Information and Communications Engineering, Aalto University, Finland

Abstract
Linear prediction methods for formant estimation are fast and
interpretable, but assume local stationarity and Gaussian resid-
uals. Hybrid neural methods are robust and accurate but lack
interpretability and differentiability. We present LP-DDSP, a
differentiable all-pole model that estimates formants by directly
optimizing log-area ratios via a novel loss function that com-
binesL 1,L 2, and temporal regularization terms. This approach
addresses the limitations of classic LP by allowing for non-
Gaussian residuals and smooth parameter tracks across frames.
We also introduce SMELP, an end-to-end neural formant tracker
that incorporates our differentiable LP loss, enabling joint learn-
ing of interpretable intermediate representations and formant
predictions. Both methods achieve competitive performance
relative to baselines: KARMA and Praat. Our methods enable
fully differentiable, interpretable, and smooth formant tracking
and can be extended to other speech processing tasks.
Index Terms: speech processing, speech analysis, formant es-
timation, linear prediction, DDSP
1. Introduction
Formants are a concentration of acoustic energy around specific
frequencies in the spectra of speech signals [1]. They corre-
spond to the resonances of the vocal tract and are critical for
distinguishing phonemes, particularly vowels and voiced con-
sonants. Formant tracking, the estimation of formants across an
entire utterance, is a common task in speech processing. The
analysis of formants is useful in linguistic research [2], speech
recognition [3], and speech synthesis [4].
The traditional method for estimating formants uses linear
prediction (LP), where an all-pole model is fitted to the power
spectrum of a speech signal and the resonances of the model are
interpreted as formants [5]. Praat is a widely used tool for for-
mant tracking that employs the LP-based Burg method [6, 7].
LP-based methods are attractive due to their computational ef-
ficiency and interpretability: the estimated parameters have a
clear physical interpretation in terms of vocal-tract resonances
[1]. However, least-squares estimation of all-pole models is sus-
ceptible to harmonic bias [8]. It also relies on a local station-
arity assumption and typically treats frames independently [9].
Moreover, classical LP assumes a Gaussian excitation, whereas
the residual of voiced speech is sometimes periodic and better
approximated by a Laplacian distribution [1].
Several extensions of classical LP have been proposed to
correct these issues. Methods such as weighted linear predic-
tion (WLP) [10] and discrete all pole modeling (DAP) [11]
aim to address issues related to non-Gaussianity and harmonic
bias. Quasi-closed phase (QCP) analysis is a specific form of
WLP that restricts the estimation of LP coefficients to regions
near glottal closure instants, where the influence of the source
is minimized, and the vocal tract response is more accurately
observed [12]. However, these approaches introduce practical
limitations. QCP requires reliable pitch tracking to construct
the weighting windows, and the resulting windowing procedure
is non-differentiable, which complicates its integration into hy-
brid neural or end-to-end learned systems. Meanwhile, time-
varying LP aims to improve formant estimation by modeling
inter-frame dependencies by introducing prediction coefficients
that share a basis function spanning multiple frames [13], while
time-regularized linear prediction achieves a similar effect by
regularizing LP parameter dynamics [14]. Further improve-
ments can be achieved by using more advanced decoding meth-
ods for formant tracking, such as Viterbi search in Praat [6] or
Kalman filters in KARMA [15].
Several hybrid formant tracking and estimation approaches
exist that use neural networks to refine or regularize LP-based
estimates, thereby increasing robustness and accuracy while
maintaining interpretability. DeepFormants uses LP-obtained
cepstral coefficients using multiple LP orders as input features
to an LSTM model for formant tracking [16]. In [17] and [18], a
deep neural network (DNN) makes initial formant predictions,
which are refined using peaks of the all-pole spectrum com-
puted by QCP-FB. Although these methods improve accuracy,
their lack of differentiability precludes their use in end-to-end
systems. A fourth approach uses differential digital signal pro-
cessing (DDSP) [19], a paradigm in which gradients of a loss
function are backpropagated through a DSP block [20]. End-
to-end LPCnet uses differentiable LP to produce features for a
neural vocoder [21]. Yu et al. [22] propose a differentiable all-
pole filter to model time-varying audio effects, in which gra-
dients are computed with respect to filter coefficients in direct
form. DDSP methods can be incorporated into end-to-end sys-
tems and can enable self-supervised training when combined
with classic linear predictive tasks.
Motivated by the limitations of classic LP and the strengths
of DDSP, we propose an unsupervised DDSP-based framework
for estimating all-pole model coefficients for formant estima-
tion. We address the Gaussian residual assumption by incor-
porating anL 1 term in the prediction error, and mitigate frame
independence with a temporal regularization term that penal-
izes large parameter changes across frames. This represents a
new framework for optimizing LP coefficients in a DDSP set-
ting. Leveraging the method’s differentiability, we introduce
SMELP (Smooth end-to-end Linear Prediction), a neural for-
mant tracking model enhanced with our DDSP loss. Evalu-
ation on the VTR Formants Database demonstrates that LP-
DDSP and SMELP achieve competitive or improved perfor-
mance compared to classical LP estimation methods and estab-
lished formant tracking tools such as Praat and KARMA.

2.3. LP Coefficients to Formants
Once the filter has been optimized using either the autocor-
relation method or LP-DDSP, formants are calculated using a
root-finding algorithm. Using the LP coefficients, we form the
companion matrix associated with the all-pole polynomial and
compute its Eigenvalues to obtain the roots. Each complex-
conjugate root pair corresponds to a potential vocal tract reso-
nance, from which we calculate a candidate formant frequency
and bandwidth [25, 26]. The final predictions are the first 4 can-
didates after filtering for frequencies between 0 Hz and 5500 Hz
with bandwidths less than 400 Hz [26].
2.4. SMELP
SMELP is an end-to-end model using differentiable linear pre-
diction to estimate formant trajectories. This method differs
from LP-DDSP in that it does not refine the LAR prediction
over several epochs on the same speech utterance. Instead,
the LARs are used to compute the same loss as in LP-DDSP,
which is then added to supervised losses and backpropagated
only once per utterance. Figure 1 shows the architecture dia-
gram for SMELP with the loss functions highlighted in blue.
SMELP comprises a convolutional neural network (CNN) fea-
ture predictor and long short-term memory recurrent neural net-
work (LSTM) decoder [27, 28].
The CNN predicts LARs from the STFT of the signal.
LAR predictions are used to calculateL reg. LARs are turned
into direct-form LP coefficients using forward Levinson recur-
sion. We take the inverse filter using the LP coefficients to
find the residual excitation and calculateL 1 andL 2. Then,
the LSTM predicts formants from the predicted LARs. There
are two supervised losses,L coeff andL F.L coeff is the mean-
squared error (MSE) between the predicted LP coefficients and

3. Experiments
We use the VTR Formants Database [29], which contains a rep-
resentative subset of 516 utterances from the TIMIT corpus [30]
with a sample rate of 16 kHz. These utterances typically range
from 2 to 5 s in duration and are partitioned into 324 train-
ing and 192 testing samples. For ground-truth evaluation, the
dataset includes hand-corrected formant trajectories sampled at
10 ms intervals, alongside corresponding timestamps for vari-
ous phoneme categories. Formants are present only for certain
phonemes: vowels, nasals, liquids, glides, voiced fricatives, and
voiced stops [1]. Therefore, we use phoneme labels and times-
tamps to compare the formant predictions only where formants
are expected. The STFT uses 512 frequency bins, a window
size of 512, and a hop size of 160, with a Hann window.
We compare the root mean square error (RMSE) between
the predicted and ground-truth formants for LP baseline, LP-
DDSP (ours), SMELP (ours), LP-LSTM, Praat, TV-QCP, and
KARMA. LP baseline is the autocorrelation method with root-
finding described in Section 2.1. We use an LP order of 16
for all our LP models. TV-QCP is a time-varying quasi-closed
phase model from [31]. KARMA is a model for formant track-
ing in a state-space framework using Kalman filters and an au-
toregressive moving-average (ARMA) model of cepstral coeffi-
cients [15]. LP-LSTM is a two-layer bidirectional LSTM with
a hidden size of 128, similar to DeepFormants, but equivalent in
complexity to our model [16]. LP-LSTM uses LP coefficients
as features and does not incorporate LP into the losses or im-
prove LP estimates during backpropagation.
The CNN in SMELP is a five-layer 2D CNN with a kernel
size of 5, and 16 hidden channels that double every layer. Each
layer contains a 2D convolution followed by batch normaliza-
tion and ReLU [32, 33]. LP-DDSP is trained using 1500 epochs
per utterance with a learning rate of 0.1. LP-LSTM and SMELP
are trained for 100 epochs using a learning rate of 0.001. All

5. Conclusions
This paper introduces a novel method, LP-DDSP, to estimate
formant tracks using differentiable all-pole filters. Our ap-
proach optimizes filter coefficients based on the residual signal,
differentiating through LARs, which represents a new frame-
work to optimize LP coefficients. To ensure temporal smooth-
ness, we incorporated a regularization term using LARs. To
account for the Laplacian distribution of voiced speech, we in-
cludedL 1 error. Ablation studies demonstrate that this regu-
larization greatly improves performance. We also integrated
our novel loss function into SMELP, an end-to-end formant-
tracking model. Evaluations against common baselines demon-
strate that both LP-DDSP and SMELP perform competitively
or outperform existing methods. Future work will investigate
self-supervised learning on a larger scale, and integrate differ-
entiable filters into an end-to-end sound synthesis model.
