Abstract
While Wav2Vec 2.0 has achieved significant success in speech
representation tasks, the mechanisms by which it encodes di-
alectal features across different layers remain underexplored.
This study applies geometric probing methods to analyze the
changes in the representations of five Chinese dialects within
the model. Experimental results reveal a three-stage trajectory:
(1) lower layers are dominated by fundamental acoustic dis-
crepancies; (2) middle layers exhibit high spatial dispersion,
preserving fine-grained phonetic details; and (3) deep layers
show significant spatial contraction, though the resulting clus-
tering remains consistent with traditional linguistic taxonomies.
Based on these findings, this study suggests that for tasks re-
quiring fine-grained phonetic information, such as accent iden-
tification, intermediate-layer features should be prioritized; and
deep-layer features are more valuable for broad dialectal classi-
fication or phylogenetic analysis.
Index Terms: Wav2Vec 2.0, Chinese dialects, geometric prob-
ing, layer-wise analysis, manifold collapse
1. Introduction
The emergence of self-supervised learning (SSL) has funda-
mentally reshaped the field of speech processing. Large-scale
pre-trained models, represented by Wav2Vec 2.0 [1], HuBERT
[2], alongside other mainstream frameworks such as CPC [3],
TERA [4], and Data2Vec [5], learn contextual representations
from massive unannotated corpora. These models have estab-
lished new state-of-the-art benchmarks across a range of tasks,
including automatic speech recognition (ASR), speaker verifi-
cation, and emotion recognition, effectively becoming the uni-
versal “backbone” of modern speech systems (e.g., SUPERB
benchmark [6]).
Despite their dominance in practice, research into the in-
ternal interpretability of these models lags behind their perfor-
mance metrics. In the adjacent natural language processing
(NLP) domain, “BERTology” studies [7] have thoroughly ex-
plored the layer-wise dynamics of Transformer models. Prior
research confirms that BERT exhibits a hierarchical process-
ing mechanism [8]: lower layers encode superficial syntac-
tic features, while upper layers encode abstract semantic in-
formation. Crucially, studies have identified the phenomenon
of anisotropy—where representations in deep layers degener-
ate into a narrow cone space, sacrificing spatial directionality to
adapt to specific tasks [9].
Inspired by these findings, speech researchers have re-
cently begun to explore the internal mechanisms of SSL models.

Layer-wise probing of Wav2Vec 2.0 has revealed that the lower
layers behave similarly to autoencoders, while the upper lay-
ers shift toward discriminative properties adapted for ASR and
language identification [10, 11]. Similarly, other studies using
canonical correlation analysis (CCA) have demonstrated that
phonetic information peaks in the intermediate layers, whereas
semantic information dominates the top-layer outputs [12, 13].
However, a critical research gap remains in this domain:
existing probing studies predominantly focus on standard lan-
guages (e.g., English), with sparse exploration of the geomet-
ric evolutionary dynamics of dialects—the internal variants of
a language. In the specific field of Chinese dialects, related re-
search has primarily concentrated on engineering applications.
Methodologies have evolved from traditional i-vector/x-vector
systems [14] to recent end-to-end SSL adaptation schemes
[15, 16]. Yet, these studies typically treat pre-trained models as
“black-box” feature extractors, defaulting to the use of top-layer
outputs or simple aggregated features [15, 16]. They rarely in-
vestigate how the model processes complex phonological vari-
ations unique to Chinese dialects (e.g., tone sandhi, retroflex
initials) [17, 18], nor do they explain whether the deep layers,
which are prone to semantic collapse, are genuinely the optimal
choice for representing dialectal diversity.
This opacity in geometric properties poses a latent risk:
without understanding the layer-wise evolutionary trajectory
of dialectal features, researchers might inadvertently discard
highly valuable discriminative information embedded in the in-
termediate layers. To bridge this gap, we propose an analyti-
cal framework based on geometric probing. By selecting five
representative Chinese dialects (encompassing Mandarin and
non-Mandarin branches) and utilizing dynamic time warping
(DTW) and multidimensional scaling (MDS), we deconstruct
the complete evolutionary trajectory of dialectal representations
across all 24 layers of Wav2Vec 2.0.
Our main contributions are as follows: (1) We quantita-
tively characterize the layer-wise evolutionary trajectory of Chi-
nese dialect representations in Wav2Vec 2.0. Using geometric
probes of distance and topology, we reveal a systematic tran-
sition from acoustic dominance to phonetic differentiation and
macroscopic linguistic abstraction. (2) We uncover the func-
tional role of deep-layer spatial contraction (manifold collapse).
By analyzing the concurrent reduction in absolute spatial dis-
tance and the emergence of linguistic clustering, we suggest
that this contraction acts as an implicit feature filter, suppress-
ing local phonetic variations to highlight broader taxonomic
structures. (3) We provide actionable empirical guidance for
downstream applications. Based on these layer-wise dynamics,
we demonstrate that intermediate layers are optimal for fine-

2. Methodology
This section details the dataset construction, preprocessing
pipeline, Wav2Vec 2.0 model configuration, and the proposed
geometric probing framework.
2.1. Dataset and Preprocessing
Dataset Construction: The data in this study is sourced from
the Chinese dialect corpus provided by the MagicHub open-
source community. 1 To construct a geometrically represen-
tative dialect space, we selected five typical Chinese dialects
covering major variants in both Mandarin and non-Mandarin
regions [19]: Southwestern Mandarin (Sichuan, Wuhan) and
Central Plains Mandarin (Zhengzhou) representing the Man-
darin branch, alongside Wu Chinese (Shanghai) and Cantonese
(Guangzhou) representing the non-Mandarin branches.
Synthetic Reference Anchor: Selecting a stable and noise-
free baseline is critical for calculating acoustic distances. To
minimize interference from differences in speech content, we
used the Microsoft Azure Text-to-Speech service (voice: zh-
CN-XiaoxiaoNeural)2 to generate Standard Mandarin samples
corresponding to the dialect texts. This approach provides an
idealized phonetic reference, so that subsequent geometric dis-
tance calculations between Mandarin and each dialect primarily
capture acoustic and phonetic deviations rather than textual dif-
ferences.
Preprocessing: Given that silence segments may induce
spurious high correlations in the representation space, we im-
plemented strict V oice Activity Detection (V AD). We calculated
short-time energy using the librosa toolkit, applying a 25dB
threshold to remove silence and background noise, retaining
only valid speech segments. All audio was resampled to 16kHz
to match the pre-training model’s input requirements.
2.2. Model Architecture
We employ the Wav2Vec 2.0 XLSR-53 model [1] for our analy-
sis. This model was pre-trained on massive cross-lingual speech
data (covering 53 languages) in a self-supervised manner, pos-
sessing robust cross-lingual representation capabilities. The ar-
chitecture consists of a multi-layer convolutional Feature En-
coder and a Context Network comprising 24 Transformer lay-
ers.
To investigate the knowledge structure natively acquired
by the model, we completely froze the model parameters dur-
ing the experiment, using it solely as a feature extractor. For
an input speech sequence X, we extracted the hidden states
Hl(l = 1 , . . . ,24) from all 24 layers of the context encoder
to construct the layer-wise representation sequence.
2.3. Geometric Probing Framework
To analyze the evolution of internal representations, we pro-
pose a set of parameter-free geometric probes across three di-
mensions, focusing on quantifying the flow of dialectal features
from the perspectives of distance, topology, and hierarchical
structure.

2.3.1. Distance Probe: Dynamic Time Warping (DTW)
To quantify acoustic differences in temporal structure across di-
alects, we employ the classic DTW algorithm [20]. For each
layer, we calculate the DTW distance between a dialect sample
sequence and the reference anchor, normalized by the sum of
their sequence lengths. This probe circumvents the influence of
speaking rate differences, directly reflecting the acoustic mani-
fold distance in the feature space.
2.3.2. Topological Probing: Multidimensional Scaling (MDS)
To intuitively reveal the relative positions and topological struc-
tures of different dialects in the feature space, we employ
MDS [21] to perform dimensionality reduction and visualiza-
tion. Since the core input for MDS is the spatial distance ma-
trix, we first calculate the global centroid for each dialect: at a
specific network layer, mean pooling is applied across the tem-
poral dimension for all input speech sequences of a dialect to
obtain fixed-length utterace-level vectors. The mean of all ut-
terance vectors for a specific dialect is computed to derive the
centroid (Eq. 1):

2.3.3. Structure Probe: Hierarchical Clustering
To validate whether the model has learned linguistic hierarchi-
cal relationships among dialects, we employ Agglomerative Hi-
erarchical Clustering [22] to construct a phylogenetic tree. By
quantitatively observing the branching structure and node dis-
tances of the cluster tree, we evaluate the model’s ability to en-
code dialectal proximity at different levels and its consistency
with classic linguistic taxonomy.

3. Results and Analysis
Based on the proposed geometric probing framework, this sec-
tion deconstructs the evolutionary dynamics of Chinese dialect
representations in Wav2Vec 2.0 across three dimensions: geo-
metric metrics, topological structures, and hierarchical relation-
ships.
3.1. Layer-wise Evolution of Geometric Metrics
To quantify the macroscopic evolution of the representation
space, we comprehensively analyzed the normalized DTW dis-
tance between dialect representations and the synthetic baseline
(Standard Mandarin) (see Figure 1), as well as the sum of pair-
wise Euclidean distances among dialect centroids (see Figure
2). As illustrated in Figure 1 and Figure 2, the evolution of geo-
metric metrics follows a non-monotonic pattern, clearly divided
into three stages:
(1) Acoustic Geometric Stage (Layers 1-8): The distance
metrics for all dialects remain at a high level (approxi-
mately 25-33) during this stage. Notably, the distances
for non-Mandarin dialects (Shanghai, Guangzhou), which
possess larger acoustic differences, are significantly higher
than those for the acoustically similar Mandarin dialects
(Sichuan, Zhengzhou). The high consistency between
the distance distribution and physical acoustic differences
indicates that the lower-layer representational geometry is
dominated by the raw acoustic manifold. The inter-dialect
distances primarily reflect variations in physical attributes
such as pitch range, speaking rate, and tonal contours. This
aligns with prior findings that early layers of self-supervised
models act as acoustic autoencoders [10], and recent works
demonstrating that DTW on SSL representations effectively
quantifies raw dialectal pronunciation differences [20, 23].
(2) Metric Stabilization Stage (Layers 9-19): As the net-
work deepens, the distance curve ceases its sharp decline and
instead maintains a stable plateau. During this feature reten-
tion phase, the relative spatial distances among dialects do
not collapse (see Figure 2), and the model robustly preserves
the dialectal discriminability formed in the intermediate
layers. This indicates that Layers 13-19 represent the core
region for retaining fine-grained phonetic features [12, 13],
providing abundant local phonetic variant information before
the ultimate deep-layer manifold collapse.
(3) Metric Collapse Stage (Layers 20-24): Approaching the
deep layers, the geometric distances for all dialects experi-
ence a precipitous drop, reaching a minimum at Layer 24.
This phenomenon provides empirical evidence for significant
anisotropy [9]—the entire representation manifold degener-
ates into a narrow cone. Geometrically, this is the result of
spatial compression: the pre-training objective forces all rep-
resentations to cluster within a highly compact area. Ulti-
mately, the collapse substantially reduces fine-grained acous-
tic differences among dialects. Although this spatial com-
pression weakens the model’s ability to distinguish between
similar sub-dialects, as subsequent analysis shows, it geomet-
rically paves the way for extracting more abstract, macro-
scopic taxonomic features.
3.2. Dynamic Reorganization of Topological Structures
To deconstruct the spatial morphology underlying the distance
variations, we utilized topological probing (MDS and hierarchi-
cal clustering) to visualize the structural reorganization of the
representation manifolds at Layers 1, 12, and 24 (as shown in
Figure 3):
• Layer 1 (Disordered Manifold): The initial MDS projection
(Figure 3a) shows the dialect points scattered randomly, and
the clustering tree fails to present a clear regional dialectal
structure. This indicates that the lower-layer representations
exhibit an unstructured, high-entropy topology. The model
has not yet developed the capability to geometrically decou-
ple dialectal features, and clustering is primarily driven by
random physical factors such as recording environments.
• Layer 12 (Phonetic Feature Differentiation Stage): The
intermediate-layer representation manifold undergoes dras-
tic, ordered reorganization. The MDS plot [21] (Figure 3b)
generally exhibits a trend of “non-Mandarin convergence,
Mandarin separation”: Wu Chinese (Shanghai) and Can-
tonese (Guangzhou) are positioned adjacently in the geomet-
ric space, indicating that the model begins to capture the pho-
netic commonalities of non-Mandarin dialects. This demon-
strates that Layer 12 is a critical region where the model
translates lower-layer acoustic discrepancies into specific
phonetic features [13]; the dialects are sufficiently pushed
apart in space, maintaining a high degree of geometric dis-
persion.
• Layer 24 (Spatial Convergence): Although minor relative
positional differences are retained, MDS (Figure 3c) reveals
a massive contraction of all nodes toward the center, with the
absolute scale of the coordinate axes shrinking sharply. This
visual phenomenon of spatial convergence corroborates the
sharp drop in inter-dialect distance observed in Section 3.1,
further confirming the global inward contraction and collapse
of the manifold structure.

Deep Layer (Layer 24): Following severe spatial contrac-

Wu Chinese (Shanghai) and Cantonese (Guangzhou) sepa-
rate earliest at the root node, forming an independent non-
Mandarin subtree (major branch). This indicates the model
effectively captures the significant phonological divide be-
tween non-Mandarin and Mandarin dialects. Southwestern
Mandarin (Sichuan, Wuhan) and Central Plains Mandarin
(Zhengzhou) form the other major branch, exhibiting close
geometric proximity. This aligns closely with classical lin-
guistic taxonomy [19, 24]. The ability of SSL embeddings to
spontaneously reconstruct these human-defined typological
and phylogenetic relationships has also been recently corrob-
orated across global languages [25].
This concurrent phenomenon provides crucial empirical ev-
idence: deep-layer manifold collapse is not merely a loss of
information, but functions as a feature filter. It drastically com-
presses fine-grained physical phonetic variants retained in the
intermediate layers (correcting the isolation of Zhengzhou di-
alect), thereby allowing the core, macroscopic linguistic differ-
ences to dominate the surviving microscopic topological struc-
ture.
4. Conclusions
In this paper, we conducted a geometric probing study to de-
construct the layer-wise evolutionary dynamics of Chinese di-
alect representations in Wav2Vec 2.0. Our results revealed
a three-stage trajectory characterized by acoustic dominance,
phonetic transition, and spatial convergence. Specifically, we
demonstrated that the spatial collapse observed in deep layers
functions as a feature filter. While this compression reduces
fine-grained phonetic variations, it simultaneously highlights a
macroscopic clustering structure consistent with traditional lin-
guistic taxonomies [24, 25]. Furthermore, this study provided
empirical guidance for downstream applications: intermediate-
layer representations are ideal for tasks requiring localized
phonetic distinctions (e.g., fine-grained accent recognition),
whereas deep-layer features offer distinct topological advan-
tages for broad dialect classification. Our future work will focus
on expanding this framework to a broader range of dialects and
larger speaker cohorts to further validate these geometric phe-
nomena.
