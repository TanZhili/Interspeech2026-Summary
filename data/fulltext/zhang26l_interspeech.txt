Text-to-Music Generation

Abstract
Text-to-music generation has recently achieved remarkable
progress. However, existing studies predominantly focus on
Western music, leaving the domain of traditional Chinese mu-
sic underexplored. A primary reason for this gap is the scarcity
of well-annotated text-music paired datasets. To bridge this
gap, we introduceCTMusic, a traditional Chinese instrumental
music dataset designed for text-to-music generation. CTMusic
comprises 741 carefully annotated text-music pairs with over 42
hours of audio, covering a diverse range of traditional Chinese
instruments in both solo and ensemble settings. We demonstrate
the effectiveness of CTMusic by fine-tuning Stable Audio Open
on it and further provide an improved model that better captures
the unique characteristics of traditional Chinese music. To our
knowledge, CTMusic is the first dataset of its kind and offers
new possibilities for Chinese music generation. The dataset is
publicly available at: https://frei-2.github.io/CTMusic.
Index Terms: text-to-music generation, traditional Chinese in-
strumental music dataset, low-rank adaptation
1. Introduction
Generative artificial intelligence has emerged as a transforma-
tive force in content creation, reshaping the paradigms of pro-
ducing content across various modalities, including text, audio,
images, and video [1, 2, 3, 4]. In this context, text-to-music
generation has attracted significant attention owing to its ex-
ceptional interactivity and user-friendliness. By enabling users
to guide and control the music creation process through natu-
ral language descriptions, it greatly lowers the barrier to music
composition and opens up unprecedented opportunities for per-
sonalized music generation [5, 6, 7].
The field of text-to-music generation has witnessed sub-
stantial progress in recent years, giving rise to a series of in-
fluential models. Among them, autoregressive approaches such
as MusicLM [8] and MusicGen [5] leverage language models to
perform sequence modeling over quantized audio tokens, which
are subsequently reconstructed into waveforms by a decoder.
To accelerate generation while maintaining competitive quality,
non-autoregressive models exemplified by MAGNeT [9] adopt
a parallel decoding scheme based on masked token modeling.
Another major paradigm is diffusion-based modeling, with rep-
resentative works including Mousai [10], AudioLDM2 [11],
and the Stable Audio series [12, 13, 14], which typically operate
in the latent space of an autoencoder. Despite these advances,
current research exhibits a clear cultural bias: the majority of
these models are trained on datasets dominated by Western gen-
res such as electronic, pop, and rock music. Recent studies have

quantified this imbalance and shown that non-Western genres
account for only 5.7% of the total duration of existing music
datasets [15], which limits the capacity of current models to
generate music from non-Western traditions [16].
Against this backdrop, several recent studies have begun
to investigate traditional Chinese music, a stylistically distinc-
tive yet underrepresented genre. For example, Cao et al. [17]
explored the generation of Chinese-style music conditioned on
video content. Chen et al. [18] proposed MusicMamba to gener-
ate traditional melodies in the symbolic domain. CCMusic [19]
and ChMusic [20] were developed to facilitate classification and
detection tasks. However, these efforts do not address the core
challenge of directly generating traditional Chinese music from
text descriptions, largely due to the lack of dedicated text-audio
paired datasets. Constructing such a dataset is itself non-trivial:
the scarcity and dispersion of high-quality recordings, the selec-
tion of representative instruments and performance styles, and
the composition of accurate, culturally nuanced textual descrip-
tions all pose considerable difficulties. This data bottleneck has
directly impeded the application and advancement of text-to-
music generation for traditional Chinese music.
To bridge this gap, we presentCTMusic, a dataset specifi-
cally curated for text-to-music generation of traditional Chinese
music. In this work, we focus on instrumental music, which is a
core and representative category of this genre. To the best of our
knowledge, CTMusic is the first text-audio paired resource ded-
icated to this specific task. We validate its quality by fine-tuning
Stable Audio Open on CTMusic, and further incorporate Test-
Time Training (TTT) [21] layers into the model to enhance its
capacity to capture the stylistic features of traditional Chinese
music. Our main contributions are as follows:
• We propose CTMusic, a text-audio paired dataset of tradi-
tional Chinese instrumental music designed for text-to-music
generation, consisting of 741 pairs with a total duration ex-
ceeding 42 hours.
• We demonstrate the effectiveness of CTMusic by fine-tuning
Stable Audio Open. Experimental results show that CTMusic
can support the model in learning and generating traditional
Chinese instrumental music.
• We improve Stable Audio Open by integrating it with TTT
layers, enabling the model to better capture the unique char-
acteristics of this musical genre.

2.1. Data collection and preprocessing
The quality of the audio corpus is crucial for building a reliable
dataset. At this stage, our goal was to collect high-quality and
diverse samples of traditional Chinese instrumental music. To
ensure this, we adopted a systematic and multi-stage data col-
lection strategy.
First, we conducted an extensive survey of traditional Chi-
nese music resources to identify potential data sources. Unlike
Western music, we found that high-quality recordings of tra-
ditional Chinese music are highly scattered and scarce, with no
single centralized academic or commercial repository available.
This situation necessitated a multi-source acquisition approach.
During our survey, we assessed various channels, including aca-
demic archives, commercial databases, and short video plat-
forms. Based on criteria such as audio fidelity, content diversity,
and metadata reliability, we ultimately identified two comple-
mentary sources. The first consists of publicly accessible digi-
tal albums released by professional institutions on online music
streaming services. These recordings provide clean acoustic en-
vironments, making them ideal for modeling the core timbre of
instruments. The second is short video platforms, exemplified
by Bilibili. The value of this source lies in its diversity, featuring
a large number of performances by professional musicians, folk
artists, and students from music conservatories. By combining
these two sources, we established an initial audio corpus.
Subsequently, all collected audio underwent a rigorous
screening process to ensure the overall quality of the dataset. We
first employed automated scripts to filter out clips shorter than
20 seconds and leveraged PANNs [22] to exclude audio contain-
ing vocals or noise. Following this automated step, the remain-
ing audio files were manually reviewed by our team members to
further remove vocals and noise. In addition, we applied differ-
ent instrumentation rules for two subsets of CTMusic. Forthe
solo subset, we required the main melody to be performed by a
single traditional Chinese instrument, whereas no such restric-
tion was imposed onthe ensemble subset. This filtering process
yielded approximately 42 hours of curated audio material.
Finally, we applied standardized preprocessing to all se-
lected audio recordings to ensure consistent data specifications.
We observed that some tracks were excessively long (exceed-
ing 20 minutes). To make full use of these valuable resources,
we manually segmented these long files into shorter clips rather
than discarding them. This segmentation procedure limited the
audio duration to within 400 seconds while aiming to preserve

2.2. Data annotation
High-quality annotations are essential for constructing CTMu-
sic. At this stage, our goal was to obtain accurate and expres-
sive text descriptions of traditional Chinese instrumental music.
Therefore, we recruited a dedicated annotation team consisting
ofthree graduates from conservatory programsspecializing in
traditional Chinese instruments, each with over five years of
training and performance experience. To promote consistency
and descriptive depth in the annotations, we designed a struc-
tured three-paragraph description template, as shown in Table 1.
This template requires annotators to craft a holistic description
from three perspectives: (1)Instrumentation and Overall Im-
pression, (2)Musical Development and Variation, and (3)Mu-
sical Emotion and Potential Application Scenarios.

calibration session to align their understanding of the template.
Discrepancies were discussed and resolved to establish a con-
sistent annotation standard. Following the annotation phase, all
descriptions were reviewed by the authors of this paper, who
checked each entry against the template for completeness and
factual accuracy. Descriptions that did not satisfy the estab-
lished standard were returned to the original annotator for re-

2.3. Dataset statistics
Detailed statistics of CTMusic are presented in Table 2. The
solo subset covers 12 distinct types of traditional Chinese in-
struments, including dizi, drum, erhu, guan, guqin, guzheng,
pipa, sheng, suona, xiao, xun, and yangqin. The distribution is
approximately balanced, with roughly 50 samples per class and
1.72–4.19 hours of audio per instrument. The ensemble subset
contains 140 audio samples featuring various combinations of
multiple instruments. Individual audio clips range from 24 to
400 seconds in duration, and text descriptions are between 20
and 148 words. These statistics suggest that CTMusic can serve
as a valuable resource for advancing text-to-music generation
in the context of traditional Chinese instrumental music. More
details about CTMusic, including annotated audio samples, li-
cense information and a comparison with other Chinese music
datasets, are available on the project website.
3. Evaluation framework
This section introduces our evaluation framework, including an
overview and details of each part.
3.1. Overview
We conduct a systematic validation of our proposed CTMusic
dataset using Stable Audio Open. Furthermore, we enhance this
model to better generate audio that aligns with the distinct char-
acteristics of traditional Chinese instrumental music. This eval-
uation framework is structured around three key components:
the model, the training strategy, and the experimental setup. In
the following subsections, we first introduce Stable Audio Open
and the modifications we made to it. This is followed by the
progressive training strategy. Finally, we present the evaluation
metrics and experimental settings.

3.2.2. Diffusion Transformer with TTT layers
Traditional Chinese music is often characterized by intricate or-
namentations, dynamically evolving textures, and subtle rhyth-
mic nuances. Generating these details requires the model that
not only learns fundamental timbral features but also possesses
a strong capacity for modeling fine-grained dynamic changes
and complex details over long sequences. Inspired by Dalal et
al. [23], we augment the generative backbone of Stable Au-
dio Open with TTT layers. We term this modified block the
Transformer-TTT Block and the improved model SA-TTT. The
overall architecture of SA-TTT is illustrated in Figure 2.
Specifically, we integrate the TTT layer within the Trans-
former Block of the DiT, placing it after the cross-attention
layer. At this position, the audio representation has already been
enriched with long-range internal context through self-attention
and infused with global semantic guidance from the text prompt
through cross-attention. Inserting the TTT layer at this juncture
enables its expressive hidden states to finely modulate the audio
representation, facilitating the generation of audio that exhibits
higher textual alignment and more intricate details.
Moreover, to prevent potential performance degradation
from inserting randomly initialized layers into the pre-trained
model, we gate the TTT layer with a learnable vector initial-
ized to a small value (0.1) as Dalal et al. [23] did. Given the
non-causal nature of diffusion models, we also apply the bidi-
rectional trick [24] to use the TTT layer non-causally. Finally,
to strike an effective balance between computational overhead
and generation quality, we apply this modification sparsely, in-
tegrating a TTT layer only into the final block of every four
consecutive Transformer Blocks. This results in a total of six
Transformer-TTT Blocks within the 24-block DiT architecture.

3.3. Progressive training strategy
We employ atwo-stageprogressive training strategy to effi-
ciently guide the model in mastering the intricate characteris-
tics of traditional Chinese instrumental music. Concretely,the
first stageinvolves fine-tuning the pre-trained model on the solo
subset. It allows the model to first establish a foundational un-
derstanding of the timbral and melodic characteristics of var-
ious instruments because the solo data features clean timbres
and less variation. Inthe second stage, we fine-tune the model
trained in the first stage on the ensemble subset, aiming to fo-
cus on learning the intricate interplay, dynamic textures, and
nuanced interactions between multiple instruments.
3.4. Experimental setup
3.4.1. Training specifics
We split the dataset as follows: 60 samples from the solo subset
(five from each instrument) are randomly selected to form the
solo test set, with the rest allocated for training. Similarly, 30
samples are randomly chosen from the ensemble subset as its
test set. The remaining samples in the ensemble subset are used
for training, with each audio clip paired with both its original
and paraphrased descriptions presented in Section 2.2.
For Stable Audio Open, we fine-tune all self-attention and
cross-attention layers using Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA) [25]
with a rank of 128, while other parameters are kept frozen. For
our proposed SA-TTT, the corresponding layers share the same
LoRA fine-tuning configuration as Stable Audio Open, while
the newly added TTT layers are trained from scratch. We em-
ploy the AdamW optimizer with a learning rate of5×10 −5 for
LoRA parameters and1×10 −4 for TTT parameters. All train-
ing follows the aforementioned two-stage training strategy with
a batch size of 16. The first stage runs for 14,000 steps, and the
second stage for 6,000 steps.
3.4.2. Evaluation metrics
In the evaluation process, we employ both objective and sub-
jective metrics to comprehensively assess the quality of the
generated audio from multiple perspectives. For the objective
evaluation, we use FD openl3 [26] and KL passt [27] to mea-
sure the difference between the generated and reference audio.
CLAPscore [28] is employed to evaluate the adherence of the
audio to the given prompt. For the subjective evaluation, we as-
sess the overall quality (OVL) and the text alignment (TA) of
the generated samples. Both subjective metrics are mean opin-
ion scores on a 5-point Likert scale.
4. Results
In this section, we report the experimental results and provide
an in-depth analysis. Representative generated samples are also
provided on the project website.
4.1. Objective results
The objective results in Table 3 yield two main observations.
SA-LoRA outperforms SA-pretrained across all metrics in both
stages, demonstrating that fine-tuning on CTMusic enables the
pre-trained model to effectively adapt to traditional Chinese in-
strumental music, a culturally unique music domain that is dis-
tinct from the model’s original training data. Notably, the rela-
tively poor performance of SA-pretrained suggests that it has
limited capacity to generate traditional Chinese instrumental
music, which highlights the necessity of constructing dedicated
datasets for underrepresented non-Western music genres.
SA-TTT consistently performs better than SA-LoRA in all
objective metrics, indicating improved distributional similarity
to real recordings and better alignment with textual prompts.
The performance gains are observed for both the solo subset
(stage 1) and the ensemble subset (stage 2), suggesting that the
incorporation of TTT layers enhances the model’s ability to cap-
ture acoustic and stylistic characteristics of traditional Chinese
instrumental music under varying levels of musical complexity.
4.2. Subjective results
We conducted the subjective evaluation with 20 participants
who were not involved in this study, one-third of whom had
prior learning experience in music or traditional Chinese instru-
ments. The corresponding results are presented in Table 3. The
subjective evaluation outcomes are consistent with the objective
findings. SA-LoRA achieves higher OVL and TA scores than
SA-pretrained, indicating that fine-tuning on CTMusic dataset
improves the perceptual quality and textual relevance. SA-TTT
further improves the generation quality by producing richer and
more nuanced details. These results provide additional evidence
that CTMusic supports effective domain adaptation, and that in-
corporating TTT layers is beneficial for generating traditional
Chinese instrumental music.
5. Conclusion
In this work, we proposed CTMusic, a traditional Chinese in-
strumental music dataset specifically designed for text-to-music
generation. Our experiments have not only demonstrated its ef-
fectiveness but also highlighted the need for datasets in cultur-
ally specific domains. Building on this dataset, we developed
an improved model capable of generating traditional Chinese
instrumental music with higher fidelity to its cultural traits.
Beyond its direct application in fine-tuning generative mod-
els, CTMusic can also serve as a complementary resource for
large multimodal models, enriching their understanding of this
unique musical genre. In future work, we plan to further expand
it in terms of overall scale and instrument coverage, and explore
the automatic annotation method while maintaining quality.
