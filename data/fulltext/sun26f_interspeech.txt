Decoding the Trade-off: A Large-Scale Analysis of Latency and Stability in
LLM-based Speech Translation Cascades
Shinyoung Sun1, Taehoon Kim1∗
1 Graduate School of Virtual Convergence, Sogang University, Republic of Korea
{steeles, taehoonkim}@sogang.ac.kr
Abstract
Real-time subtitling must balance low latency with sta-
ble translations. In segment-level cloud cascades, ag-
gressive endpointing increases request rate and can push
utilization beyond service capacity, triggering a backlog
avalanche in which queueing delay dominates and “fast”
settings become slower in steady state. We present RST,
a reproducible KO→EN subtitle pipeline that distinguishes
time-to-first-text (latency first) and time-to-stable-text
(latency stable). On the full FLEURS corpus, we show
that aggressive endpointing drives cascades into a backlog
regime (median RTF>1), where latency stable grows over
time. Whisper prompt conditioning further degrades short-
chunk streaming ASR by +14–17 pp CER and can leak instruc-
tions. Sweeps reveal a clear stability boundary. We define an
operational threshold as the most aggressive setting with me-
dian RTF≤1, showing that throughput stability is essential for
low-latency LLM-based cascades.
Index Terms: speech translation, real-time subtitling, latency,
stability, evaluation
1. Introduction
Real-time subtitling for cross-lingual meetings and lectures re-
quires both low latency and stable, readable translations. In
practice, production systems are cascades composed of audio
capture, endpointing, ASR, and MT. These systems must de-
cide when to emit text, and users typically observe two mo-
ments: an early partial output and a later stabilized subtitle.
However, most system-level evaluations report a single end-
to-end latency, obscuring the distinction between perceived re-
sponsiveness and final stability.
Segmentation (endpointing) is a central but under-analyzed
control knob. Beyond determining subtitle update frequency,
it defines the unit of work sent to upstream cloud ASR and
MT services. In segment-level (non token-streaming) cascades,
shortening segments increases the request rate. When request
arrival exceeds effective service capacity, queueing delay ac-
cumulates and can dominate end-to-end latency—even if each
individual request is fast. As a result, aggressive “fast” settings
may become slower in steady state. Despite its practical impor-
tance, this throughput–stability interaction is rarely quantified
in realistic cloud-based pipelines.
This interaction creates a trilemma between segmentation
granularity (frequent updates), throughput stability, and con-
textual completeness required for accurate translation. Under-
standing this trade-off is essential for designing sustainable real-
time subtitle systems.

In this work, we study a controlled KO→EN subtitle cas-
cade and systematically analyze how segmentation and model
combinations shift the latency–quality frontier. We explic-
itly distinguish two user-facing latency markers: time-to-first-
text (ASR completion) and time-to-stable-text (final translation
emission). Separating these allows us to determine whether
a change improves perceived responsiveness or merely redis-
tributes delay across stages.
To enable reproducible and system-level analysis, we in-
strument the running application with a logging wrapper that
records segmentation boundaries and newline-delimited JSON
events from both ASR and MT stages. This produces structured
per-utterance records with raw timestamps and derived latency
metrics. We evaluate latency distributions jointly with offline
quality metrics under a controlled replay protocol that removes
microphone and audio-device variance, ensuring that all config-
urations observe identical input waveforms.
Our contributions are as follows:
• RST, a reproducible KO→EN subtitle pipeline with struc-
tured per-utterance logging.
• A formal distinction between two user-facing latency mark-
ers,latency firstandlatency stable.
• A throughput-stable operating threshold (median RTF≤1)
identified via systematic endpointing sweeps over a2×2
ASR–MT cascade.
Our large-scale evaluation on the full FLEURS KO→EN
corpus reveals aspeed paradox: when aggressive endpoint-
ing increases request arrival beyond effective service capac-
ity, segment-level cloud cascades enter a backlog regime (me-
dian RTF>1) in which queueing delay dominates and
latency stablegrows over time. Consequently, nominally
“fast” settings become slower in steady state.
We further observe a second instability under short-chunk
streaming. Whisper prompt conditioning, commonly used to
inject contextual information, degrades KO CER by +14–17 pp
relative to prompt-free decoding and can leak instruction text
into hypotheses, suggesting that context mechanisms designed
for long-form transcription may behave unpredictably under
endpointed segmentation.
Finally, large-scale endpointing sweeps expose a clear sta-
bility boundary around median RTF≈1. Below this boundary,
latency remains bounded; above it, queueing delay accumulates
over time. Selecting the most aggressive configuration that sat-
isfies median RTF≤1yields the best latency–stability oper-
ating point. Together, these findings demonstrate that through-
put stability is a prerequisite for achieving low latency in LLM-
based cascaded systems.

2. Related Work
2.1. Simultaneous ST and Evaluation Realism
Simultaneous speech translation (SimulST) studies latency–
quality trade-offs under a range of evaluation metrics, and prior
work shows that conclusions can depend strongly on metric
choice [1]. Recent critiques emphasize realistic end-to-end
evaluation under streaming constraints, rather than assuming
pre-segmentation or ignoring component runtimes [2, 3]. In
contrast, we analyze segment-level cloud cascades and explic-
itly distinguish perceived latency from stabilized latency.
2.2. Segmentation and Endpointing
Segmentation has long been used to balance latency and quality
in cascaded streaming ST [4, 5, 6]. Prior work primarily frames
segmentation as a latency–quality knob. We instead focus on its
systems-level impact on request rate and throughput stability
in cloud-based pipelines, where shorter segments can induce
backlog even when per-request inference is fast.
2.3. End-to-End Audio LLMs
Recent multimodal LLMs perform transcription and translation
end-to-end [7, 8]. However, cascaded pipelines remain com-
mon in production due to modularity and controllability. Our
work provides a reproducible protocol and a stability-focused
analysis tailored to such cascaded systems.
3. Method
RST is a modular desktop real-time speech translation pipeline.
Incoming audio is segmented via V AD-based endpointing, tran-
scribed by a configurable STT engine, translated by a con-
figurable MT engine, and rendered as subtitles. We focus
on two user-facing latency markers,latency firstand
latency stable, which separate perceived responsiveness
from subtitle finalization.
3.1. Segmentation and Cascade
RST segments audio into utterances using WebRTC V AD with
a small set of parameters (aggressiveness, minimum/maximum
chunk duration, hangover time). Each utterance triggers a sin-
gle ASR request followed by a single MT request. In the cur-
rent implementation, both stages emit one final hypothesis per
utterance (no token-level streaming). As a result, endpointing
directly controls request arrival rate: shorter segments increase
calls to upstream cloud services, making throughput and back-
log effects first-order.
3.2. Latency Definitions
For each utteranceu, lett end(u)denote the segment boundary
time. We define:
•latency first(u):t ASR(u)−t end(u),
•latency stable(u):t MT(u)−t end(u).
The first captures time to any visible output; the second cap-
tures time to finalized translation. We compute the real-time
factor
RTF(u) = latency stable(u)
segment duration(u).
When median RTF>1, the system cannot sustain steady-state
processing and queueing delay accumulates.

3.3. Reproducible Logging
The backend emits newline-delimited JSON events for
segmentation boundaries, ASR outputs, and MT outputs.
A logging wrapper records this stream without modify-
ing application logic and produces one JSONL record per
utterance containing raw timestamps (first token ms,
stable ms), derived latency metrics (latency first ms,
latency stable ms, RTF), engine identifiers, and segmen-
tation metadata. Offline quality metrics are attached to the same
schema for joint latency–quality analysis.
4. Experiments
We evaluate how endpointing (segmentation) and model choice
jointly affect user-facing latency, throughput stability, and
KO→EN translation quality in a segment-level cascade.
4.1. Language direction and model matrix
We focus on KO→EN. We evaluate a2×2cas-
cade with STT engines{whisper-1, GPT-4o Tran-
scribe (gpt-4o-transcribe)}and MT engines
{gpt-4.1-mini,grok-4-fast-non-reasoning}.
Whisper [9] serves as an acoustic baseline, and GPT-4o
Transcribe [10] represents an LLM-based ASR service.
4.2. Segmentation regimes
Endpointing determines utterance boundaries and therefore the
request arrival rate in segment-level cascades. We compare two
presets:
fast: V AD=3, min=240 ms, max=2500 ms, hang-
over=120 ms,
stable: V AD=1, min=480 ms, max=4000 ms, hang-
over=240 ms.
Thefastpreset approximates rapid turn-taking timescales
and is used as a stress-test regime [11]. Thestablepreset
uses longer minimum duration and hangover to reduce request
pressure.

4.5. Endpointing sweep and stability threshold
Beyond the two presets, we sweep endpointing aggressiveness
by varying minimum segment duration and hangover time while
keeping a maximum duration cap. This is used to map the
boundary between throughput-stable and backlog regimes. We
define an operational threshold as the most aggressive setting
that maintains median RTF≤1on the evaluation corpus.
4.6. Metrics and replay protocol
We report KO CER, EN BLEU/chrF, and latency distributions
forlatency firstandlatency stable. Latency is
summarized by median and interquartile range (IQR). Real-
time factor is computed as
RTF= latency stable
segment duration.
For offline quality scoring, we concatenate per-utterance out-
puts back into clip units and compare against line-aligned refer-
ences.
All experiments use a controlled replay protocol derived
from FLEURS [12]. Each clip is decoded to mono PCM and
fed directly into the same V AD endpointing path used for live
audio, ensuring identical waveforms across configurations. Re-
play runs in real time, with a 2 s silence gap between clips to
reduce boundary merges. We evaluate the full KO→EN subset
(2,851 clips;∼9.6 h total audio).

5.4. Endpointing threshold from large-scale sweeps
Sweeping endpointing aggressiveness reveals a stability
boundary around median RTF≈1. Below this boundary,
latency stableremains bounded and scales well with
evaluation horizon; above it, queueing delay accumulates with
time-on-task. This motivates a simple tuning criterion for cas-
caded cloud systems: select the most aggressive endpointing
setting that keeps median RTF≤1 on representative workload
data.

6. Discussion
6.1. Whyfastloses in segment-level cloud cascades
In segment-level cascades, shorter segments increase the re-
quest arrival rateλ. Whenλapproaches or exceeds the effective
service rateµ, utilizationρ=λ/µreaches or surpasses 1, and
the system enters a backlog regime wherelatency stable
is dominated by queueing delay rather than per-request com-
pute. Little’s Law (N=λW) [13] explains why delay can
accumulate with time-on-task as in-flight requests grow. Fig-
ure 2a supports this mechanism: increasing service time shifts
the tipping point to larger chunks and steepens the queueing in-
crease.
6.2. Prompting instability under endpointed short chunks
Under endpointed short chunks, Whisper prompt conditioning
degrades ASR quality: CER increases to 43.7% (rolling) and
40.8% (static) from 26.5% without prompts, and static prompt-
ing leaks prompt text in 7.9% of hypotheses. This suggests that
prompt mechanisms tuned for long-context transcription can be
unstable when segmentation frequently resets context; prompt-
free decoding is the more robust streaming baseline.
6.3. A linguistic latency floor for KO→EN
Korean (SOV) to English (SVO) translation requires a minimum
context window. Figure 2b shows BLEU improving up to∼2–
3 s and then saturating, indicating a KO→EN context threshold
in segment-level cascades. Pushing endpointing below this win-
dow can reduce translation quality even if throughput remains
stable.
7. Conclusion
We presented RST, a reproducible KO→EN subtitle cascade, to
evaluate latency–stability trade-offs under segment-level cloud
processing. Our results show that throughput stability is a pre-
requisite for low latency: aggressive endpointing can induce
a backlog regime (RTF>1) in which queueing delay dom-
inateslatency stableand degrades both responsiveness
and translation quality. We also find that Whisper prompt con-
ditioning is unreliable under endpointed short chunks, degrad-
ing KO CER by +14–17 pp and introducing instruction leakage;
prompt-free decoding is the more robust streaming baseline.
