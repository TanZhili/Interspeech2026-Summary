VINA Y AKA: Multilingual Audio-Visual Hate Speech Detection via
Cross-Modal Fusion in Hyperbolic Space
Bhavinkumar Vinodbhai Kuwar1,2,∗, Orchid Chetia Phukan 3,∗, Rajesh Sharma 1,4
1 Plaksha University, India 2 IIIT-Delhi, India 3 NTHU, Taiwan 4 University of Tartu, Estonia

Abstract
In this work, we investigate the robustness of audio–visual (A V)
cues for Multilingual Audio–Visual Hate Speech Detection (M-
A VHSD). We proposeVINA Y AKA, a novel framework for
M-A VHSD that relies solely on A V cues for decision-making.
Along with WavLM and ImageBind, it employs Cross-modal
Fusion in Hyperbolic Space (CFHS), which models the effec-
tive alignment between paralinguistic and behavioral A V cues
to better capture hateful intent in temporal audio signals and
corresponding visual scenes. Empirical results demonstrate that
the proposed framework achieves state-of-the-art performance
in both in-distribution and out-of-distribution settings across
cross-lingual and cross-dataset evaluations. Furthermore, the
results show that the framework remains robust to ASR error
propagation.
Index Terms: Multimodal Hate Speech Detection, Audio-
Visual Learning, Paralinguistic, Behavioral, Hyperbolic space
1. Introduction
With the widespread adoption of online media platforms, the
production and consumption of short- and long-form video con-
tent have contributed to the propagation of hate speech. This
makes hate speech detection (HSD) in video content being an
important task for content moderation systems. In the real
world, video content often involves code-mixing and code-
switching across languages, especially in multilingual contexts
such as India [1], where words are mixed between languages,
or languages change across speakers. In such multilingual set-
tings, the reliance on text-based representations for HSD is of-
ten unreliable. Moreover, hateful intent in online videos is not
always conveyed through speech; visual cues may convey hos-
tility, as gestures, symbols, or scenes, even when speech is neu-
tral or absent. These challenges motivate us to investigate non-
lexical audio-visual (A V) cues and to formulate multilingual
audio-visual hate speech detection (M-A VHSD) that aims to
detect hateful intent in videos without depending on language-
specific textual cues.
Prior work treats hate speech detection as a purely text-
classification task, focusing on lexical features [2] [3] [4]. Mov-
ing away from keyword-based methods, [5] explored sequential
modeling, and [6] explored contextual representations by lever-
aging language-based transformer models. As summarized by
Paula et al. [7], despite advances in modeling, automated con-
tent moderation systems remain heavily biased towards textual
cues, with negligible consideration towards non-lexical cues.
Several works extended hate speech detection to speech. [8]
[9] [10] predominantly operate on Automatic Speech Recog-

nition(ASR) transcripts, eventually reducing speech to text. To
address the limitations of text-only settings, researchers have
begun incorporating paralinguistic features of speech. For
instance, [11] demonstrated the importance of acoustic cues.
Work on the ADIMA dataset shows that prosodic features like
loudness and jitter are significant indicators of abusive behav-
ior [11]. These findings show that the hostility is often found
in the way someone speaks rather than just the words they
use [12]. Beyond speech, efforts were made to explore static vi-
sual cues for hate or abusive content detection in the context of
imagery, symbols, or memes [13] [14] demonstrating that hate-
ful intent can also be conveyed through visuals in the absence of
speech. More recently, multimodal approaches have been pro-
posed that integrate audio, visual, and textual cues for HSD. Das
et al. [15] provide a multimodal dataset and investigate the com-
bination of audio, visual, and textual representations through
cross-modal attention mechanisms. In the context of short-
form videos, Maity et al. [16] introduced a multimodal bench-
mark for Hindi-English code-mixed content. Though these ap-
proaches [15] [16] [17] primarily rely on textual representa-
tion for decision-making, as textual cues are less susceptible
to noise. Wang et al. [18] provided a cross-cultural perspec-
tive, benchmarking hate speech detection across English- and
Chinese-language videos. Similarly, Ranjan et al. [19] explored
multilingual HSD in synthetic audio. In an adjacent domain,
Phukan et al. [20] made an effort to detect child-harmful con-
tent through audio-visual alignment. Further, for fine-grained
HSD, Zhang et al. [21] provided the largest multimodal video
dataset to date.
Despite recent strides in multimodal hate speech detection
(MHSD) [17] [22] [23], several questions remain unanswered.
The decision-making process in multimodal settings is domi-
nated by textual representations. As a consequence, such sys-
tems remain highly susceptible to ASR error propagation [24],
leading to degraded performance under cross-lingual, code-
mixed, and domain shifts. Works increasingly included audio
and visual signals but treated them as auxiliary. Their behav-
ioral and paralinguistic characteristics, such as prosody, tim-
ing, and facial dynamics, which are main indicators of hateful
intent, have not been investigated. Furthermore, the extent to
which current frameworks generalize to multilingual and out-
of-distribution settings, where textual cues are unreliable, is not
investigated. Most prior approaches operate within Euclidean
representation spaces. This leaves it unclear whether the inher-
ently hierarchical structure of behavioral cues can be effectively
modeled in an alternative geometric representations, like hyper-
bolic space.
To address this gap, we investigate the extent to which A V
cues can support robust M-A VHSD without relying on textual
representations across multilingual and out-of-distribution con-

2. Methodology
In this section, we first discuss the pre-trained models (PTMs)
used for feature extraction, then the methodologies for uni-
modal and multimodal baselines with various fusion methods,
and our proposed frameworkVINA Y AKA.
2.1. Feature Extraction
We select WavLM [25] and the ImageBind visual encoder [26]
to obtain speech embeddings from raw waveforms and visual
embeddings from sampled video frames, respectively. WavLM
is pre-trained on 94K hours of unlabeled speech data using tech-
niques such as masked prediction and utterance mixing. The
pre-training corpus spans diverse acoustic conditions, encom-
passing YouTube videos, audiobooks, and political speeches,
enabling WavLM to learn robust paralinguistic features, such
as prosodic and speaker-dependent features. ImageBind is a
multimodal model pre-trained to map six modalities, includ-
ing vision and audio, into a unified embedding space. In our
setup, we pass uniformly sampled video frames into the frozen
visual encoder, obtaining tubelet-based spatiotemporal embed-
dings that capture motion patterns, scene-level, and object-level
understanding present in the video. Both architectures are kept
frozen to obtain the embeddings for downstream processing.
2.2. Modeling
2.2.1. Unimodal
In unimodal settings, both modalities follow a similar down-
stream network: feature extraction from PTMs, a CNN-based
architecture, and a Fully Connected Network (FCN). The ex-
tracted features are then passed through the two 1D-CNN lay-
ers (64 and 128 filters, kernel size 3), each followed by max
pooling (pool size = 2). The resulting features are then flattened
and passed through the FCN block with a dense layer of 128
neurons. The output layer with a softmax activation function
produces the class probabilities.
2.2.2. Multimodal Baseline Audio-Visual Fusion Methods:
We also implement multiple fusion strategies, including fea-
ture concatenation (CON), Euclidean cross-attention (ECA),
M¨obius addition (MA), and cross-modal fusion in hyperbolic
space (CFHS) as in ourVINA Y AKAmodel. For comparison
with state-of-the-art models, we use the official implementa-
tions of MM-HSD [17] and ToxVidLM [16]. As no source code
is available for the MultiHateClip [18], we replicate its architec-
ture strictly following the methodologies described in the origi-
nal paper. All the models are trained under the same experimen-
tal settings. In CON, embeddings from WavLM and ImageBind
are passed through separate CNNs and pooled pipelines, as in
unimodal settings, then concatenated and fed to the FCN block
for classification. For ECA and MA, the same pipeline is used
until fusion and after fusion. For ECA, a cross-modal attention
mechanism is applied, and the attended features are fused. For
MA, the features are projected to separate hyperbolic Poincar ´e
balls, then fused via the M¨obius addition operation.

3. Experiments
3.1. Dataset
HateMM[15]: It contains 1083 videos (431 Hateful and 652
Non-Hateful) of approximately 43 hours and 144K frames cu-
rated from the BitChute social video-hosting platform. It serves
as a stable English-only source domain with clear A V hate cues.
ToxCMM[16]: It is a code-mix of Hindi-English language
from, a conversation dataset with 4021 utterances curated from
931 videos scraped from YouTube. We have followed the offi-
cial binary split, labeling ’Toxic’ as ’Hateful’ and ’Non-Toxic’
as ’Non-Hateful’. This dataset supports our study, as ASR and
textual representations are unreliable in code-mixed settings.
MultiHateClip[18]: It comprises 2000 samples curated from
platforms such as YouTube and Bilibili. It contains English
(MHCE) and Chinese (MHCC) videos labelled as ’Hateful’,
’Offensive’, and ’Normal’. We have followed ’Hateful’ and
’Offensive’ samples as ’Hateful’, and ’Normal’ labeled samples
as ’Non-Hateful’. It serves our purpose as a cross-cultural and
cross-domain dataset.
3.2. Training Details
For HMM (HateMM), ToxCMM, MHCE, and MHCC, we
adopted 5-fold cross-validation splits across all experiments.
For cross-dataset evaluation, all models were trained on the
source dataset and evaluated directly on the target dataset, with-
out target-domain finetuning. To obtain ASR transcripts, we
used Whisper [27] and PaddleOCR [28] for OCR wherever re-
quired for text-dependent baselines. For all models, including
unimodal and multimodal baselines, we use the Adam opti-
mizer with an initial learning rate of1e −4, a batch size of 16,
a Binary cross-entropy loss function, and early stopping based
on the validation macro-F1 score. The reported accuracy and
macro-F1 scores are averaged across folds. To show the scal-
ability ofVINA Y AKA, we follow zero-intervention policy for
all baselines and their respective modalities, unlike prior works
where [16] that relied on manual transcript cleaning.

reliance on lexical cues. WhileVINA Y AKArelies on paralin-
guistic and behavioral A V cues, it demonstrates a compara-
tively stable performance. Also, the WavLM’s prosodic fea-
tures and ImageBind’s high-level semantic representations ex-

with our motivation:text-based systems are highly susceptible
to ASR error propagation and degrade under cross-lingual and

consistently shows smaller drops and performs better than other

and behavioral A V cues, when effectively modeled via CFHS,
provide superior stability than text-centric baselines in cross-
lingual, cross-dataset settings.
4. Conclusion
In this study, we investigated the robustness of A V cues for
M-A VHSD. We demonstrated that A V cues solely, without re-
lying on textual cues shows stable and improved performance
in in-distribution and out-of-distribution settings, compared to
other text-centric baselines. This highlights the critical advan-
tage of avoiding ASR error propagation. Our novel frame-
work,VINA Y AKA, that aligns paralinguistic and behavioral
A V cues from WavLM and ImageBind via cross-modal fusion
in hyperbolic space, effectively model cross-modal interactions
and achieve state-of-the-art performance on M-A VHSD. Empir-
ically, our framework demonstrates improved stability in cross-
lingual and cross-dataset transfer scenarios. Overall, our find-
ings support our hypothesis that paralinguistic and behavioral
A V cues are more robust than text-centric representations in
multilingual and out-of-distribution settings.
