Eye and Mouth Cues in Audiovisual Perception of Mandarin Irony: Evidence 
from Eye-Tracking 
Shifeng Xia, Shanpeng Li 
MIIT Key Lab for Language Information Processing and Applications (LIPA), School of Foreign 
Studies, Nanjing University of Science and 
Technology, China

Abstract 
Irony is a form of non-literal language whose comprehension 
involves integrating auditory and visual cues. Although 
prosodic cues have been widely studied across languages, the 
role of facial cues remains underexplored. This study 
investigated perceptual strategies for different facial regions in 
decoding Mandarin ironic speech. Forty-four native speakers 
completed a perception experiment including ironic blame and 
praise and their literal counterparts under Visual -only, 
Audiovisual (quiet), and Audiovisual (noise) conditions. Eye-
tracking measured fixation duration and pupil dilation. Results 
showed distinct visual patterns: ironic blame elicited longer 
fixations on the eyes, with no pupil size differences between 
regions, whereas ironic praise prompted greater attention to the 
mouth across modalities, with larger pupil size during eye 
fixations in the quiet audiovisual condition. These findings 
reveal asymmetric visual processing across irony types and 
inform multimodal intention recognition. 
Index Terms: interpretation of irony, multimodal cues, facial 
regions, eye-tracking, Mandarin Chinese 
1. Introduction 
Verbal irony is a typical form of non-literal language in which 
speakers intentionally convey meanings opposite to their literal 
statements, assuming listeners can discern their genuine 
intentions [10, 25]. Based on the nature of the evaluative 
attitude expressed, irony is categorized into two types: ironic 
blame (i.e., sarcasm) and ironic praise. Ironic blame involves 
communicating negative intentions through positive literal 
meanings, for instance, saying “Great job!” to someone who has 
failed a task, with the actual intent of criticism. In contrast, 
ironic praise expresses positive attitudes via negative literal 
statements, typically used as jocular remarks among close 
relationships [27], such as playfully telling a partner who gave 
a thoughtful gift, “You’re terrible,” to convey appreciation. In 
real-life communication, ironic blame is far more prevalent and 
easier to recognize than ironic praise [18-19]. 
Despite the inherent risk of misinterpretation, irony is 
widely used due to its unique social functions. On the one hand, 
the discrepancy between literal meaning and genuine intent 
makes irony more humorous than direct speech [26, 30]. On the 
other hand, ironic blame mitigates the sharpness of criticism 
and reduces face threat, as proposed by the Tinge Hypothesis 
[29], making it more socially acceptable than direct reproach. 
However, the realization of these functions hinges on a critical 
premise: listeners must successfully recognize the ironic intent. 
Failure to do so may lead to literal interpretations, resulting in 
communication breakdowns or even interpersonal conflicts. 
Thus, exploring how listeners identify ironic intent has become 
a core issue in irony research. 
While the decoding of irony relies heavily on context, in 
face-to-face interactions, it is fundamentally a multimodal 
process involving the integration of information from auditory 
and visual channels [7, 15, 17, 22]. Cross-linguistic evidence 
shows that listeners effectively identify ironic blame when 
utterances are produced with an “ironic tone of voice”, with 
high accuracy rates in English, German, and French [1, 8, 9], 
yet research on ironic praise remains scarce. Li et al. [32] found 
that native Mandarin speakers achieved an accuracy rate of only 
31% in understanding ironic praise under audio-only conditions, 
indicating auditory information alone is insufficient. 
Few studies have investigated the role of visual channel in 
irony comprehension, but existing findings suggest that facial 
expressions can facilitate irony recognition. For example, facial 
expressions increased Catalan listeners’ accuracy in identifying 
ironic blame from 56% to 88% [31]. Furthermore, the effect of 
visual cues varies by irony type: in Italian, facial information 
helped 68% of listeners correctly interpret ironic praise but only 
38% for ironic blame [5]. 
Production of verbal irony is accompanied by systematic 
visual cues, primarily facial expressions including mouth 
movement such as smiles and stretched lips [6, 17, 23] and eye 
movements such as winking, gaze deviation, and raised 
eyebrows [11, 33]. A blank face has also been observed as a 
possible feature in ironic delivery [22]. However, the 
contribution of facial cues in the understanding of irony remains 
underexplored, a notable gap given the potential visual 
dominance in Mandarin irony [32]. Furthermore, previous 
studies suggest that different facial regions carry distinct 
informational weights in speech perception [3]. For example, 
Wegrzyn et al. [14] found that listeners rely primarily on eyes 
to recognize sadness and fear, and on the mouth to identify 
disgust and happiness. It is therefore reasonable to investigate 
whether eyes and mouth exert equivalent effects in irony 
processing, especially across the asymmetric categories of 
ironic blame and praise. Building on the audio-only baseline 
established in [32], the present study examines how visual cues 
are processed when irony is interpreted across different sensory 
conditions. 
Furthermore, most existing multimodal irony studies have 
been conducted in quiet laboratory environments, which lack 
ecological validity and often produce ceiling effects, also likely 
due to the concurrent presentation of clear context and 
multimodal cues [4, 16, 28]. According to the Fuzzy Logical 
Model of Perception (FLMP), degraded sensory channels 
increase reliance on the complementary modality [2]. In literal 
speech perception, noise typically shifts gaze toward the mouth 
to aid phonological decoding [13]. However, in the context of 
irony, it is unclear whether noise induces a trade-off between 
looking at the mouth for intelligibility versus the eyes for 
intention recognition. 
Additionally, behavioral measures often fail to reveal the 
real-time cognitive processes. Eye-tracking offers a more 
granular perspective: fixation duration on different facial 
regions reflects the distribution of cue information, while 
changes in pupil size indicate cognitive effort [34]. By adopting 
an eye-tracking paradigm, this study aims to address two 
questions: (1) Do listeners show divergent gaze preferences for 
the speaker’s eyes and mouth when interpreting two types of 
irony?  (2) how does environmental noise modulate attention 
allocation and cognitive load. The findings will deepen our

2. Methods 
2.1. Participants 
A total of 44 students (21 males and 23 females) from Nanjing 
University of Science and Technology participated in the eye-
tracking experiment. The age range was from 19 to 24 years old, 
with a mean age of 21.25 years (SD = 1.14). All participants 
were born and raised in Mandarin-speaking families and had no 
visual or auditory impairments. To ensure the reliability of eye-
movement tracking, all participants had a myopia degree lower 
than 500 diopters. The experiment lasted for 20-25 minutes. 
Participants signed informed consent forms and were 
remunerated for their participation. 
2.2. Materials 
2.2.1. Design of target sentences 
Adapted from materials in Li et al. [32], a total of 120 target 
sentences were designed, including 60 sentences with positive 
literal meanings (corresponding to 60 pairs of sincere praise and 
ironic blame) and 60 sentences with negative literal meanings 
(corresponding to 60 pairs of sincere blame and ironic praise). 
All target sentences were declarative statements, with 6 to 11 
syllables. 
To enable the speaker to naturally produce appropriate 
prosody and facial expressions for sentences with different 
attitudes during subsequent recordings, we adopted the 
Discourse Completion Task (DCT) method [24] and designed 
60 Mandarin scenarios. By manipulating event outcomes, each 
scenario could serve as the contextual background for both 
sincere and ironic expressions of two types. The examples 
below illustrate how one pair of target sentences was presented 
in positive-biased and negative-biased scenarios. 
(1) Positive-biased scenario 
You and A are roommates. You invite A to play poker. 
Before the game starts, A claims to be highly skilled at poker 
and rarely loses. After several rounds, you find that A indeed 
performs exceptionally well and does not lose a single round all 
night. At the end of the game, you say to A:  
● Sincere Praise Response: You really play poker well! (你
这牌打得果然很好！) 
● Ironic Praise Response: You really play poker poorly! (你
这牌打得果然很差！) 
(2) Negative-biased scenario 
You and A are roommates. You invite A to play poker. 
Before the game starts, A brags about being exceptionally 
skilled at poker and rarely losing. After several rounds, you find 
that A actually performs poorly and loses repeatedly. At the end 
of the game, you say to A: 
● Sincere Blame Response: You really play poker poorly! 
(你这牌打得果然很差！)  
● Ironic Blame Response: You really play poker well! (你这
牌打得果然很好！)  
2.2.2. Audiovisual recordings 
A 23-year-old female native speaker of Mandarin Chinese was 
recruited for audiovisual recording. She was born and raised in 
a monolingual Mandarin-speaking family with normal hearing 
and vision, and no experience in acting or broadcasting. The 
speaker provided written informed consent prior to the 
recording and was remunerated for her participation. 
The recording was conducted in a sound‑attenuated booth 
at the MIIT Key Lab for Language Information Processing and 
Applications, Nanjing University of Science and Technology. 
A SONY HXR‑NX100 camera was mounted on a tripod 
approximately 1 m from the speaker. The camera lens was 
positioned behind a teleprompter screen. An external condenser 
microphone was synced with the camera to ensure high-quality 
audio capture. The speaker stood facing the camera against a 
plain white background, with the camera framed to include her 
head and upper body. 
To ensure consistent production for each attitude (sincere 
praise, ironic blame, sincere blame, ironic praise), the four 
utterance types were recorded in four blocks, with self-paced 
rest between blocks. The speaker was required to pause for 
about 5 seconds after each target sentence recording to avoid 
carryover interference between consecutive utterances. No 
coaching or performance feedback was provided to the speaker 
during the entire session. 
2.2.3. Processing of recordings 
The video recordings were edited in Adobe Premiere Pro 2018 
to extract target clips and saved as AVI files using MPEG-4 
encoding, at 1920×1080 pixels and 30 fps. All audio tracks 
were normalized to an RMS level of 70 dB using a custom C# 
script and overlaid with 8-talker babble noise (4 male and 4 
female talkers, mixed-gender to avoid complete overlap with 
the female target speaker) at an SNR of -10 dB. This SNR value 
was determined through a pilot study to balance audibility and 
task difficulty. This SNR value was determined through a pilot 
study with four participants, who reported that target sentences 
were nearly inaudible at -12 dB but easily discernible at 0 or -6 
dB. The -10 dB was chosen to balance audibility and task 
difficulty. The video clips were re-edited to generate three 
stimulus conditions: visual-only (VO), audiovisual in quiet 
(AV-Quiet), and audiovisual in noise (AV-Noise). 
2.3. Experimental procedure 
The perception task involved three blocks, one block for each 
modality (VO, AV-Quiet, and AV-Noise). Each modality had 
60 trials in total with 15 trials for each attitude. Latin square 
randomization was used for the counterbalanced presentation of 
stimuli. 
The experiment was conducted in a soundproof laboratory. 
Ambient lighting was held constant throughout the session, 
with room lights on and curtains drawn. An Eyelink 1000 Plus 
was used to capture participants’ eye movements, utilizing a 
monocular tracking mode recording data from the left eye. The 
task was run on 19-inch 64-bit Windows 7 using Experiment 
Builder software (v2.4.1). Screen resolution was set to 800 × 
600 pixels with a refresh rate of 60 Hz. Participants were tested 
individually, wearing ECD-69 headphones and sitting 60 cm 
directly in front of the screen with a chin rest to fix head 
position, with their visual midline aligned to the monitor center. 
A 9-point calibration process was performed prior to the formal 
experiment, with a maximum allowable error of 1.0° of visual 
angle and a mean error below 0.5°. 
Before the experimental phase, a practice session of 12 
trials (four per modality) was conducted. After each video 
stimulus, two labels appeared on the screen prompting the 
participant to choose the speaker’s intended meaning using the 
“P” (赞扬 / Praise) or “Q” (批评 / Criticize) keys on the 
keyboard. Drift correction was enabled before each video, 
requiring the participant to align eye position with a central 
target. 
For eye-tracking data, three dynamic areas of interest (AOIs) 
were manually delineated using Data Viewer software (v3.2): 
the mouth, the eyes, and the whole face region (see Figure 1). 
The analysis time window for AOIs was defined as the period 
from video stimulus onset to the onset of the response prompt 
screen. Fixations were identified using the standard Eyelink 
velocity/acceleration threshold algorithm, with a velocity 
threshold of 30°/s, an acceleration threshold of 8000°/s², a 
saccadic motion threshold of 0.15°, and a minimum fixation

2.4. Data analysis 
Participants’ judgments of the speaker’s intent were coded as a 
binary variable, categorized as either correct (coded as 1) or 
incorrect (coded as 0). Specifically, a response was classified as 
correct if participants selected “criticize” for both sincere blame 
and ironic blame, or “praise” for both sincere praise and ironic 
praise; otherwise, it was coded as incorrect. For eye-tracking 
analyses, two dependent measures were adopted: proportional 
fixation duration on the eyes and mouth AOIs, calculated as the 
total fixation duration on each target AOI normalized by the 
total fixation duration on the whole face AOI (arcsine -
transformed prior to statistical modeling), and mean pupil size, 
defined as the average pupil diameter during valid fixations on 
each AOI and extracted from EyeLink Data Viewer in arbitrary 
units. No pre-stimulus baseline correction was applied for pupil 
size, as trials lacked a discrete fixation baseline period. Given 
that gaze position can systematically influence pupil size 
measurements [34] and comparisons were within-participant 
across AOIs, individual differences in baseline pupil size were 
accounted for by the by-participant random intercept in the 
linear mixed-effects models. 
For data cleaning, trials meeting any of the following 
criteria were excluded from all subsequent analyses: (1) 
reaction time exceeding 10 seconds (4 trials for ironic praise, 2 
trials for ironic blame, 1 trial for sincere praise); (2) no valid 
eye-tracking data recorded; (3) total fixation duration on the 
whole face AOI less than 100 ms. In total, 55 trials were 
removed, accounting for approximately 0.69% of all raw trials. 
For data analysis, mixed-effects analyses were conducted 
using the Gamlj module in Jamovi 2.3.28 [35]. For gaze pattern 
analysis, linear mixed-effects models were used to examine the 
effects of AOI, Modality, and Attitude on AOI total fixation 
duration and average pupil size. The model formulas were as 
follows: Dwell_Time_arcsin or Pupil Size~ AOI * Attitude * 
Modality + (1 | Participant) + (1 | Sentence). 
3. Results 
3.1. Fixation duration 
For literal blame pairs, the three-way interaction of AOI × 
Modality × Attitude (F (2, 6119) = 6.347, p = 0.002) was 
significant. The Bonferroni post hoc test showed that, as shown 
in Figure 2, when decoding sincere blame  (accuracy: VO: 
98.03%; AV-Quiet: 99.70%; AV-Noise: 96.52%), the mean 
proportion of fixation duration on the eyes significantly 
exceeded that on the mouth across all three modalities  (VO: β 
= 0.343, SE = 0.022, t = 15.587, p < 0.001; AV-Quiet: β = 0.490, 
SE = 0.022, t = 22.588, p < 0.001; AV-Noise: β = 0.233, SE = 
0.022, t = 10.560, p < 0.001). In contrast, for ironic praise 
(accuracy: VO: 92.42%; AV-Quiet: 16.62%; AV-Noise: 
67.58%), the reverse pattern emerged: mouth-looking exceeded 
eye-looking in the VO modality (β = -0.120, SE = 0.023, t = -
5.314, p < 0.001) and the AV-Noise modality (β = -0.186, SE = 
0.026, t = -7.058, p < 0.001), whereas the AOI difference was 
not significant in the AV-Quiet modality (β = -0.169, SE = 
0.054, t = -3.107, p = 0.125). 
Due to the scarcity of correct responses for ironic praise, a 
descriptive analysis of eye-movement data was conducted for 
all incorrect trials. The linear mixed model revealed no main 
effect of AOI (F (1, 1572) = 2.300, p = 0.130) or Modality (F 
(2, 1532) = 0.254, p = 0.775), but the AOI × Modality 
interaction was significant (F (2, 1572) = 13.385, p < 0.001). 
The post-hoc test showed that only in AV -Noise did 
participants spend a significantly smaller proportion of time 
looking at the eyes than at the mouth (β = -0.220, SE = 0.037, t 
= -6.013, p < 0.001); differences were not significant in VO (β 
= 0.110, SE = 0.076, t = 1.453, p = 1.000) or AV-Quiet (β = -
0.022, SE = 0.023, t = -0.965, p = 1.000). 
For literal praise pairs, the three-way interaction was again 
significant (F (2, 7238) = 3.113, p = 0.045). The post hoc test 
showed that (see Figure 3) for sincere praise (accuracy: VO: 
95.15%; AV-Quiet: 99.24%; AV-Noise: 96.97%), the mean 
proportion of fixation duration on the eyes significantly 
exceeded that on the mouth only in the AV-Quiet condition (β 
= 0.192, SE = 0.021, t = 9.219, p < 0.001), with no significant 
differences in VO (β = 0.063, SE = 0.022, t = 2.994, p = 0.214) 
or AV-Noise (β = -0.0515, SE = 0.021, t = -2.434, p = 0.987). 
For ironic blame (accuracy: VO: 94.70%; AV-Quiet: 82.67%; 
AV-Noise: 88.18%), however, the mean proportion of fixation 
duration on the eyes was consistently higher than that on the 
mouth across all three modalities (VO: β = 0.264, SE = 0.022, t 
= 12.199, p < 0.001; AV-Quiet: β = 0.310, SE = 0.023, t = 
13.499, p < 0.001; AV-Noise: β = 0.168, SE = 0.022, t = 7.576, 
p < 0.001).

4. Discussion 
The present study investigated how visual facial cues are 
processed during the comprehension of two types of Mandarin 
irony across three modalities, using eye-tracking to map visual 
attention allocation to distinct facial regions. Our findings 
revealed divergent visual attention patterns for the two types of 
irony and a clear association between gaze patterns and 
successful irony interpretation. 
For correctly interpreted ironic praise, participants in the 
AV-Quiet condition showed equal attention to the eyes and 
mouth, but fixations on the eyes were associated with larger 
pupil size. This modality presented the strongest incongruity, 
requiring integration of positive intentions from facial 
expressions and prosody alongside linguistic content from the 
mouth, consistent with research showing that upper face 
provides cues like eyebrow movement while lower face 
displays smiles [6, 11, 23]. The greater pupil size when fixating 
the eyes suggests heightened cognitive demands, possibly 
reflecting interference from negative literal meanings when 
extracting positive intentions from the eye region. Notably, this 
processing pattern differed from that for sincere praise, where 
participants showed greater attention to the eyes in the AV-
Quiet condition—confirming that participants distinguished the 
ironic nature. 
In VO and AV-Noise modalities, participants correctly 
interpreting ironic praise attended more to the mouth, with 
comparable pupil sizes for eyes and mouth fixations. As Król 
[13] noted, noisy environments increase attention to the mouth 
at the expense of eye fixation. This gaze pattern again differed 
from that for sincere praise, where equal attention was allocated 
to eyes and mouth under the same modalities, confirming that 
participants did not process ironic praise as if it were sincere. 
Across all three modalities, correct interpretation of ironic 
praise was consistently associated with greater attention to the 
mouth region. Participants who incorrectly interpreted ironic 
praise showed equal attention to eyes and mouth even in the VO
