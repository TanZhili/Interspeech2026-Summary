Abstract
The growth of audio generation has brought ethical and security
concerns over deepfakes, making deepfake detection critical to
preventing the malicious misuse of such technologies. Existing
methods largely focus on binary classification but fall short in
explaininghowaudio is manipulated,tracingorigins, orgen-
eralizingto unseen sources, limiting the explainability and re-
liability of detection. To address these issues, we presentFake-
Sound21, a benchmark designed to advance deepfake sound de-
tection beyond binary accuracy. It evaluates models across three
dimensions: localization, traceability, and generalization, cov-
ering 6 manipulation types and 12 sources. Experiments reveal
that existing models struggle to recognize forgery patterns, pro-
vide reliable explanations, and generalize well. FakeSound2
serves as a comprehensive benchmark that highlights key chal-
lenges and promotes the development of explainable, traceable,
and generalizable methods for trustworthy audio authentication.
Index Terms: AIGC, deepfake sound detection, explainability,
traceability, generalization
1. Introduction
The rapid advancement of generative artificial intelligence has
revolutionized synthetic media creation, enabling the produc-
tion of highly realistic audio content. However, the malicious
use of such technology, ranging from evidence fabrication to
disinformation campaigns, poses serious ethical and security
concerns, making robust deepfake detection imperative.
While deepfake detection has advanced significantly in vi-
sual [1] and speech [2, 3] domain, Deepfake Sound Detection
(DSD) remains understudied. Existing approaches [4, 5], typ-
ically formulate DSD as binary classification at the clip level,
determining only whether an audio sample is real or fake. Such
binary frameworks, however, offer limited forensic value: they
cannot localizewhenmanipulations occur, identifyhowaudio
was modified, or tracewherethe synthetic content was gener-
ated. These capabilities are critical for security-sensitive appli-
cations, legal proceedings, and trustworthy detection systems.
Recent generative audio models [6–8] evolve at an un-
precedented pace, exposing a critical flaw in current detection
paradigms: models trained to recognize artifacts of specific gen-
erators quickly become obsolete as soon as new synthesis meth-
ods appear. Our analysis (Section 5) reveals that state-of-the-art

By framing FakeSound2 as a diagnostic instrument rather
than a conventional benchmark, we embrace the rapid evolution
of generative models: the value lies not in comparing scores
on current sources, but in providing stable ground for investi-
gating how detection methods behave as synthesis technology
advances. We hope this work catalyzes research toward truly
robust, explainable deepfake detection that understand forgery,
not merely recognize yesterday’s artifacts.
2. Explainable DSD Definition
Establishing explainability in DSD necessitates a systematic
classification of potential audio manipulation techniques. As
outlined in Table 1, we surveyed prevalent methods and tempo-
rally divided them into clip-wise manipulation, wherein the en-
tire audio clip is synthetic, and frame-wise manipulation, where
only certain selected frames are altered. Clip-wise approaches
include (1)Generation, which synthesizes an entire audio clip
conditioned on a caption, and (2)Editing, which modifies a
given audio clip according to a target caption. Frame-wise ma-
nipulations comprise (3)Inpainting, where some frames are
masked then reconstructed based on a caption; (4)Separation,
which removes events specified by a target caption and splices
the remaining segments with genuine segments; (5)Splicing,
concatenating authentic clips guided by a caption; and (6)Ad-
dition, where an external audio segment is inserted into an orig-
inal clip according to a textual description. Each manipula-
tion method involves a distinct source of content, encompassing
generative models (1)–(4) and authentic audio sources (5)–(6).
Types (5)–(6) rely on real recordings instead of full synthe-
sis. We include them alongside generative fakes for a more
all-round benchmark covering diverse forgery patterns.
Based on the above observation, we posit that an ef-
fective DSD model should possess the key characteristics:
Localization: not only discriminate between authentic and ma-
nipulated audio but also achieve precise temporal localiza-
tion of forged segments in frame-wise manipulation scenarios.
Explainability: analyze deepfake audio in terms ofhowit was
manipulated—by identifying the specific manipulation method
andwherethe manipulated content originated—through tracing
the source of manipulated components. Generalization : main-
tain strong performance on unseen sources.
3. Fakesound2 Construction
This section details the construction of the FakeSound2 dataset,
a large-scale resource designed to address explainability and
generalization in DSD. Constructed through an automated
pipeline leveraging the AudioCaps [18] dataset, it encompasses
6distinct manipulation types derived from12audio sources
(11synthetic and1genuine). As summarized in Table 1, the
dataset ultimately comprises a training set of 369,929 samples
and a test set of 5,553 samples. Researchers can flexibly par-
tition the test subset to evaluate generalization, provided that
out-of-domain (OOD) sources remain completely unseen dur-
ing training. For instance, in our implementation, the test set is
divided into 3,896 in-domain and 1,657 OOD samples to sys-
tematically benchmark cross-domain detection robustness.
As illustrated in Fig. 2, the proposed pipeline comprises4
main stages: (1) Event localization, where semantically rich key
event segments are identified; (2) Metadata processing, which
employs large language models (LLMs) to generate target cap-
tions based on original captions and manipulation type; (3)
Audio manipulation, where generative models and rule-based
scripts are applied to modify audio content; (4) Quality filtering,
in which low-quality segments are removed to ensure dataset
reliability. This structured approach facilitates the scalable cre-
ation of a high-quality and traceable dataset suitable for rigor-
ous evaluation of deepfake sound detection systems.
3.1. Event localization
Starting from audio-caption pairs, we initially locate events in
the audio, which are referred to as key segments. These seg-
ments, which carry rich semantic information, are essential for
understanding the audio content, and any manipulation applied
to these regions produces the most noticeable and impactful
alterations [14]. Therefore, localizing the key segments is re-
garded as a critical starting point for deepfake detection.
The temporal boundaries (onset and offset) of sound
event are localized by leveraging a Text-to-Audio grounding
model [19]. While for inpainting task, segments are randomly
masked to enhance the model’s generalization ability.
3.2. Metadata Process
Leveraging the exceptional text-processing capabilities of
LLM, we employ DeepSeek [20] to simulate the human chain-
of-thought process in audio manipulation, as illustrated in part
II of Fig 2. Specifically, the framework first parses the caption
annotations into distinct event clauses; then selectively removes
existing events and inserts new ones based on the task objective.
For instance, the caption“a man talks and a sprayer
sprays”is partitioned into two separate clauses:“a man talks”
and“a sprayer sprays”. For both generation and inpainting
tasks, the target caption remains consistent with the original, un-
altered. For the other tasks, it might remove“a sprayer sprays”
and insert“a dog barks”. The inserted events are selected from
a library constructed from all parsed clauses and their corre-
sponding localized audio segments.
3.3. Audio Manipulation
To construct a dataset that addresses both explainability and
traceability, we leverage a diverse range of generative mod-
els and rule-based methods, resulting in a comprehensive and
richly annotated deepfake sound dataset. Deepfake audio is syn-
thesized by leveraging these models and scripts, conditioned on
the target caption. All models are run via their default configu-
rations. In the frame-wise scenario, forged segments are spliced
with authentic ones, resulting in a partially forged audio clip.
3.4. Quality filtering
To further ensure data quality, we conduct filtering in the test
set, resulting in a refined test-filtering (Test-F) subset. The
Contrastive Language-Audio Pretraining (CLAP) [21] model is
utilized as the evaluation metric, with the similarity between
the original audio and its corresponding caption serving as the
threshold. Any manipulated audio is discarded if its similarity
to the target caption falls below the threshold.
4. Experiment
We use FakeSound2 to evaluate model localization, explain-
ability, and generalization, uncover limitations in current DSD
methods, while also establishing a baseline for future research.

4.1. Baseline
The baseline model consists of an audio encoder and a back-
bone network, similar to Cai et al. [22], given their strong per-
formance in deepfake detection. The encoder employs a high-
performance self-supervised pre-trained model, EAT [23], to
extract fine grain representations. The backbone architecture
and parameters are identical to those in Cai et al. [22], compris-
ing a 12-layer ResNet flanked by Convolutional Neural Net-
work blocks, followed by a 2-layer Transformer encoder and a
1-layer bidirectional Long Short-Term Memory network. For
the classification component,3linear layers are used to predict
frame-level counterfeit detection labels, manipulation type, and
deepfake source, respectively. The predicted frame-level coun-
terfeit probabilities are subsequently processed with median fil-
tering.
The model is trained for10epochs using the AdamW opti-
mizer with a learning rate of1×10 −3. The encoder is frozen.
The frame-level detection task employs Binary Cross-Entropy
loss, while the manipulation type and source classification tasks
uses Cross-Entropy loss. The total loss is a weighted sum of
these losses with coefficients0.5,0.01, and0.01.
4.2. Evaluation Metric
Following ADD2023 [2], we employed F1 segment to evaluate
frame-level predictions. If the model predicts all frames within
a clip as genuine, the clip-wise label is predicted as authen-
tic; conversely, it is labeled as forged. The clip-wise iden-
tification accuracy, manipulation type accuracy, and deepfake
source accuracy are denoted as Acc identif y, Accmanipulation,
and Accsource, respectively.
5. Result and Analysis
The results in Table 2 demonstrate that, overall, current mod-
els exhibit strong localization capabilities but perform poorly in
explainability, particularly when dealing with unseen sources.
LocalizationThe model demonstrates strong localization ca-
pabilities, as evidenced by its high performance on metric
Accidentif y and F1 segment. This indicates its effectiveness
in accurately identifying and temporally localizing forged seg-
ments within the audio. A similar conclusion can be drawn from
the via t-SNE visualization in Figure 3: the features extracted
by the model exhibit considerable divergence in their spatial
distributions between authentic and forged audio, resulting in
strong binary classification performance. Meanwhile, for cate-
gories with authentic source audio (such as genuine, addition,
and splice), their feature representations are closely clustered,
indicating that the model has, to some extent, learned a robust
concept of “authenticity”.

ExplainabilityDespite proficient localization, the model ex-
hibits notable limitations in explainability. Metrics such as
Accsource and Acc manipulation remain low across certain
tasks, indicating an inability to meaningfully distinguish be-
tween manipulation types or attribute forgeries to specific
sources. For example, the Editing-type manipulations are fre-
quently misclassified as Generation-type forgeries. This short-
coming underscores the challenge of differentiating similar ma-
nipulation techniques and sources within the dataset. Figure 3
reveals that forged audio categories with similar task objectives
(e.g., Editing versus Generation) or model architectures (e.g.,
Affusion versus Audioeditor) exhibit entangled representations
in the latent space. This suggests that the model fails to learn
semantically meaningful distinctions between them.
GeneralizationThe performance exhibits significant degra-
dation on the majority of OOD tasks. As illustrated in Fig-
ure 3(b), the feature representations of X2Audio—a typical ex-
ample—appear highly scattered and nearly random, leading to
significantly lower detection accuracy compared to in-domain
evaluations. These observations indicate that the current model
fails to capture the underlying distribution of forged audio;
rather, it depends on recognizing training-set-specific artifacts,
resulting in poor generalization capability. Therefore, we have
identified several key challenges in current explainable deep-
fake sound detection: (1) Capturing fine-grained distributional
discrepancies arising from similar manipulation types or ho-
mogenized model architectures; (2) Enabling models to char-
acterize the true data distribution of authentic audio rather than
memorizing artificial forgery patterns, thereby enhancing gen-
eralization capability.
LimitationsWhile FakeSound2 focuses on the traceability
and generalizability for general audio, it has not sufficiently ex-
plored specific scenarios such as fake speech or audio-speech
joint manipulation, which will be addressed in future work.
6. Conclusion
This paper addresses a gap in deepfake sound detection by in-
troducing a new benchmark specifically designed for explain-
ability and generalization in synthetic audio identification. The
dataset aims to encourage further research within the commu-
nity, raise awareness of ethical and security concerns in AIGC
(AI-Generated Content), and provide justifiable guarantees for
the outcomes of detection models. Through a structured data
pipeline, we constructed6distinct manipulation types, along
with12manipulation sources, to provide diverse sources of
forged audio segments, thereby facilitating model training fo-
cused on source attribution. Results show that current models
perform well on real / fake classification and fake area localiza-
tion. However, they struggle with traceability tasks. This sug-
gests they do not truly understand fake audio content. We con-
tend that the primary challenge in DSD lies in (1) discerning
fine-grained discrepancies among similar manipulation types
and sources, while also (2) ensuring that models learn the under-
lying distribution of genuine audio rather than overfitting on ar-
tifactual patterns from forged training samples to enhance gen-
eralization. We hope this work establishes a solid foundation
and offers valuable insights to the field of DSD.
