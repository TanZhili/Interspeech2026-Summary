CSER: Semantic Evaluation of LLM Auto-Repair for Code-Switching ASR

Abstract
Code-switching presents significant challenges for ASR sys-
tems in voice assistants. While traditional lexical metrics like
WER, CER, and PIER measure transcription accuracy, they fail
to capture semantic preservation or downstream intent quality in
ASR–LLM pipelines. We introduceCode-Switching Semantic
ErrorRate (CSER), a metric designed to quantify the seman-
tic impact of specific errors—such as language misidentifica-
tion and minority-language substitution—while evaluating the
efficacy of LLM-based auto-repair in recovering intent. Along-
side CSER, we present an end-to-end evaluation framework and
a multi-condition Vietnamese–English code-switching bench-
mark. Our experiments across commercial and in-house sys-
tems demonstrate that lexical and semantic metrics provide
complementary insights, proving that both perspectives are es-
sential for ensuring robust intent preservation at the pipeline
level.

1. Introduction
Code-switching (CS)—the practice where speakers alternate
between languages within a single utterance—is prevalent in
multilingual communities [1, 2] yet remains a significant chal-
lenge for Automatic Speech Recognition (ASR) [3, 4]. De-
spite modeling advancements, CS-ASR evaluation still relies
heavily on traditional metrics like Word Error Rate (WER) and
Character Error Rate (CER) [5, 6]. While these metrics are
well-established for monolingual settings, they operate within
a string-matching paradigm and are fundamentally designed to
measure transcription quality at the lexical level [7, 8].
To better capture the unique challenges of code-switching,
specialized variants have been proposed. Mixed Error Rate
(MER) [9] and Phone Error Rate (PER) [7] offer greater gran-
ularity by targeting specific linguistic units, while more recent
frameworks like PIER introduce customizable points of interest
[8]. However, these metrics remain bound to the same string-
matching paradigm, leaving a complementary dimension un-
measured. ASR may output phonetic transliterations or homo-
phonic words that are lexically incorrect yet semantically equiv-
alent—errors that are meaningful to penalize in isolation but
largely inconsequential in downstream applications.
The rise of LLM-powered voice assistants fundamentally
shifts the evaluation objective from verbatim transcription to
task success [10]. In these architectures, ASR serves as the ini-
tial stage; the final user experience depends on the ability of

downstream Natural Language Understanding (NLU) modules
to recover intent from imperfect transcripts [11].
Recent advancements in LLM-based ASR error correc-
tion demonstrate that many lexical errors—particularly those in
code-switching be effectively repaired when models are pro-
vided with phonetic or semantic context [12, 13]. Whether
through specialized prompting [14], pinyin regularization [15],
or retrieval-augmented mechanisms [16], LLMs have shown a
remarkable ability to recover intent from degraded transcripts
that traditional metrics would heavily penalize. These findings
establish that ASR-only metrics no longer adequately reflect
system impact in modern pipelines [17], especially when deal-
ing with embedded-language token misrecognition, inconsis-
tent diacritics, or matrix-language-influenced phonetic translit-
erations of code-switched tokens [18].
To address these limitations, we propose a semantic-aware
evaluation framework designed to transcend surface-level lexi-
cal comparisons. By shifting the focus from verbatim accuracy
to intent preservation, this approach provides a more holistic
assessment of modern speech pipelines. Our primary contribu-
tions are as follows:
•End-to-End Evaluation Framework:We propose an ASR-
LLM pipeline assessment that introduces Code-Switching
Semantic Error Rate (CSER) as a robust, semantic-aware
complement to traditional lexical baselines.
•Comprehensive CS-ASR Benchmarking:We conduct a
multi-condition evaluation across commercial and in-house
models, demonstrating empirical divergences between lexi-
cal accuracy and downstream task success in code-switched
contexts.
•Phonetic and Semantic Analysis in Matrix Languages:
We investigate the impact of phonetically accurate transcrip-
tions in the matrix language, quantifying how these variations
preserve user intent despite surface-level differences.
Our benchmark and evaluation code are publicly available.1
2. Related Work
2.1. Lexical and Semantic ASR Metrics
ASR evaluation has traditionally relied on lexical metrics. WER
and CER provide reproducible baselines but treat all tokens
uniformly, failing to distinguish between critical semantic er-
rors and minor phonetic variations. To capture meaning be-
yond surface forms, semantic alternatives such as SWER [19]
and embedding-based metrics like BERTScore [20] have been
proposed. However, these metrics are primarily optimized for

2.2. Code-Switching Specific Evaluation
In the CS domain, evaluation has moved toward isolating
language-specific challenges through metrics like MER and
PIER [8]. PIER, in particular, identifies “Points of Interest”
to evaluate embedded-language tokens independently. While
these represent a significant step forward in CS granularity, they
remain bound to the string-matching paradigm. They cannot de-
termine if a lexical mismatch at a CS boundary actually disrupts
the downstream NLU’s ability to understand the user’s intent.
This limitation is what our proposed CSER aims to address by
shifting the focus from “did the ASR get the word right?” to
“can the system still execute the task?”.
2.3. LLM-based Error Correction and Pipeline-Awareness
The rise of LLM-powered voice assistants has shifted the focus
toward pipeline-aware evaluation. Recent studies demonstrate
that generative LLMs can effectively repair ASR outputs us-
ing specialized prompting [21] or contextual biasing [22]. For
instance, [13] showed that adaptive normalization can handle
mixed-language outputs, while [23] utilized pinyin to correct
phonetic errors in Chinese ASR.
Despite these advancements in “how to fix” errors, there is a
lack of “how to measure” the success of this repair in a CS con-
text. Current pipeline evaluations, such as the LLM-as-judge
framework by [24], focus on general ASR without address-
ing the unique phonetic-semantic overlaps inherent in code-
switching. Our work bridges this gap by introducing CSER.
Unlike previous metrics, CSER quantifies the “auto-repair” effi-
cacy of the LLM, specifically measuring how well the semantic
intent of code-switched utterances survives the transition from
ASR to NLU, even when the intermediate transcript is lexically
imperfect.

4. Experimental Setup
We evaluate all systems using four metrics (all reported in %,
lower is better):WERandCERas standard lexical base-
lines,PIERas a CS-aware lexical metric [8], and our proposed
CSERfor end-to-end semantic assessment. This multi-metric
approach allows for a direct comparison between lexical fidelity
and downstream intent preservation across diverse acoustic and
linguistic conditions.
4.1. Dataset Engineering and Data Pipeline
Due to the scarcity of naturalistic CS data, we adopt a hybrid
framework combining large-scale synthetic generation with tar-
geted human recordings. The data generation pipeline is illus-
trated in Figure 2.

The process begins by crawling YouTube metadata to ex-

2.Human Path:Recording sessions with 20 speakers of vary-

4.2. ASR Systems and Training Regimes
To establish production-relevant baselines, we evaluate both
world-class commercial engines and targeted in-house models.
Commercial Systems:We includeMicrosoft Azure Speech
(with CS language detection),Google v1 Streaming,Google
Chirp 3 Streaming, andGoogle Chirp 3(offline). These serve
as benchmarks for state-of-the-art CS handling in industry.
In-house Systems:Based on theConformer-CTC (77M)
architecture [28], we evaluate three distinct training regimes to
isolate the impact of CS data:
•VN:A monolingual Vietnamese baseline.
•VN-EN Phonetic:Trained on CS data where English tokens
are converted to Vietnamese phonetic transcriptions (e.g.,
“Facebook”→“phay buc”), targeting matrix-language pro-
nunciation patterns.
•VN-EN:Augmented with the proposed CS training data us-
ing original English orthography.
4.3. Evaluation Pipeline Configuration
The semantic probing stage (Stages 2–4 of Methodology) is in-
stantiated using Gemini 2.0 Flash as the judge model. By se-
lecting a model family distinct from the GPT-4o generator used
in the data pipeline, we mitigatefamiliarity bias—preventing
the evaluator from over-rewarding its own linguistic patterns.

5. Results and Analysis

5.1. The Phonetic-Semantic Paradox
On Set 4, theVN-EN Phoneticmodel yields a PIER of 58.65—
more than double that ofVN-EN(24.72)—yet its CSER (12.16)
is comparable toVN-EN’s (10.08). This pattern is consistent:
on Set 2 PIER diverges (51.24 vs. 32.95) while CSER remains
comparable (14.38 vs. 13.16); on Set 3, PIER is 58.66 vs. 56.63
with CSER at 19.25 vs. 18.37. The results show that LLM-
based NLU modules are robust to phonetic transliterations (e.g.,
“du tup” for “YouTube”), and that lexical metrics systematically
overpenalize strategies that remain functional in downstream
pipelines.
5.2. Commercial Benchmarking and Intent Recovery
Google Chirp 3 achieves the lowest CSER across all sets (10.47,
9.14, 22.03, 23.52 for Sets 1–4), paired with the best lexical
scores. A revealing divergence appears in Google v1 Stream-
ing: on Set 3 its CSER (48.16) is substantially higher than
Azure’s (40.35) despite comparable WER (32.51 vs. 27.85),
and on Set 4 it reaches the highest CSER of any system (51.33)
while Azure maintains 40.18. This suggests Google v1’s er-
rors concentrate onintent-bearing tokensthat the LLM can-
not repair, whereas Azure’s errors fall on matrix-language par-
ticles. Among in-house models,VN-ENachieves comparable
CSER to Chirp 3 (10.42 vs. 10.47) on Set 1 with lower WER
(9.53 vs. 10.46) and the overall best results on Set 4 (WER 9.23,
PIER 24.72, CSER 10.08).
5.3. Limitations of PIER in Capturing Intent
TheVNmonolingual baseline on Set 2 produces a PIER of
82.10, yet its CSER is only 17.20. For instance, given “bat
bai hat Despacito tren YouTube,” the model outputs “bat bai hat
de-pa-xi-to tren du tup.” PIER assigns 100% error on both enti-
ties, butMmaps them back (“de-pa-xi-to”→“Despacito,” “du
tup”→“YouTube”), preserving intent. Compared toVN-ENon
the same set (PIER 32.95, CSER 13.16), the relative CSER im-
provement is only 23.5%—far less than the 59.9% reduction
PIER suggests—confirming that PIER overestimates the func-
tional impact of CS errors in pipeline-aware settings.
5.4. Semantic Resilience of Phonetic Transcriptions
Given “mo ung dung Facebook di” (open the Facebook app),
theVN-EN Phoneticmodel outputs “mo ung dung phay buc
di.” PIER scores 100% error on “Facebook,” yet CSER= 0:
Mmaps “phay buc”→“Facebook” and the probe “What app
is mentioned?” is answered correctly. This reinforces the need
for CSER in scenarios where transcription serves merely as a
proxy for downstream task execution.
6. Discussion
The complexity of CS necessitates a shift fromstring-matching
tointent-matching. Our results confirm that CSER acts as a
“correction-aware” metric, quantifying the auto-repair ceiling
of the ASR-LLM pipeline.
LLM Judge Bias and Familiarity:We addressed potential
evaluator bias by utilizing Gemini 2.0 Flash to judge GPT-4o-
synthesized data. While a stylistic “cross-talk” between large
models is possible, the fact that CSER trends remain consistent
across both human-recorded (Sets 1 & 2) and TTS (Sets 3 & 4)
data suggests that the metric is measuring ASR fidelity rather
than LLM-to-LLM familiarity.
Inference Overhead vs. Diagnostic Value:We ac-
knowledge that CSER is more computationally expensive than
WER. However, we argue that forgold-standardbenchmark-
ing and NLU-readiness testing, the diagnostic value of knowing
whether a system preserves intent outweighs the millisecond-
latency of lexical counters. Future work onDistilled Semantic
Judgescould further bridge this gap.
7. Conclusion
This paper introducesCSER, the first semantic-aware metric
specifically tailored for Code-Switching ASR evaluation within
LLM pipelines. Our core contribution is the demonstration that
lexical metrics like WER and PIER are insufficient for mod-
ern voice assistants: they fail to account for the “semantic re-
silience” of phonetic transcriptions and overpenalize errors that
downstream NLU modules can easily rectify.
By decoupling transcription from intent preservation,
CSER provides a more accurate reflection of the end-user ex-
perience. Our multi-condition benchmark shows that targeted
CS training, even in phonetic forms, can achieve production-
grade semantic accuracy. Future work will focus on establish-
ing human-correlation baselines and optimizing the evaluator’s
efficiency for real-time development cycles.
