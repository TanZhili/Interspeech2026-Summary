Abstract
Sibilant fricatives are characterized by their spectral frica-
tion noise, which is sensitive to articulatory variations. Sibi-
lants prone to articulatory distortions, making them key targets
for early assessment in speech diagnosis. Those non-normative
sibilant productions are collectively referred to as sigmatism.
We analyzed retroflex fricative and affricate productions from
186 Polish preschool children. Analyses focused on four places
of articulation: retroflex (normative), dental, postalveolar, and
interdental. We extracted 34 frame-level features character-
izing frication noise of Polish sibilants, including noise ener-
gies, integrated energy over fricative formants, and their ra-
tios. Linear mixed-effects models were used to robustly quan-
tify articulatory variation. High-frequency energy up to 16 kHz
carried diagnostically relevant information for pediatric speech
distortions, and newly introduced features provided a voicing-
independent measure across different retroflex sibilants.
Index Terms: linear mixed-effects models, frication noise,
pathological speech, acoustic analysis, child speech
1. Introduction
Sibilants are among the most frequent consonants in language
systems [1] and are characterized by high-intensity, spectrally
structured frication noise [2, 3]. From the standpoint of its pro-
duction mechanism, this frication noise is generated by form-
ing a narrow constriction in the vocal tract, forcing the air-
flow through it. Next, it transitions to turbulent flow and en-
counters an obstacle located further downstream, usually the
incisors [3, 4, 5]. Acoustically, frication’s spectral shape is de-
termined by the resonances of the cavity anterior to the con-
striction: a smaller front cavity concentrates energy at higher
frequencies [6]. This relationship implies that, in children,
shorter anterior cavities shift sibilant spectral energy toward
higher frequencies [7, 8]. Acoustic studies of pediatric sibi-
lant productions indicate that spectral peaks for normative /s/
typically fall within the 5–12 kHz range [9]. However, chil-
dren exhibiting fronted articulations (e.g., /s/-fronting) produce
noise spectra with significantly broader energy distributions,
with peaks reaching as high as 13–14 kHz [10], This shift is
particularly relevant in the context of the Polish phonologi-
cal system, which features an exceptionally rich inventory of
twelve sibilants categorized into three series: dental, retroflex,
and alveolo-palatal [7]. Their articulatory complexity prolongs
the acquisition process: consistent fricative production is typi-
cally not achieved until the age of 6, with proper affricate artic-
ulation being an even longer process [7]. This results in sibi-
lants being prone to articulation errors, and sigmatism being the

sibilants (/ù/, /ü/, /tù/, /ãü/), which exhibit higher diachronic in-
stability than their dental and alveolo-palatal counterparts [13].
1.1. Current work
Although the high-frequency shifts are crucial for differentiat-
ing between normative and disordered speech, standard acous-
tic analyses of children’s speech often apply a low-pass cutoff,
which may lead to missing important diagnostic cues present in
the high-frequency range. In light of recent findings that high-
frequency energy patterns are highly responsive to subtle articu-
latory differences in adults [14], it is necessary to evaluate their
usefulness for characterizing atypical sibilant production in pe-
diatric populations.
The present study addresses this gap by using Linear
Mixed-Effects (LME) models to examine the spectral distribu-
tion of Polish sibilants in children’s productions up to 16 kHz,
based on Noise Energies (NE) features and their derived mea-
sures. By investigating how postalveolar, interdental, and den-
tal productions reshape the energy distribution in these typically
neglected frequency bands, we aim to provide a more reliable
and objective framework for assessing retroflex fricative and af-
fricate distortions in children, with the broader goal to support
speech-language pathologists’ diagnosis process.
2. Materials
In this study, we used samples from the PA VSig database: Pol-
ish Audio-Visual child speech dataset for computer-aided di-
agnosis of Sigmatism [15]. The database contains segmented
samples of sibilants pronounced by 201 Polish preschool chil-
dren aged 4 to 8 years. In our study, we focused especially on
retroflex fricatives (voiced: /ü/, unvoiced: /ù/), and affricates
(unvoiced: /tù/). V oiced retroflex affricate (/dü/) was not taken
into account due to its much rarer occurrence in Polish, thereby
reducing the number of samples in the analyzed dataset. Alto-
gether, the analysis comprised tokens from 26 distinct words:
13 containing the phoneme /ù/, 9 containing /ü/, and 4 contain-
ing /tù/. We chose to examine four most frequent Places of Ar-
ticulation (PoA) occurring in the dataset. PoA was assigned at
a speaker level for every phoneme individually. Speakers with
inconsistent PoA realization across analyzed sibilants were ex-
cluded (3 cases in total). Despite the imbalance in the num-
ber of speakers across PoA categories (120 retroflex/norm, 30
postalveolar, 28 dental, and 8 interdental), we retained all cate-
gories to ensure that the full range of observed articulatory vari-

3. Methods
3.1. Signal preprocessing
Individual sibilant recordings were first linearly normalized to
the range0−1to minimize amplitude variability across record-
ings. Subsequently, sibilant segments were divided into 20 ms
frames with 10 ms overlap. The next step was to limit the poten-
tial influence of neighboring phonemes by considering only the
middle50%of frames belonging to the analyzed word. Table 1
presents the total number of frames considered in the analysis,
organized by PoA.
3.2. Feature extraction
From each frame, we extracted features describing frication
noise according to the implementation by Miodonska et al. [16].
Proposed Noise Energies (NE) features were determined for
consecutive 500 Hz subbands. The energy of each subband
was obtained by summing the squared magnitudes of the DFT
(Discrete Fourier Transform) coefficients across all spectrum
bins lying between that subband’s lower and upper cutoff fre-
quencies. While the original implementation considered the
2 kHz–7 kHz range, in this study, we extended the analysis up
to 16 kHz. This extension is justified by two factors. First, the
recordings we analyzed were sampled at 44.1 kHz, providing
reliable spectral information in higher bands up to 22.05 kHz.
Second, recent studies on adult speech demonstrate that high-
frequency energy in the 11–15 kHz range is more effective at
capturing dynamic changes in frication noise than traditional
lower-frequency level measures [14]. Given that primary res-
onances in adult females already reach up to 10 kHz due to
smaller vocal tract dimensions [14], an extension of the anal-
ysis range to higher subbands is even more critical for children,
whose shorter vocal tracts shift these essential acoustic features
further into the high-frequency spectrum.

3.3. Statistical analysis
To capture both the hierarchical structure of the data and the
natural variability across speakers and phonetic contexts within
words, we used Linear Mixed-Effects (LME) models. These
models compensate for unequal group sizes by pooling infor-
mation across speakers, stabilizing estimates for sparsely repre-
sented categories while retaining all available data.
Each of the acoustic features was modeled individually as
the dependent variable. Models were calculated separately for
the three considered phonemes. The chosen model structure
focused on PoA as the fixed effect with four levels (retroflex,
postalveolar, dental, and interdental). Normative (retroflex)
pattern was treated as an intercept (the reference value). Our
random structure accounted for the hierarchical character of
the data while maintaining a parsimonious specification that
ensured stable model convergence. The final model was de-
fined as:Feature∼PoA + (1|speaker) + (1|
parent word). Before modeling, frame-level samples were
aggregated within word tokens by averaging all frame-wise fea-
ture vectors belonging to the analyzed segment. This yielded
segment-level observations. In total, the dataset comprised
4429 segments, with a summary presented in Table 2.
Model fit was quantified using marginal and conditional
R2, representing variance explained by fixed effects alone and
by the full model (fixed + random), respectively. All of the
analyses were performed in R 4.4.2 [17]. LME fitting was per-
formed usinglme4package [18] withlmerTestused for ob-
taining p–values for fixed effects [19].

4.1. Statistical analysis of frication noise features
The dental articulation is the most common distortion of Polish
retroflex sibilants with their articulation as dental phones (e.g.,
/ù/ as [s], /ü/ as [z]) [7]. This substitution pattern is consistent
with a high degree of acoustic distinctiveness in the dental re-
alization relative to the retroflex norm. Our analysis confirmed
the relationship across all investigated phonemes. For /ù/, den-
tal realization significantly affected almost all spectral bands,
with broad significance clusters in the 2–4 kHz (NE 0—NE3)
and 5–6 kHz (NE5—NE7) ranges. Interestingly, localized lack
of significance in specific subbands (e.g., NE 4) marks spectral
crossover points where the pathological and normative energy
representations intersect.
The interdental and postalveolar articulation exhibited more
localized deviations, primarily in the 3–3.5 kHz and 4–10 kHz
regions, respectively. Notably, the newly proposed aggre-
gated features FNER(12,23) and FNER(12,34) yielded the high-
est marginalR 2 values (up to 20%), outperforming individual
NE bands (e.g., NE1 at 15%).
A similar pattern was observed for the affricate / >tù/, where
the dental realization showed significant differences across
the entire spectrum, except for the 4–4.5 kHz band (NE 4).
The aggregated ratios remained robust discriminators: in case
of FNER (12,23), PoA explained nearly 30% of the variance

The voiced fricative /ü/ demonstrated a significant impact

Taken together, the results demonstrate that: (i) analysis of
sibilant frication should extend into the high–frequency range,
where diagnostically relevant cues persist, (ii) aggregated band-
ratio measures provide a compact and phoneme-generalizable
representation of articulatory misplacement. These properties
make FNER features particularly suitable for clinical assess-
ment of sibilant place errors across different voicing conditions.
5. Discussion
Acoustic analysis of pediatric speech is challenged by substan-
tial inter–group variability, which is attributed to the diversi-
fied development of speech motor control in preschool and early
school-aged children. While Zharkova [20] analyzed older chil-
dren aged 10–12 years, our findings are consistent with her ob-
servations regarding variability in sibilant articulation.
Our results align with the limited body of research on patho-
logical pediatric speech in Polish. Miodonska et al. [16, 21]
demonstrated that sibilant noise–based features are highly infor-
mative for distinguishing pathological from normative produc-
tions. Their studies analyzed /s/ and /ts/ (dental vs. interden-
tal realizations) as well as /ù/ and /ü/ (dental, interdental, and
retroflex realizations) on a smaller sample of 42 speakers, re-
stricting analysis to subbands below 7 kHz. Interdental produc-
tions in their study showed elevated energy in low-frequency
bands (2–3.5 kHz, NE 0−NE 2) and reduced energy in higher
bands (5.5–6.5 kHz, NE7−NE 8), which corresponds with pat-
terns observed in our analysis. In the present study, in addition
to previous works, we analyzed postalveolar realizations, which
proved challenging due to their close similarity to normative
retroflex productions.
Although Shadle et al. [14] focused on adult speakers, their
work provides a theoretical foundation for our findings. They
demonstrated that high–frequency energy (HFE) patterns, up
to 10 kHz, are sensitive markers of aerodynamic and articula-
tory variation, particularly in speakers with shorter vocal tracts
where resonances are shifted upward. Applying this framework
to pediatric speech is a key contribution of the present study.
Given the even shorter vocal tracts of children, diagnostically
relevant spectral signatures are expected in the higher range.
Our results confirm this expectation for more anterior distor-
tions, where energy in the highest bands shows a marked in-
crease. Significant differences extend up to 14 kHz for inter-
dental and 16 kHz for dental realizations, providing empirical
support at the highest frequencies analyzed.
We further demonstrate that, in retroflex-to-
dental/interdental substitutions, frication energy shifts
into frequency regions often excluded by standard analyses.
The use of FNER features captures the redistribution of energy
limited by formant frequencies. This confirms that HFE
analysis is not an optional extension but a necessary component
for clinical applications, enabling the detection of differences
that may be masked in standard frequency-limited analyses.
Several limitations should be considered when interpret-
ing the present findings. First, the dataset exhibited a substan-
tial class imbalance across places of articulation. Although the
Linear Mixed Effects framework mitigates unequal group sizes
through partial pooling, estimates for categories with limited
data still exhibit lower precision. Second, the analysis did not
incorporate speaker–specific anatomical factors (e.g., dentition
characteristics, number of teeth), known for influencing sibi-
lant production. The analyses were conducted in an exploratory
framework aimed at identifying potentially relevant acoustic
cues rather than testing a small set of predefined hypotheses.
Future work could address this in two ways: by expanding the
random–effects structure to capture more variance, or by apply-
ing bootstrap or resampling approaches to balance the number
of speakers across PoA categories, while carefully considering
the trade-offs in data coverage and estimate stability.
