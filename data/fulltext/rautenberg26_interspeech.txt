Hierarchical Conditional Continuous Normalizing Flows for Creaky Voice
Editing under Speaker Identity Preservation

1 Department of Communications Engineering, Paderborn University, Germany

Abstract
We propose a hierarchical conditional continuous normalizing
flow that structurally disentangles high-level speaker attributes
from low-level paralinguistic voice qualities. By introducing
a controlled, stage-wise injection of conditioning information,
the model constrains how different feature types influence the
latent representation. This design reduces unintended interac-
tions between attributes and limits the propagation of low-level
manipulations to high-level characteristics. Using the example
of manipulating the voice quality creak, we show that the hier-
archical approach has significantly less impact on the high-level
speaker attributes pitch and gender, leading to improved preser-
vation of perceived speaker identity.
Index Terms: voice editing, speech synthesis, perceptual voice
qualities
1. Introduction
With the rapid progress of generative AI in recent years, speech
synthesis systems have evolved far beyond Text-to-Speech
(TTS) generation. One emerging research direction is voice
editing, i.e., the selective manipulation of specific vocal at-
tributes in synthesized speech while preserving all unrelated
characteristics. Previous work has addressed the manipulation
of high-level speaker characteristics such as accent, gender, or
age [1]. Other approaches focus on perceptual voice qualitys
(PVQs) [2, 3], such ascreak[4, 5], as well as additional expres-
sive traits [6, 7].
The central challenge in this setting is selective control:
modifying a desired feature without unintentionally altering
speaker identity or other correlated attributes. Consider the ex-
ample ofcreakyvoice. It is typically characterized by a low
rate of vocal fold vibration combined with a constricted glot-
tis, resulting in a lower and irregularpitch[8]. According to
the prototypical form ofcreak, a speaker with highcreakprob-
ability tends to have a lowerpitch. We call this correlationin-
ter-speakercreak-to-pitchcorrelation. A deep generative model
forcreakmanipulation will learn this correlation from the train-
ing data and alter the speaker’spitchalongside modifyingcreak
probability, which, as a consequence, will alter the perceived
speaker identity [9].
For a range of applications, such as providing speech sam-
ples to teach speech therapists in training to identify certain
PVQs [10], it is desirable to modify the PVQ without altering
other attributes, and in particular without altering the perceived
speaker identity. For this, the aboveinter-speaker correlation
observed in a population of speakers is unwanted. Instead, the
training of a generative model capable ofintra-speaker variation
of a PVQ would require a dataset, where a single speaker alters
his/her voice along a single PVQ dimension. Such a dataset
is difficult to obtain because it requires a trained speaker, and
hardly exists. A notable example is presented in [11], where
a dataset was recorded with a single professional voice actor
systematically varying three articulatory-phonetic dimensions:
Glottalization,tenseness, andresonance. Such a dataset en-
ables the model to learnintra-speaker variability of the target
feature, i.e., variations that arise exclusively from the feature it-
self. However, for most voice attributes, comparable datasets
do not exist.
Conditional Continuous Normalizing Flows (CCNFs)[12,
13] have shown strong performance in speaker attribute manip-
ulation for speech synthesis and voice editing [14, 3]. One strat-
egy to mitigate unwanted dependencies in generative models is
to modify the training data itself. For example, [9] introduced
pitch-based data augmentation to artificially decorrelate mean
pitchandcreakin the training data. This enabled manipula-
tion ofcreakprobability while largely preservingpitchand per-
ceived speaker identity. While effective for this specific case,
such data-centric approaches are inherently task-specific and do
not provide a general architectural solution to disentanglement.
Architectural strategies for structured factorization have
been explored in generative modeling. For example, [15] injects
style information across layers to separate coarse and fine at-
tributes, while [16] employs hierarchical latents to model global
and local structure at different levels.
In speech generation, multi-stage diffusion and flow-based
systems employ stage-wise conditioning primarily to improve
fidelity rather than to disentangle features [17, 18]. We propose
a two-stage CCNF that factorizes conditioning by feature type.
The first stage conditions on high-level attributes and maps the
speaker representation to an intermediate latent space invariant
to them. The second stage conditions on low-level attributes to
model local variations. As the intermediate representation no
longer encodes high-level information, the second stage cannot
reintroduce such dependencies, reducing cross-feature interac-
tions.
To further encourage invariance in the intermediate repre-
sentation, we apply Domain Adversarial Training (DAT) [19],
implementing adversarial objectives that suppress targeted fea-
ture information. Similar adversarial representation learning
strategies have been used in speech processing to enforce invari-
ant latent features under different conditions, such as speaker
identity or accent [20, 21, 22]. Here, we define high-level fea-
tures as speaker-related characteristics (e.g.,gender,pitch) and
low-level features as paralinguistic voice qualities (e.g.,creak,
breathiness,roughness). We demonstrate our approach with the
task of modifyingcreakwhile preservinggenderandpitch. 12

3. Voice-Editable Text-to-Speech
Recent work has shown increasing interest in voice editing us-
ing CCNFs. The authors of [1] applied this concept to global
speaker attribute manipulation, such asgender,age, andac-
cent. This idea was extended to the manipulation of PVQs in
[3], while [5] focused on local persistent voice qualities, such as
creak. V oice editing is performed on a speaker representations

from a speech utterancex. Here, we assume that the speaker

3.2. Disentanglement via adversarial objectives
To further promote disentanglement of speaker-related at-
tributes, we apply DAT [19] to the intermediate latent represen-
tationz(tm)with respect to ¯f0 andgender. An auxiliary regres-
sion network is trained to predict ¯f0, and a classifier is trained to
predict the binarygenderlabel. The regression objective is op-
timized using the Mean Squared Error (MSE) lossL reg, while
the classifier is trained with binary cross-entropyLclf. Both pre-
dictors are implemented as feed-forward networks with a single
hidden layer of dimension 64.
Following the domain-adversarial framework [19], gradi-
ents from the auxiliary objectives are reversed before being
propagated to the parametersθof the flow networkf 1(·). While
the auxiliary predictors are trained to minimize their respec-
tive losses and recover the high-level attributesa 1,f , the flow
network is optimized adversarially to maximize these losses,
thereby removing the corresponding information fromz(tm).
This is realized using a Gradient Reversal Layer (GRL) [19]
(see fig. 2), which acts as the identity during the forward pass
but multiplies gradients by91during backpropagation. Conse-
quently,z(tm)is encouraged to be invariant to ¯f0 andgender,
promoting their modeling in earlier stages of the hierarchical
flow. The overall training objective is defined as
L=L flow +λ reg·L reg +λ clf·L clf.(7)
4. Experiments
All experiments were conducted on the LibriTTS-R dataset
[27]. All systems share the same speech synthesis backbone,
YourTTS, trained following [23]. Previous work [9] reported a
negative correlationR=90.8between ¯f0 andcreakprobabil-
ity on LibriTTS-R, with a slope ofα= 250 Hz/%using CreaPy
labels. While the presence of such a correlation aligns with
[8], the steep slope indicates that ¯f0 andcreakare highly cou-
pled across speakers. Training a CCNF directly on these labels
therefore encourages the model to captureinter-speaker vari-
ability rather thanintra-speakercreakvariation. As a conse-
quence, manipulatingcreakoften induces unintended changes
in ¯f0.
To address this issue, we introduce a hierarchical flow ar-
chitecture that explicitly separates speaker identity features like
¯f0 andgenderfromcreak-related variation without data aug-
mentation. The speaker attribute manipulation block is trained
using eq. (7), with intermediate statet m = 0.5, initial learning
rate10 94,λ reg =λ clf = 2, and batch size 200. In the follow-
ing, we refer to this model ashierarch-flow.
We compare our method against three baselines. Thebase-
flow corresponds to [5]. An extended variant, referred to as
base-extd., augments thebase-flow with additional condition-
ing on ¯f0 and adversarial learning in the transformed space,
however, with a single transformation rather than hierarchically.
Thedata-mod.-flow follows [9], where ¯f0 is modified in the
training data to artificially reduce thepitch–creakcorrelation
prior to model training. At inference time, the estimatedcreak
value is modified by adding a factoraf +βand used as condi-
tioning in the inverse flow step. The TTS system then generates
˜xβ accordingly.

4.2. Subjective listening tests
We conducted a subjective listening experiment to evaluate the
perceptual effects ofcreakmanipulation using thebaseandhi-
erarchmodels. Utterancesxwith durations between4 sand
7 swere randomly sampled from the test set and grouped ac-
cording to their meancreakprobability (>0.4:creak,≤0.4:
non-creak). The threshold was selected to ensure that thecreak
group contained a sufficient number of samples. To suppress
creak, utterances from thecreakgroup were manipulated with
β=91, while non-creaksamples were amplified usingβ= 1.
Each utterance was additionally resynthesized withβ= 0as an
unmanipulated reference condition.
Because the effect ofβdepends both on the initialcreak
level and on sample-dependent model responses, identical con-
ditioning strengths do not yield uniformcreakshifts across ut-
terances. We therefore apply a shared, sufficiently strong condi-
tioning value to ensure clearly perceptible manipulation across
conditions.
For each model and manipulation condition (suppression
and amplification), participants evaluated two male and two
female utterances. Each participant rated a total of 32 sam-
ples, including both manipulated and unmanipulated versions.
They provided overall naturalness ratings Mean Opinion Score
(MOS) and speaker similarity ratings Speaker Similarity MOS
(SMOS) following the instructions of the most recent Blizzard
Challenge [30], and additionally judged perceivedcreakiness
on a09100scale (0 = Nocreak,100 = Very strongcreak).
The listening test was implemented using the framework of
[31]. Sincecreakis not a widely known perceptual concept,
we recruited 11 voice quality experts as listeners.
The mean and computed standard deviation for all obtained
ratings can be found in table 1. To verify the differences are
statistically meaningful, we report paired Wilcoxon signed rank
tests for each of the comparisons [32], as the ratings are pre-
sumed to be non-intervallic. First, comparing the unmanipu-
lated re-synthesis to the manipulated ratings within each sys-
tem, we find no significant change in MOS for both models and
conditions (p>0.05), except for the decrease in naturalness
noted in thebasemodel when suppressingcreak(p<0.05).
Investigating the SMOS we find the differences to be signifi-
cant for both modifications and models (p<0.001), indicat-
ing a general loss of speaker identity when modifyingcreak.
Finally, we find that both models seem to succeed in ampli-
fying perceivedcreak(p<0.001), while the changes in sup-
pression is only found to be marginally significant for thebase
model (p <0.05) and not significant for thehierarchicaldata
(p>0.05). To investigate the interaction of whether the pre-
sented architecture yields a significant improvement, a paired
comparison of the differences between unmanipulated and ma-
nipulated ratings is carried out between the two models. The
data shows that neither the changes in MOS nor the slight de-
crease increakinessdifference is beyond chance between the
models (p>0.05). The change in SMOS, however, is found to
be significant (p<0.001).
Interpreting these results, it can be said that both models
successfully manipulatecreakwith no significant change in per-
ceived quality as measured by MOS for thehierarchicalmodel
and some loss in quality in the suppressed condition for thebase
model. The noted lack of significance for the observedcreak
suppression in thehierarchicalmodel can be explained by the
generally lower perceivedcreakfor the unmanipulated samples,
which made the difference less salient. Examining the differ-
ences between the two architectures, thebasemodel exhibits
a substantially larger drop in perceived speaker similarity, par-
ticularly in the amplified condition. This indicates that strong
creakmanipulation in thebasemodel is entangled with speaker
identity, whereas thehierarchicalmodel maintains speaker sim-
ilarity more consistently, while producing similar changes in
creakmodification.
5. Conclusion
Disentangling meanpitchandcreakenables controlled manip-
ulation ofcreakwhile improving speaker preservation. The
proposed hierarchical architecture reduces cross-feature inter-
ference and provides more stable attribute control. Unlike the
data-mod.-flow approach, which is specific to reducing the in-
teraction with ¯f0, we believe that the proposed hierarchical ap-
proach could also be applied to other interacting speech fea-
tures, but this remains to be explored in future work.
