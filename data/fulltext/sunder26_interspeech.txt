Abstract
Word-level timestamps are essential for many speech applica-
tions. Existing methods either degrade transcription quality when
interleaving timestamps or require separate alignment models
that do not integrate into speech LLMs. We propose a parame-
ter efficient framework for speech recognition with timestamps
(SRWT) within a single speech LLM. Our approach generates
transcriptions in the first step and regenerates them with times-
tamps in the second, preserving transcription quality. We intro-
duce modular low rank adaptation (LoRA) variants that train
only on SRWT data without degrading base model capabilities,
enabling isolated adaptation as speech LLMs scale to more tasks.
We propose novel activated LoRA (aLoRA) for SRWT, enabling
KV-cache reuse thus allowing improved performance through
base model usage while preserving modularity. Experiments
show a 27ms alignment error in English (35% relative improve-
ment over the best baseline) and 21ms in multilingual data, while
maintaining WER identical to the base model. Proposed meth-
ods also exhibit strong zero-shot transfer, achieving 37ms error
on multilingual data when trained only on English.

1. Introduction
Large language models (LLMs) have brought about a paradigm
shift in speech AI. Applications such as automatic speech recog-
nition (ASR) and speech translation (AST) are being integrated
into state-of-the-art LLMs [1–3] which follows the idea of align-
ing speech and text representations. While classic approaches
rely on explicit embedding alignment [4–8], most modern LLM
based models follow an architecture where pretrained speech en-
coders and LLMs are “stitched” together via a lightweight neural
interface. This design combines the strong acoustic modeling
capabilities of audio encoders with the linguistic knowledge
of LLMs, yielding models that excel across a diverse range
of speech tasks. SLAM-LLM [9], Granite-Speech [2], and
Qwen-Audio [3, 10, 11] are prime examples of this. Other ef-
forts have pushed these capabilities further: SALMONN [12]
augments LLaMA [13] with general audio understanding, while
AudioPaLM [14] extends PaLM-2 [15] beyond speech compre-
hension to speech generation.
Considerable effort has gone into equipping speech LLMs
with additional capabilities such as diarization [16], timestamp
prediction [3,11,17], contextual biasing [18], slot filling [19,20],
and emotion recognition [21]. In this paper, we focus on word-
level timestamp prediction. Accurate word-level timestamps are
essential for applications like closed captioning, keyword-based
audio retrieval, and transcript synchronization.
Previous work on timestamp prediction falls into two cate-
gories: two-pass and one-pass approaches.
Two-pass approachesgenerate a transcript first, then align
it to the audio. The most traditional are GMM-HMM-based
forced alignment systems available in toolkits like HTK [22] and
Kaldi [23], with accessible implementations such as the Montreal
Forced Aligner (MFA) [24]. These need pronunciation dictionar-
ies and domain-matched acoustic models. CTC-based alignment,
like NeMo Forced Aligner (NFA) [25], applies Viterbi decoding
on CTC posteriors but struggles with finer word boundaries due
to blank token modeling. More recent methods combine ASR
with neural alignment: WhisperX [26] uses Whisper [27] for
transcription and applies DTW on phoneme posteriors, though
frame-level phoneme predictions are difficult to obtain at scale.
Canary-v2 [28] pairs an encoder-decoder ASR model with a
CTC aligner. CrisperWhisper [29] uses an attention-based ap-
proach [30] to extract alignments from Whisper’s cross-attention
via DTW. Qwen3 Forced Aligner [11] uses a non-autoregressive
LLM to timestamp a given transcript. However, none of these
provide a unified solution within a single speech LLM.
One-pass approachestrain autoregressive models to produce
transcripts and timestamps in a single generation. The Canary
encoder-decoder model was adapted by [31] for timestamp-
interleaved ASR and automatic speech translation (AST) us-
ing NFA-generated training data. Qwen-Audio [3] supports
speech recognition with timestamps (SRWT) alongside other
tasks. Speech-LLM based models [2] have also been adapted for
SRWT with timestamp-specific regularization [17]. A common
problem with one-pass methods is degradation in transcription
quality [17,31]; most autoregressive models struggle to maintain
text coherence when timestamps are interleaved.
In this paper, we propose parameter efficient adaptation
frameworks to perform SRWT in two steps within a single
speech-LLM: step one generates the transcript (ASR), and step
two regenerates it with interleaved timestamps (SRWT). This
design addresses key gaps in current research:
Preserving ASR performance:One-step SRWT degrades tran-
scription quality. Our single pass,two-step designallows the
model to condition on its own ASR output, preserving coherence
while adding timestamps.
Parameter-efficient, task-isolated adaptation:Speech LLMs are
trained on large-scale multi-task data. Full fine-tuning for SRWT
risks degrading ASR/AST performance and requires costly re-
training on original tasks. We proposelow-rank adaptation
variantsthat add minimal parameters and train only on SRWT
data, leaving the base model capabilities intact.
KV cache reuse:We propose anovel activated LoRA
(aLoRA) [32] framework for timestamps, which reuses the
KV cache from the ASR step by activating timestamp-specific

2. Model Design
2.1. Base Speech-LLM
For all experiments, we use a speech-aware large language model
(speech-LLM) following the architecture described in [2]. The
speech-LLM is first trained on a variety of datasets for speech
transcription and translation tasks (illustrated in Figure 1), and
subsequently adapted for timestamp generation.

The speech-LLM comprises the following components:
Speech Encoder:We employ a 16-layer Conformer en-
coder [33] with a hidden dimension of 1024 and 8 attention
heads, trained using the CTC loss [34] on a variety of publicly
available ASR corpora [2].
Projector:The output from the penultimate layer of the speech
encoder is passed to a 2-layer Q-Former projector [2, 12]. The
Q-Former operates on non-overlapping blocks of 15 encoder
frames, using 3 learnable query vectors per block to perform
cross-attention over the encoder outputs leading to a 5× down-
sampling of the audio sequence. A final linear layer projects the
Q-Former outputs from its internal dimension of 1024 to match
the LLM embedding dimension of 2048.
LLM:We use a 1-billion parameter LLM1 consisting of 40 trans-
former layers, hidden dimension of 2048, 16 attention heads,
and SiLU activation [35]. The model employs grouped query
attention [36] with 4 key-value heads, RoPE positional encod-
ings [37], and a vocabulary size of 100,352 tokens.
LoRA:The LLM is fine-tuned using Low-Rank Adaptation
(LoRA) [38] with rank r=64 and scaling factor of 0.5, applied
to all linear projections (query, key, value, and output) across all
40 layers. We denote these base LoRA weights as LoRAA.
2.2. Speech Recognition with Timestamps (SRWT)
SRWT is the task of transcribing speech while generating word-
level timestamps interleaved with the transcription. We train
models to predict the end time for each word. Since the start time
of a word typically coincides with the end time of the preceding
word, explicitly generating both introduces redundancy. Instead,
we introduce a silence token ( ) to mark pauses between words,
allowing the start time of any word to be inferred from the end
time of the preceding token.
For example, we have a 1-second utterance “hello how
are you”, with the corresponding SRWT target sequence:

Here, “ ” denotes silence, “|” separates each word from its end
time, and all timestamps are in units of 10 ms. The start time of
a word is inferred from the end time of the preceding token. In
the above example, “hello” starts at 0.2 seconds and ends at
0.3 seconds, and the next word “how” starts at 0.4 seconds.
Some prior work [3, 17, 31] has modeled SRWT as a single-
pass generation task, where the language model directly produces
timestamped transcriptions conditioned on the input speech.
However, this approach leads to significant word error rate
(WER) degradation when timestamps are interleaved.
Thus, we use a two-step approach that leverages the strong
transcription capabilities of speech-LLMs. In the first step, the
model generates a standard transcription. In the second step,
the model performs SRWT conditioned on the speech and the
ASR transcript. This avoids WER degradation, as the model
can copy the transcript and by decoding “what” before “when”,
alignment reduces to a localization problem rather than joint
decoding, simplifying the second step.
Since speech-LLMs are typically pre-trained on tasks that
may not include SRWT, it is crucial to develop techniques that
efficiently introduce SRWT capabilities without degrading per-
formance on existing tasks. To this end, we propose three adap-
tation approaches (Figure 2): Non-Mod and Mod-aLoRA are
one-pass,two-stepmethods with KV-cache reuse, while Mod-
LoRA is atwo-passmethod requiring KV-cache recomputation.
Moreover, Mod-aLoRA and Mod-LoRA offer full modularity.

straightforward approach of continually fine-tuning the speech-
LLM to generate timestamps in the second step of LLM gen-

and LoRAA, yielding Proj ′ and LoRAf

3. Experiments
3.1. Configuration
Data.We train our SRWT models using a combination of pub-
licly available speech corpora: LibriSpeech [39], MLS (en, fr, de,
pt, es) [40], CommonV oice (en, fr, de, pt, es) [41], V oxPopuli (en,
fr, de, es) [42], AMI-IHM [43], Switchboard [44], TIMIT [45],
Buckeye [46], and YODAS [47]. For AMI-IHM, Switchboard,
TIMIT, and Buckeye, we use the available timestamp annota-
tions. For all other datasets, we obtain word-level alignments
using the Montreal Forced Aligner (MFA) [24], a GMM-HMM
based forced alignment tool. We also use MFA to insert silence
boundaries into the manually annotated datasets.
To ensure high-quality training data, we validate the MFA-
derived alignments using CTC-based forced alignment with our
speech encoder (Section 2.1). We compute the Accumulated
Average Shift (AAS) [48], the mean absolute error between
timestamps in milliseconds, for the CTC and MFA alignments
and retain only samples with the lowest alignment error: the top
95% for English and top 70% for non-English data. For the larger
datasets (YODAS and MLS-en), we cap the training data at 4M
and 5M samples, respectively. Following [17], we augment the
training data by concatenating consecutive utterances to create
sequences of 2× the original length, improving generalization
to longer audio at inference time.
Training.The base speech-LLM is trained on a variety of pub-
licly available datasets for ASR/AST tasks (see [2]) for 900k
steps with a batch size of 128 and a learning rate of 1e-4 with
linear decay after 1000 warmup steps. For SRWT adaptation,
Non-Mod is continually fine-tuned on the original tasks plus
SRWT for 800k steps at a reduced learning rate of 4e-5, while
Mod-LoRA and Mod-aLoRA are trained for 800k steps at a
learning rate of 1e-4 on SRWT data. For modular approaches,
projector LoRA is applied to all linear projections in the Q-
Former. We also use pad-free training with Flash attention [49].

A key advantage of our design is that the SRWT WER
matches the ASR WER. On English, all three proposed methods
achieve 7.3% average WER, identical to the base speech LLM,
and on multilingual data, our methods achieve 5.2–5.3% com-
pared to the base model’s 5.3%. This confirms that the two-step
design allows the model to reproduce the transcription while
adding timestamps, thereby avoiding quality degradations.
In terms of timestamp accuracy, all proposed methods outper-
form baselines. On English, Non-Mod achieves the best average
AAS of 27.1 ms, a 35% relative improvement over Qwen3-FA
(41.8 ms) and 49% over CrisperWhisper (53.1 ms). Our modular
variants achieve comparable AAS to the non-modular model
while offering practical advantages. As shown in Table 1, mod-
ular adaptation does not require retraining on original tasks;
SRWT capability can be developed independently and added
without degrading ASR/AST performance. This becomes impor-
tant as speech LLMs support more tasks.
Among the modular approaches, Mod-aLoRA achieves the
best AAS by leveraging both the base model’s KV-cache from
audio1 and a timestamp-specific audio2 encoding in a single con-
tinuous generation pass. The aLoRA design also offers flexibility
for future optimization: by avoiding attention overaudio1 during
SRWT generation, computational efficiency could be improved
while still benefiting from the transcript context.
On multilingual data, Mod-aLoRA achieves the best average
AAS of 21.2 ms, outperforming Non-Mod (22.9 ms) and Mod-
LoRA (23.9 ms). We hypothesize that, compared to English,
low-resource multilingual data benefits from preserving the base
model’s optimized KV cache representations. The non-modular
approach fine-tunes these representations and Mod-LoRA re-
computes KV representations with new LoRA weights, losing
the benefit of the pretrained cache. Mod-aLoRA strikes the best
balance by inheriting the base model’s KV cache while activating
timestamp-specific adapters only during generation.
Zero-shot multilingual analysis:Table 3 presents a setting
where we observe a clear benefit of modular approaches, in par-
ticular, Mod-aLoRA over Non-Mod. When models are trained
only on English SRWT and evaluated on multilingual data,
Non-Mod exhibits poor zero-shot transfer (339.5ms avg AAS),
while modular methods generalize substantially better. No-
tably, Mod-aLoRA (37.3ms) outperforms Mod-LoRA (43.0ms),
which we attribute to its ability to leverage the base model’s KV-
cache which preserves multilingual acoustic knowledge and aids
cross-lingual timestamp prediction. Modular adaptation learns
language-agnostic features which makes timestamp prediction
an isolated skill whereas full fine-tuning on all tasks together,
as in Non-Mod, might be learning English specific timing cues.
This strong zero-shot transfer suggests modular methods offer
a practical pathway for adding timestamp capabilities to new
languages without requiring language-specific supervision.
4. Conclusion
We proposed parameter-efficient adaptation methods for word-
level timestamp prediction in speech LLMs. Our two-step ap-
proach generates transcriptions first, then regenerates with times-
tamps, preserving transcription quality while achieving state-of-
the-art alignment. Our key contribution is Mod-aLoRA, which
combines modularity with KV-cache reuse. We also explored
Non-Mod (two-step but requires retraining) and Mod-LoRA
(modular but no KV-cache reuse) to understand design trade-
offs. Experiments demonstrate 35% relative improvement over
the best baseline while maintaining identical WER. Modular
methods also exhibit strong zero-shot transfer to new languages
without language-specific supervision.
