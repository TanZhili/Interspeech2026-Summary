Learning to Rescale: On-the-Fly Sequence Length Adaptation in
Non-Autoregressive Speech Synthesis

1 Shenzhen International Graduate School, Tsinghua University, China

Abstract
Non-autoregressive (NAR) text-to-speech (TTS) models excel
in parallel inference and style consistency. However, their re-
liance on accurate character-level or global duration predictions
remains a critical bottleneck, limiting both synthesis fidelity and
naturalness. This paper proposes ElasticDLM (Elastic-length
Diffusion Language Model), a novel variable-length NAR TTS
model that eliminates the strong dependency on accurate du-
ration predictions. We introduce two functional tokens, along
with a Differentiated Length-Scaling Training Scheme (DLTS)
and a Hierarchical Confidence-Guided Inference (HCGI) strat-
egy tailored for speech characteristics, enabling the model to
learn length-scaling features and automatically adjust sequence
length during inference. Experiments show that ElasticDLM
produces high-fidelity, natural speech from arbitrary-length in-
puts, demonstrating a more flexible and simplified approach to
high-quality speech synthesis. Audio samples are available at
https://thuhcsi.github.io/ElasticDLM/.
Index Terms: Speech Synthesis, Non-Autoregressive Models,
Variable-Length Synthesis
1. Introduction
Text-to-Speech (TTS) synthesis [1, 2, 3] aims to generate high-
fidelity, natural speech for diverse applications like intelligent
assistants [4], audiobooks [5], and assistive communication sys-
tems [6]. The core challenge for TTS lies in learning the precise
alignment between text and complex acoustics. By decompos-
ing the complicated global mapping task into simple, local con-
ditional predictions, autoregressive (AR) models [7, 8] effec-
tively tackle the alignment challenge via their sequential, token-
wise generation process. However, their token-by-token nature
inherently leads to slow inference and exposure bias [9, 10, 11].
To address these AR limitations, non-autoregressive (NAR)
models [12, 13, 14] have emerged as a compelling alternative,
prized for their parallel inference and superior global style con-
sistency. However, the parallel generation paradigm of NAR
models confronts the challenge of sequence alignment. Early
strategies relied on character-level durations, typically derived
from methods like teacher-forcing with alignments distilled
from AR models [15, 16], monotonic alignment search (MAS)
[17, 18] or targets from the Montreal Forced Aligner (MFA)
[19]. These pre-extracted durations are often time-consuming
and inaccurate, especially under noisy or in-the-wild speech
conditions, severely compromising the training. Moreover, this
reliance on a rigid and ‘fine-grained’ alignment hinders the
models from achieving human-like naturalness and rich stylistic
diversity.

To overcome the shortages of character-level duration mod-
eling, emerging works such as E2-TTS [20], F5-TTS [21], and
MaskGCT [22] have adopted masked generative modeling to
adaptively distribute word or phoneme duration according to a
total sequence length. Nonetheless, these fixed-length methods
still rely on predicting the accurate global duration as an initial
sequence length. This mechanism fundamentally restricts the
flexibility and accuracy, leading to two major failures. Firstly,
inaccurate duration predictions result in audible artifacts such
as unnatural speaking rates and fragmented prosody. Secondly,
this rigid duration paradigm prevents the model from adaptively
adjusting the generated sequence length with different semantic
information and acoustic characteristics. These flaws hinder the
practical application [23] of the NAR-based TTS models.
Recently, several studies in text generation [24], most no-
tably DreamOn [25], have attempted to address the issue of
variable-length sequence generation based on NAR models.
Diffusion Language Models (DLMs) [26] are widely adopted,
mainly leveraging its single-token-level insertion and deletion
capability. However, it is non-trivial and challenging to directly
apply this paradigm to the speech synthesis domain. Firstly,
due to the high temporal resolution of speech, where a single
phoneme often corresponds to multiple semantic tokens, and
the inherent acoustic redundancy, discrete single-token opera-
tions like merging or insertion fail to provide the discernible and
robust gradient for learning complicated length manipulations.
Secondly, the vast length of speech sequences clashes with the
limited inference budget, making it a formidable challenge to
reconcile the need for large-scale length variability with the
demand for high-fidelity generation. To tackle all these prob-
lems and overcome the fixed-length constraint in existing NAR
speech synthesis frameworks, we propose ElasticDLM: a novel
variable-length NAR speech synthesis approach. The main con-
tributions of this work are as follows:
(1) We propose a novel variable-length NAR speech synthesis
model. To eliminate reliance on accurate duration predic-
tions, it introduces two functional tokens for unified, learn-
able length regulation, enhancing robustness and flexibility.
(2) We develop a Differentiated Length-Scaling Training
Scheme (DLTS) tailored to speech characteristics, which
combines block-wise merging and insertion operations with
an auxiliary length-scaling loss, providing the model with
salient and learnable features for length regulation within
information-sparse acoustic sequences.
(3) We propose a Hierarchical Confidence-Guided Inference
(HCGI) strategy adapted to the high dynamic range of speech
sequences, enabling simultaneous length refinement and con-
tent generation within a coarse-to-fine process to ensure both
length flexibility and high fidelity.

2. Proposed Method
2.1. System Overview
Our proposed non-autoregressive TTS framework (Fig.1(a))
employs a cascaded Diffusion Language Model (DLM) to pro-
gressively refine text into speech via iterative mask prediction.
The system is driven by our novel Elastic Semantic DLM,
which first maps text to semantic tokens. These tokens are then
converted into speech by a subsequent acoustic modeling stage
(Acoustic DLM, Acoustic Decoder, and V ocoder) based on the
MaskGCT [22] architecture.
As depicted in the lower part of Fig.1(a), our Elastic Seman-
tic DLM iteratively refines an initial mask sequence (derived
from phonemes and a timbre prompt) into a semantic token se-
quence. Uniquely, it incorporates[EXPAND]and[DELETE]
functional tokens, which respectively trigger the insertion of
new mask tokens or the removal of existing segments, allowing
for dynamic length adjustment. This capability is supported by
two key components: a Differentiated Length-Scaling Training
Scheme (DLTS, Fig.1(b)) to enable the model to learn salient
length-scaling features, and a Hierarchical Confidence-Guided
Inference strategy (HCGI, Algorithm 1) to ensure both high
length flexibility and synthesis fidelity during generation.

3. Experiments
3.1. Experiment Setup
Our Elastic Semantic DLM is built upon the 16-layer Trans-
former architecture (hidden size 1536, 16 attention heads) of
MaskGCT’s Text-to-Semantic model and is initialized by load-
ing the pre-trained weights of its Transformer component. The
model is trained on 100k hours of data from the English and
Chinese subsets of Emilia-large. The training incorporates our
proposed noising strategy, where[EXPAND]and[DELETE]
operations are applied independently with a 0.5 probability,
with the[EXPAND]operation specifically merging blocks of
sizen= 5. The entire training is conducted on 24 NVIDIA
A100 (80GB) GPUs, optimized with AdamW using a 1e-4
learning rate and 32K warmup steps. For inference, we use a
default of 50 steps and a confidence thresholdτof 0.8. Sam-
pling is performed using top-k (k= 20), where the temperature
linearly anneals from 1.5 to 0 throughout the process.
3.2. Experiment Results
3.2.1. Main Results
We conducted our evaluations on the seed-test-zh and seed-test-
en benchmarks from Seed-TTS, using MaskGCT as our primary
baseline. To isolate and highlight the benefits of eliminating
accurate duration, we designed three distinct settings for the
MaskGCT baseline. The first, termedNormal, represents the
standard MaskGCT which relies on its built-in duration model.
The second,Fix, simulates a poor duration estimate by initial-
izing the sequence to a fixed length (4s for Chinese, 4.5s for En-
glish). Finally, to serve as a performance upper bound, theGT
setting provides MaskGCT with the ground-truth audio length.
Our objective metrics include Word Error Rate (WER)
for accuracy, L1-distance on sequence length (Len-L1) for
variable-length capability, and WavLM-based cosine similar-
ity for speaker similarity (Sim). For subjective evaluation, we
conducted a Mean Opinion Score (N-MOS) test for naturalness
(prosody and speech rate), where 15 professional listeners rated
10 random samples per test set. All results are presented with
95% confidence intervals.
As shown in Table 1, our proposed model (Ours-Normal)
exhibits strong performance. It achieves WER comparable to
the standard MaskGCT and even its oracleGTsetting (slightly
higher in Chinese, lower in English) with nearly identical Sim,
indicating that our variable-length capability does not com-
promise fundamental synthesis quality. More importantly, our
model secures the best results across both testsets on the Len-
L1 and N-MOS metrics. This demonstrates that our model can
effectively correct for potentially biased initial length predic-
tions, yielding an output sequence length closer to the ground
truth. This improved length control directly translates to more
natural prosody and speech rate, as reflected by the superior
N-MOS scores. In contrast, the original MaskGCT lacks this
corrective mechanism; once an inaccurate length is predicted, it
often leads to global or local unnaturalness in the final output.
Further evidence of our model’s superiority is found in the
Fixscenario. Our model shows only a minor performance
variation between theNormalandFixsettings across all
key metrics (WER, Len-L1, N-MOS). Conversely, the baseline
MaskGCT is highly sensitive to the initial length, showing a se-
vere performance drop in theFixcase. This demonstrates that
our model is independent on an accurate duration predictor and
possesses a robust mechanism for dynamic length adjustment.

3.2.3. Analysis of Different Initial Length
As shown in Table 2, experiments on theseed-test-zh
benchmark with initial lengths ranging from 1s to 16s demon-
strate the exceptional robustness of our proposed model. First,
when compared to the primary baseline MaskGCT, our model
maintains a consistently low WER across the entire spectrum,
whereas MaskGCT demonstrates a heavy reliance on accurate
duration prediction. Furthermore, we compare our approach
with more powerful NAR models, specifically E2-TTS [20] and
F5-TTS [21]. Although their continuous representation-based
architectures and superior pre-trained foundations bring better
overall performance and a certain degree of robustness, their
synthesis quality still deteriorates rapidly as the input length
varies. Ultimately, all baseline models fail completely at ex-
treme lengths (1s, 16s). Experiments also conducted on English
test set yield similar results. These findings confirm our model’s
powerful dynamic length adjustment capability, proving reli-
able under challenging duration-prediction-free conditions.
3.2.4. Analysis of Expansion Block Sizen
We performed an ablation study to determine the optimal Ex-
pansion Block Size (n), a critical and sensitive hyperparameter
controlling then-to-1merging (noising) and1-to-nexpansion
(denoising). As shown in Fig.2, the WER onseed-test-zh
exhibits a U-shaped curve asnvaries, indicating degradation
whennis extreme. A smallnfails to provide a strong compres-
sion signal, causing the model to treat expansion as noise, while
a largenresults in rigid, inflexible expansions during inference.
Consequently, we identifiedn= 5, a value approximating the
average phoneme duration, as the optimal setting, as it best bal-
ances accuracy and length-adjustment flexibility to achieve the
minimum WER, with similar result on English data.
3.2.5. Analysis of Application Flexibility
Highly expressive speech exhibits extreme prosodic variations,
making variable-length modeling flexibility urgently necessary.
To evaluate this, we fine-tuned our model and the MaskGCT
baseline on a highly expressive single-speaker dataset. An AB
preference test was then conducted by 15 professional listeners,
who evaluated synthesized held-out texts across four emotional
styles: Fearful, Sad, Coquettish, and Excited.
Results (Fig.3) reveal our model’s significant advantage in
rendering rich emotions through dynamic speaking paces. Con-
versely, MaskGCT, constrained by its external duration predic-
tor, produced rigid paces, yielding prosody that mismatched
emotional intents and degraded synthesis quality. These find-
ings validate that our variable-length architecture endows the
model with superior expressiveness and flexibility.
