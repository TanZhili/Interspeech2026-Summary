Optimal Linguistic Complexity for Dialogue System Speech in Noise:
Convergent Evidence from Automatic and Human Transcription
Lubos Marcinek1, Jonas Beskow1, Joakim Gustafson1
1 Department of Speech, Music and Hearing, KTH Royal Institute of Technology, Sweden
lubosm@kth.se, beskow@kth.se, jocke@speech.kth.se
Abstract
Spoken dialogue systems must synthesise responses that are re-
liably understood in noisy conditions, yet the optimal linguistic
complexity for both ASR and human listeners remains empir-
ically underexplored. We address this gap with two studies.
Study 1 measured ASR accuracy for 168,000 synthesised utter-
ances spanning five complexity levels, two TTS systems, twelve
noise types, and seven SNRs. Study 2 (pilot,N= 15) had
human listeners transcribe synthesised utterances at three com-
plexity levels across four noise environments and three SNRs.
Both studies show a U-shaped pattern: natural grammatical
sentences (Level 3, 9–16 words) outperform telegraphic forms
(Level 1) by 43% in ASR WER and by 45% in human tran-
scription error. The grammar advantage paradoxically grows as
conditions improve. These findings reject adaptive simplifica-
tion and establish a 9–19 words as optimal for system output.
Index Terms: dialogue systems, text-to-speech, automatic
speech recognition, linguistic complexity, noise robustness,
human-robot interaction
1. Introduction
Evaluating the intelligibility of synthesised speech has long
posed a methodological challenge. Subjective listening tests re-
main the gold standard but do not scale to the volumes required
during system development. A widely adopted alternative treats
ASR word error rate (WER) as an objective proxy: if a well-
trained recogniser consistently misrecognises a synthesised ut-
terance, a human listener is likely to encounter similar difficul-
ties. Wang et al. [1] extended this approach to noise-degraded
conditions, showing that the WER–intelligibility link holds
across TTS architectures under additive noise. Yang et al. [2]
directly compared human and ASR intelligibility in speech-
shaped noise, finding that modern recognisers transcribed ap-
proximately 10% more words correctly than human listeners at
matched SNRs. Intelligibility in noise is crucial for situated
dialogue systems such as assistive robots in a care-home or a
guide robot in a museum: Ren et al. [3] adapted pitch, rate,
and loudness to ambient acoustics and listener distance; Fis-
cher et al. [4] found that incrementally raising voice level with
noise was perceived positively over a fixed-loudness baseline;
Novoa et al. [5] showed commercial ASR engines suffer rapid
WER inflation below approximately 15 dB SNR indoors. This
frames the noise-intelligibility problem as bidirectional: the
system’s synthesised output must reach the human, and the hu-
man’s speech must reach the system’s recogniser. A third strand
concerns what we term the Listening Speaker problem: whether
a dialogue system should monitor the acoustic environment and
interlocutor feedback while speaking, and adapt its output ac-
cordingly. Existing work addresses this at the dialogue-policy
level, where noise-adaptive action selection outperforms fixed
policies [6], or at the turn-taking level, governing when rather
than how to speak [7]. To the best of our knowledge, no dia-
logue framework adapts the linguistic form of its output specif-
ically in response to ambient noise — distinct from the broader
psycholinguistic literature on syntactic complexity and noise
(Section 2), which characterises perception effects but has not
been operationalised as a noise-adaptive design principle for di-
alogue systems. The question is whether such a system should
shorten, simplify, or restructure its utterances, and whether this
improves or degrades intelligibility for either listener type.
We address this gap with two studies: a large-scale ASR
evaluation (168,000 transcriptions; Section 3) and a human lis-
tening pilot (N= 15; Section 4). Both reveal a U-shaped
complexity–error curve in which natural grammatical sentences
(Level 3, 9–16 words) outperform telegraphic forms (Level 1)
by 43% in ASR WER and 45% in human transcription er-
ror, with the advantage growing — paradoxically — as condi-
tions improve. These findings reject the adaptive simplification
heuristic and establish a 9–19 word grammatical zone as op-
timal for dialogue system speech, with direct implications for
the output-generation component of Listening Speaker archi-
tectures.
2. Related Work
Adaptive simplification in dialogue systems:Early non-
understanding recovery research compared ten recovery strate-
gies and found that effectiveness depended on conversational
content and function rather than brevity: the best-performing
strategies included a detailed help message and a strategy that
abandoned the current question to advance the task, while min-
imal repetition prompts were among the worst performers [8].
These findings were obtained with template-generated, HMM-
era output that lacked the deep linguistic integration of trans-
former architectures.
Linguistic complexity and human speech perception:
Syntactic complexity is known to affect comprehension diffi-
culty in noise: complex sentence structures are disproportion-
ately disrupted by noise [9], and complexity-related compre-
hension errors increase as SNR worsens [10]; complexity and
noise level have also been shown to affect processing effort
through partially separate pathways, with complexity driving
physiological effort markers and noise driving subjective effort
ratings [11]. Telegraphic speech is similarly harder to parse
than grammatically complete utterances in noise [12], since
grammatical structure provides syntactic prediction, prosodic
scaffolding, and redundant function-word cues. This literature
addresses syntactic complexity broadly rather than dialogue-
system output specifically, the gap the present work targets.

Linguistic factors in ASR accuracy:Short utterances are
a known challenge for ASR: sparse language-model context
limits hypothesis constraint [13], and the WER formula arith-
metically inflates error rates for short references. Robertson et
al. [14] showed that textual predictability more broadly influ-
ences transformer ASR accuracy via lexical–acoustic interac-
tions in Wav2Vec 2.0.
TTS quality and downstream evaluation:Neural TTS ar-
chitectures such as FastSpeech 2 [15] and Tacotron 2 [16] differ
in prosodic and phonetic encoding. The present work extends
this by varying the linguistic content of TTS input text system-
atically, isolating linguistic form as the experimental factor.
3. Study 1: Computational ASR Evaluation
3.1. Design
We systematically varied five factors: complexity level (L1–
L5), SNR (−15 to+15 dB in 5 dB steps), TTS system
(XTTS v2 [17], KokoroTTS [18]), voice gender (male, fe-
male), noise type (12 DEMAND environments [19]), and ASR
model (Whisper large-v3, Wav2Vec2 base-960h). Evaluating
250 sentences under all factor combinations yields 168,000
transcriptions. Crossing TTS architecture, voice gender (∼120
vs.∼220HzF 0), and ASR paradigm (seq2seq vs. CTC) cre-
ates eight independent replications of the core complexity com-
parison, testing internal generalisability across implementation
choices.
3.2. Sentence corpus
We constructed 250 semantically matched sentences (50 per
level) spanning five everyday dialogue system deployment top-
ics: commuting and navigation, scheduling and reminders, no-
tifications, weather and environment, and general information
queries. Sentences were generated using a large language model
(Claude), prompted per topic to produce five complexity-level
rewrites of a single base proposition. Outputs were reviewed
by the authors for overall coherence and appropriateness before
complexity was verified via the manipulation checks below. All
sentences within a topic express identical propositional content
at every complexity level, varying only in grammatical com-
pleteness and lexical register, so that WER differences between
levels reflect form rather than content. The five levels, illus-
trated with a commuting example, are:
•L1 Telegraphic(3–5 w):“Drive to work. ”
•L2 Simple(5–13 w):“He drives to work every morning in-
stead of taking the bus. ”
•L3 Natural(9–16 w):“He drives to work every morning
because the bus takes too long from his house. ”
•L4 Moderate(9–19 w):“He drives to work every morning
because public transportation would take much longer from
his neighborhood. ”
•L5 Complex(13–30 w):“He opts for vehicular transit each
morning because mass transit from his residential vicinity
would necessitate substantially protracted commute dura-
tions and multiple transfers daily. ”
Word-count ranges overlap intentionally: a 13-word sen-
tence may belong to L2, L3, L4, or L5 depending solely
on grammatical structure, enabling within-length comparisons
that isolate grammatical form as the operative variable. Ma-
nipulation checks confirmed monotonic complexity progres-
sion: Flesch-Kincaid gradeF(4,245) = 251.9,p <10 −85,
η2 = 0.804; word countF(4,245) = 200.2,p <10 −76,
η2 = 0.766.
3.3. Speech synthesis, noise degradation, and ASR
XTTS v2 (multilingual zero-shot) and KokoroTTS (English-
specialised) each provided male and female voices at neutral
prosody (∼150wpm), 16 kHz. Noise was drawn from the DE-
MAND corpus [19] (12 environments: bus, car, metro, traf-
fic, station, cafeteria, restaurant, meeting room, office, kitchen,
living room, washing machine) and mixed at seven SNRs.
Whisper large-v3 [20] (1.55B parameters) and Wav2Vec2 base-
960h [21] (95M parameters) provided ASR transcriptions.
WER was computed via Levenshtein distance [22] after low-
ercasing and punctuation removal.
3.4. Statistical analysis
OLS regression with two-way cluster-robust standard er-
rors [23] clustered by sentence ID (250) and noise type (12).
The model included dummy-coded complexity levels (L1 ref-
erence), SNR, a Level 3×SNR interaction, and TTS and ASR
covariates. Three length confound tests (∆R2 <0.01), a GPT-
2 log-perplexity control (∆R 2 = 0.0017), and prosodic medi-
ation tests (∆R 2 = 0.0086) verified that effects reflect gram-
matical structure. Effect sizes are Cohen’sd[24].
4. Study 2: Human Listening Experiment
4.1. Design and participants
We conducted an online pilot transcription experiment via Pro-
lific [25] to obtain preliminary human evidence on whether
ASR findings generalise to human listeners.N= 15partic-
ipants (native English speakers, self-reported normal hearing,
aged 18–65) were recruited and paid at the standard Prolific
rate. The study received ethical approval [blinded for review].
We used a within-subjects design: Complexity level (3: L1,
L3, L5), Noise environment (4: bus, cafeteria, kitchen, meeting
room), and SNR (3:−10, 0,+10 dB).
4.2. Stimuli and procedure
Sentences were drawn from the Study 1 corpus and synthe-
sised using XTTS v2 with a male voice at neutral prosody,
then mixed with noise from the four DEMAND environments
at three SNRs. Each participant was presented with 40 stim-
uli representing a balanced selection of complexity levels, noise
environments, and SNR conditions, in randomised order.
Participants used headphones (confirmed via a screening
item [26]), listened to each stimulus via an embedded audio
player with unlimited replays, and typed verbatim what they
heard. Human WER was computed using the same Levenshtein
pipeline as Study 1, enabling direct comparison.

5.2. Study 2: Human transcription results
Human transcription produced an overall mean WER of 27.5%
across 600 trials (33% perfect transcriptions). WER by com-
plexity level showed a clear U-shaped pattern (Figure 4):
L1=35.3%, L3=19.4%, L5=27.3%. A Friedman test confirmed
a significant main effect of complexity (χ 2 = 19.60,p <
0.001). Bonferroni-corrected Wilcoxon post-hoc tests showed
that all three pairwise comparisons were significant (L1 vs. L3:
p= 0.0002; L3 vs. L5:p= 0.0013; L1 vs. L5:p= 0.025),
confirming Level 3 as the unique optimum. Moving from tele-
graphic (L1) to natural grammatical speech (L3) reduced human
WER by 15.9 percentage points — a 45% relative reduction,
virtually identical to the 43% reduction observed in Study 1.
SNR had the expected large effect (Figure 4B): WER fell

5.3. Cross-study convergence
Despite the difference in scale and methodology, the two stud-
ies show remarkable directional convergence: L3 is optimal in
both paradigms, and the magnitude of the L1→L3 reduction is
43% (Study 1) vs. 45% (Study 2) — a difference of just 2 per-
centage points across paradigms separated by three orders of
magnitude in trial count, listener type, and methodology. The
paradoxical SNR scaling is present in both studies, though more
pronounced in Study 1 due to its wider SNR range. While the
pilot sample size of Study 2 precludes strong inferential claims
about human listeners independently, the near-identical effect
magnitude provides preliminary evidence that the optimal com-
plexity zone reflects a property of spoken language processing
shared across biological and artificial listeners, and motivates a
fully powered replication.
6. Discussion
6.1. Why grammatical structure helps both listener types
For human listeners, grammatical structure provides syntactic
prediction, prosodic scaffolding at clause boundaries, and re-
dundant function-word cues that support perceptual robustness
under degradation [12]. For transformer ASR, cross-attention
over grammatical sequences creates analogous top-down con-
straints, narrowing the recognition hypothesis space at each de-
coding step [27, 14]. Telegraphic speech is doubly harmful for
both listener types: it omits the predictive markers and reduces
syntactic predictability simultaneously.
The paradoxical SNR scaling follows naturally: grammati-
cal cues can only be exploited once the signal is clear enough to
detect them, so each SNR improvement unlocks more grammat-
ical information, amplifying the benefit of having that structure
present — precisely in the moderate-to-clean SNR range that
characterises most real dialogue system deployments.
6.2. Implications for dialogue system design
The consistent recommendation is to abandon adaptive simpli-
fication and maintain grammatical completeness regardless of
detected noise level. The optimal zone — 9–19 words, com-
plete SVO syntax, natural prosodic phrasing — is robust across
TTS architecture, voice gender, ASR paradigm, and human lis-
tener variability, and covers the SNR range (≥0dB) where
most operational dialogue systems function. Level 5 sentences
extending to 30 words show only 2.1 pp WER elevation over
the optimal zone, so the ASR-based rationale for truncating at
15–17 words is overly conservative for modern transformer ar-
chitectures; systems can safely extend to 19–25 words. The
cognitive load rationale is partially supported by Study 2, where
L5 sentences — the longest in the corpus — showed higher hu-
man transcription error than L3 (27.3% vs. 19.4%), consistent
with working memory constraints [28]; however, a fully pow-
ered study is needed to isolate cognitive load from other prop-
erties of complex syntax.
6.3. Limitations and future work
Study 2 is a pilot withN= 15participants. The Friedman
tests confirm significant effects of complexity and SNR, but
the sample cannot support noise-environment subgroup or in-
teraction analyses; a fully powered replication (N≥50) is
needed before strong independent claims about human listeners.
Study 1 evaluated only Whisper and Wav2Vec2; Conformer-
CTC [29] and RNN-T architectures warrant evaluation. Noise
was additive DEMAND noise without convolutive reverbera-
tion, which may modulate absolute magnitudes. Both stud-
ies are in English; morphologically rich (Turkish, Finnish) and
topic-prominent (Mandarin, Japanese) languages require inde-
pendent evaluation, as ASR performance varies substantially
across language families and writing systems [30].
7. Conclusion
We presented a large-scale computational study and a pilot hu-
man experiment establishing the optimal linguistic complexity
zone for dialogue system speech. Study 1 (168,000 ASR tran-
scriptions) showed that natural grammatical sentences in the 9–
19 word range outperform telegraphic forms by 43% in WER,
with the advantage growing as acoustic conditions improve.
Study 2 (N= 15) provides convergent preliminary human evi-
dence: the same U-shaped pattern, Level 3 optimal, and a 45%
relative reduction, with significant effects of both complexity
(Friedmanχ 2 = 19.60,p <0.001) and SNR (χ 2 = 24.13,
p <0.001). Together, these findings reject the adaptive sim-
plification heuristic and recommend maintaining grammatical
completeness in the 9–19 word zone.
