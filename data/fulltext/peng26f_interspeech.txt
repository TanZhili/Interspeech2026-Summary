Cross-Lingual Speaker Verification with Self-Supervised Pre-Trained Models
Jinghan Peng∗, Yu Zheng∗, Weiqiang Wang, Jian Liu∗∗
Machine Intelligence, Ant Group, Shanghai, China

Abstract
Speaker verification (SV) performance degrades under lan-
guage mismatch due to the entanglement of speaker identity
with language-specific acoustic cues. To address this prob-
lem, we leverage large-scale self-supervised pre-trained models
(PTMs) to learn language-agnostic speaker representations. We
utilize PTMs as robust front-end feature extractors, capitalizing
on their rich acoustic and linguistic knowledge acquired from
vast, diverse audio data. These generalized features are then
used to train a downstream speaker embedding network, ef-
fectively disentangling speaker identity from language-specific
characteristics. We validate our approach on the TidyV oice2026
benchmark, which benchmarks SV under language mismatch.
Our proposed system (teamT02) achieves equal error rates
(EERs) of 2.21% on tv26 eval-A and 2.99% on tv26 eval-U.
Index Terms: speaker verification, cross-lingual robustness,
pre-trained models
1. Introduction
Speaker verification (SV), the task of confirming a claimed
identity based on a speaker’s voice, has witnessed substantial
advancements over the past decade [1–6]. However, the real-
world deployment of SV systems in our globalized world hinges
on their ability to handle a critical bottleneck: language mis-
match [7,8]. This prevalent condition in multilingual scenarios,
where a speaker’s voice characteristics are confounded by the
acoustic properties of different languages, presents a formidable
challenge. Consequently, recognition accuracy often degrades
significantly when enrollment and verification occur in different
languages. The TidyV oice2026 benchmark [8] is established
to tackle this very issue of speaker verification under language
mismatch. The freely accessible TidyV oice2026 benchmark
tackles this issue by providing a multilingual dataset, driving
reproducible research toward effective cross-lingual solutions.
The central challenge in cross-lingual SV is to extract a
language-agnostic speaker representation that encapsulates the
unique physiological and behavioral traits of a speaker while re-
maining invariant to the linguistic content being spoken. Recent
breakthroughs in self-supervised learning (SSL) for audio offer
a promising pathway. Pre-trained models (PTMs) like Wav2Vec
2.0 [9], XLS-R [10], MMS [11], HuBERT [12], WavLM [13],
CLAP [14], w2v-BERT [15], w2v-BERT 2.0 [16], pre-trained
on millions of hours of unlabeled, multilingual speech, learn
rich, hierarchical representations that capture general acoustic,
phonetic, and even semantic information. Our core hypothesis
is that these SSL representations provide a superior foundation

for building robust cross-lingual SV systems [13]. Rather than
training an SV model from scratch on potentially limited and
language-specific data, a promising alternative is to leverage the
generalized knowledge embedded within large-scale pre-trained
models [17–19]. Chen et al. [17] employed PTMs like Wav2Vec
2.0 [9], HuBERT [12], and UniSpeech-SAT [20], using learn-
able weights to average the representations from all hidden lay-
ers, which were then fed as input features into an ECAPA-
TDNN [4] to build the speaker model. Zhao et al. [18] utilized
the encoder of Whisper Large-v2 [21] as the PTM and proposed
Partial Multi-scale Feature Aggregation to concatenate output
features from different layer modules. Similarly, Li et al. [19]
adopted the w2v-BERT 2.0 as the PTM and used Multi-scale
Feature Aggregation to concatenate features from all layers be-
fore feeding them into the downstream module. These stud-
ies have validated the effectiveness of PTM-based SV on the
V oxCeleb benchmark [22, 23] and CN-Celeb benchmark [24].
In this work, we employ w2v-BERT 2.0 as a front-end feature
extractor, apply multi-scale feature aggregation over the layer-
wise outputs, feed the aggregated representations into a pooling
layer to obtain utterance-level features, and finally project them
into a fixed-dimensional speaker embedding. To ensure sta-
ble training of this self-supervised learning PTM-based speaker
verification model, we effectively leverage a three-stage train-
ing paradigm. Additionally, we experiment with the lightweight
ReDimNet [6] as an auxiliary system for comparative analy-
sis and subsequent score-level fusion. Our systems achieve an
Equal Error Rate (EER) of 2.21% on the tv26 eval-A set and
2.99% on the tv26 eval-U set.
Here are the key contributions of our work:
• We present a powerful SV pipeline for cross-lingual ver-
ification, using a pre-trained w2v-BERT 2.0 front-end for
language-agnostic speaker embeddings and a lightweight
back-end for efficient speaker embedding extraction.
• We demonstrate strong performance on the TidyV oice2026
benchmark, achieving an EER of 2.21% on tv26 eval-A and
2.99% on tv26 eval-U, validating the effectiveness of our ap-
proach under language mismatch.
• We offer a detailed analysis of key design elements, including
pre-trained model selection, feature aggregation, and training
strategies, providing practical guidance on model selection
and training for real-world applications.

We employ on-the-fly data augmentation to improve model
robustness to acoustic variability. During training, a random set
of augmentations is applied to each utterance on a per-utterance
basis from the following four types:
•Reverberation: artificial reverberation is applied by con-
volving the signal with simulated room impulse responses
(RIRs), using RIRs from the AIR dataset [28];
•Music: a music file without vocals is randomly selected from
MUSAN [29], trimmed or looped to match the utterance du-
ration, and mixed with the original signal at a signal-to-noise
ratio (SNR) between 5–15 dB;
•Noise: generic noise segments from MUSAN are added at
one-second intervals with an SNR ranging from 0–15 dB;
•Babble: overlapping speech from MUSAN is mixed with the
original signal at an SNR of 10–30 dB to simulate crowd or
conversational environments.
During training, each utterance has a probability of 0.3 to
be reverberated, 0.1 to be mixed with music, 0.1 to be corrupted
with noise, and 0.1 to be mixed with babble speech.
2.2. Development dataset
For system development and validation, we use the
TidyVoiceX Dev[8, 25] dataset, which contains 808 speakers
spanning 40 languages. The development set provides a
large-scale trial list comprising 12 million verification pairs,
constructed to cover different language- and speaker-mismatch
scenarios. We rely exclusively on this development set for
model selection, threshold adjustment, and score calibra-
tion, ensuring that our system is optimized under the same
cross-lingual conditions as the evaluation set.
2.3. Evaluation dataset
The evaluation is performed on theTidyVoiceX2[8] dataset,
featuring two trial lists:tv26 eval-Afor partial language mis-
match (enrollment: seen languages; test: unseen languages),
and the more challengingtv26 eval-Ufor full generalization,
where both enrollment and test use a separate set of 38 lan-
guages unseen in training and development data.
3. Methodology
3.1. Model architecture
3.1.1. PTM-based speaker verification systems
The overall architecture of our primary PTM-based speaker ver-
ification system is illustrated in Figure 1(a). The pipeline begins
with an input audio signal, from which acoustic features such
as log-Mel filterbanks are extracted. These features are then fed
into a pre-trained SSL model (e.g., w2v-BERT 2.0 [16]). The
PTM typically consists of a stack of transformer or conformer
layers [10, 11, 16, 21]. We extract the hidden representations
from all layers of the SSL model. To align and project the het-
erogeneous features from different SSL layers, each layer’s out-
put is passed through a dedicatedAdapter. Each Adapter is im-
plemented as a two-layer Multilayer Perceptron (MLP), which
projects the features from their original dimension to a unified
dimension of 256.
A core component of our framework is theMulti-scale Fea-
ture Aggregationmodule, which fuses the adapted features from
all SSL layers. We investigate six distinct fusion mechanisms:
1.Mean Fusion (MF): The unified representation is obtained
by averaging the features across all layers.
2.Layer-wise Weighted Fusion (LWF): A learnable weight is
assigned to each layer, and a weighted sum of the layer fea-
tures is computed.
3.Channel-wise Weighted Fusion (CWF): Each channel in
each layer is assigned a learnable weight for a fine-grained
weighted fusion.
4.Channel Concatenation (CC): Features from all layers are
concatenated along the channel dimension.
5.Temporal Concatenation (TC): Features from all layers are
concatenated along the temporal dimension.
6.Hierarchical Cross-Attention Fusion (HCAF): This
method leverages a two-stage attention mechanism. First, it
performs cross-attention where the representation from the
last SSL layer serves as the queries, interacting with keys
and values from all preceding layers to integrate multi-scale
information. This is followed by a self-attention layer on the
aggregated output to refine the contextual relationships and
produce the final fused representation.
The resulting frame-level features from the aggregation
module are then processed by anAttentive Statistics Pool-
ing[30] layer to generate a fixed-dimensional utterance-level
representation. This representation is subsequently passed
through aEmbedding Projection Layerto produce the final 256-
dimensional speaker embedding. For training, we employ the
Additive Angular Margin (AAM) Softmax Loss[31] to optimize
the network parameters.
3.1.2. ReDimNet systems
For comparative analysis and system fusion, we also implement
ReDimNetmodels [6]. Specifically, we experiment with two
variants:ReDimNet-B5andReDimNet-B6. Both models pro-
duce 192-dimensional speaker embeddings and are trained us-
ing theSphereFace2-C (SF2-C)[32] loss function.
3.2. Training paradigm and details
In this work, we adopt different training paradigms for the
PTM-based speaker verification models and RedimNet models,
respectively. For the PTM-based speaker verification model,
we employ a three-stage training strategy, as illustrated in Fig-
ure 1(b). The model is initialized with the pre-trained weights
from the SSL foundation model, while the remaining down-
stream parameters are randomly initialized. In the first stage,
the weights of the SSL backbone are frozen, and only the down-
stream modules are trained using the full dataset described in
Section 2.1. The training lasts for 60 epochs with 2-second seg-
ments, a batch size of 16,384, and an AAM-softmax margin
penalty that gradually increases from 0 to 0.2 between epochs

4. Experiments
4.1. Results
The performance of models is assessed on the tv26 eval-A
and tv26 eval-U sets using the Equal Error Rate (EER) as
the primary evaluation metric, and the minimum Detection
Cost Function (minDCF) withP target = 0.01andC FA =
CMiss = 1as a secondary metric. We also report results on the
TidyV oiceXDev set for completeness and comparative anal-
ysis. Table 1 summarizes the performance of the proposed
systems. The w2v-BERT 2.0-based SV system trained with
AAM loss achieves 1.03% on the dev set, 3.34% on eval-A, and
4.59% on eval-U. After applying the Quality Measure Func-
tions (QMF) [34] back-end from the WeSpeaker [35] toolkit
for score calibration, using TidyV oiceX Dev as the cohort, the
EER on eval-A and eval-U is reduced to 2.73% and 2.84%.
For the lightweight ReDimNet models trained with SF2-C loss,
ReDimNet-B5 and ReDimNet-B6 obtain 1.14% and 0.98% on
the dev set, respectively. With QMF, the ReDimNet-B5 sys-
tem achieved EERs of 3.47% on eval-A and 4.84% on eval-
U. The ReDimNet-B6 system achieved 2.52% on eval-A and
3.43% on eval-U. Although the w2v-BERT 2.0-based SV sys-
tem and the ReDimNet systems are trained on the same datasets,
the former achieves a lower EER on eval-U, which involves un-
seen languages, indicating that the PTM-based SV system of-
fers stronger generalization ability.
Finally, we perform simple score-level fusion by averag-
ing the calibrated scores from all QMF-enhanced systems. The
fused system achieves the best overall performance among our
submitted systems on the official evaluation sets, with an EER
of 2.21% on eval-A and 2.99% on eval-U.

them as front-end feature extractors for speaker verification.
In addition, we evaluate a weakly supervised model, Whis-
per Large-v3 [21], which is pre-trained on large-scale multi-
lingual speech–text pairs. Specifically, we utilize its encoder

580M parameters and is pre-trained using self-supervised ob-

We experiment with several multi-scale feature aggregation
strategies as described in Section 3.1.1 on the w2v-BERT 2.0-
based SV model, using only TidyV oiceXTrain for fine-tuning.

5. Conclusions
In this work, we designed a speaker verification framework
based on self-supervised pre-trained models. The core idea
is to leverage the robust acoustic representations learned from
vast amounts of unlabeled multilingual audio through self-
supervised learning, with the goal of building more generaliz-
able and language-agnostic speaker recognition systems. We
employed the w2v-BERT 2.0 as the pre-trained backbone and
conducted a comparative analysis of various multi-scale feature
aggregation strategies. Furthermore, we designed and imple-
mented a three-stage training paradigm to progressively adapt
the model. For comparison, we also trained lightweight ReD-
imNet models. The results indicate that, given the same train-
ing data, the w2v-BERT 2.0-based SV system demonstrates su-
perior performance on evaluation sets containing unseen lan-
guages. Our final system fusion strategy achieved an EER of
2.21% on the tv26 eval-A set and 2.99% on the tv26 eval-U set
on the TidyV oice2026 benchmark.
