AdaptLingo: A Speech-to-Speech English Practice System with
Fluency-Adaptive Responses
Zackary Rackauckas1,2
1 AI Research, RoleGaku, USA 2 Computer Science, Columbia University, USA
zackary.rackauckas@rolegaku.com
Abstract
We demonstrate AdaptLingo, an open-source, speech-to-speech
English conversational agent for second-language learners. Be-
ginners may be overwhelmed by many static language learn-
ing systems, which use complex language and fast speech,
while advanced learners may disengage from simplified dia-
logue. AdaptLingo addresses this problem by predicting a
learner’s spoken fluency from temporal acoustic features, con-
straining responses to the learner’s EIKEN-aligned vocabulary
level, and adapting text-to-speech rate. In demonstration, users
speak to the chatbot and receive spoken responses adapted to
beginner, intermediate, or advanced fluency. Preliminary eval-
uation shows fluency classification works well on labeled data
but remains challenging on noisy user-study speech, and user
feedback suggests that adaptive spoken practice is promising
when matched to learner background and conversational goals.
Index Terms: CALL, spoken dialogue, fluency assessment
1. Introduction
English speaking practice is important for second-language
learners, but opportunities for comfortable, low-stakes interac-
tion are limited. Native conversation partners, teachers, and
tutors may not always be available, and existing computer-
assisted language learning (CALL) systems often do not adapt
well to different learner levels. Beginners can be discouraged by
responses that are too fast or lexically complex, while advanced
learners may find simplified dialogue unnatural or unhelpful.
To address this, we presentAdaptLingo 1, a speech-to-
speech English conversation agent. Recent work has explored
LLM-based oral practice systems that combine chatbot interac-
tion with speech interfaces for conference Q&A practice [1].
AdaptLingo extends this direction to general English conver-
sation practice by adapting response vocabulary and text-to-
speech (TTS) rate to the learner’s predicted fluency level. Our
goal is to create an interactive environment where learners can
practice spoken English without relying on a human partner.
The system has three adaptive components: fluency classifi-
cation using temporal acoustic features, EIKEN-controlled re-
sponse generation, and level-specific text-to-speech (TTS) rate.

2. Fluency-adaptive response configuration
AdaptLingo estimates learner fluency using temporal acoustic
features associated with second-language speech fluency [3, 4].

For training the fluency classifier, audio was converted to a stan-
dard waveform format, silence-trimmed, normalized, and pro-
cessed with a Praat script to calculate speech features [5]. We
trained a random forest classifier to predict three levels from
articulation rate and speech rate: beginner, intermediate, and
advanced.
In the demonstration, the user’s recorded audio is saved,
transcribed with the CrisperWhisper model, and processed to
compute the same temporal acoustic features. The trained
random forest then predicts a fluency level, which conditions
EIKEN-controlled generation and TTS speed.
The classifier achieved 95% test macro F1 on our labeled
fluency dataset, but 47% accuracy on noisier user-study speech.
This gap highlights the difficulty of in-the-wild fluency estima-
tion and motivates future work on classifier generalizability.
EIKEN is a standardized English proficiency test widely
used in Japan and organized into graded proficiency levels.
In AdaptLingo, we use EIKEN vocabulary lists as lexical re-
sources for controlling response difficulty.
After the fluency level is predicted, AdaptLingo selects the
corresponding beginner, intermediate, or advanced EIKEN vo-
cabulary collection. The system retrieves relevant EIKEN vo-
cabulary with k-nearest-neighbor search. In addition to prompt-
ing the model with the retrieved vocabulary, AdaptLingo boosts
the logits of these words during decoding, encouraging lexically
appropriate generation without relying on prompt instructions
alone.
The generated response is synthesized with gpt-4o-mini-tts;
the speaking rate is set to0.8×,0.9×, or1.0×for beginner,
intermediate, and advanced learners, respectively.
3. System Deployment
AdaptLingo is implemented as a Gradio interface mounted in-
side a FastAPI application. This design combines a browser-
based interface with backend audio processing, model infer-
ence, static assets, and deployment through a single web server.

The demo can be run locally through Uvicorn, and for remote
studies or public demonstrations it can be exposed through an
ngrok tunnel.
At runtime, the learner records speech in the browser and
submits the audio to the backend. AdaptLingo then runs the
full adaptation pipeline described above and returns the chat-
bot response as both text and autoplayed audio. The interface
includes a learner-facing avatar, step-by-step recording instruc-
tions, microphone input, a chat-history panel, and synthesized
speech playback.
The system also includes safety and study-deployment fea-
tures. A toxicity classifier is applied to both the transcribed
user input and the generated assistant response. If user input
is flagged, the system asks the learner to try again with ap-
propriate language; if model output is flagged, the response
is not spoken back to the learner. For user-study deployment,
the system captures participant identifiers from URL parame-
ters, stores timestamped user audio, and logs each interaction
with the transcribed input, generated response, predicted flu-
ency level, and saved audio path.
The Gradio interface is designed for simple turn-based spo-
ken interaction. It includes a learner-facing avatar, step-by-step
instructions, microphone recording, a submit button, a chat-
history panel, and automatic playback of the synthesized chat-
bot response. The submit button appears only after valid au-
dio is recorded, reducing user error during the study and live
demonstration.
During the demonstration, the interface exposes the pre-
dicted fluency label to make the adaptation process inter-
pretable. The same interface can also be used to compare au-
tomatic adaptation with fixed beginner, intermediate, and ad-
vanced settings, allowing attendees to hear how the same user
input changes response vocabulary and TTS rate across learner
levels.
4. Demonstration
The live demonstration lets attendees experience AdaptLingo
as learners. A participant records a short spoken message about
an everyday topic such as food, travel, hobbies, or daily life.
AdaptLingo transcribes the utterance, extracts fluency features,
predicts a level, generates a constrained response, and speaks
the response aloud. The interaction requires only a laptop with
a microphone and a modern web browser; no specialized speech
hardware is needed.
5. Voices from learners

AdaptLingo with a baseline chatbot using the same interface
but a non-adaptive LLM backend. The evaluation methodol-
ogy and survey design were modeled after Curriculum-driven
Edubot [2], with participants choosing whether AdaptLingo,
the baseline, or both systems performed better on each survey

Japanese users responded most positively to AdaptLingo,
preferring it on 15 of 19 survey items. In contrast, Mandarin
and Spanish users more often preferred the baseline chatbot, es-
pecially on items related to naturalness, coherence, flexibility,
and recommendation to other learners. However, AdaptLingo
was still preferred by Mandarin and Spanish users on whether
the chatbot recognized them as English learners, suggesting that
the adaptive design was noticeable even when the overall inter-
action quality was not uniformly preferred.
Overall, these results suggest that adaptation is useful,
but not one-size-fits-all. AdaptLingo was especially appreci-
ated when users wanted learner-centered, lower-pressure speak-
ing practice, while users pursuing broader or more complex
conversations often preferred a more flexible baseline chat-
bot. These findings motivate future personalization by native-
language background, learner expectations, and conversational
goals.
