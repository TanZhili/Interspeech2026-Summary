A preliminary exploration of stop-vowel coarticulation in M¯aori
Isabella Shields1, Hiraia Haami-Wells2, C. T. Justine Hui2, Peter J. Keegan3, Catherine I. Watson1
1Electrical, Computer, and Software Engineering, University of Auckland, New Zealand
2Acoustics and Vibrations Research Centre, University of Auckland, New Zealand

Abstract
Te reo M¯aori (the M ¯aori language) is the indigenous language
of New Zealand. M ¯aori phonetics research has predominantly
focused on vowels and diphthongs, however there has been lim-
ited exploration of coarticulatory processes which may influ-
ence vowel realisation. This study presents a preliminary inves-
tigation of the coarticulatory impacts of oral stop consonants on
vowels in M¯aori, drawing on a new read-speech corpus of 7 flu-
ent female speakers of M¯aori. A total of 524 stressed vowel to-
kens, occurring across different stop contexts (/p t k/), are anal-
ysed. Using Generalised Additive Mixed Models (GAMMs) to
analyse first and second formants, it is shown that consonant
context significantly influences these measures, suggesting con-
sonant coarticulation results in notable changes to vowel qual-
ity, particularly backness. The implications of these findings
are discussed with respect to our understanding of vowel and
consonant variation in M¯aori.
Index Terms : M ¯aori, acoustic phonetics, coarticulation,

1. Introduction & Background
Te reo M ¯aori (the M ¯aori language; henceforth M ¯aori), an
East Polynesian language, is the indigenous language of New
Zealand. While the dominant language of New Zealand is New
Zealand English (NZE), M ¯aori is spoken at varying degrees of
proficiency by a notable proportion of the population (4.3% ac-
cording to most recent Census data) [1]. In response to decline
in M¯aori language use and transmission over the 19 th and 20th
centuries, revitalisation efforts began in earnest in the 1970s [2].
These efforts have been championed by M ¯aori-language com-
munities and supported by academic research.
The acoustics of M¯aori vowels have received notable atten-
tion in the literature, primarily in studies associated with the
M¯aori and New Zealand English (MAONZE) project [3]. This
project has analysed M ¯aori and NZE pronunciation changes
over time, based on recordings from speakers born in the 19 th
and 20th centuries. The MAONZE project has documented sev-
eral aspects of M ¯aori phonetics, including changes to M ¯aori
vowel quality and quantity [4, 5], as well as limited exploration
of consonant realisation (e.g. [6, 7]). A notable gap in M ¯aori
phonetics research is detailed analysis of consonant phonetics
and exploration of coarticulation. This study begins to address
this gap in exploring how M ¯aori vowel quality is impacted by
consonantal context. In particular, changes to the dynamic be-
haviours of the first and second formants (F 1 and F 2, respec-
tively) due to oral stops is investigated. These are the bilabial
/p/, the dento-alveolar /t/, and the velar /k/. Analysis pre-
sented in this work draws on a new corpus of M ¯aori speech
recorded between 2024 and 2025.
M¯aori has a small phoneme inventory, consisting of ten
consonants (/p t k m n N f h w r/ ) and five monophthongs ( /i
e a o u/ ) that have long and short contrasts. Monophthongs
can be combined to form diphthongs and hiatuses. M ¯aori is
considered a mora-timed language, with mora consisting of a
short monophthong and optional singleton consonant in the on-
set,µ =(C)V , although the syllable (C)V(V(V)) is also attested
and used in stress assignment. According to Biggs’ stress rules,
word stress in M¯aori is placed according to a hierarchy of sylla-
bles as well as the number of morae in a word [8, 9]. In a word
with four mora or less, word stress will fall on the syllable of
highest rank that is farthest from the word’s end: e.g. the first
syllable in papa is stressed (/"pa.pa/).
In a 2009 MAONZE study, it was noted that “... with the
exception of /r/, anticipatory transitions into the initial conso-
nant of the following syllable were not common especially for
the older speakers.” [10, p. 134-5] Aside from this claim and
the analysis of /u/-fronting in alveolar contexts (see [6]) there
is limited discussion in the literature of coarticulatory changes
in vowel realisation in M ¯aori, and no acoustic analysis of this
phenomenon aside for /r/ [11, 12, 13]. Bauer notes in [9] that
there is allophonic variation of M¯aori vowels according to both
consonant and positional context. For example, she reports that
/a/ and /u/ can be retracted in the vicinity of velars (e.g. /k
N/), and that fronting of /o/ occurs in non-velar contexts.
It is known that patterns of vowel variability due to phonetic
context differ across languages [14]. Some cross-language ten-
dencies do exist, however. For example, high-front vowels, such
as /i/ and /e/, are often more resistant to coarticulatory influ-
ence than other vowels [15, 16]. It has also been observed that
languages with small vowel systems, such as M ¯aori, can often
demonstrate greater variation in the realisation of those vow-
els [17], although this is not universal (see [18], for example).
This is usually attributed to the reduced need for careful main-
tenance of the phonemic contrast between vowels, resulting in
more ‘space’ in the system in which variation may occur.
While previous research has not noted that context conso-
nants have much influence on M ¯aori vowel quality, evidence
from other languages suggests this would be very unlikely and
merits exploration. We hypothesised that notable changes in
F1 and F 2 due to consonant context would occur, and that in
particular the non-palatal vowels, /a o u/ , would show notable
variation, with the alveolar stop likely resulting in fronting of
the vowel.

Seven women speakers of M ¯aori are analysed in the present
study. The speakers are all of M ¯aori descent and proficient
speakers of the language. At the time of recording, speak-

Target stimuli consisted of CVCV words, where C is one
of the three stops ( /p t k/ ) and V is a short vowel ( /i e a o u/ ),
placed in a carrier sentence Ko [target word] te kupu (lit. [tar-
get word] is the word). These CVCV words are shown in Table
1, and are all real words save for pupu. The vowel in the first
syllable of each target word is the focus of analysis. According
to Biggs’ stress rules [8], the first vowel in each of these words
is stressed. Considering the stressed syllable ensures the vowel
is less likely to be reduced and generally less likely to undergo
coarticulation-based changes. Additionally, the initial vowel is
both preceded and followed by the same consonant. While coar-
ticulatory effects can of course occur over longer distances, the
stimuli format used in this study ensures any observed changes
are most likely due to the shared preceding and following stop.

All recordings were made in a lab environment in a Whis-
perRoom Sound Enclosure1 (model: MLD 8484 S). Recording
sessions began with a short warm up task to habituate them to
the experiment procedure. Speakers then completed the main
production task where sentences were presented one by one on a
screen in the recording booth with speakers reading these aloud
at their own pace. Each speaker produced 5 repetitions of each
carrier sentence, resulting in a total of 525 stressed vowel to-
kens. Of these 525 tokens, one token was excluded from analy-
sis due to the speaker mis-pronouncing a target word.
Data processing and analysis was undertaken using the
EmuR package (version 2.3.0) in R (v. 4.2.0) [19, 20]. Prior
to Emu database formation, recordings underwent automated
phonetic segmentation using WebMAUS General in language
independent mode (and otherwise default settings) [21, 22, 23].
Following this automated segmentation, phone start/end bound-
aries were hand-corrected. Formant trajectories were estimated
using the forest() function in the wrassp package, with all
default settings save for gender, which was set to ‘f’ indicating
‘female’ [24]. These settings set an effective window length of
12.5 ms and nominal F 1 of 560 Hz. All F 1 and F2 estimations
were then hand-corrected from vowel onset to vowel offset.

The behaviours of F 1 and F 2 were analysed using gener-
alised additive mixed modelling (GAMM), which allows a view
into the dynamic behaviours of the formants over time (see [25]
for a summary of GAMM usage in speech analysis). Mod-
els were fitted using the bam() function in the mgcv pack-
age [26], using a scaled- t distribution as residuals were heavy
tailed. While separate GAMMs were fitted for each formant,
the model definition was the same and as follows. For each
model, the response variable was bark-scaled formant values.
An interaction between adjacent consonant (three levels: /p t
k/) and vowel quality (5 levels: /i e a o u/ ) was included as a
parametric effect, allowing for consonant impacts on formants
to vary by vowel. In addition, four smooth terms were included.
The first of these was a reference smooth over time s(time).
Two difference smooths were added to account for differences
in formant trajectory shape over time due to vowel quality and
consonant context. Finally, a random smooth was added to ac-
count for any random variation at a speaker level.
3. Results
Predicted formant trajectories from the GAMMs, generated us-
ing get_predictions() from the itsadug package [27],
are plotted in two different views. In Figure 1, F 1 and F 2 are
plotted with respect to time, and in Figure 2, the trajectories
are plotted in the acoustic vowel space (F 1/F2). In Figure 1,
colour indicates consonant context. Time is normalised, with
t = 0.00 indicating the vowel onset (and preceding consonant
offset) and t = 1.00 indicating the vowel offset (and follow-
ing consonant onset). Shaded ribbons indicate 95% confidence
intervals for each trajectory. In Figure 2, colour corresponds
to vowel, with line type indicating consonantal context. Time
is also normalised in this plot, with the arrowhead indicating
the vowel offset (t = 1.00). Tables 2 and 3 show the model
summaries for F1 and F2, respectively, including significance of
parametric terms and smooth terms. As can be seen in Tables
2 and 3, all parametric terms were significant, and most smooth
terms were significant. The GAMMs had adjustedR2 values of
0.67 (F1) and 0.82 (F2).
3.1. First formant (F 1)
While all parametric terms and most smooth terms were signif-
icant for F 1 (see Table 2), it can be seen in Figure 1 that there
is very little salient difference between consonant contexts on a
vowel-by-vowel basis. This suggests that, while there are sig-
nificant changes in F 1 intercept and trajectory shape, these do
not appear to represent notable changes in vowel height across
the various consonant contexts. At a stretch, it could be noted
that there is a small reduction in F 1 as the vowel progresses in
/kVkV/ words compared to the other contexts which could sug-
gest that the velar stop results in marginal raising of the vowels,
with this most notable in /e/. In terms of the acoustic vowel
space shown in Figure 2, it is interesting that /o/ has a rela-
tively constrained F 1 range given the vacant space between it
and /a/. In a similar vein, it can be seen that there is an area
of around 1 Bark between /e/ and /a/ that is ‘unused’ F1 range
(this has also been observed in [28]).
3.2. Second formant (F 2)
As is clear in Figure 1, much more variation due to consonant
context is visible for F 2. This is evident for all five vowels,
with changes to both F2 trajectory shape and height visible. All
parametric and smooth terms were significant for F 2, save for

dorsum required to produce /k/, in turn resulting in a more
palatalised realisation of the vowels. Interestingly, the only no-
table difference in /a/ F2 occurs in the bilabial context, where
it is notably retracted compared to the alveolar and velar con-
texts. In contrast to [9], there is no retraction of /a/ in the velar
context, with almost total overlap between /tata/ and /kaka/. If
the velar consonant is notably palatal when preceding/follow-
ing /a/, it is possible that the difference in articulatory gesture
needed for /k/ and /t/ is limited to a change in using the tongue
tip or dorsum to create the constriction. This retraction with the
velar is visible for the back vowels, however, and is best seen
in Figure 2. In addition, increased F 2 during /o/ and /u/ when
surrounded by the alveolar stop ( /tVtV/ words) can be seen,
indicating these vowels are notably fronted relative to the velar
and bilabial contexts. In the case of /o/, this fronting results in
an F2 value similar to that seen for /a/.
4. Discussion
It is evident that all of the short vowels in M¯aori are influenced
by consonant context. This is coherent with vowel variation pat-
terns across various languages [14], and broadly coherent with
descriptions of M ¯aori vowels in previous work [9, 29]. While
statistically significant, few differences in F 1 across the differ-
ent stop contexts were particularly salient. Conversely, there
was notable variation in F 2 due to stop context. This suggests
that the height of M¯aori vowels is largely resistant to changes in
stop context, but notable changes in vowel backness occur.
High-front vowels often demonstrate resistance to coarticu-
lation [15, 16]. The present results for M¯aori /i/ and /e/ do not
strictly adhere to this trend, with F 2 increasing when adjacent
to /k/. It has been noted by both Bauer and Harlow that M ¯aori
/k/ can vary in place from velar to palatal [29, 9]. It is possible
then that /k/ is palatalised in these front vowel environments
and that this in turn results in greater fronting of these vowels
(evidenced by increased F 2). Conversely, /k/ is perhaps more
velar in its realisation when preceding /o/ and /u/, resulting
in a relatively retracted realisation of the vowel. This is coher-

Research considering M ¯aori vowels has shown modern
M¯aori /u/ is notably fronted compared to historical speakers
and other Polynesian languages [6]. This fronting is amplified
when /u/ is preceded by the alveolar stop/t/. This is supported
by the current study, with /u/ largely appearing as a high-
central vowel, sitting in a space posterior to /i/. Fronting of the
vowel is most notable in alveolar contexts, but is also present
in bilabial and velar contexts. This pattern of fronting was also
present for the mid-back vowel /o/. This is, to our knowledge,
the first documentation of this phenomenon for M¯aori.
In early MAONZE work it was noted that anticipatory
changes to vowels due to following consonant were not com-
mon, aside for /r/ [10]. The present results suggest that, at least
for contemporary speakers, this may not fully describe M ¯aori
coarticulation. One possible avenue for future work could re-
turn to the MAONZE corpus to consider the data using a similar
approach to the present study. This would allow comparison of
different speaker groups and exploration of possible diachronic
changes in M ¯aori coarticulatory processes. It is known that
coarticulation can be a driver of sound change [31, 32], and this
area merits exploration given the present results contrast with
previous claims. Relatedly, this study focuses only on young
women speakers. In the MAONZE project, younger women
were found to lead sound change in M¯aori [33, 34]. Considera-
tion of other speaker groups could clarify whether the patterns
of variation observed here reflect the wider speaker community
or are indicative of some sound change in progress.
It has been shown that there is very limited literature on
coarticulation for M ¯aori, and even less that considers its pho-
netic realisation. Future studies on this topic should consider

From a structural standpoint, future work could consider addi-
tional consonants (for example, the nasals), and consider other
sequences, such as /VCV/ sequences. This latter example,
common in coarticulation studies, allows exploration of antic-
ipatory and carryover coarticulation present (see [37], for ex-
ample). This question cannot easily addressed with the present
study stimuli as the consonant-vowel pairs are repeated, how-
ever, the dynamic view of formant trajectories explored here in-
dicates there may be asymmetries in the articulatory movements
into and out of the various vowels and stops. This suggests there
are probably differences in coarticulation direction that merit
exploration. This could also be complimented by exploring lo-
cus equations, a method also used to quantify vowel-consonant
coarticulation (e.g. for Australian languages [38]).
5. Conclusions
This study has demonstrated that the quality of vowels in mod-
ern spoken M ¯aori is highly dependent on consonant context
in the speech of young women speakers. In particular, vowel
backness varies notably depending on the nature of stop con-
sonants, with these differences occurring throughout the vowel,
evidenced by dynamic analysis approaches. The present study
confirms some of the findings of previous research, but impor-
tantly shows that the vowel context must be carefully consid-
ered as it is clear that there are notable, significant changes to
vowel quality due to consonantal context in M ¯aori. This study
also reaffirms the importance of undertaking and documenting
fundamental phonetics studies for under-researched languages.
