Abstract
We presentV ¯an. ¯ıSetu, a human-AI collaborative framework for
constructing high-quality speech corpora in low-resource lan-
guages. As a case study, we apply V ¯an.¯ıSetu to createKr-
ishiV¯an. ¯ı, a Hindi dataset of agricultural speech. V ¯an.¯ıSetu in-
tegrates automatic speech recognition (ASR), lightweight and
large language model-based post-correction, and structured,
multi-stage human validation implemented through an en-
hanced annotation tool,V ¯agyojaka. Our key technical contri-
bution is a role-separated, incentive-linked validation architec-
ture (Annotator→Validator→Verifier) with threshold-gated
quality control, which, to our knowledge, has not been pre-
viously deployed for low-resource Indic conversational speech
corpus creation. In a controlled annotation study, we show that
mT5-based post-correction yields a 61.1% reduction in annota-
tion effort, while preserving transcript fidelity. We further show
that smaller fine-tuned LMs, outperform larger LLMs for in-
domain correction.
Index Terms: speech recognition, human-computer interac-
tion, computational paralinguistics
1. Introduction
In multilingual, low-literacy settings such as rural India, speech
is often the most accessible interface for information access and
assistance [1]. However, robust speech technology remains dif-
ficult to deploy, not only due to modeling limitations, but also
because usable, domain-specific, real-world datasets are scarce
[2, 3]. Despite progress in high-resource ASR [4], Hindi and
other low-resource systems degrade under conditions typical of
rural communication, including spontaneous noisy speech, di-
alectal variation, and dense code-mixing [3].
Building ASR systems that work “in the wild” requires
more than better models; it demandsbetter data[5, 6, 7, 8].
However, curating high-quality speech corpora remains expen-
sive and labor-intensive: annotators must correct transcripts,
resolve speaker turns, segment and align utterances, and nor-
malize inconsistent orthography and code-mixed forms, often
costing 6-8×real time per hour of audio [9]. We address
this bottleneck withV ¯an. ¯ıSetu(Fig. 1), a human-AI collabo-
rative framework for scalable creation of domain-rich speech
datasets. V ¯an.¯ıSetu integrates automatic transcription and di-
arization, LM/LLM-based post-correction [10, 11, 12, 13], and
a layered human validation workflow supported by an enhanced
V¯agyojaka interface [14], offloading repetitive low-confidence
edits to models while reserving domain-sensitive decisions for
humans.

2. The V ¯an. ¯ıSetu Framework
Building robust corpora in low-resource, code-mixed settings
requires scalable automation with human oversight: fully man-
ual pipelines are accurate but slow, while fully automatic
pipelines degrade on noisy, spontaneous, and informal speech.
We therefore introduce V¯an.¯ıSetu, a modular human-AI frame-
work that bridges raw speech collection to quality-controlled
transcripts via four stages (Fig. 2): (i) domain-guided data
collection, (ii) automated corpus construction, (iii) LM/LLM-
based automatic post-correction, and (iv) layered data refine-
ment involving human interaction.
2.1. Data Collection Stage
In the first stage, V ¯an.¯ıSetu performs domain-guided acquisi-
tion of raw speech. For KrishiV ¯an.¯ı, we target Hindi agricul-
tural content and construct a bilingual keyword inventory using
domain glossaries, Wikipedia categories, and curated Hindi-
English lexicons. We query YouTube with these keywords
and prioritize long-form videos with unscripted, conversational
speech. We then remove near-duplicates, download eligible
videos under appropriate usage constraints, and convert audio
to 16 kHz, mono W A V . The resulting raw audio is intention-
ally “in-the-wild”, exhibiting spontaneous phrasing, regional
accents, speaker overlaps, and ambient noise that are represen-
tative of rural deployments but challenging for ASR.

2.2. Corpus Construction Stage
This stage converts raw audio into a structured corpus via auto-
mated preprocessing. It includes the following steps:
Voice Activity Detection (V AD):removes silence/background-
only regions and segments audio into utterances (≤20 seconds).
Speaker Diarization:Uses PyAnnote [17] to assign speaker
turns, preserving conversational structure and enabling speaker-
aware annotation.
ASR Transcriptionis produced using a strong generic base-
line (IndicWav2Vec [7]) and our domain-adaptive model
(KVWav2Vec), with beam-search decoding for better stability.
Forced Alignmentmaps transcript tokens to audio spans us-
ing a CTC-based aligner [18], enabling efficient navigation and
targeted edits inside the annotation interface.
2.3. Automatic Post-Correction by LM/LLMs
In this stage, V ¯an.¯ıSetu applies automatic post-correction to re-
duce workload before manual review. We support lightweight
LMs or larger LLMs as interchangeable components:
LM-based Correctionuses fine-tuned mT5 [19] and ByT5 [20]
on paired (ASR output, corrected transcript) examples. These
models are effective for common ASR artifacts in Hindi, includ-
ing lexical distortions, mistransliterations, and token bound-
ary/segmentation errors.
LLM-based Correctionevaluates larger models, includ-
ing LLaMA-3-Nanda-10B [21] by fine-tuning [22, 23] and
ChatGPT-4o [11] by ICL [24]. While LLMs can better handle
domain-specific phrases and code-switching, they may intro-
duce unsupported edits and have higher latency, which in turn
requires more careful human verification. For KrishiV ¯an.¯ı, we
usemT5as the default post-corrector and pass its outputs to the
final human refinement stage.

Hindi ASR robustness in realistic agricultural speech and (ii)
the human effort required to produce high-quality transcripts.

then detail our ASR baselines and domain-adaptive model, and
finally evaluate LM/LLM-based post-corrections. Section 4 re-
ports ASR accuracy, post-correction quality gains, and the re-
sulting reduction in human annotation effort.

4. Results and Discussions
As per Table 1, our domain-adapted KVWav2Vec model consis-
tently outperforms strong baselines on the KrishiV ¯an.¯ı-Known
and KrishiV ¯an.¯ı-Unknown splits. This indicates that incorpo-
rating even a modest amount of in-domain conversational data
(10h) can improve recognition in noisy, accented, and code-
mixed agricultural speech. On the OOD split, IndicWav2Vec
slightly outperforms KVWav2Vec, suggesting that generalist
models may retain an advantage under topic drift and domain
mismatch. Overall, these results validate blending open-domain
and domain-specific data during fine-tuning and retaining a gen-
eralist baseline for robustness under distribution shift.

correction as a first-pass refinement step before human edit-
ing. Table 2 shows that smaller models, particularly mT5-small,

4.1. Implications for human-AI Collaboration
From a system design perspective, these results demonstrate
that post-correction not only reduces the number of human ed-
its but also changes the annotator’s role. Rather than fixing
every error manually, annotators now operate asvalidators,
reviewing pre-corrected hypotheses and resolving only high-
ambiguity cases. This shift was particularly beneficial in low-
confidence regions, such as domain-specific terminology and
Hindi-English code-switching, as shown in Fig. 3. The impact
of this human-AI collaboration is further quantified in Fig. 5,
which shows a 61.1% reduction in annotation time when us-
ing V ¯an.¯ıSetu’s verification pipeline compared to fully manual
transcription. Intermediate configurations, such as ASR-only
correction or single-pass validation, also yield substantial effi-
ciency gains, suggesting that even partial automation has ben-
efits. Early annotator feedback indicated that post-correction
reduced fatigue and increased trust in the system’s outputs.
This suggests that model-aided editing, when appropriately cal-
ibrated, can improve both speed and engagement without sac-
rificing quality. Moreover, the use of layered quality control
(via validators and verifiers) enabled distributed annotation with
minimal coordination overhead, further enhancing scalability.
4.2. Annotator Feedback and Observations
To understand how the annotators interacted with the V¯an.¯ıSetu
pipeline in practice, we collected informal feedback from 8
annotators over 31 sessions. Their reflections highlight key
human-AI interaction dynamics that shaped their annotation ex-
perience. Annotators widely appreciated the pre-correction ca-
pability using mT5 and LLMs, especially in reducing repeti-
tive edits. One annotator remarked:“mT5 has been a game-
changer. Instead of fixing basic errors, I can now focus only
on the more nuanced mistakes. ”Another reported“....it felt
like editing a smart draft instead of transcribing from scratch. ”
These responses suggest a shift in role from manual transcrip-
tion to semantic review, which is aligned with our design goals.
5. Related Work
Early agricultural speech work in India focused on voice-
first access to time-sensitive information (notably commod-
ity prices), exemplified by the government-led Mandi Infor-
mation System for multilingual spoken dialogue in rural set-
tings [26]. Later studies and shared tasks established that the
main bottleneck is robust ASR in real rural conditions, spon-
taneous telephony speech with noise and strong dialectal vari-
ation, where even strong baselines can remain near 30% WER
(e.g., the Gram Vaani ASR Challenge on regional Hindi) [27].

To counteract chronic data scarcity, recent work has scaled
through synthetic and multimodal pipelines (e.g., AgriGPT-
Omni), while still noting the shortage of representative real agri-
cultural speech [28]. Field-collected datasets such as Kallaama
further underscore agriculture as a low-resource domain glob-
ally and highlight the importance of in-field capture and care-
ful annotation [29]. Inclusion-focused studies (e.g., rural Bho-
jpuri women) also show that transcription itself is a major bar-
rier due to limited standardization, scarce domain-aware tran-
scribers, and regional variation [30]. Together, these works mo-
tivate agricultural speech corpora and pipelines that prioritize (i)
real, noisy speech, (ii) standardized transcription with explicit
QC, and (iii) domain phenomena such as terminology, entities,
code-mixing, numerals, and dialectal variation.
6. Conclusion
We presented V ¯an.¯ıSetu, a human-AI collaborative framework
for constructing high-quality speech corpora in low-resource,
code-mixed settings, validated through KrishiV ¯an.¯ı, a 100-hour
Hindi conversational agricultural dataset. V ¯an.¯ıSetu’s primary
contribution is its role-separated, incentive-linked validation ar-
chitecture, combining ASR, mT5-based post-correction, and
a three-stage human validation pipeline, which reduces an-
notation effort by 61.1% (95% CI: [57.4%, 64.8%], p<
0.001, Cohen’s d = 2.14) relative to fully manual transcrip-
tion, while achieving Krippendorff’sα= 0.87. We show that
mT5 outperforms larger LLMs for in-domain correction, and
provide the first systematic error analysis explaining ByT5
and LLaMA regressions on Hindi Devanagari. V ¯an.¯ıSetu con-
tributes a reusable, domain-adaptive, and statistically docu-
mented blueprint for scalable speech dataset creation in under-
represented languages.
