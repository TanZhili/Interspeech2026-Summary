Modelling diphthong dynamics: 
A GAMM-based analysis of Australian English diphthongs 
Ksenia Gnevsheva1,*, Canaan Lan2, Gerard Docherty3, Catherine Travis1 
1 Australian National University, Australia

Abstract 
Diphthongs are inherently dynamic segments, yet they are 
typically analysed using discrete time points, reducing their 
complex trajectories to static onset and offset targets. In this 
study, we adopt a fully dynamic modelling approach using 
Generalised Add itive Mixed Models to analyse formant 
movement across the entire vowel trajectory of five Australian 
English diphthongs, and conduct comparisons across gender 
and location. Data come from two spontaneous speech corpora 
collected in one urban and one region al location and prepared 
following comparable protocols. Statistical analysis reveals an 
effect of gender for all vowels, and location for three vowels, 
with women and urban speakers being ahead in the change . 
Importantly, some differences emerged at points other than the 
onset and offset, or were more clearly captured when the full 
trajectory was modelled, underscoring the value of a trajectory-
based approach to diphthong analysis. 
Index Terms : Australian English , vowel dynamics, gender, 
regional variation 
1. Introduction 
Diphthong analyses often focus on two time points in the vowel, 
representing the onset and offset of the segment ( typically 
measured at 20% and 80% of the vowel duration respectively). 
This approach captures the overall direction of spectral 
movement while reducing the dynamic trajectory to two 
representative “targets”. By concentrating on these two anchor 
points, researchers obtain a computationally efficient and 
statistically tractable representation of vowel dynamics, 
facilitating comparison across speak ers, social groups,  and 
contexts. Moreover, target-based analyses align well with some 
phonological interpretations of diphthongs as movements 
between two vowel qualities [1, 2].  
While the focus on limited trajectory targets has been 
highly productive in advancing our understanding of 
diphthongal quality and socially -conditioned variation, 
reducing the vowel to two points necessarily discards a 
substantial portion of the acoustic information. Diphthongs are 
inherently dynamic segments and their phonetic realisation may 
differ not only in starting and ending positions but also in shape 
and rate of movement across the trajectory. By collapsing the 
temporal structure into two discrete measurements, we risk 
overlooking potentially meaningful differences in within-vowel 
dynamics.

In response to these limitations, more recent work has 
begun to represent diphthongs using multiple measurements 
throughout the vowel trajectory , including for Australian  
English [3-5]. This approach provides a richer depiction of 
formant trajectories and allows researchers to visualise fine -
grained temporal patterns. However , despite the increased 
density of measurement, statistical analysis is often still 
performed on a single selected time point. As a result, much of 
the dynamic information remains underutilised. 
Generalised Additive Mixed Models (GAMMs) offer a 
principled way to model the temporal trajectory of diphthongs 
[6]. By fitting smooth functions over time while accounting for 
random effects, GAMMs enable the statistical analysis of non -
linear formant movement across the entire vowel.  
While the application of GAMMs to the study of 
diphthongs in Australian English  is relatively new, they have 
been shown to be highly informative. Recent work , for 
example, demonstrated significant differences in diphthong 
trajectories for men across two time periods and different 
locations in Sydney [7-10].  
In this paper, we extend this line of inquiry by applying 
GAMMs to five diphthongs produced in spontaneous speech by 
men and women in two different locations in Australia (a major 
urban centre and a more rural area). The five diphthongs – FACE, 
FLEECE, GOAT, MOUTH, and PRICE – have been demonstrated to 
vary on the socio-stylistic continuum in Australian English, and 
to be undergoing change [11-13]. In the corpus of Australian 
English that we analyse here, FACE and FLEECE have been 
shown to be raising and fronting, PRICE lowering and fronting, 
GOAT raising and retracting, and MOUTH lowering and retracting 
[14, 15], with similar changes documented elsewhere  [12, 13, 
16, 17]. Socially, these changes are led by the middle class and 
women, as speakers move away from what traditionally were 
considered “broad” vowel realisations most closely associated 
with working class and men [14]. Though little has been done 
on Australian English outside of major cities, we might expect 
that speakers in urban centres would lead in change because of 
the tendency for changes to spread outward from major cities to 
smaller towns [18]. This prior work allows us to situate any 
differences across gender and location observed in the data here 
in relation to change over time in Australian English and to the 
expected progress ion of change . By modelling the vowel 
trajectory rather than discrete points, we assess whether and 
how location and gender differences emerge across the entire 
vowel trajectory, setting the scene for future studies looking at 
a wider array of linguistic and social variables.

2. Method 
2.1. Locations 
For the comparison across locations , we take one major urban 
centre (Sydney) and one more rural area (Braidwood). Sydney 
(the state capital of New South Wales) is one of Australia ’s 
largest cities with a population surpassing 5 million and is 
considered the financial hub of Australia. Braidwood is situated 
in what the Australian Bureau of Statis tics classifies as Inner 
Regional Australia, 300 km south west of Sydney and 100 km 
east of the nation ’s capital, Canberra. It has a population of 
1,720 and is a service town for several smaller towns in the area, 
including Majors Creek (population 290), Araluen (209), 
Bombay (142), Mongarlowe (121), and others. The township of 
Braidwood was established in the mid-19th century; the area 
developed rapidly during the gold rush  and grew further 
through farming. 
The populations differ socially in the two locations. 
According to the most recent national census conducted in 2021 
[19], a larger proportion of the population of Sydney is highly 
educated and works in professional and white -collar 
occupations, with the top industries of employment being 
healthcare, IT, and finance,  while in Braidwood they are 
farming, hospitality, and government administration.  
2.2. Corpora 
The data come from two spontan eous speech corpora. The 
Sydney Speaks corpus is a longitudinal sociolinguistic corpus 
comprising interviews with Australians  of diverse social 
backgrounds [20, 21]. All were living in Sydney at the time of 
the recording, and had lived there most of their lives . For 
Braidwood, we use a corpus currently being compiled for the 
Voices of Regional Australia (Regional AusE) project [22], 
which comprises interviews with people living in and around 
Braidwood. All participants had been in the region for at least 
nine years, and some were born there. In this paper, we use 
recordings from oral history interviews made by a local 
documentary film maker to create a podcast series documenting 
the Black Summer bushfires (2019-2020) which hit this region 
particularly hard [23]. 
Audio-recordings in both corpora were made in person, 
typically in the participant’s home. They were captured with a 
Zoom H4 or H5 recorder and were orthographically transcribed 
(manually for Sydney Speaks and automatically using Azure AI 
Speech [24] for Regional AusE, with a dedicated speech-to-text 
transcription platform developed at Griffith University using 
Microsoft technologies and run locally [25]). The transcriptions 
were manually corrected, anonymised, and automatically time-
aligned at the level of the segment in LaBB -CAT, where they 
are stored [26]. 
Analyses are done on a subset of each corpus to maximise 
comparability across the locations. For Sydney Speaks, we 
include only speakers of Anglo -Celtic background. For both 
corpora, we include only those who are middle -aged or older 
(in Braidwood, born between 1947 and 1982, average 1964, and 
in Sydney, born between 1954 and 1971, average 1964), and 
who have similar occupation scores (as an indication of social 
class). For this, we use the Australian Socioeconomic Index 
(AUSEI), which scores occupations fr om 0 to 100 [27]. The 
men differ statistically in their AUSEI scores across the 
locations, with the regional men included having a lower 
occupation score than the urban men (Braidwood 18.6 to 86.2, 
mean 41, Sydney 29.9 to 100, mean 62; p = 0.021) reflecting 
social differences in the two locations. This does not apply to 
the women, however, who are parallel across the locations 
(Braidwood from 26.6 to 93.6, mean 62, Sydney 25.2 to 90.7, 
mean 60; p = 0.743). In total, vowel trajectory modelling was 
conducted on 31 speakers f rom Braidwood (16 women and 15 
men) and 27 speakers from Sydney (15 women and 12 men). 
2.3. Analysis 
For acoustic phonetic analysis, we used Fast Track default 
settings [28] to estimate F1 and F2 at 10% intervals for all 
lexically stressed vowels in content words (a bespoke stop -list 
of 196 items was used to filter highly frequent, grammatical 
words). Outliers were removed by excluding any tokens with a 
mid-point F1 value that was either greater than 1500 Hz or was 
outside 2 SDs for each vowel and each speaker. The values were 
normalised using a modified Lobanov [29] technique that takes 
account of disparate numbers of tokens across vowel types [30]. 
We additionally restricted our analysis to vowels in  inter-
obstruent position to control for the effects of preceding and 
following phonological environment, and because automated 
segmentation without manual correction renders vowel 
boundaries adjacent to sonorants less reliable. 
Separate GAMMs were fitted to  data for  each vowel  
category with the mgcv package in R [31] using the bam() 
function [32]. We treated normalised F1 and F2 estimates taken 
at seven equidistant points, from 20 to 80% of the vowel 
trajectories, as the dependent variables . The models included 
parametric effects of Gender and Location, as well as their 
interaction, noting that the greater occupation differences for 
men by location than for women should be taken into account 
in interpreting any apparent location differences. All factors 
were treatment-coded. Models were run with smooth terms for 
normalised time (with by -factor smooths to capture group -
specific trajectories for Gender, Location, and their interaction), 
a covariate smooth for vowel duration to control for durational 
differences, and random factor smooths for speaker and lexical 
item, with model comparisons conducted using ML estimation 
within the itsadug R package [33]. Following previous work [6, 
34], if the overall comparison was found to be significant, the 
factor was interpreted as affecting the outcome variable in some 
significant way. Autoregressive error models [6, 35]  were 
included to account for autocorrelation in the formant 
trajectories. To enable clear interpretation and visualisation of 
all four Gender-by-Location trajectories, we fitted a simplified 
model containing only the interaction term (i.e., s(time, 
by=gender_place)), if the Gender-Location interaction emerged 
as significant in the first set of GAMMs.  
Due to space constraints, we include here only figures for 
those models which best showcase the insights obtained from a 
dynamic analysis. These figures help interpret specific 
trajectory differences , but we acknowledge the interpretive 
limitations associated with model complexity, and the visual 
outputs, showing 20-80% of normalised trajectories. All figures 
and model outputs are available online at OSF. 
3. Results 
Fig. 1 visualises the raw values of these five diphthongs as 
produced by men and women in the regional and urban 
locations. The differences between speaker groups are clearly 
visible, with regional men, for example, producing the most 
diphthongal FLEECE of all. The following analyses report the 
statistical significance of any such differences.

3.1. FACE 
Model comparisons showed that for FACE F1 (n = 11,263) , 
Gender (χ²(10) = 26.64, p < .001) and Location (χ²(10) = 10.91, 
p = .016) had a significant overall effect, but not the interaction 
(χ²(7) = 3.09, p = .518); for FACE F2, again Gender (χ²(10) = 
23.34, p < .001) and Location (χ²(10) = 27.26, p < .001) had a 
significant overall effect , but the interaction did not (χ²(7) = 
5.46, p = .142). 
Women produced a closer and fronter vowel throughout its 
duration, with the F1 trajectories being separate but parallel for 
men and women (Fig. 2) and the F2 trajectories starting out 
further apart and coming closer together at the vowel offset. 
Urban spea kers also produced a closer and fronter vowel, 
particularly in the onset (Fig. 3). Thus, women and urban 
speakers were relatively ahead in the change. 
3.2. FLEECE 
For FLEECE F1 (n = 12,691), both Gender (χ²(10) = 14.81, p 
< .001) and Location (χ²(10) = 14.42, p = .001) had a significant 
overall effect, whereas the interaction did not reach significance 
(χ²(7) = 6.20, p = .088); for FLEECE F2, Gender (χ²(10) = 29.68, 
p < .001), Location (χ²(10) = 16.46, p < .001), and their 
interaction (χ²(7) = 7.50, p = .036) all had a significant overall 
effect.  
Women (Fig. 4) and urban speakers produced a more open 
vowel at the offset. While this would generally be considered a 
more conservative realisation [16], an examination of the entire 
trajectories reveals a shallower slope for these groups of 
speakers, suggesting a more monophthongal quality of the 
vowel, which would correspond to their being ahead in the 
change. In terms of vowel frontness, urban men and women and 
regional women patterned similarly while regional men 
produced a more diphthongal (i.e. conservative) vowel quality 
(Fig. 5). 
3.3. PRICE 
For PRICE F1 (n = 5,859), Gender had a significant overall effect 
(χ²(10) = 12.38, p = .006), while neither Location (χ²(10) = 7.71, 
p = .117) nor the interaction (χ²(7) = 2.45, p = .673) reached 
significance; for PRICE F2, Gender had a significant overall 
effect (χ²(10) = 19.43, p < .001), but Location (χ²(10) = 4.36, p 
= .558) and the interaction (χ²(7) = 1.89, p = .806) did not.  
Women produced a more open (Fig. 6) and fronter vowel, 
particularly in the middle section of the trajectory. For both F1 
and F2, women are ahead in the change, but with the two gender 
trajectories not being parallel in either case, the distinction lies 
not at the 20% time-point, but at the mid-point of the vowel.

3.4. GOAT 
For GOAT F1 (n = 5,341), Gender had a significant overall effect 
(χ²(10) = 14.53, p = .001), while neither Location (χ²(10) = 5.84, 
p = .307) nor the interaction (χ²(7) = 1.43, p = .899) reached 
significance; for GOAT F2, Gender had a significant overall 
effect (χ²(10) = 9.58, p < .05), whereas Location (χ²(10) = 8.61, 
p = .070) and the interaction (χ²(7) = 5.52, p = .137) did not.  
Women produced a closer vowel throughout the diphthong 
trajectory and a fronter vowel  at the onset, which point in 
contradictory directions, being associated with being ahead and 
behind in change  respectively (but see [13] who found that 
fronter realisation s were associated with younger speakers) , 
demonstrating that  further research is needed to better 
understand the nature of the change for this vowel.  
3.5. MOUTH 
For MOUTH F1 (n = 3,493), both Gender (χ²(10) = 14.83, p < 
.001) and Location (χ²(10) = 11.67, p = .010) had a significant 
overall effect, while the interaction did not (χ²(7) = 3.75, p = 
.379); for MOUTH F2, Gender had a significant overall effect 
(χ²(10) = 26.36, p < .001), whereas Location (χ²(10) = 2.25, p = 
.923) and the interaction (χ²(7) = 1.80, p = .824) did not. 
Women produced a more open  (particularly at the onset)  
and retracted  (particularly in the middle)  vowel, and urban 
speakers produced a more open vowel with the two trajectories 
for urban and regional speakers  parallel to each other . In all 
cases women and urban speakers were ahead in the change. 
4. Discussion 
To sum up the social effects, Gender was found to be a 
significant predictor of vowel realisation for all five vowels and 
Location was significant for three out of five vowels (FACE, 
FLEECE, MOUTH). Interpreting this in relation to what we know 
about change along the socio-stylistic continuum for vowels in 
Australian English, we would say that women and urban 
speakers are ahead in the move away from broad er vowels, 
which has been a well-attested change in progress in Australian 
English over the last few decades. These results align both with 
the general pattern of women leading in sound change [36] and 
with claims that linguistic change spreads from urban to rural 
locations [18]. The difference in Location is consistent with the 
robust (but understudied) stereotype of rural Australians having 
broader, or more “ocker”, speech [37, 38], and the lack of an 
interaction between Location and Gender in most cases shows 
that this generally holds for both men and women. The one 
exception is FLEECE F2, where the interaction indicates that 
Location has an effect for men only, with regional men 
producing a more diphthongal  (that is, broader) vowel 
trajectory than the other groups. This pattern is consistent with 
the regional men’s occupation score being lower than that of the 
regional women and the urban men and women . However, 
occupation differences do not account for the other location 
effects: women in Sydney and Braidwood have comparable 
occupation scores, yet location remains significant for women 
for both FACE and FLEECE; furthermore, no interaction emerges 
for FACE or PRICE. These findings suggest an effect for location 
across the urban and regional groups, motivating further study 
into variation in different locations in Australia, in particular for 
regional and rural Australia. 
The relative strength and consistency of the gender effect 
are also noteworthy. Gender was significant in more models 
than Location and showed larger effect sizes  where both were 
significant. Moreover, the absence of interactions in most cases 
suggests that Gender and Location operate largely 
independently. This implies that gender differentiation is 
socially salient across both urban and regional settings, 
reinforcing the interpretation of gender as a primary axis of 
sociophonetic differentiation in these data. 
Crucially, modelling the full formant trajectory using 
GAMMs reveals a more nuanced picture than would be 
obtained from traditional single-point analyses. Analyses based 
on a single measurement (e.g., 20%) implicitly assume parallel 
trajectories across groups. While this assumption holds in some 
cases (e.g., FACE F1), it fails in others.  
For FACE F2, although urban and regional speakers already 
differ at the 20% point, the GAMM reveals additional 
differences in the overall trajectory shape. A single -point 
analysis would therefore underestimate the extent of social 
differentiation. Conversely, for PRICE F1, men and women 
appear similar at 20%, yet the trajectory-based analysis reveals 
systematic differences in the middle portion of the vowel, 
meaning that an onset - or offset -based analysis would have 
missed a socially meaningful distinction.  
Finally, the FLEECE results illustrate an even stronger case 
for full-trajectory modelling. An offset-based analysis of the F1 
would suggest that women produce more open, and therefore 
more conservative realisations, contradicting the more general 
pattern for women. However, inspection of the full trajectory 
shows that this difference in the offset reflects a more 
monophthongal, and thus more innovative, trajectory overall. 
Similarly, regional men seem to be producing a fronter FLEECE 
at the offset, which would go against our prediction  that this 
group would lag in change . Analysis of the entire trajectory 
once again shows that they produce a more diphthongal  (or 
broader) vowel in this respect. Here, a static measure would not 
only miss nuance but could mischaracterise the social effect. 
5. Conclusions 
The findings presented here demonstrate that trajectory -based 
GAMM analyses not only capture differences in magnitude at 
specific points in time but also uncover socially meaningful 
differences in dynamic shape that would otherwise remain 
hidden or be misinterpreted under single -point approaches. By 
modelling the entire vowel trajectory, we see that sound change 
in diphthongs does not necessarily proceed via simple parallel 
shifts of fixed onset or offset targets  but may involve changes 
in the shape or timing of spectral movement, which furthermore 
is subject to social conditioning.
