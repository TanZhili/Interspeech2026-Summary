Gender Bias in ASR: A Controlled Study of Gender Composition Across

Abstract
Gender disparities in ASR are commonly attributed to training
data imbalance, often evaluated by fine-tuning pretrained sys-
tems on gender-controlled splits. We test whether such fine-
tuning reveals a systematic relationship between training com-
position and gender disparity. Across three pretrained models
and 11 splits spanning 0–100% female representation, male and
female WERs fluctuate without consistent direction, yielding
no systematic composition–disparity relationship. To determine
whether the effect exists, we include a from-scratch system as a
controlled reference under identical conditions. In contrast, this
system exhibits consistent, directional disparity shifts as com-
position varies, confirming that the effect is real but masked in
pretrained models by representations established during large-
scale pretraining. These results demonstrate that balancing fine-
tuning data alone is insufficient to mitigate gender disparity in
pretrained ASR.

1. Introduction
Gender disparities in automatic speech recognition (ASR) are
widely documented across commercial and research systems [1,
2], yet their direction and magnitude vary inconsistently across
languages, datasets, and models. This variability suggests that
gender disparities are not inevitable consequences of intrinsic
acoustic differences between genders but are instead shaped by
factors such as data composition and training paradigms.
A widely held assumption attributes these disparities to
gender imbalance in training data. Prior controlled studies have
examined this assumption by fine-tuning pretrained models on
gender-controlled datasets [3, 4, 5, 6]. While each study re-
ports internally consistent patterns, no stable relationship has
emerged between training gender composition and recognition
performance. Findings vary across reports on the basis of mod-
els, architectures, datasets, and experimental contexts. This ap-
proach is complicated by the unknown gender distributions of
the large-scale corpora used during pretraining. Fine-tuning on
a comparatively small gender-controlled dataset is unlikely to
substantially override these entrenched representations, mean-
ing the gender signal embedded during pretraining persists into
the fine-tuned model. Whether fine-tuning can reliably re-
veal the effect of gender composition in training data therefore
presents an untested assumption underlying this body of work.
This work addresses this gap through a comparative study
spanning three datasets, four systems, and eleven gender ra-
tio configurations. Specifically, we ask whether pretrained and
from-scratch systems respond differently to identical changes
in training gender composition. We evaluate three pretrained
ASR systems alongside a from-scratch system serving as a ref-
erence, where all representations are learned exclusively from
the gender-controlled training data. Together, these 132 exper-
imental conditions provide the empirical basis for a systematic
evaluation of this question.
2. Related Work
Gender-based ASR disparities have been documented across di-
verse systems and languages, though findings are inconsistent
in both direction and magnitude. Studies on Dutch and Korean
ASR report lower error rates for female speakers [7, 6, 8], while
analyses of African American English corpora find the opposite
[9]. Broader multilingual evaluations show that the advantaged
gender varies by language, dataset, and model [1], with several
studies reporting non-significant differences [10, 11]. This vari-
ability suggests that gender disparities are shaped by contextual
and modeling factors rather than intrinsic acoustic differences
alone.
The most directly relevant prior work examines how train-
ing gender composition influences ASR performance through
fine-tuning pretrained models. Garnerin et al. [3] constructed
LibriSpeech training sets with varying female representation
and found that speaker-specific characteristics often exerted
a stronger influence than gender proportion alone. ElGhaz-
aly et al. [4] fine-tuned Whisper Small across eleven gen-
der proportion configurations on LibriSpeech, revealing non-
monotonic relationships between training balance and gender-
specific WER. They further showed that optimal gender mix
depends on acoustic coverage rather than demographic ratios.
At the pretraining level, Boito et al. [12] varied gender balance
during wav2vec 2.0 pretraining on French speech and observed
that balanced pretraining did not consistently minimize down-
stream gender gaps, suggesting that the relationship between
pretraining gender composition and observed disparity is not
straightforward and directly motivating the confound we iden-
tify in this work.
These studies share two methodological limitations. First,
prior work typically focuses on a single model and dataset, lim-
iting generalization across paradigms. Second, studies involv-
ing pretrained models are subject to the confound identified
above: the gender distribution of pretraining corpora remains
uncontrolled and typically undocumented. As a result, the ef-
fect of fine-tuning gender composition has not been explicitly
isolated from pretraining influence in prior work, meaning that
conclusions about gender effects drawn from such studies may
reflect pretraining representations rather than the effect of fine-
tuning composition alone. This work addresses that gap by pro-
viding a controlled empirical basis for evaluating whether fine-
tuning methodology is sufficient to study gender composition

3. Methodology
To test whether fine-tuning pretrained models can reliably re-
veal the effect of training gender composition on demographic
disparity, we evaluate four ASR systems, across three datasets
under eleven controlled gender ratio configurations. The gender
ratios range from a data containing 0% Male - 100% Female ut-
terances to 100% Male - 0% Female utterances with a step pro-
gression of 10% at each progressive ratio. Three of the four pre-
trained ASR systems are fine-tuned while one system is trained
entirely from scratch, all under identical conditions, ensuring
complete control over gender composition in training. In to-
tal, 132 model–dataset–ratio combinations are trained and eval-
uated, with all systems assessed on a fixed test set per dataset to
ensure direct comparability.
3.1. Datasets
Experiments are conducted on three publicly available En-
glish speech corpora that span diverse acoustic conditions and
speaker gender given as male or female. LibriSpeech [13] is
a 1000-hour corpus of 16 kHz read audiobook speech derived
from the LibriV ox project; we use the clean subset for both
training and evaluation, with 40 speakers in the clean-test set.
Indic TIMIT [14] is a 240-hour phonetically rich Indian En-
glish read-speech corpus recorded from 80 non-native speak-
ers, all of whom appear in the test set. Mozilla Common V oice
v3 [15] is a large-scale crowd-sourced multilingual corpus de-
signed for ASR research, characterized by diverse recording
conditions and a naturally skewed gender distribution of ap-
proximately 75:25 male-to-female, with 1,266 speakers in the
test set.
To ensure controlled and comparable conditions across all
three corpora, training data is capped at 71.36 hours for each
gender-ratio configuration. This corresponds to the maximum
duration of data available for a single gender in a particular
dataset, in this case, female speaker data in CommonV oice.
For each ratio, utterances were randomly sampled without re-
placement at the utterance level using a fixed random seed (42).
Speaker counts are maintained proportionally across all ratio
configurations, ensuring consistent gender composition at both
the speaker and duration levels. Training and test speakers
are strictly disjoint. Table 1 summarizes the resulting dura-
tion statistics for each configuration, consistent across all three
datasets. The test sets remain fixed across all gender ratio con-
figurations and are the same as those from the public datasets.

3.2. ASR Models
Three pretrained systems are evaluated: Wav2Vec 2.0 [16],
SPRING Wav2Vec 2.01, and Whisper Medium [17]. All three
models are initialized from publicly available pretrained check-
points and fine-tuned on the gender-controlled training sets.
These models carry representations shaped by large-scale pre-
training corpora with uncontrolled gender distributions, reflect-
ing a two-stage training setup in which pretraining on uncon-
trolled data precedes fine-tuning on gender-controlled splits.
Wav2Vec 2.0 is a transformer-based self-supervised model pre-
trained on English speech. SPRING Wav2Vec 2.0 follows the
same architecture but is pretrained on Indian-accented English
speech, providing a domain-specific pretraining variant within
the same paradigm. Whisper Medium is a large-scale weakly
supervised encoder–decoder model pretrained on multilingual
speech.
As a controlled reference, the Kaldi hybrid based on
TDNN-HMM model [18] is trained entirely from scratch with
the lattice-free maximum mutual information (LF-MMI) objec-
tive, exclusively on the gender-controlled training sets with no
pretrained representations inherited from external corpora. A
fixed trigram statistical language model, trained on an external
text corpus and held constant across all 132 experimental con-
figurations, is used for decoding, ensuring consistent language
model influence across all ratio conditions.
3.3. Experimental Setup
All experiments were conducted on NVIDIA GeForce RTX
3090 GPUs. Each model was trained independently for every
gender ratio configuration, yielding 132 distinct training runs
in total. Fine-tuning was performed using the HuggingFace
Transformers library, initializing from publicly available pre-
trained checkpoints.2 Wav2Vec 2.0 and SPRING Wav2Vec 2.0
were fine-tuned using the Adam optimizer with a learning rate
of3×10 −5 and a batch size of 8, with a maximum gradient
norm of 1.0. Whisper Medium was fine-tuned using AdamW
optimizer with a learning rate of1×10 −5 and a batch size of
4. Within each model–dataset pair, all hyperparameters were
held constant across the eleven gender ratio conditions, ensur-
ing that training gender composition was the sole varied factor
across experimental conditions.
3.4. Evaluation Protocol
System performance is measured using Word Error Rate
(WER), computed separately for male and female test utter-
ances to capture gender-specific recognition performance. To
ensure robust statistical reporting, the male and female test ut-
terances are each randomly partitioned into ten non-overlapping
subsets sampled without replacement. WER is computed inde-
pendently on each subset and summarized as mean±standard
deviation, providing performance variability estimates without
requiring repeated training runs.
Gender disparity is quantified using the Demographic Dis-
parity Score (DDS), adapted from Attanasio et al. [1], who oper-
ationalized demographic parity through a pairwise comparison
metric defined as:

4.1. From-Scratch Reference System
The from-scratch Kaldi system exhibits strong and systematic
sensitivity to training gender composition, establishing that the
composition effect is real and detectable under fully controlled
conditions. Male and female WERs shift in complementary
directions as composition changes, producing predictable and
substantial movements in DDS across all three datasets. On In-
dic TIMIT, DDS moves from+43.1under all-male training to
−37.2under all-female training, a range exceeding 80 percent-
age points, with recognition disparity flipping entirely as com-
position shifts. Common V oice shows a similar reversal, with
DDS moving from+19.1to−12.7. On LibriSpeech, DDS de-
creases from+33.2to+4.7without fully crossing zero, sug-
gesting a persistent male advantage that moderates but does not
eliminate the composition effect. Across datasets, the direction
and magnitude of disparity are largely predictable from training
composition, demonstrating that gender composition directly
shapes recognition behavior when representations are learned
from scratch.
To confirm that compositional sensitivity is not specific to
the Kaldi hybrid architecture, we additionally trained a Zip-
former [19] end-to-end model (in IceFall toolkit) on the two ex-
treme ratio conditions (0:100 and 100:0) on LibriSpeech. DDS
moved from−9.3under all-female training to+39.4under all-
male training, replicating the directional reversal observed in
Kaldi and confirming that strong compositional sensitivity is a
property of from-scratch training paradigms rather than a spe-
cific architectural choice.

4.2. Pretrained Systems
In contrast, all three pretrained systems exhibit minimal and
inconsistent sensitivity to training gender composition. Fine-
tuning on gender-controlled data produces no reliable direc-
tional relationship between composition and DDS, and male
and female WERs remain largely stable across all eleven condi-
tions. Wav2Vec 2.0 produces DDS values within±10on Com-
mon V oice without a monotonic trend, a consistent female dis-
advantage on LibriSpeech that fine-tuning does not eliminate,
and irregular WER fluctuations on Indic TIMIT with no system-
atic compositional response. SPRING exhibits closely aligned
behavior despite pretraining on Indian-accented speech, with
DDS remaining within±12across all three datasets. Notably,
near-zero DDS at the balanced 50:50 condition is also observed
under extreme imbalance, indicating that apparent parity does
not reflect sensitivity to composition. The consistency between
SPRING and Wav2Vec 2.0 suggests the insensitivity arises from
the pretraining paradigm rather than corpus-specific character-
istics. Whisper displays the strongest stability among the three,
with DDS fluctuating within±8on Common V oice and Indic
TIMIT without directional structure, and a persistent positive
DDS on LibriSpeech that compositional changes do not modify.
Collectively, these results demonstrate that gender composition
during fine-tuning exerts limited influence on pretrained system
behavior.
4.3. Discussion
Kaldi’s DDS dynamic range exceeds 80 percentage points on
Indic TIMIT and 30 points on Common V oice, while all three
pretrained systems remain within approximately ±12 on the
same datasets. This contrast indicates a systematic difference in
sensitivity to training gender composition between from-scratch
and pretrained paradigms. The Zipformer end-to-end model,
trained only on the two extreme ratio conditions, produces a
DDS range of approximately 49 percentage points, corroborat-
ing that this sensitivity is a characteristic of from-scratch train-
ing rather than a property specific to the Kaldi hybrid archi-
tecture. The pattern holds across pretrained systems with dif-
ferent architectures, including self-supervised transformer en-
coders (Wav2Vec 2.0 and SPRING) and a weakly supervised
encoder–decoder model (Whisper), suggesting that the effect is
not confined to a single model family. All three share large-
scale pretraining on corpora with uncontrolled gender distri-
butions, and the resulting representations may limit the ex-
tent to which gender-controlled fine-tuning can modulate de-
mographic disparity. Fine-tuning pretrained ASR models on
gender-controlled datasets does not reliably isolate the effect
of training composition on gender disparity. Across 132 ex-
perimental conditions spanning three datasets and four systems,
pretrained systems exhibit no direct correlation between gen-
der distribution and downstream disparity, even under identical
training conditions. Addressing gender disparity in pretrained
ASR systems therefore likely requires interventions at the rep-
resentation level, such as controlled continued pretraining, tar-
geted adaptation mechanisms, or probing approaches that ex-
plicitly assess and modify pretrained representations.
5. Conclusion
This study investigated whether fine-tuning pretrained ASR
models on gender-controlled datasets can isolate the effect of
training composition on gender disparity. Across three datasets,
four systems, and eleven gender ratio conditions, the answer
is consistent: fine-tuning does not reliably expose a system-
atic relationship between composition and disparity. All three
pretrained systems exhibit minimal and inconsistent sensitiv-
ity to gender-controlled fine-tuning. The from-scratch Kaldi
system demonstrates that the composition effect is genuine.
When representations are learned exclusively from controlled
data, disparity responds strongly and systematically to gender
proportion, reversing direction as composition shifts. The in-
sensitivity observed in pretrained systems is consistent with
representational stability inherited from large-scale pretraining,
suggesting that balancing fine-tuning data alone is insufficient
to address gender disparity in pretrained ASR systems. The
scope of this study is limited to English ASR and binary gen-
der categories, reflecting standard annotation practice and en-
abling direct comparison with prior controlled studies Future
work should therefore prioritize two directions: first, develop-
ing methods that directly account for pretrained representations
when studying gender effects in fine-tuned systems; and sec-
ond, broadening the scope to encompass non-binary speakers,
diverse language settings, and a wider range of model architec-
tures and datasets.
