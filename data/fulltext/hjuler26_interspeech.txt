Listenability of Synthetic Speech: On the Effect of Linguistic Registers in
Text-to-Speech Input

Abstract
With the rapid advancement of Large Language Models
(LLMs), LLM-generated text is increasingly being used as in-
put for speech synthesis, yet little is known about its listenabil-
ity compared to other linguistic registers. This paper presents
a preliminary study of listenability across conversational, for-
mal written, simplified written, and LLM-generated registers in
terms of listener effort and comprehension during synthesized
delivery. An experiment with 47 participants measured subjec-
tive mental effort, cognitive load, and cued recall in a within-
subjects design. Register characteristics were examined through
readability metrics and Biber’s Multi-Dimensional Analysis.
Our findings indicate higher listenability for synthesized speech
from written sources compared to radio transcripts, demonstrat-
ing the intricacies of language and register. Furthermore, LLM-
generated text was as listenable as Wikipedia-sourced text, sup-
porting its suitability in synthetic speech applications.
Index Terms: Listenability, Text-to-Speech Synthesis, Human-
Computer Interaction, Spoken Language Comprehension
1. Introduction
Advances in AI agents have advanced communication with ma-
chines, enabling a new range of interaction modalities and real-
izing the potential of new forms of human-computer interaction.
Humanlike communication is crucial for conversational agents
[1], and is considered more important than humanlike behavior
and appearance in robot companions [2]. Meanwhile, speech
is one of our primary means of communication, and there has
been a rise in the popularity of virtual assistants that offer
voice-centered interaction [3]. One way to assess synthesized
speech is through ’listenability’, or ‘listening effort’, which
is the mental and/or cognitive effort required to process spo-
ken language [4]. Most conversational Text-to-Speech (TTS)
pipelines are driven by an upstream text generator, e.g. [1, 5, 6],
and modern TTS systems are optimized for Large Language
Model (LLM) output [7]. This assumes that LLM output is suit-
able for speech generation. Yet, few studies have considered the
suitability of written sources and LLM-generated text for audio
delivery, leaving a gap in our understanding of how the linguis-
tic properties of TTS input affect the listening experience.
Language varies according to the register, functional con-
text, or communicative situation in which the language is in-
tended [8]. For example, a conversation with a close friend
differs greatly from the language used in a formal academic
paper. Biber’s Multi-Dimensional Analysis (MDA) [9] pro-
vides a framework for quantifying this variation, extracting six
functional dimensions from patterns of co-occurring linguistic
features in spoken and written English. The most prominent,
Dimension 1, ranges from involved, interactional speech to in-
formationally dense, formal prose (Table 1). Since LLMs are
predominantly trained on written text [10], the registers they
produce may not align with the demands of spoken delivery. In
general, the role of the TTS input text’s linguistic register in
driving listenability remains largely unexplored.
This preliminary study aims to highlight this research gap
and further the understanding of listenability by investigating
the influence of language variation in TTS input. Evaluations of
synthetic speech typically focus onnaturalness, rated via mean
opinion scores (MOS), orintelligibility, measured by word er-
ror rate. However, these measures assess surface-level percep-
tual quality, not the ease with which a listener can process and
retain spoken content. To support the usability of TTS systems
in real-world applications, we conduct subjective evaluations
with human listeners to assess TTS performance. We investi-
gate the followingresearch questions: How does the listenabil-
ity of speech synthesized from written registers compare to that
of synthesized conversational speech? And: Do register dif-
ferences across written sources translate into measurable differ-
ences in listenability when delivered via speech synthesis? We
find that speech generated from speech transcripts is the least
listenable compared to Wikipedia-sourced and LLM-generated
text (p<0.001), with no statistically significant difference be-
tween the written sources.1
2. Related Work
TTS research has primarily focused on the perceptual quality
of synthesized speech output, i.e., clarity, intonation, stress, and
pronunciation, evaluated through MOS and intelligibility [11].
While these metrics are well-established, they assess surface-
level acoustic quality of the output and do not necessarily cap-
ture the cognitive effort required to process and retain spoken
content [4]. Govender et al. [12] investigated the cognitive load
of listening to synthetic speech using the dual-task paradigm
and pupillometry, demonstrating that synthesizer quality affects
listening effort. However, their focus was on differences be-
tween synthesizers, i.e., output quality, rather than on the lin-
guistic properties of the TTS input text.
Text generation and simplification models have largely op-
timized their output for readability, defined as the ease of com-
prehending written text [13, 14]. Cho et al. [10] explored meth-
ods to adapt LLM-generated text to the speech domain, in-
cluding specific prompting strategies. They used automated
’speech-worthiness’ metrics for the input text, such as Flesch
Reading Ease, sentence length, and syntactic dependency depth,
some of which are readability metrics. However, as argued by
Rubin [15], listenability is distinct from readability and must be

Language varies systematically across communicative con-
texts, or registers [8], and this variation affects spoken delivery.
Biber’s MDA has been widely applied to characterize register
variation in English corpora along functional dimensions for in-
formational density and narrative stance [9, 19]. Since LLMs
are trained predominantly on written sources [10], their output
occupies a different register position than naturally spoken lan-
guage. Differences in perceived naturalness between sponta-
neous speech and read-aloud text have been documented [20],
and linguistic differences between human and LLM-generated
dialogue have been identified [21], showing greater variability
and authenticity in human dialogues. However, linguistic vari-
ation analysis has not been applied to characterize TTS input
sources, nor has register position been linked to listenability
outcomes in synthesized speech - a gap this study addresses.
3. Methods
In an online Qualtrics survey, participants were instructed to lis-
ten to a series of auditory stimuli presented in different registers.
The study employed a repeated-measures experimental design,
utilising self-report measures of mental effort and cued recall
memory questions targeting the word level. There was an in-
stitutional ethical clearance for this study (approval #8311 from
Queensland University of Technology’s Ethics Committee).
3.1. Procedure
Data collected through the online survey included basic demo-
graphic information, including age, gender, and whether En-
glish was the participant’s first language. Participants were
briefed on the objectives of the research project. They then lis-
tened to an audio sample and answered corresponding mental
effort and cued recall questions. This process was repeated for
a total of 16 audio samples; four samples for each of four types
of auditory stimuli generated from text. Each sample covered a
different topic. The stimuli were presented to the participants
in randomised order for both the type and the topic. Men-
tal effort questions were used to subjectively assess the cog-
nitive load associated with listening. These included a 5-item
NASA Task Load Index (NASA TLX) questionnaire [22], and
a 3-item Amount of Invested Mental Effort (AIME) question-
naire [15, 16], which measure the subjective effort a listener in-
vests in comprehending spoken language. One of the six items
from the original NASA TLX questionnaire was removed as
the item referred to physical demand and did not apply to the
study. Similarly, one of the four items of the AIME question-
naire was removed as it referred to a wider audience. All mea-
sures were recorded on a continuous scale from 1 to 100 us-
ing a slider interface in Qualtrics. Cued recall multiple-choice
questions were developed based on sample topics using a word-
level approach comprehension framework [23], with three recall
questions concerning the theme and contents. The order of the
multiple-choice answers to the questions was randomized.
3.2. Participants
The 47 participants were aged 18 to 50 (M= 24.7, SD = 5.9).
26 (55.3%) of the participants were female, 19 (40.4%) were
male, and two (4.3%) preferred not to disclose their gender. 30
(63.8%) of the participants identified English as their first lan-
guage. Participants were recruited through personal networks
using social media such as Facebook, Discord, Instagram, Mi-
crosoft Teams, and Slack, as well as physical posters with hy-
perlinks and QR codes around the university campus.
3.3. Stimuli selection
Four stimulus types were chosen, which occupy distinct posi-
tions on Biber’s register continuum [9]. The four stimulus types
were:ART: Transcribed multi-party talkback radio recordings
(host and callers) from the Australian Radio Talkback (ART)
Corpus [24].WSiE: Written formal simple language sample
using the Wikipedia Simple English API2.GPT: LLM response
using OpenAI’s gpt-4o-mini API3. First, four ART Corpus sam-
ples were randomly selected and manually sampled. Wikipedia
samples were then collected based on the topics of the ART
Corpus samples to ensure consistency. We sample multiple top-
ics per source to reduce the risk of results being driven by one
unusual text. The final topic list comprised:University,Hubble
Ultra-Deep Field,Mandarin Trees, andCramp. GPT-4o mini
samples for each topic were generated using OpenAI’s API us-
ing the prompt“This is a conversation between people. Be as
listenable as possible. What is{topic}”. Minimal prompt engi-
neering was employed in formulating the prompt.

3.4. Analysis
We analysed the text sources of the generated speech stimuli us-
ing automated measures of readability (Table 2). Flesch Read-
ing Ease (FRE) scores rate text on a 100-point scale, where
scores correlate positively with readability [25]. Flesch-Kincaid
Grade Level (FKGL) scores rate text at its supposed U.S. school
grade level [26]. Although FRE and FKGL are both based on
word and sentence length, they have different weighting fac-
tors. Inspired by Cho et al. [10], we also quantified sentence
complexity using SpaCy’s dependency parser 5, i.e., the depth
of the resulting dependency graph (DD). To characterize the
linguistic register of all auditory stimuli, we applied Biber’s
MDA framework using the Multidimensional Analysis Tagger
(MAT)6 [19], which quantifies register variation along six func-
tional dimensions defined by 67 co-occurring grammatical and
lexical features (e.g., in Table 3). Unlike neural embeddings,
which capture register variation implicitly, MDA provides inter-
pretable, feature-level explanations of why texts differ linguisti-
cally. MAT also offers the option to apply a z-score correction,
i.e., reduce all z-scores exceeding 5 to 5, to avoid overly inflated
z-scores and dimension scores [19]. The most similar text types
were assigned using Euclidean distance from the centroids of
the clusters reported in Biber [19, 27].
Qualtrics responses were cleaned by removing partial sub-
missions. A one-way repeated-measures ANOV A was con-
ducted with stimulus type (ART, WSiE, WStE, GPT) as a
within-subjects factor (N= 47), with separate analyses per-
formed for AIME, NASA TLX, and cued recall. Sphericity was
assessed using Mauchly’s test, and violations were corrected
using the Greenhouse–Geisser adjustment. Post-hoc pairwise
comparisons were Bonferroni-corrected to control for multiple
comparisons. Statistical significance was set atα= 0.05.

4. Results
4.1. Readability analysis of TTS input
Table 2 presents readability and linguistic complexity evalua-
tions for all four sources. For ART, FRE and FKGL are omit-
ted as they do not apply to transcribed speech. The remain-
ing measures show substantially longer words and sentences,
consistent with spontaneous spoken dialogue. WSiE texts were
the shortest and most syntactically simple, averaging the fewest
words per sentence and achieving the highest mean FRE score,
indicating the greatest ease of reading. FKGL scores placed
WSiE texts at approximately grade 10, the lowest of all writ-
ten sources, with low variability suggesting consistency across
topics. Conversely, WStE texts were consistently the most
complex, averaging longer sentences, the lowest FRE scores,
and an FKGL of approximately grade 14, indicating an under-
graduate reading level. However, a large standard deviation in
FRE scores reflects considerable variation across the four top-
ics. GPT texts fell in between on all metrics, with moderate sen-
tence length, FRE, and FKGL. DD is the smallest for WSiE on
average, but shows no clear trend for WStE and GPT, indicating
similar sentence complexity despite varying lexical difficulty.

4.3. Cognitive effort and listening comprehension
As shown in Table 4, ART imposed the highest cognitive cost as
measured by AIME, requiring more than twice the effort of the
other sources (M= 62.9 vs. 25–29). GPT required the least ef-
fort (M= 25.2), with WStE and WSiE closely matched. A sim-
ilar pattern was observed for NASA TLX: ART was a clear out-
lier (M= 46.8), whereas GPT, WSiE, and WStE clustered be-
tween 14 and 18. For cued recall, the pattern reversed. Despite
the highest reported mental effort and cognitive load, ART pro-
duced the lowest recall performance (M= 78.7). WStE achieved
the highest recall and lowestSD(M= 94.3,SD= 12.7), indi-
cating greater consistency, followed by GPT and WSiE (M=
88.7 and 82.3). ANOV A results indicated a statistically signif-
icant effect of stimulus type for both AIME and NASA TLX
(allp<0.001), with ART differing significantly from all other
conditions. This means that the ART stimuli imposed substan-
tially greater cognitive demands on listeners than any of the
other stimulus types. Additionally, ART recall was significantly
lower than WStE (p= 0.019) and numerically lower than GPT
and WSiE, though these latter differences did not reach signif-
icance. No statistically significant differences were observed
between the WSiE, WStE, and GPT stimuli.
5. Discussion
Conversational register and listenability.The results suggest
that speech synthesised from written registers was substantially
more listenable than its conversational counterpart. ART, the
source most closely resembling spoken language by register and
classified as Informational interaction, imposed more than twice
the mental effort and yielded the lowest comprehension scores
of any stimulus type. Hence, resemblance to spoken language
(high D1) does not guarantee ease of auditory processing, and
the register features that make speech engaging in face-to-face
interaction impose a cognitive cost in a TTS context.
Written register variation and listenability.Register differ-
ences across the Wikipedia-sourced and GPT-generated texts
did not translate into measurable listenability differences de-
spite a readability gradient (WSiE most readable, WStE least).
No statistically significant differences in mental effort, cogni-
tive load, or cued recall were found among the three written
sources. The MDA profiles contextualise this result: although
WStE (Learned exposition), WSiE, and GPT (General narrative
exposition) occupy distinct registers, the three overlap in several
parts of Biber’s dimensional continuum. This may be a suffi-
cient condition for non-differentiable listenability under exper-
imental conditions. A notable exception is cued recall, where
WStE achieved the highest scores (M= 94.3), significantly ex-
ceeding ART despite imposing less listening effort. WStE im-
poses similar effort to simpler sources yet yields the best recall;
this dissociation between cognitive load and retention warrants
further investigation. The listenability of GPT-generated text
is also a notable finding. Despite minimal prompting for lis-
tenability, GPT texts were statistically indistinguishable from
both Wikipedia sources across all three measures and required
the least mental effort of any source. This suggests that LLM-
generated text is a viable alternative to sourced written text for
TTS delivery, with the practical advantage of on-demand gener-
ation and controllable register. Finally, AIME and NASA TLX
independently converged on the same pattern of results, sup-
porting their concurrent validity as measures of listening effort
and suggesting that either instrument may serve in future listen-
ability studies.
Limitations and future directions.Several limitations con-
strain these findings. The stimulus set is small, and the use of a
single TTS voice, uncontrolled online listening conditions, and
the absence of controls for prior topic knowledge limit general-
isability. Furthermore, the one-way repeated-measures ANOV A
does not account for topic as a factor, and a source-by-topic de-
sign would disentangle register effects from topic-specific diffi-
culty. A more fundamental limitation is that the four conditions
do not share semantic content. The topics are matched, but the
