Acoustic Differences Between Citation and Sandhi Tones Across Three 
Generations in Xiamen Southern Min

Abstract 
This study examines whether citation tones and their 
corresponding sandhi forms are acoustically equivalent in 
Xiamen Southern Min and how their realizations vary across 
generations. F0 contours of speech data from 49 native speakers 
in three age groups (teenagers, middle-aged, and seniors) were 
extracted and analysed to compare tonal shapes and overall 
pitch height across tone categories, syllable positions, and age 
groups. Results show that sandhi tones differ from their citation 
counterparts in both F0 height and slope, even when 
traditionally transcribed with the same tone value, indicating 
subphonemic contrasts obscured by the “tone sandhi circle”.  
Teenagers show a compressed citation tonal space but enhance 
sandhi contrasts, especially /44/  > [22a] vs. /24/ > [22b]; 
seniors largely neutralize this distinction, with middle -aged 
speakers intermediate. The findings indicate stable phonetic 
divergence and an age-graded change of the sandhi system. 
Index Terms: acoustic phonetics, Xiamen Southern Min, 
citation tones, sandhi tones, generational differences 
1. Introduction 
Tone sandhi , an obligatory process of tonal alternation 
conditioned by phonological environment , is a salient 
characteristic of Southern Min Chinese. In Xiamen  Southern 
Min (“Xiamen Min ” hereafter), the domain of tone sandhi 
application is defined with reference to syntactic structure  [1], 
whereas the alternation itself is phonological in nature. The 
sandhi patterns in this variety are commonly described in terms 
of a “tone sandhi circle” (see  Figure 1, tone notations using 
Chao’s [2]  tone letter). 
 
Figure 1: Xiamen tone sandhi circle of unchecked 
tones. 
Among the five unchecked tones, a circular chain shift  
(A→B→A) applies in non-final positions, except for  /24/, 
which occurs only in the citation position. Tone sandhi in 
Xiamen Min is right-dominant: within a sandhi domain such as 
a disyllabic word.  The rightmost syllable retains its citation 
tone, while all preceding syllables surface in their sandhi forms. 
The tone sandhi circle of the unchecked tones is structure -
preserving in the sense that no novel ton e categories are 
introduced; rather, existing tones are reassigned within the 
system. Consequently, a given tone may function either as a 
citation tone or as a sandhi form  depending on its position. In 
the present study, citation tones are indicated by slashes / / and 
sandhi forms by brackets [ ], all being italic to distinguish from 
reference numbers.  For instance, /22/ surfaces as [21]  in the 
sandhi position. 
It should be noted that the tone values reported in earlier 
studies of Xiamen Min were largely based on auditory 
impression (although more acoustic evidence has been 
published for the Taiwan Southern Min variety). However, the 
auditory perception is labial and may obscure fine phonetic 
information. In Mandarin, the sandhi form of Tone 3  has 
traditionally been described as identical to Tone 2 [3]. 
Nevertheless, a growing number of research has demonstrated 
systematic acoustic differences between Tone 2 and the sandhi-
ed Tone 3, challenging the traditional description [4], [5] . In 
Xiamen Min, the situation is further complicated by the more 
complex tone sandhi patterns. This raises important questions: 
are citation tones and their sandhi forms phonetically identical, 
and do these patterns differ across generations? Addressing 
these questions is crucial for understanding how sandhi patterns 
are realized and change in speech over time.

2.2. Experiment procedure and data processing 
The experiments were conducted in Xiamen, with a native 
speaker assisting by providing instructions and explanations in 
Xiamen Min. The tasks were administered in a fixed sequence: 
monosyllabic words, disyllabic words presented with one 
picture, and disyllabic words presented with two pictures. 
Because certain monosyllabic stimuli are more commonly 
embedded in compounds, the monosyllabic picture items were 
also accompanied by Chinese characters in small print. All 
other tasks in the production experiment were presented 
exclusively with pictures. The stimuli were displayed on a 
computer screen, and participants were instructed to read aloud 
the corresponding monosyllabic or disyllabic words. Speech 
was recorded using a solid-state recorder at a sampling rate of 
44,100 Hz. Scheduled breaks were provided between sections 
and tasks. 
The audio recordings from the production experiment were 
manually segmented using Praat  [6]. Two native speakers of 
Xiamen Min (both female, aged 35 and 22) then evaluated all 
segmented tokens auditorily. They were instructed to judge 
whether the correct citation tones were produced for the 
monosyllabic items and whether the appropriate tone sandhi 
patterns were applied in the disyllabic items. 
Interrater reliability was assessed using Cohen’s kappa. 
Agreement between the two raters was almost perfect (κ = .95, 
95% CI [.94, .96], N = 4,464 , p < .001), based on 4,464 items. 
For tokens on which the two judges disagreed (726/4,464 = 
16.3%), a third native speaker of Xiamen Min was consulted. A 
token was classified as correct if at least two of the three raters 
judged it to be correct. Tokens judged incorrect, as well as those 
for which all three judges disagreed, were excluded from the 
acoustic analysis, resulting in a total of 238 (5.3%) excluded 
tokens. 
Fundamental frequency (F0) values were obtained using 
automatic F0 tracking in ProsodyPro [7] with Praat [6]. Values 
were extracted at ten equidistant points along the duration-
normalized syllable  (excluding voiceless onsets). Tokens 
exhibiting creaky phonation were carefully inspected by trained 
phoneticians, and glottal pulses were manually adjusted to 
ensure accurate F0 measurement. An additional 59 tokens were 
excluded due to severe creakiness. As a result, a total of 4,167 
tokens were valid for acoustic analysis. The extracted F0 values 
were subsequently z -score normalized within each speaker to 
allow comparability across participants.  
Acoustic analysis was conducted for the 5 unchecked tones: 
Tones /44/, /24/, /53/, /21/, and /22/. Z-score-normalized 
logarithmic F0 data from all valid tokens  were statistically 
modelled using generalized additive mixed models (GAMMs) 
in R. The model was fitted with the bam () function from the 
mgcv package [8], with additional support from the itsadug 
package [9].  
The fixed effects were as follows: (1) Tone value , with 
sandhi tone values entered for syllables occurring in sandhi 
position; (2) Syllable position (S1 vs. S2), where S1 referred to 
monosyllabic words or the first syllable of disyllabic words, and 
S2 referred to the second syllable of disyllabic words, which 
retains its citation tone; (3) Stimulus type  (monosyllabic vs. 
disyllabic); and (4) Age  group (Teenager, Middle-aged, 
Senior). These four predictors were fully crossed and 
subsequently combined into a single composite factor, with 
each level representing a unique combination of the 
experimental conditions included in the model.  A global 
smooth term  (normalized time points ) and a by -condition 
smooth term were also added in order to capture the variation.

3.1.1. Level tones  
The two level tones, /44/ and /22/, in Xiamen Min have highly 
similar contours, with pitch height being their primary 
distinguishing feature. As shown in  Figure 3 (yellow and red

these two tones compared with middle -aged and senior 
speakers. To quantify this difference, we calculated the mean 
F0 of the 10 measure points for each level -tone token  and 
expressed the pitch contrast as the ratio F0 /44/ / F0/22/ [10]. The 
average and SD of the ratio across age groups are shown in

3.1.3. Falling tones 
The two falling tones in Xiamen Min, /53/  and /21/, differ in 
both pitch height and slope (fall magnitude). As we still need to 
capture generational differences, a simple ANOVA is not 
suitable for this  multifactorial comparison. Therefore, w e 
conducted a linear mixed -effects analysis to examine the F0 
trajectories of the two falling tones across different age groups. 
The model included Tone, Age group, and a quadratic function 
of Time as fixed effects, along with their interactions. Random 
intercepts and slopes for the time polynomial were included for 
each Participant. 
Tone had a significant effect (F(1, 815) = 3067.03, p 
< .001), confirming a substantial pitch difference between the 
tones. Age groups alone was not significant (F(2, 46) = 0.77, p 
= .469), while time point was significant (F(2, 46) = 185.17, p 
< .001), reflecting the falling contours. 
The Tone*Age group interaction (F(2, 815) = 65.14, p 
< .001) were significant, indicating that F0 differences between 
/53/ and /21/ varied across groups. Specifically, F0 differences 
between the two tones are  largest in the senior group and 
smallest in the teenager group. I nteraction between T one and 
Time (F(2, 820) = 164.43, p < .001) is also significant, 
confirming /53/ being steeper than /21/. Interactions involving 
Age group and Time or the three -way interaction were not 
significant. The results indicate that the two falling tones differ 
in both pitch and temporal trajectory, with shape maintained  
across age groups.

The primary difference between the citation tone /53/  and its 
sandhi counterpart /21/ > [53] lies in the overall pitch height  
(see Figure 5). A linear mixed-effects model was fitted for  F0

main effect (F(1, 815) = 786.27, p < .001), indicating higher F0

46) = 0.07, p = .934). 
Significant interactions were found between Tone type and

sandhi [53], and the Tone type effect interacted with both Age

4. Discussion 
The present study examines the phonetic patterns of the citation 
tones and sandhi tones in Xiamen Southern Min. First, the 
acoustic comparison between citation and sandhi tones 
confirms a fundamental point: although they are transcribed as 
phonologically identical, sandhi tones in Xiamen Min are not 
acoustically equivalent to their citation- tone counterparts. Lo  
[11] noted early on in 1930 that the sandhi tones in Xiamen Min 
were not phonetically identical to their citation forms and 
argued that the so-called structure-preserving “sandhi circle” 
served primarily as a notational convenience. Similarly, Chen  
[12], on the basis of a spontaneous speech corpus of Taiwan  
Southern Min, proposed that systematic subphonemic F0 
differences exist between sandhi forms and their citation 
counterparts. 
Within each age group, comparisons between citation and 
sandhi realizations show that the overall contour shapes of the 
sandhi forms align closely with those of the corresponding 
citation tones, which explains the traditional “sandhi circle” 
analysis. Nevertheless, most sandhi tones are realized at a 
slightly higher pitch level than their citation equivalents in 
monosyllabic contexts. In addition, a shallower falling slope 
was observed for /21/ > [53] and, more prominently, for /22/ > 
[21], relative to their citation counterparts, across all age 
groups. This difference is partly  attributable to tonal 
coarticulation. Because tone sandhi in Xiamen Min is right -
dominant, the sandhi syllable in a disyllabic word necessarily 
precedes another syllable and is therefore subject to 
anticipatory tonal coarticulation from the following tone , 
although previous studies have shown that anticipatory tonal 
coarticulation is much weaker than carryover tonal 
coarticulation in different tone languages [13]. 
Second, the generational differences are substantial. In the 
citation tones, the teenage group exhibited a more compressed 
tonal dispersion than the middle -aged and senior groups, as 
evidenced by a smaller mean F0 separation between /44/  and 
/22/ and a reduced rise magnitude for /24/.  
The most striking pattern concerns the split between /44/ > 
[22a] and /24/ > [22b], which is typologically unusual. Among 
teenagers, the sandhi contour /44/ > [22a] is realized at such a 
high pitch level that it overlaps with their citation /44/, 
suggesting a stronger influence from the underlying citation 
tone and a more gradient implementation of sandhi. In contrast, 
senior speakers show near-complete overlap between [22a] and 
[22b], producing them as acoustically indistinguishable. The 
middle-aged group appears transitional: a split is emerging, but 
the magnitude of the distinction is smaller than that observed in 
the younger generation.  As the present analysis is based on 
group-level data, further investigation, particularly into 
individual variation, effects of specific tonal environments and 
children’s acquisition patterns, may yield a more detailed 
understanding of the trajectory and mechanism of this split. 
Similar to  Mandarin, Xiamen Min also shows subtle 
acoustic differences between citation and sandhi tones, with 
particularly intriguing patterns across generations. These 
findings highlight the importance of examining how citation 
and sandhi tones interact in other Chinese dialects, potentiall y 
revealing broader patterns in tonal variation and change.
