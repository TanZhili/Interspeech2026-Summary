Phonetic evidence for contrastive length in Nakanamanga monophthongs

1 Australian National University, Australia
shubo.li@anu.edu.au
Abstract
This paper presents the first acoustic phonetic investigation of
contrastive vowel length in Nakanamanga, an Oceanic language
of Vanuatu. Previous research has been unclear about whether
vowel length is contrastive, and if it applies to all five vowel
qualities. In addition, the status of vowel length in languages of
central Vanuatu in general remains unclear. Recent phonologi-
cal analyses argue for a length contrast across all vowel quali-
ties based on minimal pairs, but the durational characteristics of
Nakanamanga monophthongs have not yet been examined. This
study investigates if vowel duration provides phonetic evidence
for the proposed phonological contrast. Results show that long
and short monophthongs differ significantly in duration across
all vowel qualities, with long vowels averaging 2.08 times the
length of short vowels. The findings provide clear acoustic sup-
port for a phonological vowel length contrast in Nakanamanga.
Index Terms: Nakanamanga, Vanuatu, Oceanic, vowel length,
acoustic phonetics, vowel duration, monophthongs
1. Introduction
Nakanamanga (also known as Nguna, Ngunese, or North Efate;
ISO 639-3: llp; Glottolog: nort2836) is a Southern Oceanic
language spoken in central Vanuatu, by approximately 10,000
people [1]. Descriptive materials include comparative lexical
work [2], a grammatical sketch [3], and a recent dictionary [4].
Detailed phonological research has until recently been limited,
and the only phonetic study to date is preliminary [5]. One un-
resolved issue has been whether vowel length is contrastive in
monophthongs [3, 4]. Recent phonological analyses argue for
contrastive vowel length based on minimal pairs [6], but pho-
netic evidence for such contrast has not been examined. This
study addresses this gap by examining vowel duration as the
primary acoustic correlate of vowel length.
1.1. Duration as the acoustic correlate of vowel length
According to the UPSID database [7], about 11% of the world’s
languages have a phonological vowel length contrast, mostly
involving a binary long vs short distinction. V owel duration is
widely recognised as the primary acoustic cue for contrastive
vowel length across languages. In languages with a two-way
vowel length contrast, long vowels are typically about twice
as long as short vowels [8], though the ratio varies across lan-
guages. For example, in Qatari Arabic, long vowels are on aver-
age 2.27 times longer than short vowels [9, p. 97], while a ratio
of 2.33 has been reported for other Arabic varieties [10]. For
Japanese and Thai, long vowels are respectively 2.56 and 2.22
times longer than short vowels [10, p. 132].

V owel duration can be influenced by factors such as sylla-
ble structure, word length, and vowel quality, for example with
close vowels generally being shorter and open vowels being
longer [11]. In languages where vowel length is contrastive,
robust durational differences between long and short vowels re-
main statistically reliable even when such factors have effects
[12, 13, 14, 15, 16]. However, irrespective of the presence
of phonemic length, duration remains relatively understudied
compared to other vowel-related acoustic correlates: fewer than
30% of phonetic studies on vowels explicitly address duration
[17, pp. 181–182]. Furthermore, most acoustic phonetic studies
are heavily skewed towards Indo-European languages [17].
1.2. V owel length in Oceanic languages
V owel length is attested in many of the approximately 450 lan-
guages of the Oceanic branch of the Austronesian language
family [18], particularly within the Polynesian subgroup. How-
ever, the extent of acoustic phonetic investigation varies con-
siderably across languages. In Niuean, a Polynesian language,
vowel length is supported by phonetic evidence: all five vowel
qualities exhibit contrastive length, and the long vs short ratio
is approximately 2.22 [19, p. 120]. For other Polynesian lan-
guages such as Hawai’ian, M ¯aori, Samoan, and Tongan, vowel
length is generally inferred phonologically. Descriptive studies
document minimal pairs or phonotactic patterns consistent with
long vs. short vowels, but systematic measurement of vowel
duration is often absent [20, 21, 22, 23, 24, 12]. In the Southern
Oceanic subgroup, several languages of New Caledonia have
been examined acoustically. In Iaai, long vowels are approxi-
mately 1.85 times (women) to 2.0 times (men) longer than short
vowels across ten vowel qualities [25, p. 171]. In Drehu, long
vowels average 175 ms and short vowels 126 ms, with an over-
all ratio of 1.39 that varies by vowel quality (e.g., /E/: 1.26;
/i/: 1.48) [26, p. 2616]. These studies demonstrate that vowel
length is reliably reflected in duration.
1.3. V owel length in languages of central Vanuatu
Vanuatu is known for its high language density, with over 130
languages spoken by a population of just over 300,000 [27, 28],
yet many remain underdescribed [29]. The majority of the
indigenous languages of Vanuatu are Southern Oceanic [27].
Within these languages, vowel length has received limited at-
tention, and where it is discussed, its status often remains un-
certain. In central Vanuatu, Namakura has been described as
exhibiting possible vowel length, but the evidence is limited and
the analysis remains tentative [30, pp. 70–73]. Similarly, for
Lelepa, a length contrast has been proposed only for /5/ versus
/5:/, while the status of length for other vowel qualities remains
unclear [31, pp. 14–15]. Nafsan is currently the only central

2. Research questions
Building on the recently updated phonological analysis for
Nakanamanga monophthongs [6], this paper addresses two re-
search questions: (i) Is the proposed phonemic vowel length
contrast in Nakanamanga supported by durational measures?
(ii) Does this apply to all five vowel qualities?
3. Method
3.1. Participants
Data was collected with 14 native speakers of Nakanamanga
(8 female, 6 male; aged 22–80). All speakers originate from
Tongoa Island, though some currently reside in Port Vila, the
capital of Vanuatu. The participants are multilingual, as is typ-
ical for Vanuatu [34]. Besides Nakanamanga, they also speak
Bislama and English or French with varying degrees of profi-
ciency. They also have knowledge of neighbouring languages
such as Namakura and languages of Efate due to contact, lin-
guistic relatedness, and marriage.
3.2. Materials
Through extensive piloting based on the dictionary [4], a 50-
item wordlist was designed to capture the proposed vowel
length contrast, with five words per phoneme. The stimulus
set was restricted to disyllabic CV .CV lexical items, with the
target vowel occurring in the initial syllable. Consonants were
mostly obstruents. Within this set, nouns consistently contained
long vowels in the target position, whereas verbs could contain
either long or short vowels. This distribution appears to be an
intrinsic property of Nakanamanga, for which no clear histor-
ical or morphophonological explanation is currently available.
Lexical items were arranged in pseudo-random order to evenly
distribute vowel categories throughout the list. Items included
minimal pairs, e.g., /lot ”u/ ‘to pray’ vs /lo:t ”u/ ‘to explode’.
3.3. Data collection
Speech data was collected in relatively quiet indoor field set-
tings on Tongoa Island and in Port Vila, with a Zoom H6
portable recorder and a RØDE NT3 cardioid microphone (96
kHz, 24-bit). Stimuli were presented as images to avoid or-
thographic influence, with verbal prompts in Bislama when

Short monophthongs occur only in verbs within the wordlist
materials, which are less reliably elicited than nouns.
3.4. Data processing
Audio was downsampled to 44.1 kHz, 16-bit for analysis. V ow-
els were manually segmented in Praat [36] at word, syllable,
and phoneme levels. V owel onset was defined as the start of
regular periodicity after the preceding consonant, and offset as
the final glottal pulse before the following consonant. Exam-
ple segmentations are shown in Figure 1, including /lesi-5/ “to
soothe-TR” and /le:si/ “papaya”. A total of 2,622 tokens were
analysed (Table 2), after excluding those obscured by noise.

and long vowels 177 ms (SD = 36), with long vowels approx-
imately 2.08 times longer. Despite some overlap, long vowels
consistently occupy a higher durational range, providing clear
acoustic support for the phonological length contrast.

4.2. Long vs short monophthongs by vowel quality
Given that vowel length contrast was reported for only some
and not all vowel qualities in neighbouring languages [31], it
is essential to test whether contrastive length is consistently re-
flected in durational measures across all five vowel qualities.
Figure 3 and Table 4 show mean duration for every monoph-
thong phoneme, including long vs short vowel pairs across five
vowel qualities. A consistent durational difference is found
across all five vowel qualities, where all five vowel qualities
show near-doubling of duration. Although the magnitude of the
ratio varies slightly, ranging from 1.88 for /e/ to 2.24 for /u/,
the durational difference is substantial in every vowel quality.
Estimated marginal means derived from the interaction model
(Model 2) confirm that the vowel length contrast is significant
for the durational measures of long vs short vowels of each
vowel quality (all p<.001), with model-adjusted differences
ranging from 71.6 ms for /o/ to 92.8 ms for /5/.
To examine whether the magnitude of the durational differ-
ences between short and long vowels differs across vowel quali-
ties, Model 2 includes the interaction between V owelLength and
V owelQuality as a fixed effect. Similar to the additive model,
the interaction model also shows a strong and highly significant
effect of V owelLength: long vowels are estimated to be 92.75
ms longer than short vowels for the reference vowel quality /5/
(SE = 10.11, t = 9.18, p<.001).

Crucially, however, the interaction between V owelLength
and V owelQuality is not significant. This indicates that the
magnitude of the long vs short durational difference does not
differ substantially across vowel qualities. It is important to
note that this does not contradict the significant main effects
of vowel quality observed in the additive model. In the addi-
tive model (Model 1), significant effects for /i/ and /u/ show
that close vowels are intrinsically shorter than /5/ overall. The
interaction model (Model 2), by contrast, tests a different ques-
tion: whether the size of the length contrast (i.e., the difference
between long and short vowels) varies across vowel qualities.
The absence of significant interaction effects demonstrates that,
although high vowels are shorter overall, the long vs short du-
rational gap remains comparable across all five vowel qualities.
In other words, vowel quality affects baseline duration, but it
does not meaningfully affect how the phonological length con-
trast is implemented. The near-doubling of duration observed
in the descriptive statistics in Section 4.1 is therefore not driven
by a subset of vowels; rather, it reflects a stable and systematic
pattern across the entire monophthong inventory. The descrip-
tive statistics and mixed-effects analysis in this section indicate
that the durational contrast between short and long vowels is
highly significant and consistently substantial across all vowel
qualities, providing further phonetic confirmation of contrastive
vowel length in Nakanamanga.
5. Discussion and conclusion
This study set out to address two research questions: (i) whether
the proposed phonemic vowel length contrast in Nakanamanga
is supported by durational evidence, and (ii) whether such sup-
port holds across all five vowel qualities. The results provide
clear affirmative answers to both. Long vowels are on aver-
age 92 ms longer than short vowels, corresponding to a ratio
of 2.08:1. This near-doubling of duration is consistent across
speakers and lexical items, and is confirmed by descriptive
statistics and mixed-effects modelling. Crucially, the effect of
vowel length is robust for all five vowel qualities. These find-
ings provide phonetic support for the revised phonological anal-
ysis proposing a ten-vowel system of five short–long pairs [6].
Typologically, Nakanamanga aligns closely with other
vowel quantity languages. Within central Vanuatu, Nafsan
shows a comparable long vs short ratio of 1.91:1 [32], situating
Nakanamanga within an emerging regional pattern of acous-
tically robust vowel length contrasts. Beyond Vanuatu, simi-
lar durational ratios have been reported for Southern Oceanic
languages of New Caledonia such as Iaai and Drehu [25, 26],
as well as for well-studied quantity languages worldwide [10].
Meanwhile, the results raise broader questions for Oceanic lin-
guistics. In several neighbouring languages, including Lelepa
and Namakura [31, 30], vowel length has been proposed only
tentatively or for restricted vowel qualities. In many Polynesian
languages, length is described phonologically but lacks system-
atic acoustic confirmation [20, 24, 23, 21]. The present findings
therefore contribute an important empirical baseline for evalu-
ating vowel quantity across the Oceanic family.
The modelling results further clarify how vowel length in-
teracts with other factors known to influence duration. V owel
quality affects baseline duration: high vowels such as /i/ and
/u/ are intrinsically shorter than low vowels, in line with well-
established cross-linguistic patterns [11]. However, the inter-
action between vowel quality and vowel length is not signifi-
cant. This indicates that intrinsic durational differences do not
weaken or mask the phonological contrast. Similarly, number
of syllables exerts a modest compressive effect on duration, and
random variation across speakers and words is present, but none
of these factors undermines the stability of the long–short dis-
tinction. In other words, while duration is shaped by multi-
ple phonetic influences, phonological length remains the pri-
mary and dominant predictor. This pattern agrees with cross-
linguistic findings that contrastive vowel length remains statis-
tically robust even when other temporal factors are at play [15].
Several directions for future research follow from this
study. First, the present analysis has focused on duration as
the primary acoustic correlate of vowel length. Spectral fea-
tures, including formant values and formant trajectories, have
been extracted for all tokens, but their analysis is beyond the
scope of the present study. This is because the focus here is on
vowel length, while vowel quality distinctions are not in ques-
tion in Nakanamanga or in related Oceanic systems. Second,
the present study is based on production data. Perceptual ex-
periments would clarify whether listeners rely exclusively on
duration or integrate multiple cues when distinguishing short
and long vowels. However, such work lies beyond the scope
of the present paper, given the need to first establish a solid
production baseline for this underdescribed language. Third,
extending the investigation to naturalistic speech, inter-speaker
variation, and to other varieties of Nakanamanga would also
provide insight into the stability and variation of the contrast
across prosodic and sociolinguistic contexts. Demographic in-
formation has been collected from all speakers, but a detailed
analysis of speaker-specific and sociolinguistic variation is be-
yond the scope of the present study.
In conclusion, this study provides the first systematic acous-
tic phonetic evidence for phonological vowel length. Long vow-
els are consistently and significantly longer than short vowels
across all five qualities, with effect sizes that align with regional
and cross-linguistic patterns of vowel quantity. By integrating
controlled elicitation, acoustic measurement, and mixed-effects
modelling, the study provides phonetic evidence supporting the
phonological analysis that vowel length in Nakanamanga is a
categorical, system-wide contrast [6]. These findings not only
confirm the revised phonological inventory but also contribute
to a broader understanding of vowel quantity in underdescribed
languages of Vanuatu and the Oceanic region.
