Human-like cross-language generalisation in deep neural speaker embeddings
and its acoustic foundations
Tianze Xu ID 1,∗,∗∗, Xiyang Li ID 1,∗, Xiaoming Jiang ID 2, Volker Dellwo ID 1
1 Department of Computational Linguistics, University of Zurich, Switzerland

Abstract
Generalisability is a central question for deep neural networks,
including the cross-language generalisation of speaker embed-
dings. This study examined whether deep neural speaker
embeddings generalise across languages and how such gen-
eralisation relates to human perception and acoustic struc-
ture. Using Cantonese–English bilingual speech presented
in within- and cross-language conditions, we derived similar-
ity from 18 state-of-the-art models and compared it with hu-
man ratings and acoustic features using linear mixed models
and representational similarity analysis. Embeddings exhibited
cross-language generalisation that broadly paralleled human be-
haviour, reflecting accurate identity discrimination and sensitiv-
ity to language switching. This generalisation relied on largely
shared acoustic cues with language-sensitive weighting, mirror-
ing human patterns. Together, these findings provide converg-
ing evidence for human–machine alignment in cross-language
voice similarity processing.1
Index Terms: speaker embeddings, cross-language generalisa-
tion, voice similarity, human–machine alignment
1. Introduction
In speech and language processing, a pressing contemporary
goal is to understand whether and how neural network models
can generalise—namely, transfer representations, knowledge,
and strategies acquired from prior experience to novel inputs
[1, 2]. One important case concerns cross-language generali-
sation (e.g., from English to Cantonese). In speaker modelling
(typically via speaker embeddings; see [3] for a review), this
issue is particularly relevant because languages differ systemat-
ically in their phonetic and phonological structure. Such differ-
ences can modulate acoustic realisations of voice, potentially
challenging downstream applications including cross-lingual
speech synthesis [4, 5] and automatic speaker recognition [6].
By contrast, humans are widely recognised as exhibiting a
remarkable capacity for generalisation [2, 7]. For example, Xu
and Jiang [8] showed that listeners generalise across languages
when judging voice similarity. English-speaking participants
with and without Cantonese proficiency rated the voice similar-
ity of speech pairs presented in Cantonese-only, English-only,
or mixed-language conditions. Same-speaker pairs were consis-
tently judged more similar than different-speaker pairs. Mixed-
language pairs were rated as more dissimilar than Cantonese- or
English-only pairs, particularly for same-speaker comparisons,

Indeed, similarity judgement is a fundamental cognitive op-
eration [9], providing a principled testbed for human–machine
alignment. Unlike tasks that rely on long-term memory for hu-
mans (e.g., recognition [10]), similarity evaluation can be mod-
elled more directly in both humans and machines. Demonstrat-
ing this, Liu et al. [11] compared perceptual voice similarity
with similarity derived from embeddings from seven deep neu-
ral network models using English speech. They found no sig-
nificant correlations between perceptual and machine similarity
under fine-grained control, whereas machine similarity corre-
lated with acoustic similarity computed from an aggregate of
24 acoustic variables. Importantly, this study did not examine
cross-linguistic generalisation. Other work has done so, but pri-
marily using earlier models based on Mel-frequency cepstral
coefficients [12, 13]. For instance, Gerlach et al. [12] reported
positive correlations between perceptual and machine similar-
ity for English speech, observed in both English and German
listeners, with stronger effects for English listeners.
Despite substantial engineering efforts to improve the
cross-language robustness of speaker embeddings for down-
stream applications (e.g., [4, 5, 6]), it remains underexam-
ined whether and how deep neural speaker embeddings gen-
eralise across languages, particularly in relation to human
perception. The present study addresses this gap by di-
rectly comparing machine, perceptual, and acoustic similar-
ity structures across within- and cross-language conditions us-
ing Cantonese–English bilingual speech. Cantonese and En-
glish differ markedly in phonological structure, offering a strin-
gent test of cross-language generalisation while enabling di-
rect comparison with existing perceptual data [8]. We investi-
gated what drives state-of-the-art speaker embeddings’ capture
of voice similarity using linear mixed models (LMMs). Ap-
plying representational similarity analysis (RSA), we examined
how speaker embeddings relate to human listeners’ representa-
tions of speaker identity and the fine-grained acoustic features
underlying these representations. To ensure generalisability, the
models analysed spanned a wide range of architectures, pa-
rameter scales, and pretraining corpora. Crucially, language-
background effects were tested by comparing pretrained and
Cantonese fine-tuned embeddings, providing a principled as-
sessment of human–machine alignment in voice processing.

2. Methods
Speech and perceptual data were drawn from [8]; the present
study further derived acoustic measures and speaker embed-
dings from the speech recordings for subsequent analyses.
2.1. Speech data
Speech recordings were sourced from the SpiCE corpus [14],
which comprises recordings from early Cantonese–English
bilinguals. Xu and Jiang [8] included 10 female speakers (aged
19–32 years,M= 22.60,SD= 3.89) who were highly pro-
ficient in Hong Kong Cantonese and North American English.
Two semantically comparable festive greeting recordings were
selected in each language: Cantonese (duration 1.17–1.43 s,
M= 1.34,SD= 0.12): “新年快樂” (Happy New Year)
and “恭喜發財” (Congratulations on happiness and prosper-
ity); English (duration 1.18–1.87 s,M= 1.45,SD= 0.17):
Have a spooky HalloweenandRelax on your holiday break.
2.2. Perceptual data
Forty participants (aged 18–32 years,M= 22.25,SD= 3.75;
20 native Cantonese–English bilinguals and 20 native English
speakers with no Cantonese knowledge) completed a voice sim-
ilarity rating task. On each trial, participants listened to two
speech samples and rated voice similarity on a 9-point scale (1
=very similar, 9 =very different). After 10 practice trials, 220
main trials were presented in four randomised blocks. The ses-
sion was self-paced and lasted about 45 min.
Two utterances in Cantonese and two in English produced
by each of the 10 speakers yielded four 10×10 speaker–
utterance matrices (excluding identical-utterance pairs). As-
suming symmetry in similarity judgments, only the lower tri-
angle of each matrix (including the same-speaker pairs on the
diagonals) was sampled, resulting in 220 trials in total. Tri-
als varied along two factors: speaker identity (40 same-speaker
vs. 180 different-speaker pairs) and stimulus language (55
Cantonese-only, 55 English-only, and 110 mixed-language).

•Pitch:f 0 (reflecting the rate of vocal fold vibration).
•Formant structure:F 1–F4 and formant dispersion (FD; an
index of vocal tract length) [16].
•Harmonic source: relative amplitudes of selected harmonics

All measures except SR and FD were extracted every 5 ms using

utterance duration and syllable count. For each utterance of
each speaker, we calculated means and coefficients of variation
(CVs) for all measures except SR and harmonic spectral shape
measures. SR was represented by a single mean value. Because
the mean values of harmonic measures can be zero, we com-
puted SD instead of CV (differing from [8]) and mean. In total,
29 acoustic variables were obtained per speaker–utterance.
2.4. Speaker embedding extraction
Speaker embeddings were extracted using the Wespeaker (Ver-
sion 1.2.0) toolkit [23] in Python. To ensure generalisability
of the findings, we employed 18 pretrained models spanning a
wide range of architectures, parameter scales, and training data:
•Seven residual network (ResNet) models[24]: ResNet34-
VX, ResNet34LM-VX, ResNet152LM-VX, ResNet221LM-
VX, ResNet293LM-VX, ResNet34-CN, and ResNet34LM-
CN. ResNet is a deep convolutional architecture with skip
connections that enable very deep training [25].
•Four simple attention module (SimAM) ResNet models
[26]: SimAMResNet34-VB, SimAMResNet34-VBVX,
SimAMResNet100-VB, and SimAMResNet100-VBVX.
SimAM is a lightweight, parameter-free attention mechanism
that enhances feature discrimination [27].
•Five emphasized channel attention, propagation, and
aggregation (ECAPA) models[28]: ECAPA1024-VX,
ECAPA1024LM-VX, ECAPA512-VX, ECAPA512LM-VX,
and ECAPA512DINO-VX. ECAPA enhances channel at-
tention and multi-scale feature aggregation within time-
delay neural network (TDNN) [29] architectures to improve
speaker discrimination [28].DINOdenotes self-distillation
of knowledge withnolabels during pretraining [30].
•Two context-aware masking (CAM++) models[31]:
CAM++-VX and CAM++LM-VX. CAM++ employs
context-aware masking and multi-granularity pooling to
enhance contextual feature modelling [31].
Numbers denote network depth. Models markedLMwere
trained with large-margin softmax objectives [32]. The suf-
fixes-VX,-CN, and-VBindicate pretraining on the V oxCeleb
[33], CN-Celeb [34], and V oxBlink2 [35] corpora, respec-
tively.-VBVXdenotes V oxBlink2 pretraining followed by

comprises speech from Chinese celebrities (largely Mandarin
sources, with unspecified dialect distribution). Thus, despite
being state-of-the-art models pretrained on large-scale datasets,
their validity for Cantonese speakers remains uncertain.
To expose the models to Cantonese, we fine-tuned them on
the ASR-SCCantDuSC [36] (∼4 hours, 10 speakers) and the
ASR-SCCantCabSC [37] (∼5 hours, 10 speakers) corpora. Us-
ing the Wespeaker toolkit, pretrained weights were updated on
the new data under the original training objectives, allowing
adaptation to Cantonese characteristics while preserving gen-
eral representations from large-scale pretraining.
Speaker embeddings were extracted using the 18 pretrained
models and their Cantonese fine-tuned counterparts. Each ut-
terance from each speaker was passed through each model to
obtain high-dimensional embeddings.
2.5. Data analyses
To investigate factors influencing machine representations of
speaker identity, we fitted an LMM predicting cosine similar-
ity [23] between embeddings in each speech pair. Fixed effects
were speaker identity (same vs. different), stimulus language
(Cantonese-only, English-only, mixed), fine-tuning status (orig-
inal pretrained vs. Cantonese fine-tuned), their full interactions,
and model architecture (ResNet, SimAM, ECAPA, CAM++).
Random intercepts were specified for model and speech pair,
with a random slope for speaker identity by model retained af-
ter stepwise reduction [38].
RSAs [39] were then used to examine how machine repre-

sentations relate to perceptual and acoustic structures. We first
constructed perceptual, acoustic, and machine representational
dissimilarity matrices (RDMs). Perceptual RDMs were drawn
from [8] and comprised 220 speech pairs per participant, yield-
ing 40 RDMs (20 Cantonese–English bilinguals, 20 English
monolinguals). Acoustic RDMs (29 in total) were computed
as absolute differences between paired speech samples for each
variable. Machine RDMs were defined as1−cosine similar-
ity between embeddings for each speech pair, resulting in 36
RDMs (18 original and 18 fine-tuned models). Each machine
RDM was correlated with perceptual or acoustic RDMs using
Spearman’s rank correlation, separately by stimulus language,
listener language background, and fine-tuning status.
Statistical significance was assessed using one-sided
Wilcoxon signed-rank tests [40] with false discovery rate (FDR)
correction (α=.05). Correlations from the machine–
perceptual RSA were analysed using an LMM, with fixed
effects of stimulus language, listener language background
(Cantonese–English vs. English), fine-tuning status, their full
interactions, and architecture. Random intercepts were speci-
fied for model, with no random slopes retained following step-
wise model reduction. Correlations from the machine–acoustic
RSA were compared across fine-tuning status using paired
Wilcoxon signed-rank tests.
3. Results
3.1. LMM on machine-derived similarity
Machine similarity scores ranged from 0.41 to 0.92 (M= 0.63,
SD= 0.09; Figure 1). The LMM revealed significant main
effects of speaker identity (χ 2(1) = 671.39,p < .001), stim-
ulus language (χ 2(2) = 175.15,p < .001), fine-tuning status
(χ2(1) = 17.30,p < .001), and architecture (χ2(3) = 94.70,
p < .001). Among the interactions, only speaker identity×
stimulus language was significant (χ 2(2) = 81.49,p < .001).
Neither speaker identity×fine-tuning status (χ 2(1) = 0.05,
p=.827) nor stimulus language×fine-tuning status (χ 2(2) =
4.15,p=.126) reached significance. The three-way interac-
tion was also not significant (χ2(2) = 0.53,p=.765).
For the main effect of speaker identity, machine similarity
was higher for same-speaker (M= 0.78) than for different-
speaker (M= 0.60) pairs. For stimulus language, English-
only pairs showed the highest mean similarity (M= 0.65),
followed by Cantonese-only (M= 0.63) and mixed-language
(M= 0.62) pairs. For fine-tuning status, original pretrained
models yielded slightly higher similarity (M= 0.64) than Can-
tonese fine-tuned models (M= 0.63). Among architectures,
SimAM achieved the highest similarity (M= 0.65), followed
by ResNet, ECAPA, and CAM++ (allM= 0.63). For speaker
identity×stimulus language, post hoc pairwise comparisons
with Holm correction revealed that the speaker identity effect
(same–difference contrast) was attenuated for mixed-language
pairs (∆M= 0.16,z= 25.61,p < .001) compared with
Cantonese-only (∆M= 0.19,z= 28.82,p < .001) or
English-only pairs (∆M= 0.21,z= 30.85,p<.001).

3.3. RSA between machine and acoustic representations
Machine–acoustic RSA identified acoustic features signifi-
cantly associated with machine-derived similarity (Figure 2).
Across language and fine-tuning conditions, the strongest cor-
relates included SR and the means ofF 1–F4 and SHR. Some
language-specific patterns emerged. For Cantonese-only pairs,
the strongest correlates were the means ofF 2 (ρ=.29),F 4
(ρ=.33), andH ∗
2kHz−H5kHz (ρ=.47), averaged across origi-
nal and fine-tuned models. For English-only pairs, the strongest
ones were the means off 0 (ρ=.30), FD (ρ=.31), and CPP
(ρ=.43) and CPP CV (ρ=.29). Mixed-language pairs
showed a more balanced pattern. Furthermore, correlations
were largely stable before and after fine-tuning, with only a few
significant changes (tested only when the original or fine-tuned
correlation was already significant): nine for Cantonese-only
pairs (e.g.,F 1 mean, CPP mean, HNR CV), two for English-
only pairs (f 0 mean and FD mean), and eight for mixed pairs
(e.g.,F 1 CV ,F3 CV , SHR mean). However, the magnitude of
these differences was small (all|∆ρ|<0.12).

The LMM on machine similarity revealed three key pat-
terns. First, same-speaker pairs consistently received higher
similarity than different-speaker pairs, indicating accurate iden-
tity discrimination. Second, this same–different contrast
was attenuated in the mixed-language condition compared
with Cantonese-only or English-only, suggesting that language
switching disrupts identity cue integration. Third, Cantonese
fine-tuning did not improve similarity estimates for Cantonese
pairs: original pretrained models yielded higher similarity
scores than fine-tuned models across all conditions (Figure 1).
These findings closely parallel human behaviour [8, 21]; for in-
stance, on the same speech materials, human listeners showed
no effect of language background on similarity ratings, consis-
tent with our observation here [8].
Machine–perceptual RSA revealed moderately strong cor-
relations across stimulus language, listener language back-
ground, and fine-tuning status conditions, suggesting that
speaker embeddings exhibit cross-language generalisation
broadly comparable to human performance. However, this gen-
eralisation is not perfect: both humans and models occasionally
make errors (e.g., low similarity ratings for same-speaker pairs).
Machine–acoustic RSA showed that machine similarity
was most strongly associated with SR, the means ofF 1–F4
and SHR. Language-specific patterns emerged for some fea-

in English-only pairs, while mixed-language pairs exhibited a
more balanced profile. This result mirrors findings in human lis-

remained largely stable after fine-tuning, though more vari-
ables changed for Cantonese-only and mixed-language pairs
than for English-only pairs, likely reflecting exposure effects
on Cantonese-specific properties. Together, these results sug-
gest that both machines and humans rely on partially language-
sensitive but largely shared acoustic cues, supporting cross-
language generalisation in voice similarity processing.
Importantly, the absence of a fine-tuning benefit is unlikely
due to the relatively small size of the Cantonese datasets. If ad-
ditional exposure were effective, fine-tuned models should have
shown higher similarity for same-speaker Cantonese pairs and
lower similarity for different-speaker pairs relative to original
models. Instead, only the latter pattern emerged (and uniformly
in other conditions), while same-speaker similarity decreased.
Insufficient data would more plausibly yield no change than a
reversal. Nevertheless, future work could examine large-scale
Cantonese fine-tuning [43]. It would also be informative to
use longer speech samples from more speakers for both ma-
chines and humans [11], while balancing listener fatigue. More
broadly, our finding that speaker embeddings exhibit genuine
