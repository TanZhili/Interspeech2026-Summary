Abstract
Transformer-based models have become the state of the art
across multiple domains, from natural language processing to
machine listening, enabled by the attention mechanisms. How-
ever, the attention layers require a large number of parame-
ters and high-end hardware for both training and inference.
We propose a novel channel-pruning technique explicitly tar-
geted at the attention mechanism, decoupling the pruning of
each head and the four matrices in the attention block: query,
key, value, and output projection matrices, employing a second-
order metric to score the network’s parameters. We compare
our technique against head-pruning strategies and magnitude-
driven scoring metrics, investigating the effects of pruning on
Audio Spectrogram Transformer (AST) and Whisper. Our re-
sults show that even after pruning 50% of the parameters in the
attention block, performance is largely preserved. The code is
available on GitHub.
Index Terms: Speech recognition, human-computer interac-
tion, computational paralinguistics
1. Introduction
Transformer-based models [1] have set the state of the art across
a wide range of domains, including computer vision [2], nat-
ural language processing [3], and more recently, machine lis-
tening [4]. In the audio field, transformer-based architectures
are applied to tasks such as sound classification [5, 6] as well
as source separation, with models like TF-GridNet [7] and TF-
Locoformer [8], and machine transcription or translation like
Whisper [9].
Transformers are known to scale effectively with model and
dataset size [10]. This led to the development of large architec-
tures with up to a billion parameters [11, 12, 13]. However,
the high number of parameters leads to longer training times,
as well as increased memory and energy requirements for stor-
age and execution, which limits the devices that can support the
network. To address this challenge, pruning techniques were
designed to remove redundant parameters without affecting the
network performance.
Pruning [14] is based on the idea that deep neural net-
works (DNNs) are over-parametrized with respect to down-
stream tasks. There are two main approaches to pruning: struc-
tured and unstructured. In structured pruning, entire neurons,
convolutional filters, attention heads [15, 16], or even layers, are
removed. Unstructured pruning, on the other hand, focuses on
each parameter independently. Although structured pruning is

less flexible in which parameters to prune, it can directly speed
up the network forward pass by changing the network topol-
ogy, without the need for special hardware, unlike unstructured
pruning [17]. In the case of transformers, a third pruning tech-
nique, called token pruning [18, 19, 20], consists of removing or
merging tokens that become redundant at specific points in the
network, thus reducing the number of inputs in the following
layers.
In machine listening, various strategies have been explored.
PARP [21] applies unstructured weight pruning to speech mod-
els but produces irregular sparsities that do not lead to any real
speed-up. Consequently, structured pruning has gained atten-
tion: for instance, Peng et al. [22] applied L0-based structured
pruning to Wav2Vec 2.0, eliminating whole attention heads and
feed-forward channels and achieving≈50%compute reduc-
tion with negligible performance loss. Other works, such as
Wang et al. [23], similarly introduced fine-grained attention
head pruning to mitigate accuracy loss in structured compres-
sion, achieving a 72% parameter reduction and a2×speedup
with no significant performance drop. Additionally, token prun-
ing has been extended to audio Transformers: dropping low-
importance spectrogram tokens can reduce attention computa-
tions by 30–40% with under 1% accuracy loss [24]. Dynamic
pruning methods further adapt the model at inference: [25], for
example, proposes context-driven gating that skips less criti-
cal attention blocks on-the-fly, preserving performance while
reducing latency by≈30%. However, structured pruning of
attention modules in audio remains underexplored. Most prior
works remove entire heads or tokens; few address finer-grained
pruning (e.g., Q/K/V channels), a direction explored in NLP
Transformers [26] but underexplored in audio.
In this paper, we propose a channel-wise structured pruning
strategy specifically targeting the attention block, and the use of
Fisher information as a scoring metric for parameters. We com-
pare against more standard head-wise pruning and magnitude-
based scoring methods. We present the results of our method on
two tasks: audio classification using the AST [5] architecture
and audio transcription/translation using the Whisper architec-
ture. Our results show that it is possible to reduce the number
of parameters in the attention layers by 50% with only a very
minor loss in performance.

Motivated by [27], the following observations and hypothe-
ses are assumed. First, not all layers require the same num-
ber of parameters. In fact, the number of heads and chan-
nels can vary across layers. Second, the projection matrices
Wq,W k,W v,W O do not need to be pruned in the same way.
In fact, onlyQandKmust have one equal-length side to per-
form the dot product. In the same way,W o in Fig. 1 processes
Z∈R n×dv to produce the output ofO∈R n×d, so they share
the same dimensiond v. This implies that, when pruning the
parameters in the layer, we only have two constraints:W q and
Wk must have the same output dimension, andWv’s output di-
mension must matchW o’s input dimension. While each head
may have a different number of channels, keeping the number
of channels constant across the heads of a single layer is com-
putationally more efficient.
Pruning schemePruning along the head dimension (the en-
tire head scheme, orEHfor brevity) is the preferred method in
the literature [15, 16, 23] for two main reasons: first, most atten-
tion implementations are optimized for the head dimension, so
removing entire heads yields greater benefits in terms of infer-
ence speed. Second, heads are independent subspaces, so prun-
ing entire heads usually hinders performance less. Nonetheless,
channel-wise pruning can be beneficial; it still improves infer-
ence speed while achieving competitive performance. In this di-
rection, we propose a novel pruning scheme that independently
selects, for each head, the set of channels to prune, to preserve
the most important dimension of each head. Fig. 2 shows the
difference in pruning patterns between per-head pruning and
head-wise pruning. We formulate per-head channel pruning
(PH) as a budget allocation problem, aiming to select the num-
ber of channels to achieve the desired sparsity while minimiz-
ing the score of the pruned parameters. To solve the problem,
we employ a greedy approach that, for each layer, defines a to-
tal budget (the number of channels to remove in QK and VO),
computes the importance score for each channel, and iteratively
selects the channels to prune. At each step, the set of channels
with the lowest cost that fits within the budget is selected, and

Scoring parameter metricIn addition to our pruning
scheme, we also adopt a second-order metric to score the pa-
rameters. Most structured pruning approaches use the raw pa-
rameter magnitudes (MAG), typically computed as norms (L1
or L2), to rank them. The parameters with the lowest scores
are pruned because they are deemed less impactful on the net-
work’s output. On the other hand, parameter magnitudes can
vary substantially across layers; layers closer to the input tend
to have smaller magnitudes than those in later layers, which
favors pruning earlier layers. Fisher information (FI) is very
attractive as a metric because not only is it less sensitive to the
scaling problem, but it also minimizes the total loss of informa-
tion in the network. Fisher information measures the amount
of information each parameter in the network carries about the
output. The lower the value is, the less impactful the parameter
is. We can show this starting from the formulation of Fisher
Information as:

3. Results
Experimental setupWe test our pruning approach on two
architectures: AST for audio classification tasks and Whisper
for machine transcription and translation. For AST, we present
results on AudioSet (the balanced version available on Hug-
ging Face) and SpeechCommand 2. For Whisper, we use the
”medium” model, with weights provided by OpenAI via Hug-
ging Face and evaluate on both transcription and translation
tasks. We test all combinations of pruning schemes, scoring
metrics, and threshold approaches on both architectures. Prun-
ing is applied iteratively for10steps; at each iteration, we prune
10% of the parameters from the attention blocks. For AST, we
fine-tune the model after each iteration using LoRA [28] for3
epochs with a learning rate of10 −4 and the AdamW optimizer.
For Whisper, we use a mix of LibriSpeech [29], MultiLingual
LibriSpeech (MLS) [30], CommonV oice [31], V oxPopuli [32],
FLEURS [33], and CoV oST [34], totaling 33k hours of audio
for fine-tuning, with a learning rate of10 −4 and the SGD opti-
mizer. We report the tables with the performance of all models
in the GitHub repository.
Importance scoreFig. 3 shows how Fisher information and
magnitude select which layers to prune. Magnitude favors lay-
ers closer to the input because they have a smaller scale than
later layers. Fisher, on the other hand, selects both the later and
earlier layers. We believe that pruning in earlier layers occurs
because mel spectrograms are highly sparse images, making the
features learned by those layers easier to condense.

4. Conclusions
In this paper, we present a novel pruning pattern for the channel
dimension in the Attention block, combined with Fisher Infor-
mation to rank the parameters. In particular, we evaluate the
effect of this type of pruning on AST and Whisper architec-
tures, comparing it against more standard techniques, such as
head-wise pruning and magnitude ranking. From the experi-
mental results, it appears that careful channel-wise pruning can
be on par with head-wise pruning, especially when combined
with Fisher Information. Several future directions arise from
this work, including exploring other fields, such as CV and NLP,
and pruning simultaneously along both the head and channel di-
mensions. In particular, we believe that relaxing the constraint
of keeping the same number of channels across all heads could
yield a better pruning pattern, although careful optimization of
the scaled dot product is needed to avoid bottlenecks that would
hinder inference speed.
