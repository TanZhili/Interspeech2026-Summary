Comparing Ganong Patterns in Mandarin Consonants and Lexical Tones

Abstract
The Ganong effect shows how lexical knowledge biases the per-
ception of ambiguous speech, yet whether this mechanism gen-
eralizes across segmental and suprasegmental levels remains
unclear. Using a within-subject design (N= 57), we com-
pared acoustic-phonetic processing (identification slopes) and
lexical reliance (Ganong effect) for Mandarin tones and conso-
nants. Across levels, neither measure was reliably correlated
within individuals, indicating that both capacities are largely
level-specific. Yet the underlying weighting mechanism was
shared: at both levels, individuals with less precise acoustic-
phonetic processing showed a larger Ganong effect. These find-
ings support a reliability-based weighting principle: although
the processing resources devoted to specific cues vary across
levels, individuals apply a common strategy, weighting lexical
knowledge against acoustic-phonetic reliability to resolve am-
biguity.
Index Terms: speech perception, individual differences, seg-
ments and suprasegments, Ganong effect
1. Introduction
Speech perception is an interactive process where bottom-up
information is continuously integrated with top-down linguis-
tic knowledge. A classic manifestation of this interaction is the
Ganong effect, where ambiguous phonetic tokens are percep-
tually shifted toward valid lexical candidates [1]. For instance,
an ambiguous sound between /t/ and /d/ is more likely to be
perceived as /d/ when followed by /-ark/. While early research
focused on the robustness of this effect at the group level [2],
recent attention has shifted toward the substantial variability ob-
served across listeners. Notably, this variability is not random
noise but rather reflects stable cognitive and experiential differ-
ences among individuals [3], as evidenced by the high test-retest
reliability of the Ganong effect over time [4]. Indeed, individ-
ual differences in lexical reliance have been linked to a range of
internal and external factors, including aging [5], cognitive load
[6], phonological deficits in dyslexia [7], compromised percep-
tion in noise [8], and even autistic traits [9, 10]. These findings
suggest that the degree of lexical reliance serves as a cognitive
signature, characterizing how specific listeners rely on higher-
level knowledge to resolve perceptual ambiguity.
To explain the mechanism driving these individual differ-
ences, recent work points to a compensatory role of lexical

perspective, initially grounded in studies of dyslexia, posits that
listeners with deficits in phonological processing may increase
top-down lexical reliance to resolve perceptual ambiguity [7].
Extending this framework to typically developing adults, Gio-
vannone and Theodore (2023) observed a negative relationship
between acoustic-phonetic processing and lexical reliance [4].
Specifically, listeners with less precise acoustic-phonetic pro-
cessing (shallower identification slopes) tended to show larger
Ganong effects. This inverse relationship suggests a reliability-
based adaptation [11]: when acoustic-phonetic processing is
less reliable for some individuals, they compensate by rely-
ing more heavily on higher-level lexical knowledge to main-
tain perceptual accuracy. Conversely, listeners with more ro-
bust acoustic-phonetic processing rely less on top-down sup-
port. However, while this compensatory pattern is theoretically
compelling, its generalizability from segmental to supraseg-
mental levels remains unverified.
A critical limitation of existing evidence is its almost ex-
clusive focus on segmental contrasts, particularly V oice Onset
Time (VOT) in stop consonants, which mainly rely on rapid
temporal processing. However, speech also conveys critical
meaning through spectral features, such as lexical tones, which
rely primarily on processing of the pitch contours [12, 13].
Given that temporal and spectral processing engage distinct
neural pathways [14], it remains unclear whether processing
of acoustic-phonetic cues and lexical knowledge is a domain-
general ability shared across both levels. Furthermore, the in-
tegration mechanism itself remains largely unexplored at the
suprasegmental level. Although previous studies have con-
firmed the presence of Ganong effects in tonal perception [15,
16, 17, 18], the specific interplay between acoustic-phonetic
processing and lexical reliance has not been systematically
compared to the segmental level within the same individuals.
Consequently, it remains unknown whether the trade-off ob-
served for consonants generalizes to lexical tones, or whether
the segmental and suprasegmental levels rely on distinct pro-
cessing strategies to resolve perceptual ambiguity.
To address this, the present study employed a within-
subject design to systematically compare the integration of lex-
ical and acoustic-phonetic cues across Mandarin tonal and con-
sonantal contrasts. By measuring acoustic-phonetic processing
(identification slopes) and lexical reliance (Ganong effect) in
the same listeners, we aim to separate processing capacity (pro-
cessing of acoustic-phonetic cues and lexical knowledge) from

2. Methods
2.1. Participants
Sixty-two university students were initially recruited via social
media and campus flyers. Five participants were excluded from
the analysis: three failed to complete the experiment, and two
were removed due to a low average accuracy (<80%) on the
endpoints across all continua. The final sample consisted of
57 native Mandarin speakers (28 females, 29 males; mean age
= 24.85 years, SD = 2.71; range: 22–29 years). All partic-
ipants reported normal or corrected-to-normal vision, normal
hearing, and no history of neurological or developmental disor-
ders. Written informed consent was obtained from all partici-
pants prior to the experiment.
2.2. Materials
The stimuli consisted of Mandarin Tone 1 (high-level)–Tone 2
(rising) contrasts and unaspirated /t/–aspirated /t h/ consonant
contrasts (see Table 1). For each contrast, the continuum var-
ied from a real-word syllable to a pseudo-word, or vice versa.
Natural endpoints were recorded by a female native Mandarin
speaker from northern China using Praat at a sampling rate of
44.1 kHz. The most natural tokens were selected from multiple
repetitions for resynthesis.

For tonal contrasts, 16-step continua were synthesized us-
ing Tandem-STRAIGHT [19]. To control for acoustic vari-
ability, pitch contours were standardized across each pair. For
consonantal contrasts, 16-step VOT continua (range: 5–76 ms)
were generated using scripts adapted from Winn (2020) [20].
All stimuli were normalized to a duration of 350 ms and an in-
tensity of 75 dB SPL.
A pilot study (N= 6) located the category boundary be-
tween steps 8 and 9. Following the sampling protocol in [4],
eight steps were selected for the experiment: two clear end-
points (steps 1 and 16) and six ambiguous steps covering the
boundary range (i.e., steps 6, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11) to accommodate
individual boundary variations.
2.3. Procedure
The experiment was implemented using E-Prime 3. Participants
performed a two-alternative forced-choice (2AFC) phoneme
categorization task. The main session consisted of 320 trials:
2 contrasts (tonal/consonantal) × 2 pairs × 2 bias conditions × 8
steps × 5 repetitions. Tonal and consonantal stimuli were inter-
mixed and presented in a fully randomized order.
Each trial began with a fixation cross (500 ms) followed
by a blank screen (200 ms). Subsequently, the auditory stim-
ulus was presented simultaneously with two on-screen Pinyin
options (romanized Chinese syllables). Participants identified
the sound by pressing the corresponding key. Prior to the main
session, participants completed a 16-trial practice block using
only endpoint stimuli to familiarize themselves with the task.
Self-paced breaks were provided every 40 trials.
2.4. Data Analysis
All analyses were performed in R. To evaluate group-level
identification patterns, Generalized Linear Mixed-Effects Mod-
els (GLMMs) were fitted separately for tonal and consonantal
contrasts using the lme4 package with a binomial distribution
[21]. The dependent variable was the binary response (1 =
Tone 1/unaspirated; 0 = Tone 2/aspirated). Fixed effects in-
cluded continuum step (centered and scaled) and bias condi-
tion. Bias condition was sum-coded (Tone 2/aspirated = -0.5,
Tone 1/unaspirated = 0.5) to ensure the intercept represents the
grand mean and the coefficient reflects the full difference be-
tween conditions. Following Barr et al. [22], we fitted a max-
imal random effect structure specified as: Response∼Step×
Bias + (1 + Step×Bias|Subject).
To examine individual differences, we next calculated sepa-
rate metrics for each participant using only the ambiguous steps
(Steps 6-11) to avoid ceiling and floor effects at the endpoints.
Acoustic-phonetic processing (Slope) was defined as the abso-
lute value of the Step coefficient from individual GLMs. Higher
values indicate sharper categorization boundaries. Lexical Re-
liance was measured as Cohen’s d, representing the standard-
ized difference in identification rates between the two bias con-
ditions. To assess whether perceptual difficulty or the extent
of lexical reliance varied by material, we compared the cate-
gorization slopes and Ganong effect sizes using paired t-tests.
Finally, we used Spearman’s correlations to test the relation-
ship between acoustic-phonetic processing and lexical reliance
across tonal and consonantal contrasts.

16. The mean accuracy was high for both Tones (98.0%) and
Consonants (97.6%), indicating that participants could reliably
identify the typical stimuli in both contrasts. This validates the
subsequent analysis of boundary shifts and slopes.

3.2. Cross-Contrast Comparison
We examined whether acoustic-phonetic sensitivity and lexical
reliance differed between segmental and suprasegmental levels
using paired t-tests. Results showed no significant difference
in identification slopes (t(56) = 1.30,p=.199) (Figure 2A),
suggesting that the tonal and consonantal tasks were compa-
rable in perceptual difficulty. Similarly, the magnitude of the
Ganong effect (Figure 2B) did not differ significantly between
segmental and suprasegmental levels (t(56) =−1.09,p=
.281). This indicates that participants relied to a similar extent
on lexical knowledge for both consonantal and tonal contrasts.

Spearman’s rank correlations revealed a significant negative
relationship between identification slopes and Ganong effect
magnitudes (see Figure 4). For the tonal contrast, steeper

tal contrast (ρ(55) =−0.466,p < .001). These results sug-
gest a cognitive trade-off mechanism: Listeners with more pre-
cise acoustic-phonetic processing relied less on top-down lexi-
cal knowledge to resolve perceptual ambiguity.

4. Discussion
4.1. Integration of Acoustic-Phonetic and Lexical Informa-
tion
Our results demonstrate that Mandarin speakers integrate lexi-
cal knowledge into speech perception at both the segmental and
suprasegmental levels. We observed that listeners shifted their
perceptual boundaries towards the lexical ends for both con-
sonantal and tonal contrasts. This finding aligns with interac-
tive activation models (e.g., TRACE [23, 24]), suggesting that
top-down lexical feedback systematically modulates sub-lexical
processing.
Unexpectedly, the interaction between continuum step and
lexical bias condition was significant for consonantal contrasts
but did not reach significance for tonal contrasts. This discrep-
ancy may stem from subtle imbalances in syllable frequency.
Although all real-word syllables in our study were selected from
elementary school textbooks to ensure high familiarity [25],
their natural spoken frequencies might not have been perfectly
matched across the bias conditions. For consonants, a higher
spoken frequency at one endpoint could induce stronger lex-
ical activation, thereby sharpening the identification slope for
that specific bias condition. Conversely, the lack of this interac-
tion for lexical tones suggests either better frequency matching
within the tonal stimuli or that tonal categorization is less sen-
sitive to such syllable-level frequency variations. Future studies
explicitly controlling for spoken syllable frequency are needed
to verify this hypothesis.
4.2. Generality vs. Specificity
Paired-samplest-tests showed no significant differences be-
tween the tonal and consonantal contrasts for either the iden-
tification slope or the magnitude of the Ganong effect, indicat-
ing that the two sets of stimuli were comparable in difficulty
and in the amount of lexical reliance they elicited. Importantly,
this group-level similarity rules out task difficulty as an expla-
nation for the pattern reported below: because the two tasks
imposed comparable demands, any dissociation in individual
performance more likely reflects differences in the underlying
processing mechanisms rather than a confound of the design.
However, this group-level comparability does not imply
that the underlying capabilities generalize across levels within
individuals. Although both contrasts elicited a robust Ganong
effect of similar magnitude, indicating that lexical integration is
a general property of perception at both levels, neither acoustic-
phonetic processing nor lexical reliance was significantly corre-
lated across the two levels within individuals. In other words,
an individual’s categorization or reliance on lexical knowledge
in processing consonants did not reliably predict their perfor-
mance in processing lexical tones. This dissociation implies
that the processing resources engaged by the two contrasts are
largely level-specific, consistent with the view that spectral
(tonal) and temporal (consonantal) cues are handled by at least
partly distinct channels [14]. Ultimately, while the integration
of lexical knowledge to resolve perceptual ambiguity appears
general, the specific processing capacities it recruits may oper-
ate relatively independently for segmental and suprasegmental
speech cues.
4.3. Universal Trade-off Mechanism
Although the processing resources for perception of Mandarin
tones and consonants appear to be separate, the underlying strat-
egy for combining information remains the same. At both seg-
mental and suprasegmental levels, we observed a clear negative
correlation between the identification slope and the Ganong ef-
fect. This pattern aligns with reliability-based weighting princi-
ples [11, 26, 27]. Specifically, the identification slope serves as
an index of acoustic-phonetic processing precision. A steeper
slope indicates that the system is extracting clear, reliable pho-
netic categories from the acoustic signal; consequently, the
weight assigned to top-down lexical knowledge is minimized.
Conversely, a shallower slope signals uncertainty in acoustic-
phonetic processing. When the bottom-up acoustic-phonetic
information is less reliable, the perceptual system dynamically
increases its reliance on lexical knowledge to resolve the ambi-
guity.
Extending this framework to Mandarin tones demonstrates
that this trade-off is not confined to the segmental level. The
observation of this trade-off between bottom-up processing and
top-down lexical reliance across both segmental and supraseg-
mental levels suggests a general optimization strategy, indepen-
dent of the specific acoustic dimension. This consistency sug-
gests that the trade-off may be a fundamental property of speech
perception rather than a temporary adjustment. Thus, although
lexical tones and consonants may engage different processing
resources, the brain applies a unified policy to balance acoustic-
phonetic cues with lexical knowledge.
5. Conclusions
In summary, utilizing a within-subject design, this study re-
veals a nuanced dissociation between processing resources and
processing strategy. We found no reliable cross-level correla-
tion for either acoustic-phonetic processing or lexical reliance,
suggesting limited shared cognitive resources. Despite these
level-specific patterns, the underlying processing mechanism
remained shared. In both tonal and consonantal perception,
individuals exhibit a trade-off between acoustic-phonetic pro-
cessing and lexical reliance. These results suggest that while
the processing resources for categorizing Mandarin tones and
consonants may vary, the perceptual system applies a common,
reliability-based weighting principle to resolve perceptual am-
biguity across levels.
