Abstract
Hate speech targeting the LGBTQIA+ community is under-
explored in low-resource languages, particularly in speech. We
address this gap by creating thefirst Dravidian speech datasets
for homophobic and transphobic hate speech in Telugu and
Malayalam, consisting of an Elicited Speech Dataset and a So-
cial Media Audio Extract Dataset . These corpora fill a critical
resource gap by capturing paralinguistic cues, which are essen-
tial for identifying hate speech from audio. We additionally pro-
vide a multimodal baseline that fuses Wav2V ec 2.0 acoustic em-
beddings and IndicBERT linguistic embeddings using attention-
based fusion, and evaluate robustness across matched, cross-
domain, and hybrid conditions. Results show strong perfor-
mance in-domain but degradation under domain shift, highlight-
ing challenges in acoustic transfer. Our primary contribution is
the development and analysis of the first Dravidian LGBTQIA+
speech hate-speech datasets, enabling future research on speech-
based detection.

1. Introduction
Hate speech against the LGBTQIA+ (Lesbian, Gay, Bisexual,
Transgender, Queer, Intersex, and Asexual) community is a
growing concern in online communication. Although text-based
detection has made progress in resource-rich languages, low-
resource Dravidian languages such as Telugu and Malayalam
remain under-studied due to the lack of annotated corpora and
models [ 1]. Moreover, most prior work focuses on text, over-
looking the role of paralinguistic features such as tone, pitch,
and prosody that are central to how hate is expressed in speech,
especially on audio-based social platforms.
The central contribution of this work is the creation of
the first annotated speech datasets for homophobic and trans-
phobic hate speech in Telugu and Malayalam. To capture both
controlled and real-world conditions, we construct two com-
plementary corpora: (1) an Elicited Speech Dataset recorded
from native speakers reading curated sentences; and (2) a So-
cial Media Audio Extract Dataset reflecting spontaneous speech
with noise, emotive variability, and natural discourse. Build-
ing on the datasets, we establish a multimodal baseline combin-
ing Wav2V ec 2.0 and IndicBERT (Indic Bidirectional Encoder
Representations from Transformers) embeddings via attention-
based fusion, and analyse robustness under matched and cross-

2. Related Works
Hate speech detection identifies abusive content targeted at at-
tributes such as race, gender, religion, and sexual orientation [2].
A bibliometric study by Sánchez et al. reviews 203 Scopus-
indexed papers (1997–2022) and underscores the importance of
automatic detection systems for homotransphobic discourse [3].
Although significant progress exists in English and other high-
resource languages, Indian languages remain in early stages due
to data scarcity, linguistic diversity, complex morphology, and
code-mixing [2]. Shared tasks have begun to mitigate this gap:
Hegde et al. (2023) released benchmark datasets for homopho-
bia/transphobia detection [4], and Chakravarthi et al. (2022) cu-
rated multilingual and code-mixed datasets from sources such
as Y ouTube [5].
Traditional text based approaches relying on n-grams and
TF-IDF (Term Frequency–Inverse Document Frequency) [ 6]
struggle with context, sarcasm, and slang, while transformer-
based methods (BERT, mBERT, IndicBERT, Telugu-BERT)
show improved performance in multilingual/code-mixed set-
tings [2, 7]. Further advances include zero-shot XLM-R (Cross-
Lingual Model-RoBERTa) [ 8], ensembles [ 9], MuRIL-based
(Multilingual Representations for Indian Languages) based Tel-
ugu systems [10, 11], and IndicBERT achieving strong weighted
F1-Score on Malayalam/Tamil social media comments [ 1].
Multi-task frameworks that leverage sentiment to counter label
scarcity also help [12]. Multimodal hate speech classification is
emerging, with fusion challenges explored in [ 13].
Gap: Despite these advances, prior work is overwhelm-
ingly text-centric. There are no publicly reported speech
datasets for Dravidian homophobic/transphobic hate speech.
Our work addresses this gap by creating the first Telugu and
Malayalam speech corpora spanning elicited and spontaneous
conditions, and by providing strong baseline evaluations.

3.2. Elicited Speech Dataset
We first curated a textual corpus containing homophobic, trans-
phobic, and neutral comments from social media (Y ouTube,
Instagram, Facebook, etc.). Table 1 shows examples of cu-
rated prompts. We then recruited native speakers (diverse in
age/gender) to record these texts. Each participant was given an
equal number of sentences (10 sentences per each class, totaling
30 per speaker) and asked to read them loud and record at stan-
dard sampling frequency of 16 kHz and 16-bit resolution, keep-
ing the mic at a distance of 10-15 cm. The recorded audio files
were then reviewed to ensure quality and content. If recording
did not meet the required quality, the corresponding sentence
was rerecorded. The resulting dataset represents read speech
with clear articulation and reduced background noise. Speaker
demographics were slightly weighted toward males, following
sociolinguistic evidence on gender-based differences in hostility
expression [14].
3.3. Social Media Audio Extract Dataset
To capture realistic usage, we scraped audio from Y ouTube
and Instagram (interviews, reactions, news, debates, LGBTQ+
awareness). Candidate videos were manually screened for po-
tential homophobic/transphobic content (topics about gender
identity, sexual orientation, and social controversies). Audio
was downloaded via Y2Mate [15] and trimmed with Clideo [16]
to remove non-speech segments. Segments were then manually
annotated into Homophobia, Transphobia, and None classes.
3.4. Ethics, Consent, and Privacy
All elicited recordings were collected with informed consent,
and participants could withdraw at any time. For social-media
audio extracts, we included only publicly available material, re-
moved personally identifying segments when identifiable, and
used short excerpts strictly for research under fair-use scholar-
ship norms. All data handling followed local institutional ethical
guidelines.

4. Baseline Methodology
We conducted a series of experiments to benchmark the col-
lected datasets using unimodal and multimodal models under
diverse domain conditions. Figure 1 depicts the flow diagram of
the multimodal framework fusing speech and text embeddings
via an attention mechanism.
4.1. Data Preprocessing
Even though the speakers were instructed to record the audio
files at a 16 kHz sampling rate and 16-bit resolution, all audio
was converted to .wav, 16 kHz, mono using FFmpeg to ensure
uniformality. Non-speech regions were trimmed with Google’s
Web Real-Time Communication V oice Activity Detector [We-
bRTC V AD](aggressiveness 2)1 using 30 ms frames.

4.2. Feature Extraction
Speech: We extract embeddings using pre-trained Wav2V ec 2.0
models for Malayalam 2 and Telugu 3. The last hidden layer is
mean-pooled over time to obtain 1024-D utterance embeddings.
Text: Audio was transcribed using whisper Malayalam 4
and Telugu models 5. Transcripts were encoded with a Multilin-
gual Masked Language Model 6. We use a max sequence length
of 256 with truncation/padding, and take the last hidden state of
the [CLS] token (768-D) as the sentence embedding. To match
spaces, speech (1024-D) and text (768-D) embeddings are lin-
early projected to a shared 768-D latent space.
4.3. Multi-Scale Fusion Classifier
A lightweight attention module (Linear + ReLU + Softmax)
computes modality weights and forms a weighted sum of speech
and text embeddings. A multi-scale classifier with two parallel
branches (512 and 256 units; LayerNorm+ReLU+Dropout) pro-
duces logits for three-class prediction.

5.1. Dataset Splits
We evaluate robustness under three configurations:
• Config 1 (In-domain): Stratified 80/20 split on the elicited
dataset for train/test, with 20% of train used for validation.
• Config 2 (Cross-domain): Train/validate on the elicited
dataset; test on the social-media dataset.
• Config 3 (Hybrid): Train/validate on elicited plus a small
elicited test subset; along with the social media data for test.
5.2. Training Details
We use cross-entropy loss and Adam with learning rate 1e−5.
Metrics are accuracy and macro F1. We save the best validation
checkpoint and use early stopping (patience 20). Predictions use
argmax over logits.
6. Results and Discussion
6.1. Multimodal Performance Across Splits
Table 3 shows that the multimodal approach performs best in-
domain (Config 1), confirming that acoustic and linguistic cues
are complementary when distributions match. Under domain
shift (Config 2), performance degrades by 50.6 % in Malayalam
and and 31.7% in Telugu due to noise, slang, and transcription
errors that impair cross-modal alignment. This drop in Config 2
specifically highlights the challenge of the acoustic variations
in real spontaneous speech (such as varying recording environ-
ments and spontaneous emotional peaks when delivering homo-
phobic/transphobic hate speech) in contrast to controlled elicited
versions. Partial recovery in the hybrid condition (Config 3)
indicates that limited exposure to target-domain characteristics
improves robustness.
6.2. Comparison with Unimodal Baselines
Unimodal systems follow the same preprocessing. Speech-
only: Wav2V ec 2.0 embeddings→ BiLSTM with attention →
dense + softmax. Text-only: IndicBERT embeddings → task-
specific head. Table 4 shows that in-domain, multimodal fu-
sion outperforms unimodal baselines, highlighting the benefit
of combining paralinguistic and semantic cues. The multimodal
approach provides a relative gain of 8.7% over speech-only and
15.2% over text-only baselines in Malayalam. For example, in
Malayalam, the fusion model can detect instances where seem-
ingly positive lexical tokens (e.g., “purogamanavadi” meaning
progressivist) are accompanied by sarcastic prosodic cues to
convey transphobic intent. Often, text-only systems often fail
to capture this. Under domain shift, text-only models are more
resilient than speech-only and multimodal models, indicating
that lexical patterns of hate are more stable across domains than
acoustic conditions. For example, in this configuration, text-
only outperforms the multimodal approach by 50.4% in Malay-
alam highlighting that acoustic features, while indicative of ag-
gression in controlled settings, appear highly sensitive to the
signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) fluctuations found in cross-domain
data. In the hybrid setup, multimodal approach improves over
speech-only but remains below text-only, suggesting that simple
fusion is sensitive to modality quality asymmetry. The perfor-
mance of the multimodal approach when compared to the text-
only baseline in Config 3 suggests that although speech adds
value, the model prioritizes the stable linguistic features when
acoustic reliability is compromised.
7. Discussion and Limitations
The introduced corpora facilitate the first systematic investiga-
tion of homophobic/transphobic hate speech within Dravidian
languages. The dataset serves as critical resource for analyz-
ing paralinguistic cues, multimodal modeling, and to study the
impact of domain shift. The limiation is that speaker demo-
graphics are modest and exhibit a male skewness. Also, social-
media samples may reflect platform-specific discourse. Future
extensions will try broadening demographics, dialects, and plat-
forms. Further, social-media audio includes noise, overlapping
speech, slang, and ASR errors, which hurt cross-modal align-
ment. This mirrors real deployment challenges and motivates
stronger noise-robust and ASR-aware fusion. We include only
public content, minimize Personally Identifiable Information,
and restricted to short excerpts curated for scholarly use. Never-
theless, careful governance is required for any downstream pub-
lic release due to the sensitive nature of the content.
8. Conclusion
This study primarily addresses the resource gap in LGBTQIA+
hate speech detection for low-resource Dravidian languages
by creating the first annotated speech datasets for Telugu
and Malayalam, spanning elicited and spontaneous conditions.
These datasets provide essential material for investigating par-
alinguistic cues and enable multimodal modelling. Our mul-
timodal baseline (Wav2V ec 2.0 + IndicBERT) shows strong
in-domain performance but reduced cross-domain robustness,
highlighting the challenge of acoustic generalisation from read
to social-media speech. While text-only models exhibit greater
stability under domain shift, results show that speech-based cues
are valuable when domain consistency is maintained. Future
work will focus on domain adaptation, noise robustness, and
richer fusion to fully leverage the datasets introduced here.
