Abstract
Recent advances in neural speech tokenizers rely on low-
framerate, semantically rich latent representations that facilitate
downstream generative modeling tasks such as text-to-speech.
However, achieving high perceptual quality at low bitrate and
framerate remains computationally demanding, as it typically
requires joint compression, adversarial training, and semantic
supervision, resulting in expensive training.
In this work, we introduceZ-CODEC, a two-stage latent
speech codec. In the first stage, we train a high-framerate varia-
tional autoencoder (V AE) with adversarial objectives to capture
fine-grained acoustic details and absorb the complexity of ad-
versarial training. In the second stage, we perform final com-
pression within the latent space of the first stage using flow
matching and incorporate semantic supervision, enabling high-
quality low-framerate tokenization.
Our approach achieves state-of-the-art reconstruction qual-
ity for both low-bitrate discrete tokenizers and continuous set-
tings, outperforming or matching strong baselines. Importantly,
the staged design ofZ-CODECmakes training tractable on a
single RTX 4070 GPU, enabling high-quality, low-framerate
speech tokenizer reproducible on consumer-grade hardware. 1
Index Terms: speech codec, speech tokenizer, text-to-speech
1. Introduction
Text-to-speech (TTS) systems have increasingly adopted neu-
ral speech tokenizers that represent audio as sequences of to-
kens [1, 2, 3]. This formulation makes it possible to train TTS
models with the same architectures and scaling strategies that
have proven effective in natural language processing, in partic-
ular large Transformer models. Transformers are well suited
to this setup: they capture long-range dependencies, scale pre-
dictably with data, and support both autoregressive and non-
autoregressive generation. However, speech representations are
typically inefficient compared to text. Even after compression
by a neural codec, token rates remain much higher than those of
natural language, leading to long sequences that are expensive
to train on and slow to decode. Consequently, recent work has
focused on improving bitrate efficiency and reducing temporal
resolution through low-frame-rate speech tokenizers [4, 5, 6].
These approaches learn compact discrete representations that
shorten sequence length while preserving perceptual quality and
downstream performance. Discrete tokenizers usually rely on
vector quantization and codebooks. Achieving stronger com-
pression often requires increasing vocabulary size or using mul-
tiple codebooks through residual or hierarchical quantization.

While effective in lowering frame rates, these designs intro-
duce additional challenges during training. Large codebooks
are prone to collapse, where many entries remain unused, and
often require auxiliary techniques such as commitment penal-
ties [7], exponential moving average updates [8], codebook re-
initialization [1], or alternative quantization strategies. Multi-
level quantization further complicates downstream modeling
and decoding, requiring specialized sampling strategies such as
delayed codebook prediction [9] or depth-wise token genera-
tion [4]. As a result, although discrete codecs provide an effec-
tive speech-to-token interface, their reliance on large or hierar-
chical codebooks introduces practical challenges when scaling
TTS systems.
In parallel, an alternative line of work has explored contin-
uous latent representations for speech modeling [10, 11, 12].
Typically implemented using variational autoencoders, these
approaches avoid explicit quantization and therefore bypass is-
sues such as codebook collapse and complex residual quantiza-
tion schemes.
In addition, many speech tokenizers incorporate semantic
supervision during training to ensure that the latent representa-
tion preserves linguistically relevant information while discard-
ing redundant or uninformative acoustic details. Such supervi-
sion is often combined with adversarial waveform reconstruc-
tion objectives to maintain naturalness, which increases training
complexity. Different forms of semantic guidance have been
explored, including CTC-based objective [13], joint ASR or
language modeling objectives [14], and distillation from self-
supervised speech models [4, 15, 16]. These techniques help
maintain intelligibility and semantic consistency in highly com-
pressed speech representations. However, the combination of
adversarial training, semantic supervision, waveform modeling,
and compression leads to expensive training setups that pre-
clude reproduction on consumer-grade GPUs.

3. Method
3.1. Two-Stage Latent Speech Patching
3.1.1. Two-Stage Speech Tokenizer
Our approach relies on a two-stage continuous speech codec.
The motivation is to reduce the computational and memory
cost of directly modeling raw waveform, which typically re-
quires adversarial training and complex setups, making scaling
up models impractical on consumer-grade GPUs. In the first
stage (WavV AE), we produce a mildly compressed latent repre-
sentationzthat preserves high fidelity at a relatively high frame
rate. This stage abstracts away the complexity of waveform
modeling, simplifying the task for the next stage. In the sec-
ond stage (PatchAE), these latents are further compressed into
a lower-frame-rate representation˜zwithout operating directly
on the waveform nor requiring adversarial training. Semantic
supervision is achieved during this second stage. This hierar-
chical design enables an order-of-magnitude larger batch size
in the second stage, keeping both memory and compute man-
ageable even in the presence of semantic supervision.

3.1.2. AR Continuous Modeling
Our TTS backbone consists of an encoder–decoder T5 Trans-
former, equipped with a lightweight velocity prediction head.
Specifically, a 3-layer MLP predicts the velocity field˜vof a flow
that maps gaussian noise to continuous speech latents˜zpro-
duced by the second-stage speech tokenizer (PatchV AE). This
simple MLP is mostly similar to VibeV oice [12] as opposed to
DiTAR[10] which directly predicts patches of higher-framerate
latents.
3.2. First Stage (WavV AE): High-Framerate Waveform
Compression with V AE-GAN
GAN-based autoencoders produce perceptually convincing au-
dio representations but are difficult to scale due to the cost of
waveform modeling and discriminator training. To mitigate
this, we train a high-framerate autoencoder with a relatively
low compression rate. This design mirrors mel-spectrogram
3972
vocoders, which operate at 80–100 Hz with moderate compres-
sion. By avoiding aggressive compression at this stage, adver-
sarial training becomes more stable and computationally effi-
cient.
Our architecture follows the time-domain encoder intro-
duced in SNAC [19], using cascading downsampling layers
with strides [3,4,4,5], and a ConvNeXt decoder inspired by V o-
cos [20] that predicts STFT features. We employ a V AE with
bottleneck dimension 24, producing continuous latents at 100
Hz.
Training largely follows the V ocos setup with three modifi-
cations: (1) the MRD loss weight is increased from 0.1 to 1.0,
(2) training is reduced to 500k steps (instead of 1M), and (3) a
KL-divergence loss with weight2×10 −4 is added. Training
takes approximately four days on a single RTX 4070 GPU. The
training data consists of HiFiTTS2 [21] together with the full
LibriTTS training split.
3.3. Second Stage (PatchV AE/PatchFSQ): Low-Framerate
Latent Compression with Flow-Matching
The second stage compresses the first-stage latent sequencezto
the target framerate and bitrate. It operates as an autoencoder
overpatchesof latents, grouping consecutive tokens (patchify,
see Figure 2b). The encoder uses [20] blocks with base di-
mensionality 768, while the decoder mirrors this architecture
and incorporates non-overlapping windowed self-attention lay-
ers (window size 32) to capture longer-range dependencies.
A velocity head, composed of ConvNeXt blocks with affine
modulation conditioned on the flow timestep and decoder out-
put, reconstructs the original high-framerate latent patches. In
practice, 100 Hz first-stage latents are grouped into patches of
8, producing a second-stage sequence˜zat 12.5 Hz.
We explore both continuous tokenization using a V AE (dis-
tinct from WavV AE) and discrete tokenization using FSQ [22],
a popular alternative to RVQ in speech tokenizers [5, 13, 23].
Both variants use 32 latents. For FSQ, each scalar is quantized
over 7 values, resulting in a bitrate of approximately 1.1 kbps.
To encourage semantic consistency, an auxiliary branch from
the velocity head (two MLP layers) maximizes cosine similarity
with semantic features extracted from the 6th layer of WavLM-
large [24]. We use the same training dataset as WavV AE. Train-
ing runs for 300k steps on 2.56 s segments with batch size
128 using AdamW and a cosine learning rate schedule from
1×10 −4 to1×10 −5. We use a KL weight of2×10 −3, gra-
dient clipping at 1.0, and randomly drop decoder conditioning
in the velocity head with probability 0.1. Training completes in
approximately six days on a single RTX 4070 (12GB).
3.4. TTS backbone: T5 transformer with velocity predic-
tion
Our TTS model adopts an encoder–decoder architecture with
cross-attention, similar to the backbone used in several recent
TTS systems [25, 26]. Text is tokenized using byte-pair encod-
ing (BPE) at the character level, with a vocabulary of 256 entries
trained on the same corpus as the model. The backbone employs
a hidden dimension of 1024 and an attention head size of 128.
The text encoder is a 6-layer self-attention transformer, while
the audio decoder is a 12-layer gated linear attention (GLA)
transformer [27], following prior designs [28, 29]. Finally, a
lightweight 3-layer MLP similar to [18], conditioned on autore-
gressive decoder outputs, is used to predict velocities˜vof the
latent speech representation˜z. We trained the TTS model with
a batch size of 50k PatchV AE tokens per update (corresponding

4. Evaluations
Our baselines for speech tokenizers include:
1.Mimi[4], a causal discrete RVQ speech codec operating at
12.5 Hz.
2.WavTokenizer[35], a single-VQ speech codec operating at
40 and 75 Hz, which does not explicitly model semantic in-
formation.
3.Higgs v2[31] a production-grade low-framerate RVQ tok-
enizer including semantic supervision.
4.XY-Tokenizer[14], a low-framerate RVQ tokenizer includ-
ing semantic supervision.
5.Semanticodec[15], a latent speech codec using semantic
inputs and a diffusion-based decoder. Unlike our method,
diffusion is trained separately from the latent representation
and compression.
6.DAC[8], a general RVQ codec that operates at high framer-
ate without semantic supervision.
We also compare our WavV AE against two vocoders [20, 30]
since our first stage latent space share comparable framerate and
dimensionality to mel-spectrogram features. For TTS, we com-
pare against three comparable baselines:F5-TTS[32], a non-
autoregressive flow-matching TTS model;Spark-TTS[33], a
discrete token-based model built on Qwen [36]; andXTTS-v2
[34], a multilingual zero-shot TTS system. Following standard
practice in diffusion/FM models, we apply classifier-free guid-
ance (CFG) during inference using a guidance scale of 2.0 for
the PatchAE and 1.3 for the TTS velocity head. We use 10
ODE steps for both PatchAE and the TTS velocity head. Under
this settingZ-CODECinference speed is measured 130 faster
than real-time at encoding-decoding on a RTX 4090 at batch
size 1 on LibriTTS test-clean samples. We report differential

4.1. Continuous Speech Tokenizer Evaluation
Table 1 reports objective metrics for WavV AE and the full two-
stage system (WavV AE + PatchV AE), compared with vocoder-
like and codec-like baselines. CER is computed by transcribing
both the original and reconstructed audio using the same ASR
model. The first-stage WavV AE achieves the highest PESQ and
comparable STOI and UTMOS scores. This indicates that high-
framerate compression with adversarial training can produce
high-quality reconstructions and provides an alternative to Mel-
spectrogram representations, offering a higher compression rate
(10×vs.≈3×) with competitive performance. When com-
bined with PatchAE to form ourZ-CODEC, the two-stage sys-
tem reduces the framerate to 12.5 Hz while preserving semantic
content as measured by dCER. The impact of SSL distillation
is measured through an ablation on the PatchFSQ variant and is
especially noticeable on the dCER that more than doubles, with
values similar to WavTokenizer at comparable bitrate. In sub-
jective evaluation (see Figure 3),Z-CODECperforms best along-
side Higgs, both in discrete and continuous setups.Z-CODECis
found to have comparable objective measures with state-of-the-
art low-framerate and semantic rich baselines.

4.2. TTS Evaluation
For simplicity we limit downstream TTS evaluation to con-
tinuous features (PatchV AE) as it requires simpler architec-
ture choice at low-framerate. We conducted a listening test
on Prolific with 50 native English participants, each evaluat-
ing 15 samples drawn from the LibriTTS test-clean split. Stim-
uli consisted of prompt-target pairs with total durations con-
strained to be under 20 seconds. Participants rated naturalness
(NMOS) and speaker similarity (SMOS) on a 5-point scale. Ta-
ble 2 compares our TTS model against recent large-scale TTS
baselines. Despite having fewer parameters (0.24B), our model
achieves competitive CER, naturalness, and speaker similarity,
on par with larger models such as SparkTTS (0.5B) and F5-TTS
(0.3B). Again this confirms the suitability of our continuous to-
kenizer to downstream task while offering tractable training of
the TTS model on a single RTX 4090 (24GB) permitted by the
low-framerate.
5. Conclusion
5.1. Limitations
Our current speech codec relies on non-causal convolution
and attention layers, limiting its applicability in low-latency or
streaming scenarios. Extending the architecture to causal set-
tings is left for future work. In addition, all experiments in this
paper are limited to English speech.
5.2. Conclusion
We introducedZ-CODEC, a two-stage speech tokenizer. By first
addressing waveform compression with WavV AE and then ag-
gregating latent patches with PatchAE, our method balances
high-fidelity reconstruction with efficient low-framerate repre-
sentation and is effective for both continuous and discrete rep-
resentations. Experiments demonstrate thatZ-CODECachieves
competitive fidelity and is effective for TTS, while remaining
fully trainable on a single consumer-grade GPU.
