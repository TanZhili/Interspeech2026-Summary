Abstract
Cochlear implants (CIs) enable speech understanding in indi-
viduals with profound hearing loss, yet music perception of-
ten remains limited. As CI users typically prefer music with a
clear beat and reduced instrumental complexity, music enhance-
ment strategies commonly emphasize vocals or percussive in-
struments. We propose a complementary approach that explic-
itly adapts to the rhythmic structure of a music signal. Per-
cussive events aligned with the main beat are amplified, while
weaker off-beat events are attenuated within a music mixing
framework. Objective measures indicate reduced complexity
compared to both the unprocessed signal and a baseline remix-
ing approach. In a listening experiment, the proposed Beat-
Gain method was significantly preferred over all reference con-
ditions in terms of rhythmic clarity, and in all but one compar-
isons in terms of overall quality. These results support rhythm-
focused processing as a promising extension of music enhance-
ment methods for CI listeners.
Index Terms: music processing, cochlear implants
1. Introduction
Cochlear implants (CIs) restore access to sound for individ-
uals with profound hearing loss by electrically stimulating
the auditory nerve via an electrode array implanted in the
cochlea. While a high level of speech intelligibility can of-
ten be achieved, music perception remains substantially limited
and is frequently described as unnatural or less enjoyable [1, 2].
These limitations are primarily related to constraints of elec-
tric hearing. Due to current spread within the cochlea, only
relatively broad frequency regions can be stimulated indepen-
dently, resulting in reduced spectral resolution. In addition,
place–frequency mismatches and compression of the acoustic
frequency range further distort spectral cues [3]. As a conse-
quence, pitch and timbre perception are particularly impaired,
whereas temporal envelope cues are comparatively well pre-
served [4]. CI listeners tend to prefer music with a clear and
stable beat, prominent vocals, and reduced instrumental com-
plexity [1, 5, 6]. They also favor mixes in which vocals, drums,
and bass are emphasized relative to other instruments [7].
These findings have motivated existing music enhancement
approaches for CI users, which can broadly be categorized
into two directions. The first relies on source separation and
remixing. For instance, harmonic–percussive sound separation
(HPSS) has been used to decompose music into harmonic and
percussive components [8], or source-separation techniques to

Combinations of these techniques, integrating HPSS-based per-

have been investigated in [14] and have also shown improve-
ments in speech intelligibility in noise for CI users [15, 16].
While these approaches primarily address spectral balance
and the relative weighting of stems, rhythmic structure itself has
not been targeted explicitly. Rhythmic clarity depends not only
on the prominence of percussive elements but also on their tem-
poral organization. Percussive events may interfere with con-
current spectral components, which is particularly detrimental
for CI listeners given their limited spectral resolution. This
effect may already be alleviated partially by music processing
strategies that globally emphasize rhythmic stems and percus-
sive elements. This was observed, for instance, in [14] and [17]
where listening tests revealed significant improvements of dis-
tinctness of rhythm in processed music.
A second aspect, relevant for both normal-hearing listeners
and CI users, concerns the intrinsic structural complexity of the
musical rhythm itself. From an information-theoretic perspec-
tive, rhythmic complexity increases when events become less
regular and less predictable [18]. However, music-theoretical
considerations highlight the importance of metrical position.
Western tonal music typically follows a metrical framework
defined by tempo and time signature. In this most common
case of quadruple meter (4/4 time), a measure is divided into
four beats of approximately equal temporal spacing. Relative
to the prevailing tempo, these beats correspond to quarter-note
durations, which can be further subdivided into eighth and six-
teenth notes. Within a measure, different temporal positions
carry different perceptual weights according to a metrical hi-
erarchy. Events occurring on metrically strong beats, such as
quarter-note positions, reinforce the established structure and
are typically perceived as stable. In contrast, when strong beats
are omitted and events instead occur on metrically weaker po-
sitions, the resulting syncopation increases perceived rhythmic
complexity [19, 20].
Based on these considerations, we hypothesize that explic-
itly enhancing temporally strong events while attenuating metri-
cally weak events can reduce rhythmic complexity and increase
pulse clarity. We therefore propose a processing strategy that
uses beat tracking to locate metrical positions and selectively

In principle, a wide range of gain patterns could be defined,
with individual gain factors assigned to each sixteenth-note po-
sition and to each stem (two illustrative examples are shown
in Figure 2). In this proof-of-concept study, we deliberately
employ a simple and interpretable gain pattern (blue curve in
Figure 2) that emphasizes metrically salient positions. All tem-
poral positions aligned with the main beat and its regular sub-
divisions (quarter and eighth-note positions) are amplified by
a factor of two, whereas intermediate sixteenth-note positions
that do not coincide with this grid are attenuated to zero. This
pattern reinforces the metrically strongest and perceptually sim-
plest positions of the rhythm, strengthening the dominant pulse
while suppressing off-grid events that would otherwise increase
rhythmic complexity, particularly in the case of syncopation.
3. Evaluation
3.1. Parametrization of evaluated models
Results reported in [11] indicated that a remixing strategy pre-
serving the vocal stem and the percussive components of the re-
maining stems, while suppressing their harmonic components,
achieved the most favorable subjective ratings. Following this
finding, we setβ bh =β dh =β oh = 0, thereby discarding
the harmonic components of the bass, drums, and other stems.
The vocal components are preserved without level modification
by choosingβ vh =β vp = 1. To investigate the role of per-
cussive amplification, assumed to be a key factor for rhythmic
salience, we vary the gain of the remaining percussive compo-
nents according toβbp =β dp =β op =α, whereαis a tunable
parameter. This parametrization allows us to compare a uni-
form amplification of all percussive content with the proposed

a comparison to the original signal, are shown in Figure 3. The
visualizations reveal a substantial sparsification of the spectral
content. For V+P, bass components are attenuated, and several
harmonic structures are removed, as indicated by the absence of
horizontal spectral lines (e.g., between approximately 1.2 s and
2.3 s). V ocal components, visible e.g. as horizontal structures
around 0.5–1.5 s and after 2 s, are preserved, as well as percus-
sive elements characterized by vertical structures. While the
vertical components in V+P remain relatively dense and tem-
porally irregular, they are further amplified in V+2P and both
amplified and temporally sparsified in BeatGain.
3.2. Instrumental Measures
To compare the proposed method with mixing approaches and
to analyze parameter-dependent effects, the instrumental com-
plexity measure proposed by Buyens et al. [25] is employed.
This measure was developed to approximate results of listening
experiments in which participants rated the perceived complex-
ity of rock and pop music excerpts. It combines signal fea-
tures such as tempo, roughness, pulse clarity, and crest factor
using optimized weighting factors to yield a complexity score
between 0 (very simple) and 100 (very complex). Although
originally validated with normal-hearing listeners, the measure
was specifically designed for music processing in CI applica-
tions, motivated by findings that complexity ratings of CI users
strongly correlate with those of normal-hearing listeners [26].
In addition, the scale-invariant signal-to-distortion ratio (SI-
SDR) [27] was computed to quantify deviations of the pro-
cessed signal from the original mixture. By compensating for
global level differences, SI-SDR reflects structural distortions
introduced by the processing. Since the aim is to enhance rhyth-
mic clarity while preserving the musical character, large de-
creases in SI-SDR would indicate undesirable alterations.
The measures were computed forα∈[0,5]for both
BeatGainα and V+αP. Evaluation was conducted on all test sig-
nals of the IKA CI Pop Music Dataset [24], which comprises
fifteen short excerpts of rock and pop music containing activity
in all stems (vocals, drums, bass, and others).
3.3. Listening experiment
The proposed method withα= 1was evaluated against the un-
processed signal, the reference conditions V+P and V+2P using
a two-alternative forced-choice (2AFC) paradigm 1. Seventeen
normal-hearing participants (mean age: 29.4 years, SD = 7.5)
took part in the experiment. For each trial, they indicated their
preference between two presented signal variants with respect
to overall impression and rhythmic clarity. Ten short pop/rock
excerpts from the IKA CI Pop Music Dataset [24] were selected
(samples 3, 4, 5, 6, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12, and 15). All excerpts con-
tain vocals, drums, bass, additional instrumentation, and rhyth-
mic components that include subdivisions finer than the eighth-
note grid. As the subjects were normal hearing listeners, but
the method is intended for CI listeners, we used a noise-excited
band-pass vocoder [28] for simulating the auditory perception
of CI listeners. The vocoder used eight band pass filters equally
spaced on the Greenwood scale [29] in a frequency range from
100 Hz to 8 kHz and a sampling rate of 16 kHz. After vocoding,
the signals were loudness normalized to an equal loudness level
of -27 loudness units relative to full scale (LUFS) [30]. The ex-
cerpts were presented in randomized order via Beyerdynamics
DT-770 M headphones.

4.2. Listening experiment
Figure 5 shows the preference results averaged across all sub-
jects and excerpts. Statistical significance was evaluated for
each pairwise comparison using a binomial test, with Bonfer-
roni–Holm correction applied to account for multiple compar-
isons at a significance level of 5%. For both overall impression
and rhythmic clarity, all processed conditions were significantly
preferred over the unprocessed signal (denoted as “Unproc” in
the figures). Furthermore, increasing the gain of the percussive
components (V+2P and BeatGain) was significantly preferred
over the condition without additional amplification (V+P). For
the BeatGain vs. V+2P comparison, BeatGain obtained higher
mean preference ratings. Although this difference did not reach
statistical significance for overall impression, it was statistically
significant for rhythmic clarity. This finding suggests that met-
rically guided enhancement provides an additional perceptual
benefit beyond uniform percussive amplification.

prominence, the present method incorporates metrical infor-

Objective measures indicated a decreased complexity com-
pared to both the baseline mixing-based algorithm and the un-
processed signal while adding only minor additional distor-
tions. Listening experiment results further demonstrated im-
proved perceived rhythmic clarity and an enhanced overall im-
pression for both the baseline mixing strategy and the proposed
method. An increased gain of percussive components was pre-
ferred across conditions. Although differences in overall im-
pression between the proposed BeatGain processing and the
baseline mixing strategy were not consistently significant, a
preference emerged in the rhythmic clarity ratings when Beat-
Gain was applied. This pattern underlines the inherently mul-
tidimensional and subjective nature of music perception: im-
provements in a specific attribute, such as rhythmic clarity, do
not necessarily translate directly into proportional gains in over-
all preference, as multiple perceptual factors interact.
Overall, the findings demonstrate that explicitly targeting
rhythmic structure constitutes a viable and promising exten-
sion of existing music pre-processing strategies. The results
encourage further investigation of rhythm-focused processing
approaches, particularly in studies involving CI users. Future
work may also explore alternative enhancement patterns, other
metrical structures beyond the 4/4 case considered here, as well
as signal-feature– and listener-specific parameterizations to bet-
ter understand their relationship to overall preference and to op-
timize perceptual outcomes.
Rhythm-focused processing approaches may also be rele-
vant for speech perception which similarly relies on hierarchical
temporal structure [31]. Enhancing perceptually relevant tem-
poral events, such as speech onsets, helps to improve speech
intelligibility in CI users [32]. Also music-inspired strategies
[15, 16] have been shown to improve speech perception, sug-
gesting potential for future applications.
