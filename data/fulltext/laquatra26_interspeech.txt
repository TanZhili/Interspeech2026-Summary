Abstract
Standard ASR systems ignore clinical context about speak-
ers’ neurological conditions, limiting their effectiveness on
dysarthric speech. We propose etiology-aware speech language
models that incorporate clinical knowledge to improve recog-
nition. We compare four strategies: standard fine-tuning, aux-
iliary etiology classification, providing the condition as input
context (hinting), and predicting the condition before transcrib-
ing within the same autoregressive stream. We find that predict-
ing the condition before transcribing is the most effective strat-
egy, achieving 7.77% WER on the Speech Accessibility Project
dataset, a 6.4% relative reduction over standard fine-tuning.
This approach requires no clinical labels at inference. Auxil-
iary classification reaches higher etiology accuracy yet fails to
reduce WER, showing that clinical reasoning must occur within
the generation stream to benefit recognition. Cross-dataset eval-
uation on TORGO confirms consistent gains across conditions.
Index Terms: Dysarthric speech recognition, speech language
models, clinical auxiliary tasks, etiology prediction
1. Introduction
Dysarthric speech recognition represents one of the most chal-
lenging problems in automatic speech recognition, affecting
millions of individuals worldwide with neurological conditions
such as stroke, Parkinson’s disease, amyotrophic lateral scle-
rosis (ALS), cerebral palsy, and Down syndrome [1]. These
conditions produce highly variable speech patterns with im-
paired articulation, altered prosody, and reduced intelligibility,
substantially degrading the performance of standard ASR sys-
tems [2, 3]. Traditional approaches have focused on acoustic
modeling improvements through speaker adaptation [4], robust
features [5, 6], and domain-specific training [7]. Recent ad-
vances have explored fine-tuning strategies [8–10], pathology-
aware representations [11], and post-processing error correc-
tion [12]. However, these approaches treat clinical information
about speakers’ neurological conditions as secondary, missing
the chance to leverage condition-specific acoustic-phonetic pat-
terns [13, 14].
The emergence of speech language models (SLMs)
presents new opportunities for incorporating clinical reasoning
into dysarthric speech recognition [15–17]. Unlike traditional
encoder-decoder models [18–20], SLMs can naturally han-
dle multi-task scenarios and auxiliary prediction tasks. How-
ever, how to optimally incorporate clinical knowledge into
these models remains unexplored. In this work, we investi-
gate etiology-aware SLMs that incorporate information about
the speaker’s underlying neurological condition (i.e., the eti-
ology of their dysarthria) to improve transcription. We com-
pare four training approaches:standard supervised fine-tuning
(SFT), where models learn transcription without clinical infor-
mation;etiology classification (EC), where an auxiliary classi-
fication head predicts the speaker’s condition but does not feed
this prediction to the decoder;etiology hinting (EH), where clin-
ical information is provided as input context; andetiology pre-
diction (EP), where the model generates the speaker’s condi-
tion as text before transcription within the same autoregressive
stream. We find that explicit etiology prediction significantly
outperforms all alternatives.
Our contributions are threefold. First, we present a system-
atic comparison of strategies for incorporating clinical knowl-
edge into speech language models for dysarthric speech recog-
nition. Second, we show that the placement of clinical reason-
ing within the generation stream is critical: auxiliary classifi-
cation achieves 81% etiology accuracy yet yields higher WER
than standard fine-tuning, while in-sequence prediction reaches
72% accuracy but reduces WER from 8.30% to 7.77%. Third,
we evaluate our approach across five neurological conditions on
the SAP dataset [1] and confirm cross-dataset generalization on
TORGO [21].
2. Related Work
In recent years, large pre-trained models have been used to ad-
dress automatic speech recognition of dysarthric speech using
parameter-efficient fine-tuning [22], curriculum learning [9],
and model merging techniques [10]. Data scarcity has mo-
tivated various augmentation strategies, including adversarial
perturbation methods [23] and voice conversion approaches
that synthesize new dysarthric utterances from limited speaker
data [24]. Generating synthetic dysarthric speech has been ex-
plored using multi-speaker text-to-speech systems with sever-
ity controls and pause insertion mechanisms [25]. While these
data-side approaches address sample scarcity, they do not lever-
age information about the speaker’s neurological condition.
Clinical knowledge integration has primarily been exploited at
the representation level. Pathological speech analysis shows
that condition-specific knowledge is crucial for detecting and
characterizing speech disorders [26,27], supporting the value of
clinical information for speech-based tasks. Pathology-aware
speech encoders use etiology-specific modeling during pre-
training to create better acoustic representations [11]. These ap-
proaches focus on encoding clinical knowledge into feature ex-
traction processes rather than incorporating clinical information
into the transcription task itself. Post-processing methods com-
bined traditional ASR with language model-based error correc-

The emergence of speech language models (SLMs) has
opened new possibilities in speech recognition [15–17]. Un-
like traditional encoder-decoder architectures [18–20], SLMs
can naturally handle additional tasks alongside transcription.
Their autoregressive text generation framework enables chain-
of-thought reasoning [28], where intermediate predictions in-
form subsequent outputs, making them suitable for clinical rea-
soning processes. However, no prior work has systematically
compared strategies for integrating clinical etiology knowledge
into SLMs for dysarthric speech recognition, nor investigated
whether the position of clinical information within the model
architecture determines its benefit for transcription quality. We
address this gap and find that the placement of clinical reason-
ing within the model, not its accuracy, determines whether it
benefits transcription.
3. Methodology
Beyond standard supervised fine-tuning (SFT), we propose
three approaches for incorporating clinical knowledge into
speech language models, as illustrated in Figure 1, which differ
in how clinical information is integrated into the model.
3.1. Problem Formulation
Given an audio sequencexfrom a speaker with a neurologi-
cal conditione∈E={ALS, Parkinson’s, Stroke, Down Syn-

Traditional approaches modelP(y|x), ignoring clinical context
e; we exploit etiology information to improve transcription.
Standard Transcription.Our baseline approach follows the
conventional speech language model SFT. The model learns to
generate transcripts directly from audio through autoregressive
token prediction:

The model receives a standard transcription prompt and learns
to map acoustic patterns to text tokens without explicit clinical
awareness. This establishes our performance baseline for incor-
porating clinical knowledge.
Etiology Classification (EC).This approach adds an auxiliary
classification objective to standard fine-tuning. A classification
head attached to the audio encoder predicts the speaker’s neu-
rological condition:
P(e|x) =softmax(W·pool(h(x)) +b)
whereh(x)denotes the speech encoder output and pool(·)per-
forms temporal mean pooling. The total training loss combines
the standard transcription objective with the classification term:
L=L LM +L CE
whereL LM is the autoregressive language modeling loss over
transcript tokens,L CE is the cross-entropy classification loss
over etiology labels. Crucially, the etiology prediction from
this head does not feed into the language model decoder. The
decoder prompt remains identical to standard fine-tuning, re-
ceiving no clinical information. This variant isolates the effect
of condition-discriminative encoder features from the effect of
conditioning the decoder on clinical information.

Etiology Prediction (EP).Our etiology prediction approach re-
quires the model to perform explicit clinical assessmentbefore
transcription. The model learns a joint distribution over both
etiology and transcript:
P(y,e ∗|x) =P(e ∗|x)·P(y|x,e ∗)
wheree ∗ represents the model’s predicted etiology for the in-
put speech. During training, the model generates a structured
output that first predicts the speaker’s neurological condition,
then produces the transcript (Figure 1). At inference, we re-
move the etiology prefix from the generated text and compute
WER only on the transcript portion. The model is required to
analyze acoustic-pathological patterns in the speech signal to
infer the underlying neurological condition, then uses this pre-
diction to condition subsequent transcription decisions. This
establishes a causal chain: acoustic analysis→etiology predic-
tion→condition-aware transcription. Unlike EC, the etiology
prediction is generated within the same autoregressive stream as
the transcript, so every subsequent token is causally conditioned
on the model’s clinical assessment.

4.2. Results
SAP Results.Table 1 presents our core results on the SAP
dataset. Fine-tuning strongly improves all models, with SLMs
matching or outperforming encoder-decoder models (Kimi:
8.30% vs. Parakeet: 8.73% WER). Among etiology-aware
strategies, EP achieves 7.77% WER, with relative improve-
ments over SFT (6.4%), EC (8.5%), and EH (5.5%), all statis-
tically significant withp <0.001(paired t-test). Notably, EC
achieves 81% classification accuracy, substantially higher than
EP’s 72%, yet yields 8.49% WER, worse than both SFT and
EP. This indicates that condition-discriminative encoder fea-
tures alone are insufficient; clinical reasoning must reach the
decoder to improve transcription. Although EC multi-task train-
ing improves the encoder, the etiology label never enters the de-
coder’s generation context; EP, by inserting an explicit etiology
token into the autoregressive stream, gives the decoder direct
access to the clinical assessment. EP also achieves the high-
est SemScore (91.05), consistent with the WER improvements
across all conditions.

data availability. ALS (5.75% WER) and Parkinson’s disease
(6.03% WER) achieve the lowest error rates, benefiting from
substantial training data (22% and 49% of samples, respec-
tively). Down syndrome (17.90% WER) and cerebral palsy
(13.76% WER) present the greatest challenges, reflecting more
severe articulatory impairments that affect intelligibility across
all training strategies. Stroke reveals the most informative pat-
tern across training strategies: despite having the fewest train-
ing samples (4%), both EC and EP improve over SFT (9.93%
and 8.80% vs. 10.87% WER), while EH degrades performance
(11.18%), indicating that passive clinical context can hurt when
training data is scarce. EC’s improvement shows that auxiliary
etiology supervision helps the encoder in low-resource settings,
but EP’s 19.0% relative reduction over SFT confirms that feed-

Cross-Dataset Generalization.Table 2 shows that SAP-
trained models generalize to the out-of-distribution TORGO
corpus without any TORGO-specific fine-tuning. TORGO
contains speakers with cerebral palsy and ALS (two con-
ditions present in SAP training) plus matched healthy con-
trols [21], providing a genuine test of cross-domain transfer.
The performance hierarchy observed on SAP remains consis-
tent: EP achieves the best overall WER (15.8%), followed
by EH (16.2%), EC (16.7%), and standard Kimi (18.0%).
All three etiology-aware strategies improve over standard fine-
tuning, confirming that clinical knowledge generalizes beyond
the training domain. TORGO, however, includes healthy speak-
ers with no corresponding SAP etiology label. For EH, we
replace condition-specific hints with binary prompts indicat-
ing whether the speaker is healthy or has a neurological con-
dition, as TORGO does not provide per-speaker etiology la-
bels, introducing a train-inference mismatch. EP instead pre-
dicts an etiology for all speakers, including healthy ones, re-
quiring no prompt adaptation. Despite this forced misattri-
bution, EP achieves comparable healthy performance to other
strategies. EC achieves the lowest WER on healthy single-
word recognition (18.5% vs. 20.3% for EP), though this ad-
vantage disappears on sentences where all methods perform
comparably (2.4-2.5% WER). However, EP shows the largest
gains on non-healthy speakers, reducing WER from 57.2% to
50.1% on single-word tasks and from 28.6% to 24.2% on sen-
tences compared to standard Kimi, driving the best overall per-
formance. Single-word tasks remain more challenging than
sentences across all models (50.1% vs. 24.2% WER for non-
healthy speakers with EP), aligning with established literature
on contextual benefits for dysarthric speech recognition [12].
Impact of Etiology Misclassification.To assess whether in-
correct etiology predictions degrade transcription, we stratify
EP results by prediction correctness. Samples with correct
predictions achieve 7.78% WER, while samples with incor-
rect predictions achieve 7.71% WER, showing no degradation
from misclassification. This suggests that the act of predict-
ing etiology, rather than the prediction’s correctness, forces the
model to attend to pathologically relevant acoustic features.
Per-condition analysis supports this interpretation: misclassi-
fication correlates with speech characteristics rather than model
failure. For conditions with generally low intelligibility (cere-
bral palsy, ALS), misclassified samples achieve substantially
lower WER (e.g., cerebral palsy: 7.18% incorrect vs. 18.11%
correct), suggesting these speakers may exhibit atypical, more
intelligible speech patterns for their condition. Conversely, for
Down syndrome, misclassified samples tend to be more severe
(28.65% vs. 13.97% WER).
Etiology Prediction Analysis.The results above reveal a con-
sistent pattern: the placement of clinical reasoning within the
model determines its impact on transcription. EC’s high clas-
sification accuracy (81%) does not translate into lower WER
because condition-discriminative features remain confined to
the encoder. In contrast, EP places clinical reasoning within
the autoregressive generation stream, where etiology predic-
tions causally condition all subsequent transcript tokens. The
superior performance of EP over EH further shows that mod-
els benefit more from generating clinical assessments than from
receiving them as input. EP also offers a practical advantage:
while EH requires clinical information at inference time, EP
eliminates this dependency by internalizing clinical knowledge
during training. Notably, EH receives oracle etiology labels at
inference, giving it strictly more information than EP, yet EP
achieves lower WER (7.77% vs. 8.22%). This confirms that
self-generated clinical reasoning is more effective than exter-
nally provided context.
5. Conclusion
We proposed etiology-aware speech language models that in-
corporate clinical reasoning into dysarthric speech recognition
via in-sequence clinical prediction. Our key finding demon-
strates that etiology prediction outperforms standard transcrip-
tion (8.30% WER), auxiliary classification (8.49%), and etiol-
ogy hinting (8.22%), achieving 7.77% WER while eliminat-
ing the need for clinical information at inference time. No-
tably, auxiliary classification achieves higher etiology accuracy
(81%) than prediction (72%) yet fails to improve transcription.
This shows that classification accuracy is not the limiting factor:
what matters is whether clinical reasoning reaches the decoder,
where it causally conditions each subsequent transcript token.
This result suggests that explicit clinical prediction improves
speech recognition through active reasoning rather than passive
context provision. Future work could extend this framework
to predict multiple clinical attributes, such as severity levels or
symptom profiles, though progress depends critically on devel-
oping consistent and clinically validated annotation protocols
for such auxiliary tasks.
