Abstract
This study investigates how normalization methods shape L2
vowel perception modelling. We trained multi-layer percep-
trons to classify L1 Spanish vowels using F1-F2 data from
DIMEx100, varying only the normalization (raw, gender-wise
Z, Lobanov, Nearey 1/2, Gerstman). English vowel data
from TIMIT were then fed to compare each model’s pre-
dicted categorization of L2 English vowels into L1 Spanish
ones with human data. Lobanov yielded the strongest fit (low-
est MAE/RMSE, highest correlation and category match), de-
spite expectations it would overnormalize cross-linguistic dif-
ferences. Using the L2LP framework, we argue that Lobanov’s
collapsing of between-language variation simulates L2LP’s full
copying of L1 categories and normalization patterns for Span-
ish listeners with no English knowledge. These results suggest
that optimal normalization depends on the target listener profile,
with Lobanov capturing the initial state and Nearey potentially
better modelling further L2 development.

1. Introduction
Neural network models have proven useful for modelling non-
native speech perception. They allow researchers to manipulate
language exposure through training parameters and to incorpo-
rate multiple acoustic cues simultaneously by adjusting input
dimensionality [1]. However, neural networks are sensitive to
the relative scale of their input features: when cues differ sub-
stantially in magnitude, models tend to weight the higher-scaled
cue disproportionately. In the context of vowel perception mod-
elling using vowel formant frequencies, this means that speaker
normalization of the acoustic input is not merely a methodolog-
ical convenience but a necessity for maintaining balanced train-
ing across cue types.
Many studies have evaluated vowel normalization meth-
ods across several aspects. The most comprehensive acoustic
comparison is presented in [2], where 12 normalization pro-
cedures were tested on a large sample of Dutch vowels pro-
duced by 160 speakers, evaluating how well each method pre-
served phonemic information, retained sociolinguistic varia-
tion, and reduced anatomical/physiological differences between
speakers. The results established that vowel-extrinsic, formant-
intrinsic methods performed best overall, with Lobanov normal-
ization achieving the strongest balance across all three criteria.
This finding has been widely replicated [3, 4]. Escudero and
Bion [5] proposed a sequential model of vowel perception in

In more recent work, Persson et al. [6] found that Lobanov

is not necessarily the one that best models perception.
However, these prior normalization comparisons share a
common limitation: they are almost exclusively conducted
within a single language. Few studies have systematically com-
pared normalization methods in a cross-linguistic context in-
volving vowel comparison between an L1 and a new language
within the same listener, requiring preservation of phonetically
meaningful differences between the two systems—an assump-
tion held by the Second Language Linguistic Perception (L2LP)
model [10, 11, 12], which posits separate perception gram-
mars for each language, as opposed to models assuming a com-
mon phonological space [13, 14]. Additionally, Barreda and
Nearey [15] raised the concept of ”overnormalization,” whereby
a normalization method may collapse acoustically meaningful
distinctions—a concern particularly relevant in cross-linguistic
settings where the acoustic distance between two vowel inven-
tories carries perceptual significance. Rankinen and de Jong
[16] provided indirect evidence for this, finding that Nearey’s
log-mean method better preserved vowel-space differences as-
sociated with ethnic heritage than Lobanov’s Z-score approach.
Beyond normalization itself, the broader task of computa-
tionally modelling L1 and L2 vowel perception has been ex-
plored extensively in earlier works [10, 11, 17]. In particu-
lar, [17] showed that computational linguistics (a Stochastic OT
model with the Gradual Learning Algorithm) was superior to
machine learning (Nearest Neighbor and Naive Bayes classi-
fiers) in modelling Spanish learners’ perception of Dutch vow-
els. More recently, [1] showed that a neural network trained
on L1 Cypriot Greek vowels could predict L2 English vowel
classification patterns. However, neither [17] nor [1] included
a comparison of normalization procedures, as both studies used
a single method. How normalization choice affects such cross-
linguistic modelling, particularly when evaluated against human
listener data, remains unaddressed.

2.2. L2 vowel perception and the L2LP model
The L2LP model [11, 10] provides a framework for understand-
ing how L2 learners perceive non-native sounds. A central
claim of L2LP is that naive listeners—those with no exposure to
the L2—perceive L2 sounds entirely through the filter of their
L1 grammar. According to the Full Copying hypothesis, the
initial L2 perceptual grammar is a direct copy of the L1 gram-
mar: all L1 phonological categories, including their associated
acoustic-phonetic mappings, are transferred to the L2. With suf-
ficient L2 input and exposure, the Full Access hypothesis pre-
dicts that learners can develop new perceptual categories that
eventually approximate those of L2 native speakers, signalling
that the perceptual grammar is not permanently constrained by
the L1 but develops in response to L2 input. Under this view,
the initial state of L2 perception involves a complete mapping
of L2 acoustic input onto the closest available L1 category. We
propose that Full Copying extends not only to phonological cat-
egories but also to the perceptual normalization processes that
operate on the acoustic signal: if naive listeners copy their L1
categories, they should also copy the normalization behaviour
associated with those categories. This yields the prediction
that the normalization strategy best fitting cross-linguistic per-
ception of the perception or the perception of target language
sounds by listeners who have limited or no knowledge of the
target language will be the one that most closely mirrors the
listener’s L1 perceptual normalization.
Escudero and Chl´adkov´a [18] tested how Peruvian Spanish
listeners with no knowledge of English perceived synthesized
American English (AE) and Standard Southern British English
(SSBE) vowels with F1 and F2 values based on published pro-
duction data. Listeners categorized each English vowel token as
one of the five Spanish monophthongs (/i, e, a, o, u/) in a forced-
choice identification task. Their results showed that Spanish lis-
teners classified the vowels of the two English varieties differ-
ently, and that the classification patterns largely followed acous-
tic proximity in F1-F2 space. The AE categorization data from
this study provide the human baseline against which we evalu-
ate our computational models. The authors predicted that Span-
ish learners of English will indeed show the same patterns of

3. Method
3.1. Data
For L1 Spanish, we used the DIMEx100 corpus [31], which
contains read speech from Mexican Spanish speakers. We ex-
tracted F1 and F2 values at vowel midpoints for the five Span-
ish monophthongs (/i, e, a, o, u/). For L2 American English,
we used the TIMIT corpus [32], extracting F1 and F2 for nine
monophthongal vowels (/i,I,E,æ,A,2,O,U, u/).
3.2. Normalization procedures
Each formant dataset (Spanish and English) was processed un-
der six procedures: (1) raw Hz (no normalization), (2) gender-
wise Z-score, (3) Lobanov, (4) Nearey 1, (5) Nearey 2, and
(6) Gerstman. For methods that require speaker-level statistics
(Lobanov, Nearey 1, Gerstman), the normalization parameters
were computed from each speaker’s own data. For gender-wise
Z, means and standard deviations were computed within each
gender group. For Nearey 2, the grand log-mean was computed
across formants and vowel tokens for each individual speaker.
To simulate cross-linguistic influence of L1 Spanish on L2 En-
glish perception, all five normalization methods applied param-
eters extracted from Spanish samples to normalize English data,
on grounds that a virtual L1 Spanish listener would have access
only to Spanish-derived parameters when normalizing incom-
ing acoustic input.
3.3. Model architecture and L1 Spanish modelling
We trained multi-layer perceptrons (MLPs) with two input neu-
rons corresponding to F1 and F2, two hidden layers of 128 neu-
rons each, and five output neurons corresponding to the five
Spanish vowel categories. All six models shared identical ar-
chitectures and hyperparameters, differing only in the normal-
ization applied to their input data. The models were trained on
the DIMEx100 Spanish vowels with the objective of classifying
tokens into their correct vowel categories. Training continued
until an early stopping criterion halted optimization when clas-
sification accuracy failed to improve for 20 consecutive epochs.
The number of training and test samples for L1 Spanish model-
ing were 123,884 (44 male and 46 female speakers) and 14,091
(5 male and 5 female speakers), respectively.
3.4. Modelling cross-linguistic vowel perception
To simulate how L1 Spanish listeners perceive English vow-
els, each trained model received American English vowel to-
kens (from TIMIT) as input—simulating a naive Spanish lis-
tener encountering English vowels with no prior English expo-
sure, which within the L2LP model framework represents a case
of cross-linguistic or non-native vowel perception. For each En-
glish vowel, the model produced a probability distribution over
the five Spanish categories. These proportions were arranged
into a 5×9 categorization matrix (Spanish categories×English
vowels) for each normalization method. A total of 10,825 to-
kens from the TIMIT test set were used for this simulation; the
training set was not used in this study as it will be used to sim-
ulate an advanced L2 English listener in future work.

5. Discussion
The finding that Lobanov normalization best fits the human
perception data was unexpected. Given that Lobanov applies
per-speaker Z-score standardization—and has been identified
as susceptible to overnormalization [15]—we had anticipated
that it would collapse meaningful acoustic differences between
the Spanish and English vowel systems. Nearey, which applies
a uniform log-scale shift and is generally understood to better
preserve between-group variation, was expected to outperform
it.
We propose that this result is explained by the specific
listener profile being modelled. Our model, an L1 Spanish-
trained MLP classifying English vowels with no prior English
exposure, simulates what the L2LP framework regards as be-
ing cross-linguistic or non-native perception, representing a lis-
tener whose perceptual system is entirely shaped by L1 cate-
gories. In cross-linguistic or non-native perception, the prop-
erty of Lobanov that makes it problematic for other applica-
tions, collapsing between-language acoustic variation, becomes
advantageous, as it effectively simulates a listener who treats
all incoming vowels as belonging to its L1 system. Interpreted
within the L2LP framework, this finding supports a novel ex-
tension of the Full Copying hypothesis: a listener with no prior
knowledge of the target language will use their L1 phonologi-
cal categories, and they will also use their L1 speaker normal-
ization patterns, if in fact they are language-specific. In other
words, Full Copying of L1 categories may extend to Full Copy-
ing of L1 normalization behaviour. Lobanov’s strong fit to the
naive listener data is consistent with this view, since it applies
a single, language-agnostic standardization that mirrors the per-
ceptual state of a listener who has not yet differentiated L1 and
L2 acoustic spaces.
This interpretation generates a testable prediction. An L2
learner (perhaps even from early stages in development) who
has developed an independent L2 phonological grammar no
longer maps L2 input exclusively onto L1 categories but main-
tains distinct representations and exploits acoustic contrasts be-
tween them. For modelling this listener, a method that preserves
cross-linguistic distinctions, such as Nearey, may prove more
appropriate. This account aligns with the L2LP developmen-
tal trajectory [11, 12], progressing from full L1-based percep-
tion (where Lobanov’s collapsing is beneficial) toward differ-
entiated perception (where preserving cross-linguistic contrasts
matters). The choice of normalization should, therefore, be cal-
ibrated to the proficiency level of the listener being simulated.
Furthermore, the L2LP’s Full Access hypothesis predicts that
with sufficient L2 exposure, listeners would develop a sepa-
rate normalization strategy for each language, so that their per-
ception increasingly resembles a language-specific pattern such
as Nearey within each language. Novice learners, by contrast,
would apply a single undifferentiated normalization across lan-
guages, consistent with the Lobanov-like behaviour observed
in the present simulation. Testing this prediction by compar-
ing normalization fit across learner proficiency levels is a key
direction for future work.
Two limitations should be noted. First, the study used only
F1 and F2; additional cues such as F3 or duration may affect rel-
ative method performance. Second, the MLP architecture was
kept deliberately simple, and more complex architectures might
interact differently with normalization.

Spanish listeners’ non-native or cross-linguistic perception of
American English vowels. Lobanov normalization provided the
closest match to human data across all metrics, despite expecta-

able for cross-linguistic modelling. Interpreted through L2LP,
