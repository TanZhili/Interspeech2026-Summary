Abstract
Word duration in spontaneous speech reflects interactions
between lexical access, contextual predictability, and speech
planning. While effects of word length, lexical frequency, and
speaking rate are well established, semantic context is typically
approximated indirectly and modeled linearly. In this study, we
investigate whether semantic relevance, a metric that quantifies
the semantic relatedness between a target word and its local
context, predicts word duration in conversational speech. Using
the Buckeye Corpus, we analyze word-level timing data with
generalized additive mixed models (GAMMs) to capture poten-
tial non-linear effects while accounting for speaker variability
and established linguistic controls. Results show that seman-
tic relevance is a significant predictor of word duration, inde-
pendent of word frequency and length. Exploratory analyses
suggest that its effect may be frequency-dependent. Increased
semantic relevance generally facilitates production, but highly
context-central words exhibit longer durations, particularly for
low-frequency items. These findings demonstrate that seman-
tic relevance captures production-specific contextual effects on
speech timing beyond linear approximations and highlight fun-
damental differences between language production and compre-
hension.

1. Introduction
Word duration in spontaneous speech is shaped by a rich set
of cognitive and linguistic factors. Longer, less frequent, and
phonologically complex words are consistently produced with
greater duration, while higher speaking rates compress articula-
tion [1, 2, 3]. Beyond these lexical and prosodic determinants,
a robust finding across languages and registers is that more
predictablewords are produced with shorter durations [4, 1]:
speakers reduce articulatory effort when the upcoming word is
highly expected, reflecting efficient allocation of planning re-
sources.
Predictability in prior work has been operationalized pri-
marily throughlexical frequencyandn-gram probability, de-
rived from local word transitions [1]. These measures capture
form-based repetition and transitional statistics, but they model
context in a shallow way: they do not encode thesemanticco-
herence between a word and its surrounding discourse. Yet
theories of lexical access in production [5] posit that speakers
navigate a rich semantic network during word retrieval, mean-
ing that the semantic relationship between a target word and
its context should directly modulate retrieval ease, articulatory
preparation, and ultimately word duration. Semantic priming

words that are highly central to a dense semantic neighborhood

ing forces, namely facilitation from semantic overlap and inhi-
bition from lexical competition, imply that the effect of seman-
tic context on duration is inherently non-linear, a possibility that
linear modeling frameworks cannot capture.
While lexical and n-gram measures track surface-level pre-
dictability, they overlook the deeper conceptual relationships
that drive message planning. Recent work has introducedse-
mantic relevance, a metric that quantifies the degree to which a
target word is semantically related to its immediate context us-
ing distributional word embeddings and recency-weighted sim-
ilarity [8]. Critically, semantic relevance has been shown to pre-
dict fixation durations during reading [9, 8, 10], electrophysio-
logical (EEG) responses during discourse comprehension [11]
and and visual attention [12], demonstrating that it captures
contextual facilitation effects beyond lexical frequency. How-
ever, its role in speech production has not been examined, even
though speech production differs from reading in several key
respects: the direction of processing is reversed [13, 14, 15],
planning demands are different, and semantic competition is a
key bottleneck.
This gap motivates the present study. We investigate
whether semantic relevance predicts word duration in sponta-
neous conversational speech, independently of established lex-
ical and prosodic controls, and whether its effect is non-linear
or frequency-dependent. Using the Buckeye Corpus and gener-
alized additive mixed models (GAMMs), which can flexibly re-
cover non-linear smooth functions from large-scale speech data,
we provide the first production-side test of semantic relevance
as a predictor of speech timing. The roadmap of the present
study is illustrated in Figure 1.
2. Methods
2.1. Corpus
The data come from the Buckeye Corpus of Conversational
Speech [16], which contains high-quality recordings of spon-
taneous American English produced by 40 speakers during so-
ciolinguistic interviews. The corpus includes orthographic tran-
scriptions, time-aligned phonetic annotations, and detailed seg-
mental information for approximately 307,000 word tokens.
Although elicited through interviews, the speech is largely
monologic and spontaneous, making it suitable for investigat-
ing natural speech timing.

2.2. Semantic Relevance
Semantic relevance quantifies the degree to which a target word
is semantically supported by its immediately preceding linguis-
tic context. The metric is motivated by well-established cogni-
tive constraints on attention and working memory, which limit
the amount of contextual information that can actively influence
lexical processing at any given moment [18, 19]. Rather than as-
suming uniform access to all prior context, semantic relevance
models contextual influence as graded and distance-dependent.
Evidence from incremental language processing suggests
that recent words exert the strongest influence on ongoing lexi-
cal retrieval, with rapidly diminishing contributions from more
distant context [18, 19]. Accordingly, semantic relevance is
computed over a local context window consisting of the three
words immediately preceding the target word.
Each word is represented using 300-dimensional
fastTextword embeddings [20] pretrained on the
Common Crawl corpus. Static distributional embeddings were
chosen because they capture stable lexical-semantic knowledge
while remaining independent of contextualized prediction
mechanisms. This allows semantic relevance to reflect locally
computed semantic structure rather than information already
encoded by neural language models [9, 10].
Formally, for a target wordw t, semantic relevance is de-
fined as a weighted sum of cosine similarities between the em-
bedding of the target word and those of its preceding context
words:

2.3. Statistical Analysis
To model potential non-linear effects of semantic relevance and
other predictors on word duration, we employed Generalized
Additive Mixed Models (GAMMs). GAMMs extend general-
ized linear mixed models by incorporating smooth functions
that capture non-linear relationships between predictors and the
response variable, while simultaneously accounting for random
effects [21, 22, 23].

tors to assess potential multicollinearity. In the Buckeye Cor-
pus, syllable count shows a strong correlation withWordLength

with all other predictors (|ρ|<0.15), confirming that it cap-
tures an independent dimension of contextual information. To
avoid multicollinearity,syllable countwas excluded from sub-
sequent models. The primary model fitted in this study was:

3. Results
This section focuses on the effect of semantic relevance on word
duration, progressing from its overall contribution to its non-
linear and frequency-dependent properties.
3.1. Model Comparison
We compared five GAMM specifications using AIC (Table 2).
Model M1, which includes semantic relevance alongside stan-
dard control predictors, yielded the lowest AIC, indicating the
best overall fit. Removing semantic relevance from the model
(M4 vs. M1) resulted in a substantial loss of fit (∆AIC = 23.2).
Replacing the separate smooth terms for word length and fre-
quency with a tensor-product interaction (M2) did not im-
prove performance (∆AIC>90 relative to M1). Removing
both semantic relevance and the tensor-product interaction (M3)
yielded a further decline, and the most reduced model (M5, ex-
cluding both semantic relevance and log frequency) performed
substantially worse.
3.2. Overall Effect of Semantic Relevance
In the optimal model (M1), the smooth term for semantic rele-
vance is highly significant (p <0.001), indicating that the se-
mantic relationship between a word and its context contributes
independently to speech timing, above and beyond word length,
lexical frequency, phrase rate, and phonological deletions.
3.3. Non-linear Effect of Semantic Relevance
Beyond its overall significance, semantic relevance exhibits a
clearly non-linear relationship with word duration (Figure 3).
At low to moderate levels of semantic relevance, increases are
associated with shorter word durations, consistent with contex-
tual facilitation. However, at higher levels of semantic rele-
vance, the effect reverses, with word duration increasing as se-
mantic relevance continues to rise.

3.4. Frequency-dependent Modulation
3.4. Frequency-dependent Modulation
As an exploratory follow-up, we stratified the data by lexi-
cal frequency and fitted an additional interaction model to as-
sess whether the effect of semantic relevance varied across
the frequency spectrum. The effect of semantic relevance
was stronger for low-frequency words: semantic relevance
showed a robust effect for low-frequency words (F= 18.90,
p <0.0001), whereas it was absent for high-frequency words
(F= 1.29,p= 0.256). When lexical frequency was excluded
from the model entirely, the effect of semantic relevance be-
came substantially stronger (F= 646.35,p <2×10 −16),
and an exploratory GAMM including a tensor-product smooth
te(LogFreq, SemRel)indicated a frequency×semantic
relevance interaction (F= 3622.26,p <2×10 −16). These
supplementary analyses suggest that semantic relevance may
play a greater role in shaping speech timing when lexical ac-
cess is less automatized, consistent with accounts in which low-
frequency words rely more heavily on contextual support during
production.
4. Discussion
The present study demonstrates that semantic relevance signif-
icantly shapes word duration in spontaneous speech, indepen-
dently of established lexical and prosodic predictors, as sum-
marized in Fig. 1. Three main findings emerge: a non-linear
production profile that diverges from comprehension, a strong
frequency-dependent modulation, and methodological implica-
tions for modeling speech timing.
Non-linearity and production–comprehension asymme-
try.The U-shaped effect of semantic relevance on word dura-
tion, with facilitation at low-to-moderate levels and lengthen-
ing at high levels, contrasts with the monotonic facilitation re-
ported for reading. Using the same weighted cosine-similarity
metric, [9] and [8] show that in both Chinese and English,
greater semantic relevance consistently shortens fixation dura-
tions, with effects increasing from first-pass to total fixation
time. This monotonic pattern generalizes across 13 languages
in the MECO reading corpus [10], with no reversal at high co-
herence in any language or fixation measure.
The divergence is theoretically informative. In compre-
hension, high semantic coherence reduces processing load
by constraining interpretation [24, 25]. In production, the
same contextual centrality may increase selection costs. The
WEA VER++ model [5] predicts that highly relevant words ac-
tivate dense semantic neighborhoods and increase lemma com-
petition, a prediction consistent with cumulative semantic in-
hibition effects in picture naming, where naming latencies in-
crease as more semantically related items have been produced
[7]. Production and comprehension thus appear to rely on
a shared representational substrate but exploit it in opposing
ways, challenging strict parity models [5]. The reversal at high
semantic relevance in speech, absent in reading, likely reflects
production-specific costs arising from increased lexical compe-
tition, message-level planning, and self-monitoring demands for
context-central words.
Frequency moderation and differential processing
routes.Semantic relevance predicts duration for low-frequency
words (F= 18.90,p <0.0001) but not for high-frequency
words (F= 1.29,p= 0.256). This pattern is consistent
with accounts suggesting that high-frequency words are ac-
cessed via automatized retrieval routines that are relatively in-
sensitive to semantic context, whereas low-frequency words de-
pend more heavily on context-driven computation during pro-
duction [1, 26]. By contrast, the reading literature shows no
comparable frequency moderation of semantic relevance effects
[8], which reinforces the asymmetry between production and
comprehension. Semantic integration appears to be broadly en-
gaged in comprehension but more selective in production, op-
erating most strongly when lexical access is least automatized.
These conditional effects are difficult to detect with linear mod-
els, making GAMMs and frequency-stratified subset analyses
essential for recovering them [8, 9].
In sum, semantic relevance is a cognitively grounded
predictor of word duration whose non-linear and frequency-
dependent profile reveals how semantic context shapes produc-
tion through competing mechanisms, including lexical facili-
tation at moderate levels, semantic competition and articulatory
monitoring at high levels, and differential automatization across
the frequency spectrum. These findings establish a productive
dialogue between speech production and reading research, sug-
gesting that the semantic dimension of contextual processing is
shared across modalities but operates through qualitatively dif-
ferent mechanisms depending on the direction of language use.
5. Conclusion
This study shows that semantic relevance reliably predicts word
duration in spontaneous speech. Its effect is non-linear, with fa-
cilitation at moderate levels but lengthening for highly context-
central words, especially low-frequency items. This U-shaped
profile contrasts with the monotonic facilitation observed in
comprehension, suggesting a production-comprehension asym-
metry driven by incremental planning and lexical competition.
The findings also suggest that speech-generating AI models
based mainly on next-token prediction may miss production-
specific timing dynamics, and that cognitively grounded pre-
dictors such as semantic relevance could improve models of
human-like speech timing.
