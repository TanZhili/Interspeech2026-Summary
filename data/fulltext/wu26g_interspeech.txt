Abstract
While autoregressive (AR) models dominate end-to-end speech
recognition for their accuracy, their sequential nature limits
inference efficiency. Non-autoregressive (NAR) models offer
faster parallel decoding but often at the cost of recognition qual-
ity. In this paper, we propose Semi-Autoregressive Speculative
Decoding (SASD), an algorithm that bridges this gap by lever-
aging NAR models for high-confidence tokens and AR models
for low-confidence ones. Built upon the joint CTC-attention
framework, SASD uses CTC greedy search for initialization
and selectively refines “harder” tokens through the attention de-
coder. Experiments on AISHELL-1, WenetSpeech, and indus-
trial datasets demonstrate that SASD achieves Character Error
Rates (CER) comparable to state-of-the-art attention-rescoring
while providing a 2.8x to 3.5x speedup across various pretrained
models.

1. Introduction
End-to-end (E2E) models have achieved remarkable success
in automatic speech recognition (ASR) [1], with three pri-
mary approaches: connectionist temporal classification (CTC)
[2], recurrent neural network transducer (RNN-T) [3], and
attention-based encoder-decoder (AED) [4]. While AED mod-
els are dominant due to their superior accuracy, their autore-
gressive (AR) nature—generating tokens step-by-step—limits
parallelism and impacts inference efficiency in large-scale sys-
tems.
To accelerate inference, non-autoregressive (NAR) models
generate sequences in parallel. Prevailing iterative NARs [5, 6]
approach decoding as a masked language modeling task, refin-
ing predictions over multiple iterations. However, competitive
performance still requires several passes, constraining practi-
cal speed. Single-step NAR models [7, 8, 9] eliminate tem-
poral dependencies via acoustic representations or Continuous
Integrate-and-Fire (CIF) mechanisms [10]. Despite progress in
models like Paraformer [7] and E-Paraformer [11], these often
struggle with semantic modeling due to incomplete alignment
or the inherent difficulty of single-pass length prediction.
Recently, speculative decoding [12] has emerged as an ef-
fective paradigm for acceleration by running large models in
parallel on the outputs of smaller models. Several recent stud-
ies have adapted speculative decoding for speech recognition.
For instance, some approaches utilize parallel masked decoding
[13], draft-and-verify strategies for LLM-based ASR [14], or
audio-prefixed low-rank adaptations [15]. While these methods

typically rely on separate drafting models or multiple inference
passes, our proposed SASD offers a streamlined alternative. In
SASD, the fast but less accurate CTC greedy search acts as the
“drafting” mechanism, while the more accurate but slower at-
tention decoder within the same model selectively “verifies”
and corrects only the low-confidence tokens. While sharing a
similar motivation with partially autoregressive (PAR) inference
[16], SASD operates directly at the token level using CTC’s
peaky distribution rather than complex masked-span regenera-
tion. This simpler refinement design ensures high compatibility
with chunk-based streaming decoding and keeps latency much
closer to pure CTC greedy search. This approach yields three
advantages: (1) selective calculation of attention scores for ef-
ficiency, (2) avoidance of complex CTC-prefix scoring bottle-
necks, and (3) seamless integration with existing AR models
without retraining.

2.2. AED decoding
For decoding, there are five methods in Figure 1: (1) CTC
greedy search, (2) CTC prefix beam search, (3) Attention beam
search, (4) Attention rescoring, and (5) Hybrid CTC/attention
decoding.
These methods are effectively implemented in two popu-
lar ASR toolkits: WENET [17] and ESPNET[18]. In our ex-
amination of WENET’s decoding methods, several key obser-
vations emerged. The CTC greedy search, while offering the
best RTF, yields the lowest performance in terms of recognition
accuracy. CTC prefix beam search performs similarly to CTC
greedy search but does not support batch processing, limiting
its efficiency. Attention beam search outperforms CTC prefix
beam search in terms of accuracy but requires autoregressive
processing, which becomes less effective with longer output se-
quences. Although the attention rescoring strategy can paral-
lelize rescoring of one-pass results, it first necessitates perform-
ing CTC prefix beam search. Another common approach is the
traditional hybrid CTC/attention decoding strategy. However,
the exact computation of the CTC prefix score in this strategy
often becomes a bottleneck due to its complex computations
and increased demands with longer utterances.

The SASD algorithm is described in detail in Algorithm 1

•CTC greedy searchWe use CTC greedy search to obtain an
initial hypotheses sequence. The speed of CTC greedy search
ensures fast decoding. It should be noted that no beam search
is performed in this step.
•Generate confidence maskDue to the peaky distribution of
CTC probabilities, we use these probabilities as a confidence
threshold to generate a confidence mask. This mask is then
used to guide the selection of tokens for the next attention
computation.
•Calculate CTC token probabilityState-of-the-art results
generally involve interpolating attention scores and CTC
scores. However, conventional methods require either com-
puting the precise CTC prefix score or performing CTC pre-
fix beam search. These complex computations can disrupt
GPU parallel processing and become a bottleneck. In con-
trast, the SASD algorithm calculates the mean probability of
duplicate tokens during greedy search, eliminating the need
for this computation during beam search.
•Speculative beam searchBased on the confidence mask re-
sults, for low-confidence tokens, we rescore the correspond-
ing segment of the sequence by generating new hypothe-
ses using the autoregressive attention decoder. This gener-
ates a list of topkvalues and their corresponding IDs. The
attention-based scores are then interpolated with CTC scores
to predict the final token. High-confidence tokens are directly
predicted using the CTC greedy search results. Importantly,
because the attention decoder only replaces low-confidence
tokens at their specific index positionsiwithout altering the
overall sequence structure, the final output length remains
strictly identical to the initial CTC greedy search hypothesis.
•Choose best resultsSelect the best token sequences based
on the maximum score from the beam search.

We conducted experiments using two Chinese datasets:
AISHELL-1 [20] and WenetSpeech [21]. AISHELL-1 has
7,176 utterances, totaling about 5 hours. WenetSpeech includes
TestNet (23 hours of internet speech) and TestMeeting (15 hours
of far-field, conversational, and meeting speech).

3.3.2. RTF
In the Paraformer paper [7], the open-sourced model trained on
AISHELL-1 has a model size of approximately 185M. To make
a fair comparison, we selected the U2++Conformer to test RTF
performance.
The CPU and GPU RTF results are listed in Table 3 and
Table 4. It is important to note that the RTFs of existing
NAR models are cited from their original papers, which were
evaluated under different hardware environments and thread
settings. Therefore, a strict numerical comparison is cur-
rently not feasible. For GPU RTF, the SASD algorithm is

slower than CTC greedy search but 2.8 times faster than atten-
tion rescoring. With the SASD algorithm, we maintain perfor-
mance and achieve an RTF of 0.0188. For CPU RTF, the SASD
algorithm achieves a value of 0.0270, which is comparable to
CTC greedy search. It offers a 2.1 times speedup compared to
attention rescoring. Although the model size and structure differ
from published results, the theoretical efficiency gains can still
be observed. The CTC prefix beam search requires intensive se-
quential merging that severely disrupts parallelism in attention
rescoring, which explains why the RTF of attention rescoring
on a GPU (0.0535) shows nominal improvement over a CPU
(0.0576). Although SASD involves calculating the autoregres-
sive decoder as in attention mechanisms, it achieves an RTF
similar to that of CTC greedy search.
Compared to GPU-based evaluations of other NAR mod-
els at a batch size of 1 (Table 4), Paraformer, E-Paraformer,
and TSNAT report extremely low RTFs of 0.0168, 0.0069,
and 0.0185 respectively. However, their single-pass generation
nature restricts their recognition accuracy, resulting in higher
CERs of 5.20%, 4.79%, and 5.60%. In contrast, our proposed
SASD achieves a highly competitive CER of 4.73% while main-
taining a fast RTF of 0.0188. SASD employs the attention
decoder to speculatively revise low-confidence tokens dynam-
ically. Because the vast majority of tokens are already highly
confident from the initial CTC greedy search (as shown in Fig-
ure 3), SASD avoids significant computational bottlenecks and
keeps latency comparable to pure NAR models.
Furthermore, to fully leverage GPU parallelism and bench-
mark against non-autoregressive models in large-batch infer-
ence scenarios, we evaluated SASD with a batch size of 8. The
results are shown in Table 5. We compare our method against
competitive NAR models evaluated in similar high-throughput
settings: CTC-Enhanced and Paraformer. While Paraformer
and CTC-Enhanced achieve RTFs of 0.0026 and 0.0037 respec-
tively in literature, their CERs remain at 5.20% and 5.90%.
Conversely, SASD successfully bridges the precision of AR
models and the throughput speed of NAR models, maintaining
its superior CER of 4.73% while achieving a fast RTF of 0.0098
on our hardware.

4. Conclusions
This paper introduces the SASD algorithm, designed to lever-
age the strengths of both AR and NAR end-to-end ASR sys-
tems. During training, the model is trained as an AR model
to ensure high performance. For decoding, SASD uses NAR
models for “easier” tokens and AR models for “harder” tokens,
combining the benefits of both. This approach eliminates the
need for CTC prefix scoring and CTC beam search. Experi-
ments with five public pretrained models and three test datasets
demonstrate that SASD achieves comparable CER performance
while maintaining high inference speed.
