Abstract
Recent Korean TTS studies often use direct grapheme input due
to the phonetic transparency of the Korean alphabet, Hangul.
However, Korean orthography is also morphophonemic, and
grapheme sequences do not always correspond to the actual pro-
nunciation. Since the mapping between graphemes, phonemes,
and allophones is almost deterministic in the Standard Ko-
rean Pronunciation, we propose Selective Allophone-Level To-
kenization (SALT), which incorporates a selected set of allo-
phonic phenomena into the input rather than relying on im-
plicit learning by the TTS model. In experiments fine-tuning
a pre-trained model, SALT-N, which improves token distribu-
tional balance, outperformed baselines. In a 12.75-hour dataset,
SALT-N obtained a CER of 3.30% and achieved the highest
NMOS, whereas grapheme input resulted in a CER of 4.74%.
In an extremely low-resource 1-hour setting, SALT-N was the
only method to achieve a CER below 10% (8.19%), whereas all
baseline models suffered from severe degradation.
Index Terms: text-to-speech, allophone, text processing, Ko-
rean, TTS, SALT
1. Introduction
Text-to-speech (TTS) synthesis has recently advanced through
data-driven methods trained on large-scale datasets [1, 2]. No-
tably, many modern architectures achieve high synthesis quality
without grapheme-to-phoneme (G2P) conversions [3, 4, 5, 6, 7].
Recent Korean TTS studies often follow this trend. They use
raw grapheme (jamo) inputs to avoid cascading errors from G2P
modules. This is possible because the phonetic nature of the
Korean alphabet allows models to learn pronunciation implic-
itly [8, 9]. However, while speech resources for English and
Chinese are generally abundant, Korean remains comparatively
under-resourced [10].
This lack of data limits the effectiveness of implicit learn-
ing. Models need sufficient exposure to diverse morphophone-
mic contexts. Furthermore, modern Korean orthography creates
a mismatch between written forms and actual pronunciation.
In low-resource settings, this ambiguity is hard to resolve from
data alone. As a result, it can disrupt training.
G2P conversions explicitly address the orthography–
pronunciation mismatch by mapping written forms to phoneme
sequences. However, phonemes are still abstract units that can-
not fully capture detailed acoustic variations [11]. Due to the
one-to-many correspondence between a single phoneme and
multiple allophones, phoneme-level inputs still leave residual

phoneme inputs as baselines, allowing us to evaluate how effec-

Previous Korean automatic speech recognition (ASR) and
pronunciation assessment studies have improved performance
by modeling allophonic variations [13, 14, 15, 12]. However,
indiscriminately incorporating all possible allophones substan-
tially increases the vocabulary size and produces very high-
or low-frequency tokens. This expansion disrupts the statisti-
cal balance of the token distribution and degrades learnabil-
ity [16, 17].
To balance phonetic precision with token efficiency, we in-
troduce Selective Allophone-Level Tokenization (SALT). SALT
integrates a targeted subset of allophones into the input, serving
as an effective linguistic inductive bias without suffering from
severe data sparsity.
2. Related works
2.1. Explicit surface-level features in speech processing
Some languages have complex orthography-to-sound map-
pings. For these, implicit learning from graphemes or even
phonemes is often insufficient. For instance, in Japanese, a
pitch-accent language, accents can change meaning (e.g.,/ame/
can mean candy or rain). Consequently, Japanese TTS systems
typically rely on surface-level labels. These labels serve as a
linguistic inductive bias [18, 19, 20].
However, Korean is not a pitch-accent language. Lexical
ambiguity is rarely resolved by prosodic features in contempo-
rary spoken Korean pronunciation. Instead, the acoustic ambi-
guity from morphophonemic orthography and allophones is the
main issue in Korean [11].
For this reason, Korean speech processing research actively
models these allophones. Prior studies in ASR and pronuncia-
tion assessment utilize allophones. This approach improves ac-
curacy [13, 14, 15, 12]. We extend this success to speech syn-
thesis. We propose applying allophone-level tags as an inductive
bias for Korean TTS.
2.2. The trade-off between disambiguation and balance
Finding the optimal vocabulary size has been studied in neural
machine translation. Previous studies show that increasing vo-
cabulary size leads to a skewed token distribution, producing
very high- or low-frequency tokens. This imbalance degrades
model performance [16, 17]. Similarly, in Korean TTS, apply-
ing all possible allophonic rules may cause the same problem.

3. SALT
In this section, we propose Selective Allophone-Level To-
kenization (SALT) as a method for addressing both the
orthography–pronunciation mismatch in Korean and the token
distribution efficiency issue discussed above.
3.1. Explicit allophone tagging
SALT departs from the conventional approach of decomposing
text into grapheme or phoneme units. Instead, we attach special
tags to phonemes that indicate their allophonic realization con-
text. Rather than relying on the model to infer these phonetic
rules implicitly, we inject linguistic priors as an explicit induc-
tive bias.
We designed three tags. They represent the five major allo-
phone phenomena shown to improve ASR performance [14].
3.1.1. The onset rules (V , P)

initial positions and as voiced in medial positions. To specify
this acoustic distinction, we attach the i tag to word-initial lax
consonants. Although Korean lax consonants are underlyingly

3.2. Balancing resolution and efficiency
If all allophone rules are applied to tokenization (SALT-VCP:
attaching i, c, and j), acoustic resolution is maximized. How-
ever, each phoneme may split into up to three tokens (e.g.,ㄱ,
ㄱi,ㄱ c), expanding the vocabulary and producing many low-
frequency tokens. This lowers R ´enyi efficiency and degrades
performance.
To find the optimal balance between resolution and statisti-
cal efficiency, we analyzed the vocabulary size, Gini coefficient,
and R´enyi efficiency of various allophone tag combinations on
the KSS dataset.
As shown in Table 3, grapheme and phoneme methods ex-
hibit low R ´enyi efficiency on the 12.75h KSS dataset. This is
because data is heavily concentrated on specific tokens (e.g.,
‘ㅇ’ and ‘ㄴ’ without distinguishing onset from coda). This
concentration reflects the real phoneme distribution of Korean
speech: comparing the token distribution of the 12.75h KSS
dataset with the general Korean frequency statistics from a prior
study [22], we found remarkably high correlation scores (Pear-
sonr= 0.97, Spearmanρ= 0.97,p<0.001). By applying all
tags, as described above, the SALT-VCP method mitigates this
imbalance, but because the vocabulary expands to 56 tokens,
R´enyi efficiency does not increase sufficiently.
We therefore explored selective tagging combinations that
maximize efficiency while minimizing vocabulary expansion.

into at most two variants. Among them, SALT-NL and SALT-

3.3. SALT-N
In Korean spontaneous speech, nasal codas (ㅁ,ㄴ,ㅇ) account
for 72.5% of all codas [11]. This concentration causes simple
grapheme- or phoneme-level tokenization to produce heavily
imbalanced distributions. SALT-N addresses this by selectively
separating only nasal codas from their onset counterparts, de-
composing only the most frequent tokens in the corpus. This
minimizes vocabulary expansion while maximizing R ´enyi effi-
ciency.
4. Experiments and results
4.1. Experimental setup
4.1.1. Dataset and preprocessing
For the experiment, we used the KSS (Korean Single Speaker)
dataset, a high-quality Korean speech corpus. Among the to-
tal 12.86 hours of data, we randomly extracted 100 sentences
and split them into 50 validation and 50 test samples. The re-
maining 12.75 hours of data were used for training. Addition-
ally, to check if the model can learn effectively even in an ex-
tremely low-resource environment, we randomly sampled a re-
duced training set of only 1 hour.
In the text preprocessing, we normalized text input by ap-
plying the N2gk+ [9] module that converts numbers, words
written in the Latin alphabet, and symbols into Hangul notation.
After that, we applied G2P conversion to obtain a sequence of
phonemes from the text input. To this sequence of phonemes,
we applied SALT to generate a sequence of allophones as the
final token sequence, including allophone tags. The speech data
was downsampled to 24kHz and used for training.
4.1.2. Model architecture and training details
We adopted F5-TTS, a flow matching-based non-autoregressive
model. Using PEFT-TTS [23], we fine-tuned the F5-TTS model
pre-trained on English and Mandarin datasets [6]. We fully fine-
tuned the text embedding module and text encoder (ConvNeXt
V2) so the model can learn the newly defined Korean allophone
vocabulary. For the remaining modules, we applied LoRA. We
set the rank of the prompt adapter to 64, and the rank of the DiT
LoRA adapter to 16 [23].
Training was conducted on two NVIDIA A100 (80GB)
GPUs with a batch size of 19,200 frames per GPU. We used
AdamW (lr = 1e-5). It took 33–36 hours per model for a total
of 150K training steps. To synthesize the waveforms from the
generated mel spectrograms, we used V ocos [24].
As demonstrated in the original F5-TTS paper, models
trained on small single-speaker datasets often exhibit overfitting
during training [6]. To account for varying convergence rates
among tokenization strategies and to ensure a fair comparison
of their peak performance, we monitored the CER on the valida-
tion set at 10K-step intervals. For each model, we measured the
objective metrics on the test set using the optimal checkpoint.
4.1.3. Evaluation metrics
We evaluated the synthesized speech using objective and sub-
jective metrics. After transcribing the synthesized speech using
Whisper-Large-v3 [25], we measured the Character Error Rate
(CER) and Word Error Rate (WER) with the ground truth text.
For speech quality, we measured the objective acoustic natural-
ness using UTMOS [26]. Additionally, we assessed how well
the speaker timbre from the KSS dataset is preserved using the
cosine similarity (SIM) between speaker embeddings (reference
eref and generatede gen) extracted via WavLM [27]:

For subjective naturalness, we conducted a Naturalness
Mean Opinion Score (NMOS) test. Twenty-seven native Ko-

the test set per model on a 5-point scale. Based on our ob-
jective findings, we selected five key models for the NMOS
test: the reference (ground truth), two baselines, SALT-VCP,
and SALT-N. To verify the statistical significance of the results,
we performed a one-way repeated-measures analysis of vari-
ance (ANOV A) with Greenhouse-Geisser correction, followed
by post-hoc Holm-Bonferronit-tests [28].
4.2. 12.75h dataset results
On the KSS 12.75-hour dataset, SALT-N outperformed all base-
lines (Table 4). The grapheme method obtained a CER of
4.74%. Phoneme-level input reduced this error to 3.74%. Fi-
nally, SALT-N achieved the lowest CER of 3.30% and a WER
of 11.24%. These results confirm that resolving phonemic and
allophonic ambiguities improves model performance. However,
the SALT-VCP method, incorporating all major allophone phe-
nomena with the largest vocabulary size, obtained a CER of
6.03%, falling behind even the grapheme baseline.
Regarding naturalness, SALT-VCP achieved a slightly
higher UTMOS (3.00) than SALT-N (2.96). However, as shown
in Table 5, in NMOS evaluation, evaluators rated the naturalness

4.3. Extremely low-resource environment
In addition, we tested SALT under an extremely low-resource
setting. We used a 1-hour subset of the KSS dataset. All train-
ing configurations remained identical, except for the use of a
single A100 GPU. During training, we monitored the validation
CER (Figure 1). For the final evaluation, we compared SALT-N
against the grapheme, phoneme, and SALT-VCP (Table 6). The
baselines failed to drop below a 10% CER. SALT-N success-
fully broke this barrier, achieving the lowest CER of 8.19%.
This performance gap is larger than in the 12.75h setting.
This confirms that 1 hour of data is insufficient for the
model to infer Korean morphophonemic mappings or resolve
allophonic ambiguities. More importantly, although SALT-VCP
provides the maximum number of explicit phonetic hints, its
expanded vocabulary exacerbates data sparsity. In such an ex-
tremely low-resource setting, the model fails to observe these

By explicitly resolving only the most critical acoustic am-
biguities—such as the nasal codas—while keeping the vocab-
ulary compact, SALT-N provides the essential linguistic induc-

ultimately demonstrate that, in extremely low-resource settings,
strategically balancing acoustic resolution and statistical effi-
ciency is the key to preventing performance degradation.
5. Conclusion
We proposed Selective Allophone-Level Tokenization (SALT)
to address the pronunciation error problem in Korean TTS. By
injecting linguistic priors as an explicit inductive bias, SALT re-
solves the acoustic ambiguity inherent in grapheme or phoneme
sequences. SALT-N optimizes the token distribution’s statisti-
cal balance (R´enyi efficiency) while minimizing vocabulary ex-
pansion, reducing CER from 4.74% (grapheme) to 3.30% and
achieving the highest NMOS on the 12.75h KSS dataset. Fur-
thermore, even under an extremely low-resource 1-hour condi-
tion, SALT-N maintained a CER of 8.19%, preventing the se-
vere performance degradation observed in the baseline models.
These results demonstrate that efficient input representa-
tion design alone—without architectural changes or additional
data—can substantially improve TTS performance by encoding
language-specific phonological and phonetic knowledge as an
inductive bias. This stands in contrast to the prevailing trend of
relying on large-scale data for implicit learning.
Our experiments are limited to a single-speaker, small-scale
dataset (KSS). Future work will apply SALT to large-scale
multi-speaker corpora such as CoreaSpeech [9], and investigate
its applicability to other languages with complex but determin-
istic phonological and phonetic rules.
