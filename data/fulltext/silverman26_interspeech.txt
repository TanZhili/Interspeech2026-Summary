Abstract
Self-supervised learning (SSL) has emerged as a powerful
paradigm for improving downstream performance when anno-
tated data is limited. However, most existing audio SSL meth-
ods process single-channel inputs and focus on learning spec-
tral representations, which are often insufficient for spatially
sensitive tasks like direction-of-arrival or reverberation estima-
tion. In this work, we propose a self-supervised framework
for binaural audio that integrates spatial priors directly into the
pre-training objective. We introduce the Soft Acoustic Con-
trastive (SAC) loss, a geometric objective that structures the
latent space by aligning embeddings based on shared acoustic
properties, such as directionality and diffuseness, without re-
quiring ground-truth labels. Experimental results demonstrate
that our method consistently outperforms baselines across five
downstream spatial parameter estimation tasks. 1
Index Terms: self-supervised learning, representation learning,
acoustic parameter estimation
1. Introduction
The ability to analyze and synthesize spatial acoustic envi-
ronments is fundamental for emerging technologies, from
immersive AR/VR to autonomous robotics and hearing aids.
Central to these applications is the estimation of room acoustic
parameters, such as Time Difference of Arrival (TDOA), re-
verberation timeT 60, and Direct-to-Reverberant Ratio (DRR),
which characterize the geometry of the auditory scene and are
critical for robust downstream spatial processing [1].
Current supervised methods often extract these parameters
from measured room impulse responses (RIRs) [2]. However,
because true RIR capture is intrusive, alternative blind estima-
tion techniques operate directly on audio recordings, utilizing
Convolutional Recurrent Neural Networks (CRNNs) for local-
ization [3] or supervised contrastive paradigms to encode acous-
tic parameters [4] and joint RIR embeddings [5]. However,
these models rely heavily on massive, labeled datasets. Acquir-
ing precise spatial annotations is expensive, creating a bottle-
neck that limits generalization to diverse real-world conditions.
To address data scarcity, self-supervised learning (SSL)
has gained prominence by leveraging unlabeled audio across
diverse paradigms. For instance, generative [6] and discrim-
inative [7, 8, 9, 10] frameworks predict masked acoustic
content, while contrastive [11] and bootstrapping [12] methods
maximize agreement between augmented views. However,
these models are designed for single-channel recordings and
their objectives are often ill-suited for spatial audio, prioritizing

semantic content while treating reverberation and channel

Recent works have begun to bridge this gap. Jiang et al.
[13] investigated contrastive learning for spatial sound represen-
tation through data augmentations like channel swapping and
time stretching. While effective for localization, such augmen-
tations risk distorting the subtle spatial cues required for fine-
grained room parameter estimation. Spatial HuBERT [14] and
feature distillation frameworks [15] adapted self-supervision for
multi-channel audio, though the latter typically requires isolated
clean speech. Notably, Yang and Li [16] introduced a Cross-
Channel Signal Reconstruction (CCSR) pretext task to learn
dependencies without labels. However, implicitly relying on
reconstruction may not fully isolate physical room properties.
In this work, we propose a novel self-supervised framework
for binaural audio that integrates classical spatial priors directly
into the representation learning process. We introduce the
Soft Acoustic Contrastive loss (L SAC), a geometric alignment
objective inspired by soft contrastive learning frameworks
[17, 18]. Crucially, our approach operates without any ground-
truth labels, access to isolated source signals, or metadata
regarding room properties or source positions. Instead, we
utilize analytical acoustic features derived solely from the input
signal to serve as soft similarity targets. This objective aligns
the encoder’s latent geometry with the physical properties
underlying downstream tasks, effectively decoupling metric
learning from signal reconstruction. Experimental results
demonstrate that this physics-guided approach yields superior
performance on spatial estimation tasks compared to supervised
and SSL baselines.
Our main contributions can be summarized as follows:
• We introduce a geometry-aware regularization for binaural
SSL using a soft acoustic-guided contrastive objective.
• Our approach uses only unlabeled data, requires no metadata,
and can integrate seamlessly with other SSL objectives.
• We show consistent improvements across spatial parameter
estimation tasks under linear and fine-tuning protocols.

2.2. Model Architecture
Our framework utilizes a dual-stream Multi-Channel Con-
former (MC-Conformer) [16] designed to capture both spatial
and spectral dependencies (see Fig. 1a). The input audioXis
processed by two parallel branches: a spatial encoder, produc-
ing a spatial embeddinge spat(X), and a spectral encoder, pro-
ducing a spectral embeddinge spec(X). The two representations
are concatenated and fed into a shared decoderd(·)that outputs
a reconstructed signal ˆX, which is optimized against the input
Xvia a Mean Squared Error (MSE) reconstruction loss.
To force a distinction between the representations learned
by each encoder, [16] suggested a masking strategy. Specifi-
cally, random time frames are masked in the first audio channel.
For the spatial encoder, the identical time frames are masked in
the second channel, forcing the branch to capture inter-channel
spatial differences to reconstruct the signal. Conversely, for
the spectral encoder, an inverse mask is applied to the second
channel, encouraging the network to rely on global spectral
context. Accordingly, the MSE reconstruction loss is computed
exclusively over the time frames masked in the first channel.
While the decoder is optimized for signal restoration, we
explicitly enforce a geometric structure within the spatial en-
coder’s latent space by attaching a parallel projection headg(·)
(detailed in Section 3.2) to the output of the spatial encoder
espat(·). This module maps the encoder representation to a la-
tent vectorz=g(e spat(X)), which is optimized specifically for
geometric alignment, effectively decoupling the metric learning
objective from the signal reconstruction task.
For the downstream phase, the spectral encoder, projection
head, and decoder are removed, and a linear prediction head
f(·)is attached to the spatial encoder (see Fig. 1b), which is
initialized with the learned pre-training weights to output the
final spatial parameter estimates.

3. Experiments
We evaluate the performance of the proposed method for the
blind estimation of spatial acoustic parameters from multi-
channel recordings. We follow the standard SSL protocol con-
sisting of a pre-training phase on large-scale unlabeled data, fol-
lowed by a downstream fine-tuning phase on a smaller labeled
set to estimate the spatial parameters described in Section 3.3.
3.1. Datasets
We constructed a spatial audio dataset by convolving clean
speech from the Wall Street Journal (WSJ) corpus [23] with
simulated RIRs. Following [16], room dimensions were
sampled uniformly from 3 m×3 m×2.5 m to 15 m×10 m×6 m,
withT 60 drawn from [0.2, 1.0] s. The receiver was modeled as
a two-channel array with 3 cm to 20 cm microphone spacing,
positioned within[20%,20%,10%]to[80%,80%,50%]of the
room volume. Sources were placed randomly with a minimum
distance of 30 cm from the array and all room surfaces.
Spatially diffuse white noise was added with Signal-to-Noise
Ratios (SNR) sampled from [15, 30] dB. The pre-training
dataset comprises 50,000 training samples, alongside 4,000
validation and 4,000 test samples (for visualization only).
For the downstream phase, we generated train, validation,
and test sets comprising 8, 20, and 20 room configurations, re-
spectively. Room configurations do not overlap across the three
splits and are completely separate from the pre-training data.
From each room, we sampled 100, 50, and 200 utterances to
construct the corresponding splits. For the downstream tasks,
we perform 4 independent training trials using different seeds
and report average performance.
3.2. Model and Training Settings
The spectral encoder (Dspec = 512) consists of a single Con-
former block [24], while the spatial encoder (D spat = 256)
is composed of three blocks. All Conformer layers utilize 4
attention heads, a kernel size of 31, and an expansion factor
of 4 [16]. Our projection head consists of a Linear layer
(Dspat →D spat), Batch Normalization, ReLU, and a final
Linear projection (D spat →D proj), withD proj = 128.
The model contains approximately 17.6M parameters and
was trained on a single NVIDIA RTX 6000 Ada GPU, with
self-supervised pre-training taking roughly 4.5 hours and
downstream fine-tuning requiring about 3 hours per task.
Input Representation: Audio signals were resampled to
16 kHz and cut to a fixed duration of 4 s. We computed the
STFT using a 32 ms Hanning window with a 16 ms frame shift,
resulting inF= 256frequency bins andT= 256frames.
Following [16], the input spectrograms were normalized by the
mean magnitude of the reference microphone.
Pre-training: We trained the network for 30 epochs with
a batch size of 128, using Adam optimizer with an initial
learning rate of1e −3 following a cosine-decay schedule. We
setτ= 0.15andσ= 1.0based on downstream validation set
performance. We adopt a 50% masking rate for CCSR.

3.3. Evaluation
As in [16], we evaluate the model on five spatial parameter
estimation tasks: TDOA, DRR,T 60, Clarity Index (C 50), and
Mean Absorption Coefficient (ABS). For all tasks, we report the
Mean Absolute Error (MAE) between the estimated values and
the ground truth. We evaluate the models under two standard
protocols: Linear Evaluation, where the pre-trained encoder is
frozen to assess the intrinsic quality of the learned representa-
tions, and Full Fine-Tuning, which measures the model’s maxi-
mum adaptation capacity. We compare our performance against
CCSR [16]. In addition, we evaluate several variants of our pro-
posed method, including: using standard unmasked MSE recon-
struction loss (‘AE’), using masked CCSR objective, and train-
ing exclusively withL SAC. Finally, we include a ’No Pretrain’
baseline, which is trained from scratch on the downstream task
set without utilizing the pre-training data.
3.4. Results
Results are summarized in Table 1. It can be seen that incor-
poratingL SAC consistently improves upon the CCSR baseline
across all five spatial tasks under both evaluation protocols.
Furthermore, combiningL SAC with masked CCSR outperforms
the unmasked AE variant in almost all geometric parameters,
confirming that masking enhances robustness. The standalone
LSAC variant achieves lower error rates than the CCSR baseline
across all metrics. However, CCSR+L SAC is almost always
preferable, especially with linear evaluation, implying the
contribution of the masked reconstruction loss for improving
representation quality. Comparing these to the ’No Pretrain’
baseline confirms self-supervised pre-training is decisive
for complex sound field parameters. The supervised baseline
achieves slightly lower error on the ABS task, a simpler spectral
property less dependent on geometric regularization. Figure 2
confirms these findings:L SAC yields a more structured latent
manifold than the CCSR baseline, most clearly for TDOA,
which forms a smooth gradient. Although separation for T 60
and DRR is weaker—likely due to their looser connection to
the selected features—our method still reduces local variance
and produces more coherent clusters than the baseline.
4. Discussion
We introduced a novel self-supervised framework that bridges
the gap between signal reconstruction and geometric represen-
tation learning. Our results indicate that by explicitly forcing
the encoder to capture the underlying physical properties of the
acoustic environment, we can significantly reduce estimation
errors for complex spatial parameters (TDOA,T 60, DRR)
beyond what is achievable with reconstruction alone. However,
evaluation was conducted on simulated data, warranting further
validation on real-world reverberation scenarios. Moreover, the
effectiveness ofL SAC depends directly on the reliability of the
target acoustic features (e.g., GCC-PHAT). In highly adverse
environments with very low SNRs, the degradation of these
baseline estimators may reduce the regularization benefits of
our proposed loss. Future work will address these constraints
through real-world evaluations and deeper robustness analysis
across diverse noise conditions.
5. Conclusions
In this work, we proposed a self-supervised framework that
integrates signal processing priors into spatial representation
learning. We introduced the Soft Acoustic Contrastive Loss,
which organizes the latent space by aligning it with physical
acoustic properties. Our experiments show that this approach
yields superior performance on spatial estimation tasks com-
pared to reconstruction-only baselines. The resulting repre-
sentations exhibit a clear geometric structure, confirming that
guiding the learning process with acoustic cues significantly en-
hances model robustness and interpretability.
