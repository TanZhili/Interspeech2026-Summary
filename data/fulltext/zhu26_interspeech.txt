Abstract
Current LLM-based speech recognition systems use fixed-rate
downsampling for acoustic representation, which overlooks
speech content dynamics and causes information loss or re-
dundancy. We propose a content-aware dynamic acoustic map-
ping method via the Continuous Integrate-and-Fire (CIF) mech-
anism, adaptively aligning speech embeddings with transcrip-
tion token counts. This enables content-guided downsampling
during training and inference, cutting LLM input length while
preserving or boosting accuracy. On AISHELL-1 and Lib-
riSpeech, we achieve 12-26% relative error reduction at compa-
rable average speech embedding length (ASEL). While main-
taining comparable performance, we achieve over 45% ASEL
reduction with 7-19% lower time to first token (TTFT) across
utterance lengths. GigaSpeech results confirm scalability. Our
work establishes an efficient paradigm improving the accuracy-
efficiency trade-off in LLM-based speech recognition.

1. Introduction
Recent progress in large language models (LLMs) [1, 2, 3]
have broadened their applicability to diverse speech and audio
understanding applications. Particularly in ASR, the integra-
tion of speech encoders with large language models has opened
new frontiers [4, 5, 6]. A common paradigm involves con-
verting speech into a sequence of features via an encoder (e.g.,
Whisper [7], StepAudio2 [8], KimiAudio [9], Seed-ASR [10],
Firered-ASR [11], ), which are then projected into the semantic
space of an LLM through an adapter module [12]. To manage
computational cost, these systems typically employ fixed down-
sampling strategies to compress the acoustic feature sequence.
However, existing downsampling methods often rely on lo-
cal operations over speech features, such as feature concatena-
tion or sliding-window convolution. As the downsampling ratio
increases, these approaches tend to discard fine-grained acous-
tic information, leading to a noticeable degradation in recog-
nition performance [13].This work [14] presents TRIM, a to-
ken reduction method for visual question answering to allevi-
ate the heavy computational burden of multimodal large lan-
guage models. The approach in [15] leverages cosine similar-
ity between feature vectors to reduce the frame count input to
the large model, enabling efficient edge deployment. This pa-
per [16]proposes Token Merging (ToMe) that merges similar
tokens in transformers to greatly boost the throughput of ViT
models. Although these approaches significantly reduces infer-
ence time, the latter two methods inevitably lead to a decline in
recognition performance. This static compression also ignores
the fundamental variability in speech content and the dynamic
nature of acoustic-phonetic boundaries.
In this work, we address this mismatch by introducing a
content-aware dynamic acoustic mapping mechanism based on
the Continuous Integrate-and-Fire (CIF) algorithm [17]. Un-
like traditional fixed downsampling, our approach considers
both the acoustic features and the corresponding textual content
length, dynamically aligning the number of generated speech
embeddings to the token count of the transcription. This en-
ables the model to perform content-guided adaptive downsam-
pling, where the compression ratio is adjusted according to the
speech rate and linguistic content.
Originally proposed for non-autoregressive ASR, CIF is
a soft and monotonic alignment mechanism that dynamically
”fires” acoustic embeddings based on accumulated attention
weight, effectively determining the output sequence length
on-the-fly. We adapt CIF to serve as a dynamic front-end
for speech-enabled LLMs. Our proposed mapper produces
a variable-length sequence of acoustic embeddings where the
length is conditioned on the corresponding text transcription
during training, allowing the model to learn a content-aware
compression rate.
Our contributions are threefold:
1. We propose a novel dynamic acoustic mapping framework to
replace fixed downsampling in speech LLM interfaces.
2. We introduce a content-aware downsampling strategy using
CIF that dynamically aligns acoustic frames with text tokens,
improving both efficiency and robustness.
3. We demonstrate that our method significantly reduces the se-
quence length input to the LLM, leading to faster training
and inference, while maintaining competitive recognition ac-
curacy.
2. Proposed Method
The overall architecture of our model is illustrated in Figure 1.
It consists of three main components: a speech encoder, a CIF-
based dynamic mapper, and a large language model with an
adapter.
2.1. Speech Encoder
We employ the audio encoder fromStepAudio2[8] orFir-
eRedASR[11] as our speech encoder, which processes the raw
waveform and outputs a sequence of high-level acoustic fea-
turesH=h 1,h 2,...,h T , whereTdenotes the number of
frames. A key characteristic of this encoder is its output frame
rate of25 Hz, which provides a balanced temporal resolution
suitable for capturing phonetic and prosodic details without ex-
cessive computational overhead.

2.3. Adapter and Language Model
To effectively integrate the variable-length acoustic embedding
sequenceE a∈R B×N×D from the dynamic mapper into the
input space of a pre-trained LLM, we design a lightweight
Adapter module. This module is responsible not only for feature
dimension alignment but also incorporates a temporal down-
sampling mechanism to further compress the sequence length,
thereby enhancing the overall inference efficiency of the model.
The processed embedding sequenceE out is then fed into
the pre-trained LLM (e.g., Qwen [18]), which subsequently per-
forms the final task, such as speech recognition. The entire sys-
tem is trained end-to-end with a cross-entropy loss function.
3. Experiments
3.1. Experimental Setup
3.1.1. Datasets
We evaluate our method on four widely used ASR benchmarks:
•AISHELL-1[19]: A Mandarin speech corpus containing
178 hours of training data with 400 speakers.
•LibriSpeech[20]: An English speech corpus with 960 hours
of training data.
•GigaSpeech[21]: A large-scale multilingual speech corpus
containing 10,000 hours of labeled data.
3.1.2. Model Configurations
We explore multiple model configurations:
Speech Encoder:We employ two different pre-trained speech
encoders:
•StepAudio2-Mini encoder[8]: 637M parameters, output
frame rate of 25 Hz
•FireRedLLM-ASR encoder[11]: 710M parameters, output
frame rate of 25 Hz
Adapter Architecture:The adapter module is implemented in
three variants:
•Conv1D + MLP: Incorporates a 1D convolutional layer for
feature downsampling before a two-layer MLP, with fixed
temporal downsampling rates of 2, 4, or 6
•Concat-MLP: Uses feature concatenation for downsampling
before a two-layer MLP with fixed rates of 2, 4, or 6
•Dyn-MLP: Our proposed dynamic downsampling method
using CIF mechanism
2462
Large Language Model:We use two scales of the Qwen3
model family:
•Qwen3-1.7B: 1.7 billion parameters, 28 layers, hidden di-
mension 2048
•Qwen2-7B: 7 billion parameters, 32 layers, hidden dimen-
sion 3584
Tokenizer:For dynamic mapping, we explore two tokenizers:
•Qwen3 Tokenizer: The default tokenizer from the Qwen3
model family
•SenseVoice-Small Tokenizer[22]: A specialized BPE-
based [23] subword tokenizer for better acoustic-text align-
ment
3.2. Training Strategy
We adopt a carefully designed two-stage training strategy to sta-
bilize learning and efficiently leverage pre-trained models.
Stage 1 (S1): Training Adapter and Dynamic Mapper
In this initial stage, we freeze both the speech encoder and the
LLM, training only the CIF-based dynamic mapper and adapter
module. We use the AdamW optimizer [24] with a learning
rate of 5e-5 and apply gradient clipping [25] with a threshold
value set to 5. The number of training epochs is set to 100 for
AISHELL-1, 10 for LibriSpeech, and 5 for GigaSpeech, respec-
tively, to accommodate dataset sizes and avoid overfitting.
Stage 2 (S2): LLM Fine-tuning
After stable training of the adapter and mapper, we introduce
LLM fine-tuning. Only LLM parameters are updated. For Gi-
gaSpeech, we use a learning rate of 1e-5 and train for 5 epochs
in this stage.
Considering the varying data scales across datasets, we
apply different training strategies. On small-to-medium scale
datasets (AISHELL-1 and LibriSpeech), only Stage 1 training is
conducted to learn the dynamic mapper and adapter efficiently
without overfitting. On the large-scale dataset (GigaSpeech),
both Stage 1 and Stage 2 training are employed to fully adapt the
LLM. This adaptive training schedule ensures efficient use of
computational resources while maintaining robust performance
across datasets of different scales.
3.2.1. Evaluation Metrics
We employ comprehensive evaluation metrics to assess model
performance:
Accuracy Metrics:
•Character Error Rate (CER)for AISHELL
•Word Error Rate (WER)for LibriSpeech and GigaSpeech
Efficiency Metrics:
•Average Speech Embedding Length (ASEL): Representing
the average number of encoder frames input to the LLM
•Time to First Token (TTFT): Representing the latency from
input speech to the generation of the first output token
3.3. Main Results
3.3.1. Comparison with WEST Baseline
We first compare our dynamic mapping method against a strong
fixed-stride baseline based on the WEST toolkit [26]. As shown
in Table 1, the baseline system uses the FireRedLLM-ASR en-
coder, a Conv+MLP adapter with a fixed downsampling rate
of 2, and achieves CERs of 4.17% (Qwen3-1.7B) and 4.01%
(Qwen2-7B) on AISHELL-1, with an ASEL of 70.
In contrast, our Dyn-MLP method significantly reduces
the input length while improving recognition accuracy. With
Qwen3-1.7B, we achieve a CER of 3.53% with an ASEL of
27, representing a 15.3% relative improvement in CER. With
Qwen2-7B, we further lower CER to 2.67% while maintaining
the same ASEL of 27, corresponding to a33.4% relative CER
reduction. The reduction in ASEL from 70 to 27 (approxi-
mately 61.4% ) leads to proportional decreases in computational
cost.

4. Conclusion
We proposed a dynamic acoustic mapping method for LLM-
based speech models using Continuous Integrate-and-Fire. By
replacing fixed downsampling with a content-aware compres-
sion mechanism, our approach allows the model to adaptively
determine the number of frames fed into the language model.
Extensive experiments show that this leads to a more efficient
use of computational resources, achieving a better accuracy-
efficiency trade-off than strong fixed-stride baselines. This
work paves the way for more flexible and efficient architectures
for speech-enabled large language models.
