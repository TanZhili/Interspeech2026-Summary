Hearing Smiles in the Crowd: How Babble Noise Shapes
Smiled Speech Perception

1 Human Media Interaction, University of Twente, the Netherlands

Abstract
In everyday communication, the auditory perception of
smiled speech can be hindered by noise. We examined how
multi-talker babble noise affected human auditory perception
of smile-like speech, consisting of affectiveamusedand me-
chanicalspread-lipspeech. Listeners completed two catego-
rization tasks: 1) smile-like detection (smile-like vs. neutral
speech) and, within smile-like speech, 2) smile-type categoriza-
tion (amused vs. spread-lip speech). Results showed that in-
creasing noise reduced perceptual sensitivity in both tasks while
systematically altering response bias. In Task 1, as noise in-
creased, participants’ accuracy not only decreased but they were
also less inclined to report hearing a smile. Similarly, in Task
2, an initial bias toward “amused” responses in quiet reversed
under strong noise. These findings demonstrate that smile per-
ception in noise reflects not only degraded sensory evidence but
also adaptive shifts in decision strategy under uncertainty.
Index Terms: smiled speech, speech perception in noise
1. Introduction
When the contraction of the zygomaticus major muscle pulls the
corners of the mouth upward, we usually call that expression a
“smile.” Smiles are ubiquitous in everyday interaction but are
functionally diverse. They can signal amusement, happiness,
embarrassment, discomfort, or even sadness [1, 2], and they
support social functions ranging from affiliation and bonding
to hierarchy negotiation [3]. While these functional categories
exhibit distinct facial configurations [1], the signaling of a smile
is not limited to the visual modality. Smile-like expressions are
also conveyed through the voice, where they are reflected in
systematic variations in prosody and voice quality. Although
research on smiled speech is less extensive compared with smil-
ing faces, existing studies suggest that smiling leaves detectable
traces in the speech signal. For instance, smiling in speech can
increase the second formant (F 2), particularly in rounded vow-
els, and often correlates with increased intensity [4, 5]. Percep-
tual modeling using psychophysical reverse-correlation further
indicates that auditory smiling has a characteristic spectral sig-
nature, involving upward shifts inF 1/F2 and enhanced higher-
formant energy [6, 7].
Beyond these acoustic signatures, behavioral studies
demonstrate that listeners can reliably perceive and differenti-
ate smile-related speech styles from voice alone. Early foun-
dational work showed that spreading the lips, even without
emotional intent, produces a consistent auditory impression of
“smiling” [8]. This motivates a distinction between spread-
lip speech (mechanical smiling) and amused speech (affec-

“amused” than spread-lip smiling [9], and further evidence sug-

distinguish among functionally different smile types based on
vocal signals, with smiles associated with genuine enjoyment
typically being the most recognizable [11]. Notably, such audi-
tory smiles have been found to trigger spontaneous facial imita-
tion (zygomatic activation) in listeners even when the cues are
not consciously detected [12].
Despite this evidence, auditory smile perception has largely
been studied under clean laboratory listening conditions, while
in many real-world interactions, e.g., telephone conversations
or contact-center settings, visual cues are absent and the speech
signal is often corrupted by background noise, including com-
peting talkers. Multi-talker babble is a common masking noise
that degrades sensory evidence and increases cognitive de-
mands [13]. Prior work on speech emotion perception in noise
suggests that degradation effects are not uniform across dif-
ferent emotion categories: noise can disproportionately disrupt
certain affective information [14, 15] and shift the acoustic cues
listeners rely on for emotion judgments [16]. These findings
raise a critical question regarding the perceptual survival of
smiled speech under babble: as noise increases, can listeners
still detect the presence of smiling in speech, and does noise af-
fect different smile types equally in subsequent categorization?
To address these questions, we investigated the perceptual
robustness of smiled speech under multi-talker babble noise at
varying Signal-to-Noise Ratios (SNRs). Using stimuli from the
AMuS corpus [17], we asked what the effect of decreasing SNR
levels is on listeners’ ability 1) to detect asmile-likequality in
speech, and in particular, within smile-like speech, 2) to catego-
rize thesmile-typeas either amused or spread-lip smiles.
2. Methods
2.1. Task Definitions
We defined two binary perceptual classification tasks to investi-
gate listeners’ perception of smiling in speech:
•Task 1 (Smile-like detection):smile-like vs. neutral classi-
fication. The positive class pooled all smiled speech produc-
tions (amused and spread-lip), testing whether listeners could
detect the presence of smiling in speech.
•Task 2 (Smile-type categorization):amused vs. spread-lip.

2.2. Stimuli and Noise Conditions
Stimuli were selected from the AMuS corpus [17], which
provides a comparative set of sentences recorded in different
speech styles: amused, spread-lip, and neutral speech. For
amused speech, speakers were instructed to read sentences
while sounding amused without laughing. In contrast, spread-
lip speech was recorded by asking speakers to maintain a lip
spreading gesture while reading, specifically without attempt-
ing to convey a happy or amused emotional state.
The corpus poses challenges for generalization due to lim-
ited speaker diversity and partial confounding of speaker, lan-
guage, and gender, as well as unbalanced class coverage across
speakers. For instance, Speaker A provides no spread-lip
samples, and Speaker B, who produced materials in English
and French, provides spread-lip speech only in French. We
therefore restricted the study to Speaker B (French, male) and
Speaker C (English, female), the only speaker–language subsets
with full coverage of amused, spread-lip, and neutral speech.
The stimuli were prepared as follows. First, leading
and trailing silences were removed using the AMuS forced-
alignment labels. All clean speech waveforms were then nor-
malized to a global intensity of 70 dB using Praat [18]. Second,
the speech materials were mixed with multi-talker babble noise
from the NOISEX-92 set [19], which originates from a cafe-
teria environment with approximately 100 concurrent speakers.
We manually selected only noise segments without intelligible
speech content or audible laughter for mixing. For each trial, a
noise segment was cropped to be 400 ms longer than the speech
signal (200 ms leading and trailing noise), resampled to 16 kHz,
and scaled to the target SNR. A 50 ms linear fade-in and fade-
out was applied to the noise to prevent transient clicks, and sig-
nals were mixed into single-channel (mono) waveforms.
Three listening conditions were tested 1:Quiet(clean),
−3dB, and−6dB. In communication acoustics, an SNR of
approximately−3dB is widely regarded as the lower bound
for sufficient speech intelligibility, below which verbal commu-
nication becomes unreliable [20]. The−6dB SNR has been
widely adopted in the CHiME benchmarks [21, 22] to repre-
sent adverse domestic and social noise conditions, where nat-
urally occurring background sounds can produce instantaneous
SNRs as low as−6dB. Together, these SNR levels span optimal
listening, a functional intelligibility threshold, and an ecologi-
cally realistic extreme noise condition, enabling us to test how
smile-related paralinguistic information persists under progres-
sive acoustic masking.
2.3. Experimental Design
Materials were presented to participants fluent in the language
of the respective test speaker: Speaker B’s (French) materials
were presented to French-fluent participants, and Speaker C’s
(English) materials to English-fluent participants. To prevent
sentence repetition effects, each unique sentence occurred only
once per participant across the two tasks. This was achieved
by constructing two non-overlapping base content lists for each
speaker and task. In Task 1, each list comprised 84 trials (21
amused, 21 spread-lip, 42 neutral), whereas Task 2 comprised
42 trials (21 amused, 21 spread-lip).

2.4. Participants and Procedure
The target population consisted of healthy adults aged 18-65
years with self-reported normal hearing and no known cogni-
tive impairments. Participants were recruited via Prolific and
were required to be fluent in the target language (French for
Speaker B; English for Speaker C). Language eligibility was
determined based on self-reported fluency rather than native-
language background. Participants also provided demographic
information, including gender, age band, country of residence,
and native language.
Both tasks were administered as binary forced-choice
(Yes/No) judgments on audio stimuli. In Task 1, participants
answered the question “Does the talker’s speech sound smile-
like?”. In Task 2, they were asked: “Does the talker’s speech
sound amused?”. Within each task, trials were presented in a
randomized order for each participant. Participants were ex-
plicitly instructed to listen to each clip before responding, and
audio replay was available if needed. Headphone use was ver-
ified at the beginning of the experiment using a screening task
based on the McDermott Lab paradigm [23]. This started with a
volume-calibration step in which participants adjusted playback
to a loud but comfortable level. Only participants who passed
the headphone check were allowed to proceed to the main ex-
periment. In addition, two auditory attention checks that in-
structed participants to select a specific response were embed-
ded within the experiment. Participants were excluded from
further analysis only if they failed both checks.
After applying all screening criteria, the final valid sample
comprised 38 English-fluent and 37 French-fluent participants.
The median age band in both groups was 25–34 (range: 18–24
to 55–65). The English-fluent group was gender-balanced,
whereas the French-fluent group was moderately male-skewed.
While recruitment targeted high fluency in the task language,
both groups exhibited heterogeneous native-language and resi-
dency backgrounds (Table 1), providing a broader representa-
tion of high-proficiency listener populations.
The study was approved by the Ethics Committee Com-
puter & Information Science, University of Twente (260534),
and all participants provided informed consent.
2.5. Statistical Analysis
We used trial-level generalized linear mixed-effects models
(GLMMs) with a binomial (logit) link function [24] to ana-

lyze the effect of noise on listeners’ classification accuracy.
Complementary Signal Detection Theory (SDT) analyses were
computed from participant-level hit and false-alarm rates within
each noise condition [25, 26] to dissociate perceptual sensi-
tivity from response bias. Sensitivity was quantified usingd ′,
stimulus-level discrimination using the Area Under the ROC
Curve (AUC), and decision strategy using the SDT criterion (c).
All analyses were conducted in R [27].
For both tasks, the GLMM fixed-effects structure was spec-
ified a priori based on the experimental design and research
questions. Our primary question was whether the effect of back-
ground babble noise differs across smile categories, motivating
inclusion of the Noise×Category interaction. In addition, be-
cause stimuli were produced by different speakers (across lan-
guage groups), and because speaker-related variability may sys-
tematically differ across categories and noise conditions, we
also included Speaker×Category and Speaker×Noise inter-
actions. The same fixed-effects structure was applied to both
tasks. All models included random intercepts for Participant
and Stimulus to account for participant- and item-specific vari-
ability. Age and Gender were initially entered as covariates
but were removed as they did not improve model fit and were
not part of the variables of interest. Noise was treatment-coded
(Quiet reference), Category was treatment-coded with the task-
specific reference level (neutral in Task 1; spread-lip in Task 2),
and Speaker was sum-coded.

4. Discussion and Conclusion
Across Tasks 1 and 2, noise-masking consistently reduced sen-
sory evidence available for smile judgments, and also made lis-
teners shift to a more conservative decision strategy as uncer-
tainty increased. While the current study did not directly mea-
sure a specific cognitive mechanism, this shift seems to align
with models of optimal decision-making under uncertainty [28].
A classic example is the judgment of a “scowling face.” If
falsely attributing anger is socially costly, observers adopt a
conservative bias. They require high certainty before labeling
the face as angry and tolerate more misses to avoid the social
costs of being wrong. While no explicit social consequences
were attached to our participants’ responses, they may still have
relied on internalized expectations about the social meaning of
smile-related cues. Similarly, the tendency to interpret ambigu-
ous stimuli as non-smiling or non-emotional also conceptually
aligns with the “negativity hypothesis” [29], which suggests that
the brain defaults to neutral or negative interpretations, requir-
ing additional cognitive evidence to override this baseline.
These findings have practical implications for everyday
communication in realistic noisy settings, where listeners may
not merely “miss” smiles because the signal is masked. They
may also shift toward conservative defaults that downplay pos-
itive affect. This has important implications for evaluating and
designing speech technologies intended for real-world, noisy
interactions, especially when conversation partners lack visual
context. In such settings, noise does not simply degrade what is
heard, but also changes what people are willing to conclude.
Results from Task 1 also showed that amused speech
was recognized more accurately than spread-lip speech, which
is consistent with earlier findings that amused smiles carry
richer vocal cues than mechanically produced, spread-lip smiles
[9, 10]. At the same time, Task 1 revealed speaker-dependent
differences, particularly for neutral and spread-lip speech.
Speaker C’s neutral baseline productions may have carried
voice-quality or prosodic characteristics that listeners asso-
ciate with greater affiliation and friendliness. Alternatively, the
acoustic consequences of lip spreading may have been weaker
or less consistent for this speaker. Notably, no reliable speaker
difference was observed for amused speech. This suggests that
the cues supporting the perception of affective smiling may be
more consistent across speakers than the cues linked to pure
lip spreading. This interpretation should nevertheless be treated
with caution, as the study included only two selected speak-
