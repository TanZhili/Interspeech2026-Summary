Two-stage semi-supervised learning with pseudo-labels: A case study on
Northern Sámi ASR

Abstract
While Self-Supervised Learning (SSL) has advanced Automatic
Speech Recognition (ASR), limited labeled data remains a bot-
tleneck for under-resourced languages, and smaller, parameter-
efficient models underperform larger counterparts given equal
supervised data. We explore semi-supervised learning with SSL
models for Northern Sámi, an under-resourced agglutinative
language. We compare two approaches: (i) all pseudo-labels
generated by a teacher model and (ii) a subset from WER-based
multi-teacher agreement. We show that a larger teacher im-
proves out-of-domain generalization of a smaller model by 3.8-
33.3% relative CER with no additional labeled data. With com-
parable data volume, the student benefits as much from pseudo-
labels as from human-labeled data. For combining human and
pseudo-labeled data, two-stage fine-tuning outperforms single-
step mixed training. Overall, we show that lightweight models
can approach larger models’ performance even after a single
pseudo-labeling iteration.
Index Terms: semi-supervised learning, pseudo-labeling, low-
resource ASR, Northern Sámi, knowledge distillation
1. Introduction
Northern Sámi (davvisámegiella) is a Finno-Ugric language,
with around 20,000 indigenous speakers in Norway, Sweden,
and Finland [1]. It is morphologically rich, characterized by ex-
tensive inflection, derivation, and productive compounding [2].
It has also been described as a minority and under-resourced
language in natural language processing [3, 4, 5], and classi-
fied as definitely endangered by UNESCO [6]. Due to a small
speaker population and scarce linguistic resources, obtaining
sufficient labeled data or human annotators remains challeng-
ing, making efficient use of readily available untranscribed
speech material a particularly promising direction.
Recent advances in Self-Supervised Learning (SSL) have
reduced reliance on labeled data, enabling Automatic Speech
Recognition (ASR) and other speech tasks for under-resourced
languages. However, large corpora are still required for reason-
able accuracy and generalization, leaving dependence on su-
pervised data a major challenge in low-resource settings. To
address this, research has increasingly turned to data-efficient
approaches via semi-supervised learning [7]. An example of
such a method is Pseudo-Labeling (PL) based semi-supervised
learning, earlier seen in the form of self-training, where a model
trained on supervised data assigns labels to unlabeled data to
expand the training set [7].
As summarized in [8], PL is used across several unsuper-
vised learning subfields, including self-supervised learning and
knowledge distillation. It can achieve strong performance with
limited labeled data, reducing reliance on manual annotation
[9]. Iterative PL [10] regenerates hard pseudo-labels (refers to
the most probable label prediction) over many iterations dynam-
ically, and is particularly useful for CTC-based ASR models
in low-resource scenarios. To avoid propagation of erroneous
pseudo-labels over iterations, a language model (LM) can be
used for mitigating them. However, [11] noted that Iterative PL
with an LM for ASR may cause acoustic model to overfit to the
text domain of the LM than the underlying speech distribution.
Hence, the same study also shows that pseudo-labels need not
rely on LM.
In other works, filtering erroneous pseudo-labeled samples
is also a common strategy to improve training data quality.
Confidence-based filtering is one such approach, described in
detail by [9], where hard labels are generated for unlabeled
data and then filtered according to confidence scores; low-
confidence cases are iteratively refined to mitigate noisy la-
bels. This approach has been applied in works such as [12],
which, along with [13], also demonstrate the utility of PL in
low-resource scenarios. However, confidence-based filtering is
not always beneficial; [14] show that it can fail to improve per-
formance or even degrade Word Error Rate (WER). Another
approach is model-agreement based filtering, which selects in-
stances in which multiple models show the highest agreement
[13, 15]. [16] has explored PL in the context of large language
models, where a larger model is used to generate pseudo-labels,
which are then employed to train smaller models. Furthermore,
[14] reports that student models trained on pseudo-labels by a
strong teacher model achieve lower WER than those trained on
human-labeled data.
Building on prior work, we investigate PL for Northern
Sámi, a language with limited resources for speech recognition.
This work contributes to ongoing efforts to support its preser-
vation and use. We explore two scenarios: (i) using all pseudo-
labels generated by a large teacher model, and (ii) agreement-
based filtering, retaining pseudo-labels only when the predic-
tions of the student model align with that of the teacher. Across
both scenarios, we evaluate fine-tuning strategies combining
pseudo-labeled and human-annotated data to optimize perfor-
mance. Our contributions are two-fold: first, we systematically
assess the impact ofagreement-based pseudo-label filtering;
second, we propose and evaluate atwo-stage fine-tuning ap-
proach, where pseudo- and gold-labeled data are incorporated
sequentially. Our empirical results demonstrate that pseudo-
labels can substantially improve the performance of the base
model, even with a single PL iteration. In addition, we compare
the strengths and limitations of different fine-tuning strategies.

2. Data
We utilized approximately 75 hours of unlabeled Sámi Parlia-
ment recordings collected by the Arctic Univ. Norway (UiT).
The speech utterances are primarily in Northern Sámi, though
they contain dialectal variation and segments of code-switching
in Norwegian, Swedish, and English, which were not filtered
from the raw corpus to avoid laborious pre-processing steps.
For supervised fine-tuning, we used 20 hours of recordings
from Sámi Parliament meetings1 transcribed by UIT . This data
was used both to train the large model and establish the baseline
for the small model. For validation and in-domain testing, a
predefined 1-hour set was used, following [17].
To ensure fair evaluation, we tested our models on three
out-of-distribution test corpora. First, we utilized approx. 85
minutes of mixed read-aloud and spontaneous speech (282utt)
with variable audio quality, consisting of 282 utterances with
an average length of 9 seconds. Next, to see how the models
handle spontaneous speech, we transcribed 1 hour of Sámi pod-
casts (YLE Sámi podcast)2, with each segment averaging 11.7
minutes. The transcriptions were made by a native speaker, to
contribute towards developing speech technology for the lan-
guage. Lastly, we evaluated theUIT-SMEcorpus 3 containing
about 8 hours of Northern Sámi recordings originally created
for TTS development at UiT. It comprises 4.7 hours spoken by
a male speaker and 3.3 hours by a female speaker.
3. Methods
Predictions from the small and large models were obtained on
the unlabeled Sámi Parliament speech data. To filter inaccurate
pseudo-labels, we adopt an agreement-based approach between
the two models. Specifically, we selected utterances where the
WER between the small and large model predictions was below
a predefined threshold (10%), established via preliminary ex-
periments. This yielded approximately 28 hours of speech, rep-
resenting segments where both models showed high agreement
and which are therefore likely to be correctly recognized. For
these cases, we used the pseudo-labels produced by the large
model. The assumption behind this approach is that the pseudo-
labels of the large model are more reliable when the small model
produces similar predictions, suggesting agreement can serve as
a proxy for label quality. In addition, we considered pseudo-
labels generated by the large model on the full 75-hour unla-
beled dataset to compare filtered and unfiltered (noisy) training
data. Our experimental setup uses a single round of pseudo-
label generation to isolate the effect of pseudo-labels them-
selves and omits an LM in order to evaluate generalization to
out-of-domain tasks such as conversational speech, given the
limited and genre-biased availability of Sámi text data.

4. Training setup
We consider two Sámi foundation models based on wav2vec
2.0 architecture. The smaller model,wav2vec2-base-sami-22k 4
has 95M parameters and 12 transformer blocks. The larger
model,wav2vec2-large-sami-22k 5 has 317M parameters with
24 transformer encoder layers. Both models were pre-trained
on 22.4k hours of unlabeled Sámi speech from the KA VI ra-
dio and television broadcasts [17]. The training for fine-tuning
was carried out on a single NVIDIA V100 V olta GPU with 32
GB of VRAM. Runs typically lasted 18-20 hours. Models were
trained with the AdamW optimizer, a learning rate of 5e-4, 0.25
warmup ratio with linear decay, and a batch size of 16.
We report model performance using the WER and Char-
acter Error Rate (CER) metrics. We should note that WER is
known to be unreliable for morphologically rich languages or
those lacking clear word boundaries [18]. In such cases, CER is
often preferred, particularly for agglutinative languages, since
even small errors such as splitting a compound word can dis-
proportionately inflate WER [19].
5. Results
To select models with the best checkpoints, we first used held
out 10% of the pseudo-labeled data for validation. During train-
ing, we consistently observed two local minima in training loss
and evaluation metrics on the held-out split: one early in train-
ing and another later. To determine which checkpoint gener-
alized better, we evaluated both on a 1-hour parliament vali-
dation set. The earlier minimum consistently yielded superior
performance, so we adopted it as our model selection criterion
across experiments. This behavior was confirmed across two
small model variants and multiple random initialization seeds
(2-3 per variant). Initially, most models were trained for the full
60 epochs. However, after identifying this pattern, we applied
early stopping at the early minimum, which reduced the number
of training epochs in subsequent runs.

with all pseudo-labeled data.

5.2. Two-stage fine-tuning
Empirical results ofHL + PL-Filtered (mixed)method show de-
graded performance relative to the baseline. In Fig 1, it has the
second-highest substitution errors across all test sets. One pos-
sible explanation is that the model encounters conflicting super-
vision from the two datasets within the same batch. The mis-
match between distributions of human and pseudo-labels may
prevent the model from learning consistent representations.
To test our hypothesis, we fine-tuned the small model first
on supervised data and then on pseudo-labels, which improved
performance compared to the mixed approach. However, re-
versing the order and starting with pseudo-labels and then fine-
tuning on transcriptions, as inPL-Filtered + HL (2-stage)and
PL-Full + HL (2-stage), yields greater improvements, with the
largest gains observed when all data is used(PL-Full + HL).
Using this approach on UIT-SME, which was designed for TTS
and is thus acoustically convenient, yielded marginal gains as
the baseline was already reasonable, with both methods PL +
HL (2 stage), as seen in Table 1 and show similar error distribu-
tions across substitution, deletion and insertions, as seen in Fig
1. Improvements through this approach could be because ex-
posure to pseudo-labels early may encourage broader represen-
tation learning, while training with human labels subsequently
calibrates these representations. Hence, these results demon-
strate that the order of two-stage fine-tuning is crucial, with the

6.2. Character-level recognition and error propagation
To analyze the effects of two-stage semi-supervised fine-tuning,
we examined character-level confusion matrices (CM). For
characters in the upper half of the CM (a-v) in Figure 3, fine-
tuned models generally maintained or improved recognition
compared to the baseline. In contrast, several underrepresented
characters in both pseudo- and human-labels such asä, ö, ø, ˇc,
·,andNwere recognized less reliably, likely due to their low
frequency in the training data. By contrast, more frequent char-
acters such asáandšbenefited from fine-tuning. Hence, PL
may strengthen representations for common characters, while
rare ones suffer.
We also observed systematic weaknesses. For instance,wwas
consistently confused withv, particularly by the large model,
with fine-tuned models inheriting this error. Similarly,äand
æwere often mistaken foraora/e, and this confusion per-
sisted after fine-tuning. In some cases, fine-tuning introduced
new errors:å, already difficult for earlier models, was predom-
inantly mapped too. Likewise,z, recognized more reliably by
the baseline than the large model, was misclassified once fine-
tuned models reproduced the large model’s mistakes.
Many errors involve characters outside the Northern Sámi al-
phabet (e.g.,w, å, ä, ö, æ, ø), which mainly occur in loan-
words or code-switching and may explain their low frequency
and poor recognition. Some confusions may also reflect cross-
Nordic phonological similarities (e.g., Finnishöand Norwegian
øboth map to[ø]in IPA, while in Swedishöcan mean either
[ø]or[œ]). In contrast, characters such asN, ˇc,·, though part of
the alphabet, remain relatively rare in practice and thus difficult
for the models.
Overall, these patterns highlight a limitation of PL based meth-
ods. When the initial model makes systematic errors, PL does
not provide a mechanism to correct them; instead, biased or in-
correct classifications are reinforced during training and carried
forward [7].

7. Conclusion
We investigated PL for Northern Sámi, an under-resourced
language in speech recognition, focusing on fine-tuning with
human- and pseudo-labeled data. A two-stage strategy, in which
the model is first trained on pseudo-labeled data and then on
human annotations, outperformed both individual sources and
their direct pooling, within a single PL iteration. Comparison of
agreement-based filtering with using all pseudo-labels showed
that retaining all labels, despite added noise, consistently re-
duced error rates in most settings. Error analysis revealed that
PL amplifies systematic patterns, often benefiting frequent char-
acters but disadvantaging rare ones, consistent with prior find-
ings in semi-supervised learning. While we did not explore mit-
igation strategies such as data augmentation and regularization
(e.g., Mixup [20], noisy student training [21]), or confidence-
based filtering [22], our analysis of error propagation under
resource constraints highlights directions where these methods
could be applied.
