Abstract
Factorized neural speech codecs often rely on distillation from
pretrained self-supervised learning (SSL) encoders or auto-
matic speech recognition (ASR) systems to disentangle con-
tent and speaker information. However, this dependency lim-
its their applicability to low-resource languages where reliable
teacher models are unavailable. In this paper, we propose
CycleCodec, a distillation-free factorized codec trained from
scratch. CycleCodec introduces cycle-consistent speaker swap-
ping, a codec-internal self-supervision signal that reduces cross-
stream leakage during speaker-conditioned generation. To fa-
cilitate speaker-content disentanglement, CycleCodec also lim-
its temporal capacity and improves speaker modeling with a
query-based Transformer aggregator and a speaker contrastive
loss. Experiments on in-domain (English) and unseen lan-
guages (Mandarin and Vietnamese) show that CycleCodec out-
performs distillation-free baselines and achieves more robust
speaker-content disentanglement.
Index Terms: neural speech codec, disentangled representation
learning, cycle-consistent training
1. Introduction
Neural speech codecs have evolved rapidly from waveform
compression modules [1, 2, 3, 4] into general-purpose tokeniz-
ers that provide discrete and compact representations for down-
stream speech generative tasks [5]. Beyond high-fidelity re-
construction alone, a growing line of work has further inves-
tigated factorizing codec representations into a time-varying
stream that carries content and prosody, and a time-invariant
stream that captures speaker identity and other global attributes
[6, 7, 8, 9, 10]. This factorization is particularly attractive be-
cause it enables controllable synthesis and robust cross-speaker
manipulation while retaining a discrete interface that is well
suited for modern speech language models [6, 8, 11].
Most existing factorized neural speech codecs achieve dis-
entanglement by distilling knowledge from pretrained teacher
models. In these frameworks, the content space is typically
regularized by automatic speech recognition (ASR) objectives
or mapped to phone-like representations from self-supervised
learning (SSL) models such as wav2vec 2.0 [12]. For instance,
FACodec [6] employs an ASR objective, specifically a CTC loss
on phoneme sequences, to learn the content subspace. Sim-
ilarly, LSCodec [10] and FreeCodec [9] rely on representa-
tions from WavLM [13] and speaker verification models [14]
to guide the separation between content and global speaker at-
tributes. Although effective on high-resource languages, this
dependency raises two practical concerns. First, it may not
transfer robustly to unseen languages. When the target lan-
guage falls outside the coverage of the pretrained teachers, rep-
resentation shift can distort the supervision signal and weaken
the disentanglement between content and speaker factors [15].
Second, it can be infeasible for non-codified, indigenous, or
low-resource languages, such as Hadza [16] and Mohawk [17],
where ASR-style transcripts are unavailable and reliable teacher
models may not exist for the target language or domain, making
teacher-guided disentanglement difficult to apply. TiCodec [7]
is one of the few factorized codecs that avoids pretrained teacher
models, introducing time-invariant codes and an encoding con-
sistency loss to encourage within-utterance stability. However,
TiCodec is primarily designed for efficient tokenization rather
than speaker–content controllability, which can leave residual
cross-stream leakage. These limitations motivate a distillation-
free alternative that enforces disentanglement through codec-
internal constraints rather than external teacher supervision.
In this paper, we propose CycleCodec, a distillation-free
factorized neural speech codec model trained from scratch. Cy-
cleCodec factorizes speech into frame-level discrete codes that
carry time-varying information and an utterance-level global
representation that captures time-invariant speaker traits. The
core idea of CycleCodec is cycle-consistent speaker swapping,
a codec-internal self-supervision signal for controllable synthe-
sis that reduces cross-stream leakage without distillation from
pretrained teacher models. Built upon TiCodec [7], we con-
strain the time-varying stream by reducing the codebook size
to limit speaker leakage, and strengthen global speaker model-
ing with a query-based Transformer aggregator regularized by
a speaker contrastive loss. Unlike CycleGAN-based unpaired
translation [18] or voice conversion (VC) [19], which learn bidi-
rectional mappings between distinct domains using adversar-
ial training, our cycle-consistency objective is defined entirely
within the codec. It acts as a self-supervised constraint that
stabilizes content under speaker injection through the process
of speaker swapping, re-analysis, and swap-back reconstruc-
tion. Specifically, we decode the source frame-level codes un-
der a target speaker condition to synthesize swapped speech, re-
encode it to recover the corresponding representations, and then
swap back with a cycle-consistency reconstruction constraint.
We evaluate CycleCodec under a challenging train–test
language mismatch setting. The codec is trained on English
speech and tested both in-domain (English) and zero-shot on
two phonologically different languages, Mandarin Chinese and
Vietnamese, to probe robustness on unseen languages. We use
Mandarin and Vietnamese as evaluation languages because they
are outside the training distribution and have phonotactic dis-
tributions that differ substantially from English, while still al-

2. Method
This section presents CycleCodec, a distillation-free factorized
neural speech codec trained from scratch. As shown in Figure 1,
CycleCodec decomposes each utterance into two complemen-
tary streams: a time-varying discrete sequence intended to cap-
ture content and prosody, and a time-invariant continuous global
embedding intended to capture speaker traits. Built on TiCodec
[7], CycleCodec adopts its encoder-decoder backbone and time-
varying quantization pipeline, as well as its design of extracting
a global feature from the encoder and injecting it into the de-
coder. Given an utterancey, the encoder produces frame-level
featuresz∈R T×d z and a global embeddingg∈R dg . The
time-varying stream is further quantized into a discrete feature
sequenceq∈R T×d q [20]. The decoder reconstructs speech as
ˆy=D(q,g)from the quantized featuresqconditioned ong.
2.1. Architectural Design for Factorization
Before introducing the cycle-consistent training signal, we first
adopt two supporting architectural choices in CycleCodec that
reduce cross-stream leakage at the representation interface.
First, we reduce the codebook size of the quantizer to constrain
the information capacity of the time-varying stream. An overly
expressive codebook can encode not only content and prosody
but also residual speaker information, weakening the intended
separation between the time-varying and global streams. Limit-
ing codebook capacity encourages the temporal stream to focus
on time-varying information, while leaving speaker-related in-
formation to the global embedding.
Second, we strengthen global speaker modeling with a
query-based Transformer aggregator. Speaker cues are often
unevenly distributed over time [21, 22], so uniform pooling
over all frames may produce a weak global speaker represen-
tation, leaving residual speaker information in the time-varying
stream. We therefore replace pooling-based aggregation with a
query-based Transformer aggregator that can selectively sum-
marize speaker cues over long contexts. LetH∈R T×d h de-
note the intermediate frame-level features extracted from the en-
coder for global speaker modeling. We introduceNlearnable
query tokensQ∈R N×d h and concatenate them withHalong
the temporal dimension, forming a sequence of lengthN+T.
This sequence is processed by a Transformer encoder withL
stacked self-attention blocks [23]. We take only the outputs at
the query positions as speaker summary tokens, flatten them,
and apply a lightweight projection head to obtain the utterance-
level embeddingg. The lightweight projection head consists of
a single linear layer followed by LeakyReLU.
The segment-level consistency objective inherited from
TiCodec is often insufficient to make the global embedding
strongly speaker-discriminative. We thus add a standard con-
trastive loss [24] as an auxiliary regularizer:

2.2. Cycle-Consistent Speaker Swapping
The modifications above increase stream separation, but do
not fully ensure content preservation under speaker-conditioned
generation. In particular, a capacity-constrained temporal
stream can still lead to content drift and residual cross-stream
leakage during speaker swapping. To improve controllable syn-
thesis without relying on pretrained teacher models, we propose
cycle-consistent speaker swapping, which uses the codec itself
to verify and enforce the intended use ofqandgduring gen-
eration without external supervision. This requires no parallel
cross-speaker utterances and operates on non-parallel samples
from different speakers within each mini-batch.
As illustrated in Figure 1, the cycle procedure consists of
two forward passes. Within a batch, we form source–target
pairs by permuting utterances such that each source utterance
ysrc is matched with a targety tgt from a different speaker. In
theswap forward pass, the shared encoder extracts the source
time-varying featuresq src fromy src and the target speaker
embeddingg tgt fromy tgt. We then synthesize a swapped
utterance by conditioning the decoder on the source time-
varying features and the target speaker embeddingy swap =
D(qsrc,g tgt). Ideally,y swap should preserve the content and
prosody carried byq src, while adopting the target speaker iden-
tity specified byg tgt.
To provide a self-supervised consistency signal, we use a
cycle forward passthat first re-analyzesy swap using Cycle-
Codec’s own analysis path and then reconstructs the source
utterance through swap-back decoding. Specifically, we pass
yswap through the shared encoder and quantizer again to obtain
qswap andg swap. We impose two complementary constraints:

2.3. Objective and Optimization
We adopt a two-stage training strategy. In the first stage, we
train CycleCodec with the original TiCodec objectiveLcodec and
the speaker-discriminative contrastive termL spk. This stage es-
tablishes a high-quality reconstruction model while encourag-
ing factorization between the time-varying and global streams.
In the second stage, we introduce cycle-consistent speaker
swapping as the primary codec-internal self-supervision sig-
nal to verify and stabilize content preservation during speaker-
conditioned generation. The encoder and quantizer are frozen
to prevent the model from trivially satisfying the constraints by
shifting the analysis space, thereby forcing the decoder to pro-
duce speech that remains consistent under a fixed analysis trans-

3. Experiments
3.1. Implementation Details
CycleCodec is built on TiCodec and follows its original training
recipe. Unless otherwise specified, all hyperparameters are kept
the same as in TiCodec. To constrain the information capacity
of the time-varying stream, we use a single codebook of size
256 in the quantizer, yielding a bitrate of 0.6 kbps. For the
query-based Transformer aggregator, we useN= 8learnable
query tokens and a Transformer encoder withL= 4layers.
The model is trained on the 24 kHz LibriTTS corpus [25]. The
loss weights in Eq. (5) are chosen to keep each loss term on
comparable scales during training.

3.3. Baselines
We compare CycleCodec against two baselines, TiCodec [7]
and LSCodec [10], using the officially released checkpoints 2.
We evaluate the single-codebook variant of TiCodec and the
50 Hz configuration of LSCodec. For TiCodec, we condition
the decoder on the continuous global features extracted from
the encoder. TiCodec serves as a strong distillation-free base-
line. In contrast, LSCodec uses a pretrained WavLM model to
supervise both the content and speaker streams, so its results
should be interpreted as an upper bound for teacher-guided fac-
torization and not a strictly comparable distillation-free setting.

3.5. Disentanglement Performance
3.5.1. Standard Zero-Shot VC
We first evaluate disentanglement through a zero-shot VC set-
ting, which tests whether speaker identity can be controlled by
the global stream while the time-varying stream preserves con-
tent. Results on three languages are reported in Table 2. On
LibriTTS, LSCodec achieves the strongest VC performance,
which is consistent with its use of pretrained WavLM supervi-
sion for both content and speaker pathways under matched lan-
guage and domain. In the distillation-free setting, CycleCodec
improves over TiCodec by increasing speaker similarity and re-
ducing WER, indicating a cleaner separation between the time-
varying and global streams. Under language shift, the gap be-
comes clear. While LSCodec remains strong in speaker similar-
ity, its content preservation degrades substantially on Mandarin
and Vietnamese. However, CycleCodec maintains competi-
tive speaker similarity with lower WER. This indicates that en-
forcing factorization via codec-internal consistency constraints
leads to more stable controllability when pretrained teachers
are unavailable or do not transfer reliably across languages. A
small-scale listening test on English and Mandarin VC outputs
supports these trends: using 15 non-parallel pairs and 10 na-
tive listeners per language, CycleCodec improves naturalness
Mean Opinion Score (MOS) over TiCodec from 3.171/3.465 to
3.301/3.687 on English/Mandarin, and is preferred in A/B tests
for content preservation and speaker similarity by 57.5%/53.8%
and 59.3%/57.9%, respectively.
