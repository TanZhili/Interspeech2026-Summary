Vocal Effort Modulation Strategies: A Cross-Corpus Taxonomy with Noise
Robustness and ASR Implications
Lubos Marcinek1, Jonas Beskow1, Joakim Gustafson 1
1 Department of Speech, Music and Hearing, KTH Royal Institute of Technology, Sweden
lubosm@kth.se, beskow@kth.se, jocke@speech.kth.se
Abstract
Speakers adapt vocal output through diverse acoustic strate-
gies, yet inter-speaker variability remains poorly characterized.
We analyze 50 speakers from the A VID corpus at four effort
levels, fitting linear slopes for F0, RMS energy, spectral tilt,
and speaking rate, then applying K-means clustering. Three
stable clusters emerge: High Modulators (broad F0/energy
elevation), Spectro-Temporal speakers (spectral redistribution
and rate reduction; female-leaning), and Conservative Modu-
lators (minimal changes). ANOV A confirms significant dif-
ferences (allp <10 −5,η 2 = 0.25–0.55); gender shows a
directional but non-significant association with cluster mem-
bership within A VID (χ2 = 4.37,p= 0.113). A corrected
fold-wise LOSO classifier achieves 96–98% accuracy (macro-
F1 = 0.97). Three validations confirm robustness: noise recov-
erability (SNR≥+10dB, 12 noise types), French Lombard
replication (cosine = 0.873), and systematic ASR WER differ-
ences in Whisper and Wav2Vec2.
Index Terms: vocal effort, Lombard effect, speaker clustering,
acoustic strategy, noise robustness, speech recognition
1. Introduction
The Lombard effect, the reflexive vocal modification in noise,
is among the most studied phenomena in speech science [1, 2].
Speakers increase intensity, raise F0, alter spectral characteris-
tics, and reduce speaking rate [3, 4, 5], but the relative weight-
ing of each cue varies across speakers [6, 7, 8]. Despite
three decades of research, this inter-speaker variability has been
treated as noise around population averages rather than as a
meaningful signal [9, 10].
A review of the literature reveals a gap: to our knowl-
edge, no prior work has applied formal clustering to identify
distinct speaker strategy types from multidimensional acous-
tic profiles [9, 10]. Shen et al. [9] noted considerable varia-
tion without characterizing its structure; Traunm¨uller and Eriks-
son [11] identified gender-level differences without finer ty-
pologies; Tuomainen et al. [6] documented lifespan variability
in speech modifications without a strategy taxonomy.
This gap has direct consequences for speech technology.
Effort-controllable TTS systems apply uniform acoustic trans-
formations regardless of speaker identity [12, 13, 14]. These
systems condition on vocal effort but do not address speaker
strategy types. The A VID corpus [15] notes that inter-speaker
variability renders scalar effort labels noisy; we demonstrate
this variability reflects three distinct, stable strategies.
Our contributions are: (1) the first formal clustering of ef-
fort strategies in the A VID corpus, yielding three interpretable,
stable types; (2) a directional, non-significant gender associ-
ation with strategy type (χ 2 = 4.37,p= 0.113), partially
echoed by FLombard’s Spectro-Temporal female lean; (3) 96–
98% cross-validated cluster predictability (macro-F1 = 0.97);
(4) noise robustness validated across 840k noisy speech files
and 12 noise types; (5) cross-corpus replication in French Lom-
bard speech (cosine = 0.873); and (6) cluster-stratified ASR
analysis revealing systematic WER differences.
2. Related work
2.1. Inter-speaker variability in vocal effort
Individual differences in Lombard adaptation are well-
documented but rarely systematized. Severijnen et al. [16]
found distinct prosodic profiles in Dutch speakers, with some
speakers relying predominantly on F0 and others on duration
to mark prominence. Smiljanic and Gilbert [17] showed that
acoustic adaptations to noise follow similar patterns across chil-
dren, young adults, and older adults, with age-related differ-
ences primarily in the magnitude of adjustments rather than
in adaptation strategy type, confirming that non-F0 dimensions
vary systematically across speaker populations. Tuomainen et
al. [6] showed that the degree of speech modification in noise
varies widely across speakers and age groups, further motivat-
ing strategy-level modeling. Cavalcanti et al. [18] confirmed
that the discriminatory power of acoustic-phonetic parameters
varies across speakers and speaking styles.
To our knowledge, no prior work applies formal clustering
to derive a data-driven effort taxonomy. Shen et al. [9] docu-
ment large inter-speaker variability in clear-Lombard modifica-
tions across 78 speakers, but do not characterise its structure or
derive a speaker taxonomy. Spectral tilt is theoretically impor-
tant yet underexplored, flattening with increased vocal effort in
studies of Lombard and loud speech [19, 20]. Rate reduction is
among the most consistent Lombard findings [9, 6]. Nechansky
et al. [21] and Dahl et al. [22] provide complementary evidence
that speakers modulate voice quality and pitch independently
through distinct control mechanisms, consistent with the multi-
dimensional strategy view we adopt.
2.2. Effort control in TTS
Raitio et al. [12], Akti et al. [13], and Nishihara et al. [14] devel-
oped effort-conditioned TTS systems, each conditioning on vo-
cal effort without addressing speaker strategy types. Gustafson
et al. [23, 24] implemented articulatory effort as a continuous
parameter in conversational characters, enabling casual versus
projected speech styles without connecting to discrete speaker
strategy types. Toubar et al. [25] showed that office acoustic
environments systematically shape vocal level and communi-
cation style, further motivating speaker-adaptive effort mod-
eling for real-world scenarios. Jacquelin et al. [26] proposed

3. Method
3.1. Corpus
We use the A VID corpus [15]: 50 speakers (25 F, 25 M)
recorded at four effort levels (soft, normal, loud, very loud),
each producing 25 fixed sentences across two sessions (10,000
utterances total). We use the sentence task for controlled multi-
speaker comparison across identical phonetic material; gender
labels are from official A VID metadata.
3.2. Feature extraction
We model each speaker’s acoustic behavior across all four effort
levels by fitting ordinary least squares slopes, with effort level
as an ordinal predictor (soft = 0 through very loud = 3). Four
features are extracted:
F0 slope (Hz/level):Rate of change in median F0, ex-
tracted with Praat [28] via parselmouth (floor 75 Hz, ceiling
500 Hz, voiced frames only). Reported in Hz; a per-speaker
semitone re-analysis yields a moderately different partition (ad-
justed Rand index, ARI = 0.57 vs. Hz) with a significant gen-
der association under semitone-based clustering (χ 2 = 10.78,
p= 0.005), unlike the non-significant result for the primary
Hz-based taxonomy used throughout this paper (χ 2 = 4.37,
p= 0.113).
RMS slope (dB/level):Rate of change in RMS energy
(20·log10 scale). All slopes positive, confirming universal loud-
ness increase across all 50 speakers.
Spectral tilt slope:Rate of change in log-ratio of
high- to low-frequency energy (>/<1 kHz), following Lu and
Cooke [19], applied to Hann-windowed 25 ms frames, 10 ms
shift.
Rate slope (syll/s/level):Rate of change in syllables per
second via amplitude envelope peak-counting (r=−0.39
vs. independent measure,p= 0.04). Leave-one-feature-out
analysis confirms rate is a secondary structural driver (LOFO
ARI = 0.486) but diagnostically important for the Spectro-
Temporal cluster.
All features are z-score standardized. No per-session
normalization was applied; session effects are acknowledged
(ARI = 0.40 when per-session normalized).
3.3. Clustering andkselection
K-means clustering (scikit-learn, k-means++, 100 restarts) is
applied to the four-dimensional standardized feature space.
Three metrics guidekselection (k= 2–6): silhouette score
(best atk= 4: 0.248), gap statistic (best atk= 5: 0.844),
and stability via pairwise ARI across 30 random subsamples of
40 speakers (best atk= 3: ARI = 0.651; 1 = identical parti-
tions, 0 = chance agreement). We adoptk= 3based on highest
stability, balanced cluster sizes (12, 19, 19), and interpretability.
Critically,k= 2merely separates high from low modulators,
recovering information already encoded in the soft/loud anno-
tation. A sensitivity analysis atk= 4(Section 4.5) isolates a
small extreme subgroup (n= 5) that merges cleanly into C1 at
k= 3.

4.1. Cluster profiles
Table 1 shows mean slope values per cluster. Three distinct
strategies emerge, each coherent across all dimensions.
C1 – High Modulators(n= 12, 9M/3F, male-
leaning): Highest F0 slope (+32.9Hz/level) and RMS slope
(+7.7dB/level), both significantly exceeding other clusters
(Tukey HSD, allp<0.01).
C2 – Spectro-Temporal(n= 19, 7M/12F, female-
leaning): Significantly positive tilt slope (+0.12,p <
0.01vs. both other clusters) and strongest rate reduction
(−0.21syll/s/level,p <0.01). These speakers redistribute en-
ergy toward higher frequencies and progressively slow as effort
increases.
C3 – Conservative(n= 19, 9M/10F): Smallest
changes across all dimensions (F0:+11.8Hz/level; RMS:
+5.4dB/level; tilt:≈0; rate:−0.11syll/s/level).

4.4. Cluster predictability
Multinomial logistic regression evaluated by LOSO cross-
validation achieves 96–98% accuracy across K-means random
initializations (ARI = 0.93; macro-F1 = 0.97). The corrected
LOSO protocol recomputes clusters on the 49 training speakers
per fold and assigns the held-out speaker to the nearest centroid.
The single misclassified speaker (sp43, true C2 assigned to C1)
reflects shared F0/RMS elevation between the two clusters. Per-
class F1 scores: 0.97 (C1), 0.98 (C2), 0.98 (C3). The circular-
ity caveat applies: this characterizes internal separability rather
than generalization from independent features. Performance de-
grades under limited enrollment: 76% from 2 utterances per
effort level, rising to 84% with 10, suggesting practical applica-
bility even with minimal reference recordings.

4.5. Sensitivity analysis:k= 4
Atk= 4(silhouette = 0.248 vs. 0.239 atk= 3), C1 splits into
a main group (n= 7) and an extreme-modulation subgroup
(n= 5, 4M/1F) with the highest F0 and RMS slopes in the
corpus. These five speakers merge cleanly into C1 atk= 3,
confirming them as an extreme subset of the high-modulation
strategy. All other assignments are stable.
5. Validation 1: Noise robustness
We apply the full pipeline to 840,000 noise-mixed utterances:
all 50 speakers (A VID), 4 effort levels, 7 SNR levels (−15to
+15dB, 5 dB steps), and 12 noise types (bus, car, metro, traffic,
station, cafeteria, restaurant, meeting room, office, kitchen, liv-
ing room, washing machine). For each SNR, K-means (k= 3)
is applied independently and compared to clean-speech assign-
ments via ARI.
Cluster assignments are strongly recoverable at SNR≥
+10dB across all noise types (ARI = 0.86 overall; stationary:
0.86; speech/babble: 0.85). A steep stability transition oc-
curs between0and+5dB. Recovery falls near or at chance
below−5dB for speech and babble noise (ARI = 0.00 at−15
and−10dB), while stationary noise retains partial stability at
low SNRs (ARI = 0.24 at−5dB; 0.11 at−10dB). Stationary
and speech-like conditions converge above+10dB but diverge
at lower SNRs, confirming that SNR is the dominant determi-
nant of recovery while noise type governs the degradation floor.
These results establish+10dB SNR as the practical boundary
for cluster-based speaker profiling in noise.
6. Validation 2: Cross-corpus replication
We apply the same pipeline to FLombard [30], recorded at
four noise-induced Lombard levels (0, 65, 75, 85 dB SPL).
The Conservative type (n= 20) matches A VID C3 with co-
sine = 0.873—the strongest cross-corpus correspondence. Gen-
der replicates only partially: the Spectro-Temporal female
lean holds in both corpora (FLombard 4M/11F vs. A VID C2
7M/12F), but FLombard’s male-leaning Conservative cluster
(13M/7F,χ 2 = 5.31,p= 0.070) is not mirrored in A VID
C3, which is near-balanced (9M/10F).
To confirm corpus-specific clustering as the appropriate de-
sign, joint K-means on the pooled 88-speaker dataset yielded
assignments associated with corpus origin rather than strategy
type (χ2 = 62.34,p<0.001; Davies-Bouldin 1.37 vs. 1.04 for
corpus-specific).
7. Validation 3: ASR consequences
We compute WER per cluster using Whisper-base and
Wav2Vec2-base on the 840,000 noise-mixed utterances with
TIMIT reference transcriptions (A VID corpus). Contrary to the
prediction that larger modulation would improve ASR robust-
ness, C3 (Conservative) consistently shows lower WER than
C1 (High Modulators) across all SNR levels and both models
(Wav2Vec2 ANOV A:η2 = 0.026,p <0.0001). The order-
ing C3<C2<C1 holds at all SNR levels tested. All clusters
show a U-shaped WER pattern across effort levels; C3 shows
the flattest deterioration at maximum effort. This reversed or-
dering suggests current ASR models penalize the large acoustic
deviations of High Modulator strategy more than they benefit
from increased acoustic contrast—likely because both systems
are trained predominantly on modal speech—with direct impli-
cations for cluster-stratified training data augmentation.
8. Discussion
The three clusters represent distinct strategies with large ef-
fect sizes (η 2 = 0.25–0.55) and strong predictability (96–
98%, macro-F1 = 0.97). Full trajectory modeling is more in-
formative than endpoint contrasts for the acoustic clustering
itself: predictability improves from 84% (pairwise soft→loud
features) to 96–98% (all-levels slopes). Gender, by contrast, re-
mains directional but non-significant within A VID alone (p=
0.113), though the Spectro-Temporal female lean is echoed
in FLombard. These results establish the acoustic foundation
for cluster-conditioned speaker-adaptive TTS: a Conservative
speaker (+5.4dB/level,+11.8Hz/level) and a High Modulator
(+7.7dB/level,+32.9Hz/level) require fundamentally differ-
ent synthesis strategies. Effort-conditioning systems by Raitio
et al. [12], Akti et al. [13], Nishihara et al. [14], and Gustafson
et al. [23, 24] all lack this speaker-strategy dimension; our
lightweight classifier (4 features, logistic regression) can be
prepended to any TTS architecture with negligible overhead.
Beyond these synthesis implications, the physiological basis of
each strategy merits closer scrutiny.
The Conservative cluster warrants attention: qualitative
inspection suggests possible increased breathiness, consistent
with a different phonatory mechanism—not simply less effort.
This aligns with Dahl et al. [22] showing that speakers modu-
late pitch via distinct sensorimotor mechanisms, and Nechansky
et al. [21] demonstrating that voice quality can be deliberately
controlled (e.g., breathiness, creakiness) with distinct acoustic
and perceptual effects. These acoustic and physiological ob-
servations are nonetheless preliminary and come with several
caveats.
Limitations:The silhouette score atk= 3(0.239) is mod-
est, reflecting the continuous nature of phonatory strategies.
GMM yielded a different partition (ARI = 0.23 vs. K-means);
stability (ARI = 0.651) remains the primary evidence for robust-
ness. The FLombard replication is partial: the High Modula-
tor profile differs between English instructed effort and French
noise-induced Lombard, potentially reflecting language differ-
ences in F0 use under noise. Gender did not reach significance
(p= 0.113); the directional female lean in C2 should be repli-
cated in larger corpora [9, 6, 18] before mechanistic conclusions
are drawn.
9. Conclusion
We present the first formal clustering analysis of vocal effort
strategies in the A VID corpus, validated across three indepen-
dent analyses. K-means on linear acoustic slopes yields three
stable, interpretable types: High Modulators (broad F0/energy
elevation), Spectro-Temporal speakers (spectral redistribution
and rate reduction; female-leaning), and Conservative Modu-
lators (minimal changes, roughly gender-balanced). All clus-
ter differences are statistically significant (η 2 = 0.25–0.55);
cluster membership is predicted with 96–98% accuracy (macro-
F1 = 0.97). Noise robustness analysis confirms strong recov-
erability (ARI>0.85) at SNR≥+10dB across 12 noise
types. Cross-corpus replication in FLombard shows Conserva-
tive cluster correspondence (cosine = 0.873) and a directionally
replicating female lean in the Spectro-Temporal cluster, though
the A VID gender association itself remains non-significant (p=
0.113). Cluster-stratified ASR analysis reveals systematic WER
ordering (C3<C2<C1). These results bridge a documented gap
between the Lombard effect literature and the practical demands
of noise-robust, speaker-adaptive speech technology.
