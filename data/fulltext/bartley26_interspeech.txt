Bootstrapping Endangered Language ASR with Short-Form Corpora

Abstract
Nearly half of the world’s languages are endangered. Speech
technologies, such as Automatic Speech Recognition (ASR),
have been shown to be central to revival efforts, yet most lan-
guages remain unsupported because standard pipelines expect
utterance-level supervised data. In this paper, we explore using
speech-text pairs aligned at the level of words or short phrases
as a more accessible entry point than conventional utterance-
level corpora. Using a standard English ASR pipeline, we show
how much performance is impacted by substituting a conven-
tional corpus with short-form speech. We then source short-
form data for 5 typologically diverse endangered languages
and use them to force-align long-form speech, creating new
utterance-level speech datasets for each language 1. Finally, we
build ASR systems and compare them to SOTA multilingual
models (OmniASR, MMS, Whisper), showing that better out-
of-domain (OOD) performance can be achieved at a fraction of
the computational cost.
Index Terms: automatic speech recognition, low-resource
1. Introduction
More than 1,400 of the world’s∼7,000 languages have fewer
than 1,000 speakers, and one disappears every two weeks on
average. Reversing this trend depends on community effort
to strengthen transmission and expand learning opportunities
[1]. Speech technologies, like Automatic Speech Recogni-
tion (ASR), have been shown to be central to these efforts
[2, 3, 4, 5, 6], making oral archives more accessible to learn-
ers, educators, and researchers. However, most endangered lan-
guages remain unsupported by modern systems as they are as-
sumed to lack what is believed to be the necessary resource,
an utterance-level speech corpus (3–15 s speech segments with
verbatim transcriptions).
Whether endangered or not, languages with limited re-
sources are often referred to as ”long tail languages” [7],
as they comprise the sparsely represented portion of glob-
ally distributed language data. In ASR, the prevailing strat-
egy to address this problem is to pretrain models on vast
amounts of multilingual speech and transfer this knowledge
to underrepresented languages, either through zero- or few-
shot generalisation [8] or by fine-tuning on a limited amount
of target-language data [9]. Omnilingual ASR scales this
paradigm further, pretraining a 7B-parameter self-supervised
feature encoder and combining it with various decoder architec-
tures and training end-to-end (E2E) on 120k hours of labelled

marginalise endangered-language communities via two mech-

Secondly, these systems typically require supervised speech
aligned at theutterance-level. This narrow formatting require-
ment advocated by industry and academia ignores a plethora
of transcribed resources that already exist in many endangered
languages.
By contrast, language communities tend to create speech
resources in different formats. The most common is long-form
speech, such as text narrations or recorded interviews. If tran-
scriptions exist, they are typically contiguous blocks of text
and are thus unsuitable for speech systems due to the lack of
utterance-level alignment. Another common resource is short-
form speech (0-2 s segments), where spoken words or phrases
are transcribed for educational purposes e.g., asay this word
pronunciation exercise. Examples includeForvo[11], a crowd-
sourced pronunciation resource covering over 430 languages;
Talking Dictionariesprovides short-form data in over 200 en-
dangered languages [12];FirstVoicesprovides spoken word
data for 93 Native American languages and dialects [13], all
of which are endangered. Additionally, a plethora of language
language-specific sources exist, such as theLearnManx Spoken
Dictionary[14], theHawaiian Language Pronunciation Guide
[15], and Lakota language learning resources [16]. In contrast
to long-form data, short-form can be used directly for super-
vised speech technologies, however the effectiveness of such an
approach for ASR has not been explored.
In this work, we askDoes using short-form corpora for ASR
incur a drop-off in performance compared to utterance-level?,
Can leveraging in-the-wild speech help to overcome computa-
tional and resource barriers?, andHow do SOTA multilingual
models perform on OOD tests in endangered languages?We
address these questions with a series of experiments and make
the following contributions:
i) Using English, we establish the viability of ASR using short-
form speech data instead of utterance-level corpora.
ii) We use short-form speech ASR to force-align and segment
long-form recordings to better leverage existing data.
iii) We create and open-source utterance-level speech corpora for
five endangered languages.
iv) We compare the OOD performance of state-of-the-art

2. Prior Work
2.1. Independence Assumptions in ASR
Independence and conditional independence assumptions have
historically been a key driver of computational tractability in
ASR systems. At the lowest level, Hidden Markov Models
(HMMs) assume conditional independence of acoustic frames
given the hidden state sequence [17]; a feature that was criti-
cised as insufficient for modelling the temporal complexities of
natural speech [18]. More recently, however, frame-level con-
ditional independence assumptions are seldom found in ASR
systems due to the shift towards neural architectures [19, 9, 7].
Instead of frames, these models operate over a bounded con-
text window defined by a training unit that roughly corresponds
to a unit of language. These commonly consist of phrases or
sentences, and are most often assumed to be independent and
identically distributed (IID).
At the other end of the scale, work in long-context ASR ex-
pands the IID assumption, accommodating dependencies across
the typical 30 second boundary [20]. Recent work takes this fur-
ther, finding significant gains in modelling dependencies in se-
quences up to 1-hour in length, especially during domain shifts
[21]. In the middle of these extremes is the utterance; the de
facto training unit that has governed standard practice in ASR
since the 1990s [22, 23]. While there is no consensus on ex-
actly what constitutes an ”utterance”, speech corpora are com-
monly distributed in sentence-like speech-text pairs, typically
3-15 seconds in length [24, 25]. Utterances ensure computa-
tional tractability and are considered to be linguistically mean-
ingful. Yet, they often don’t make sense in isolation. Whether
utterances are in fact more informative than sub-utterance units,
such as words or short-phrases, has not been explored.
2.2. Supervised Learning with Limited Resources
Isolated words and short phrases are commonly used in speech
tasks outside of ASR, such as spoken digit recognition [26], in-
tent recognition [27], and keyword spotting [28]. Such tasks are
referred to aslimited-vocabulary speech recognition[29] and
are considered to be separate from continuous speech recog-
nition systems. On the other hand, limiting resource quan-
tity is a much more explored topic in ASR. For instance, the
IARPA BABEL program used as little as three hours of tele-
phone speech [30, 31] to build ASR. Zero- or few-shot adapta-
tion have pushed requirements lower [32, 33], although the suc-
cess of these approaches often depends on similarities between
the target language and those seen during pre-training [34].
Driving down supervision requirements is also a common
topic in a variety of NLP tasks [35, 36, 37], however this re-
search often considers not just quantity, but also other formats,
particularly those which are expected to be common to many
languages. For instance, bilingual dictionaries have proven
highly effective in machine translation, both as the primary
source of supervision [38] and as components integrated di-
rectly into model architectures [39]. Similarly, MTOB (Ma-
chine Translation from One Book) is a benchmark that asks
large language models to translate Kalamang (∼200 speakers)
by reading a single grammar book [40]. Similar techniques are
yet to be applied in ASR.
3. Experimental Setup
We begin our experiments with English to establish the viabil-
ity of training on word-level rather than utterance-level data,
quantifying the performance trade-offs between the two (sec-
tion 3.1). In section 3.2, we evaluate whether a seed model
trained on short-form speech can be used to force-align and
segment long-form recordings into new utterance-level corpora
for five endangered languages. Finally, in section 3.3, we use
these corpora to train ASR models, assessing their performance
against SOTA multilingual model (Omnilingual, MMS, Whis-
per) on both in-domain and out-of-domain (OOD) test sets.
All experiments are conducted using HMM-based systems
built with the Kaldi toolkit [41]. The reason for this choice
is threefold: it ensures computational accessibility for lan-
guage communities since training and inference can be per-
formed entirely on CPU; it provides recipes for training models
from scratch, allowing for fairer comparisons between different
forms of supervision; HMM-based systems have been proven
to work well for the forced alignment of established datasets
like LibriSpeech [24], making it an ideal choice for segmenting
long-form recordings.
3.1. From Utterances to Words
To measure the effect of utterance length, we focus on English
data to ensure comparability with established ASR research. We
first obtain shorter-form versions of LibriSpeechtrain-clean-
100using the same forced alignment and segmentation steps de-
scribed in the paper [24]. We adjust silence and min/max length
thresholds to produce progressively smaller segments, with the
shortest corpus having an average length of 0.35s which is close
to the average word duration in LibriSpeech (0.30s).
For training, we use the standard LibriSpeech recipe from
the Kaldi toolkit 2. The final model is a triphone GMM-HMM
system trained with Linear Discriminant Analysis (LDA) en-
hanced with Maximum Likelihood Linear Transforms (MLLT)
and Speaker Adaptive Training (SAT) using fMLLR. While the
standard recipe uses speaker-level CMVN, we calculate CMVN
statistics on a per-segment basis to reflect endangered language
scenarios where speaker information is likely unavailable. We
usetest-cleanto report our results and use the TIMIT [23] and
TED-LIUM [25] test sets to assess OOD performance. We re-
port %WER results using the pre-built 4-gram ARPA language
model used in LibriSpeech (fglarge). This process is repeated
for each granularity, ranging from 12.03 s to 0.35 s utterances
(avg. duration). To better simulate low-resource conditions, we
perform the same test for 10-hour, 3-hour, and 1-hour subsets
oftrain-clean-100.
Across domains and subset sizes, figure 1, shows that
%WER results remain stable or improve until the average ut-
terance length moves below 1 second. This stability suggests
that a second of speech provides the HMM with enough acous-
tic context to model coarticulation across word boundaries; past
this point, WER rises significantly. The average relative rise in
%WER from the originaltrain-clean-100to 0.35 s is +19.97 for
test-cleanand +14.27 for TED-LIUM and TIMIT, suggesting
that the effect of shorter utterances is less pronounced for OOD
data. A similar effect can be seen at smaller data budgets, with
degradation being the smallest for the 1-hour set (100h: +17.05;
10h: +17.80; 3h: +15.66; 1h: +14.16). Taken together, these
suggest that the relative importance of the standard utterance-
level format diminishes as data becomes scarce.

3.2. Aligning Long-Form Speech
Given we have established short-form resources as a viable al-
ternative, we ask whether we can use such resources to boot-
strap the forced alignment of pre-existing long-form data in
endangered languages. To do this, we choose 5 endangered
languages from 4 typologically and geographically diverse lan-
guage families: Indo-European (Cornish, Manx), Austronesian
(Hawaiian), Koreanic (Jejueo), and Iroquoian (Mohawk). We
collect data from various sources 3 and split them according to
language and whether they are short- or long-form. We then
convert all audio to a common format of 16-bit PCM, single-
channel W A V , with a sampling rate of 16 kHz. We take the same
approach to text normalisation for all languages: we standard-
ise whitespace and remove all punctuation except for intra-word
punctuation (e.g.,mother-in-law) to preserve lexical meaning.
We replace diacritic markers with their canonical variants (e.g.,
C ¸→C;´E→E; ˜N→N). We then remove non-ASCII characters
and convert to uppercase (exc. Jejueo due to Hangul script). To
perform forced alignment, we use the same LibriSpeech [24]
recipe as before with two changes (figure 2). Firstly, we adopt
a Unicode graphemic lexicon [42] since most endangered lan-
guages do not have a phonemic pronunciation dictionary. Sec-
ondly, instead of using a pre-built acoustic model, we train a
monophone GMM–HMM 4 on our short-form speech collec-
tions which is then used to bootstrap Viterbi forced alignment
[17] of the long-form audio. We train a triphone model using
these alignments which is used together with biased language
models (LMs) to decode the long-form audio. The subsequent
Smith–Waterman alignment and segmentation steps remain the
same as [24].

model capable of force-aligning a larger set of long-form data
with>70% success. These enabled bootstrapped triphone sys-
tems which, when used for decoding, resulted in highly accu-
rate hypotheses (<5% WER) for 3 out of 5 languages. Cornish
in particular aligned smoothly; we attribute this to the Cornish

For training, we combine thetrain-shandtrain-utsets. For
our base multilingual models, we use the HuggingFace infer-
ence pipelines for Omnilingual ASR (LLM 7B) [7], MMS (1B-
all) [8], and Whisper (large-v3) [9] to decode our test sets.
To explore a fine-tuning approach, we also fine-tune Whisper
(large-v3) using the SpeechBrain toolkit [44], applying LoRA
for 1 epoch on each language’s training set. For the HMM-
based models, we reuse the standard LibriSpeech training script
ending on the triphone GMM-HMM (LDA+MLLT+SAT). Ad-
ditionally we include the training step that uses the GMM-
HMM alignments to bootstrap a TDNN model trained with
Lattice-Free Maximum Mutual Information (LF-MMI). As pre-
viously, we build the word list and 4-gram LM from the train-
ing text. The total words and out-of-vocabulary (OOV) rates for
these are shown in table 2.
The OOV rates for our word lists built from our training
data are not ideal, with the worst being Jejueo (52.30%). To
mitigate this, we collect external text data from various sources,
including MAD-LAD-400 [45] for Manx, Cornish, and Hawai-
ian, and language specific text corpora for all languages. We
use the same text normalisation described in 3.2 and make a
new word list for each language by extracting unique words
that occur more than once in the data. We then make a final
pass through the word list to cull obvious gibberish/malformed
words. Table 2 shows an average improvement in %OOV of
13.11 per language from this step. Jejueo and Mohawk bene-
fitted the least due to the text scarcity. From here, we train a
4-gram language model which we use for a second decode of
the HMM-based systems.
Our ASR results are summarised in table 2. Our most naive
model (GMM) beats the best zero-shot multilingual model re-
sults in all but one language (Jejueo). This highlights the po-
tential gains from leveraging existing resources and is encour-
aging to those with limited compute resources. The strongest
system overall was the DNN-HMM model with an augmented
LM, suggesting that language-specific models built with CPU
are still preferable to end-to-end (E2E) models such as Whisper.
The external LMs were key to this performance, accounting for
an average WER reduction of 5.82% per language across the
HMM-based systems. Where text was scarce, we found E2E
fine-tuning to be superior, as demonstrated by our Whisper-FT
model achieving the best performance on Jejueo and Mohawk
(ood). Importantly, Jejueo and Mohawk remain extremely chal-
lenging regardless of model class, highlighting the need for fur-
ther research into endangered language ASR, particularly those
with diverse typologies.
4. Conclusion
ASR support for endangered languages remains sparse, princi-
pally due to a lack of utterance-level supervision. Addressing
this technological gap, we have shown that ASR can be attained
for endangered languages that have no utterance-level super-
vised data by leveraging resources such as short- and long-form
corpora. Our results highlight the flexibility of HMMs in inte-
grating short- and long-form supervision and text information
to deliver usable ASR (both HMM and E2E based) from un-
conventional resources. Ultimately, this paper has shown that
developing speech technology for low-resource and endangered
languages does not require utterance-level corpora as a starting
point. Collecting new and clean resources is an important task,
but future work would do well to make sense of the data that al-
ready exist and continue to be created. Doing so may mean the
difference to the most urgent cases of language endangerment.
