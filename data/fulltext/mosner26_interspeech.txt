Abstract
We present our submission to the TidyV oice 2026 Chal-
lenge along with a systematic study of language-variability
compensation across the entire speaker verification pipeline.
We investigate multilingual fine-tuning and language compen-
sation techniques at the embedding, back-end, and score lev-
els. Each approach independently yields consistent, compa-
rable gains, confirming that explicitly addressing language ef-
fects is crucial. We further show that powerful back-ends such
as PSVM and SG-TPSDA perform strongly with language-
entangled embeddings, but their advantage decreases when lan-
guage variability is mitigated at the front-end via adversarial
training with gradient reversal. Our submitted system achieves
2.53% EER on tv26 eval-A.
Index Terms: TidyV oice, language variability compensation,
gradient reversal, domain invariance
1. Introduction
Research in the speaker verification (SV) field has been mostly
fostered by two driving forces: 1) extensive datasets and chal-
lenges built around them, and 2) a series of speaker recogni-
tion evaluations (SRE) organized by the National Institute of
Standards and Technology (NIST). Within the former category,
multiple large-scale training and/or evaluation datasets, such as
V oxCeleb [1], V oxBlink2 [2], or CN-Celeb [3], have been pub-
lished. However, these datasets predominantly contain mono-
lingual speakers (English or Chinese) and focus on wideband
(16 kHz) audio. In contrast, the NIST SRE series has histori-
cally prioritized telephony/narrowband data, with recent itera-
tions moving toward non-mainstream languages. Specifically,
SRE21 [4] and SRE24 [5] introduced multilingual trials; how-
ever, these evaluations also introduced confounding variables,
such as mismatched enrollment/test sources (e.g., telephone
speech vs. audio from video), which can obscure the isolated
impact of language variation on system performance.
The recently announced TidyV oice 2026 Challenge [6] and
dataset [7] focus on the influence of language diversity across
speaker recognition trials, complementing the above-mentioned
directions. The challenge organizers provided a comprehensive
benchmark comprising training and development data from 40
languages. To evaluate model generalization beyond the 40 lan-
guages, the evaluation set includes additional 38 unseen lan-
guages. Although training data was provided, the challenge al-
lowed for the use of external data sources (excluding Mozilla
Common V oice) and pre-trained models.
Speaker and channel variability modeling has been a
long-standing research problem, primarily addressed by back-
ends [8, 9]. We drew inspiration from recent developments [10]
to address the specific channel of interest – language. Language
invariance can also be framed as domain invariance and adver-
sarial training has been shown to be effective in promoting it
across various speech-related tasks [11, 12, 13, 14]. Enabled by
TidyV oiceX, we explore it in the context of SV .
In this paper, we detail our submission to the TidyV oice
2026 Challenge with the following contributions: 1) We show
that the language variability compensation in speaker verifica-
tion leads to consistent improvements in a language-diverse sce-
nario. 2) We analyze the compensation at different levels: em-
bedding extractor, back-end, and scores. We show that similar
gains can be achieved independently with different techniques.
3) We also analyze the impact of additional multilingual data
during fine-tuning of the embedding extractor.
2. Language variability compensation
2.1. Embedding extractor level
Conventional speaker embedding extractors are typically
trained using a cross-entropy objective with speaker identity la-
bels. In multilingual datasets such as TidyV oiceX, where speak-
ers speak in multiple languages, the network may reduce train-
ing loss by exploiting both speaker and language cues. This
can result in language information being entangled within the
learned embeddings. While a sufficiently powerful back-end
might compensate for this effect, we instead promote language-
agnostic representations directly at the embedding level.
Inspired by adversarial domain adaptation [15, 16], we uti-
lize the joint availability of speaker and language labels in
TidyV oiceX to suppress language-dependent information in the
latent space. As shown in Figure 1, in the upper branch su-
pervised with the ArcFace loss (L s) [17], frame-level features
are pooled into an utterance-level representation and projected
into speaker embedding space. In parallel, the utterance-level
features are fed to a language classification branch without em-
bedding normalization, serving to compute a cross-entropy lan-
guage loss (L l). A Gradient Reversal Layer (GRL) is inserted
after pooling in the language branch to encourage language-
invariant representations in the shared backbone. The network
is trained using a multi-task objective:L=L s +L l.Through
this adversarial setup, the language branch is trained to identify
the spoken language, while the shared front-end is simultane-
ously updated to suppress language-discriminative information,
effectively “purifying” the speaker embeddings.
2.2. Feature Domain Augmentation with DSU
To improve robustness under cross-lingual mismatch, we adopt
a feature domain augmentation strategy based on modeling Do-
main Shifts with Uncertainty (DSU) [18], which has been ef-
fectively used in spoofing detection [19].

DSU perturbs internal feature statistics during training to
simulate assumed unseen domain shifts. First, channel-wise
mean and standard deviation are computed for each sample by
accumulating the feature maps in convolutional networks over
the spatial dimensions. These quantities define means of two
distributions estimated over the batch, from which samples are
drawn to perturb internal features. We refer the reader to [18]
for a detailed description.
2.3. Back-end level
To address language variability, we also investigated two back-
ends: the Pairwise Support Vector Machine (PSVM) of [20, 21],
which has proven robust against channel and language variabil-
ity [10], and a novel Spherical-Gaussian variation (SG-TPSDA)
of the Toroidal Probabilistic Spherical Discriminant Analysis
of [9], which replaces the spherical likelihood of the original
TPSDA with an isotropic Gaussian likelihood. The back-end
models have been chosen for their proven effectiveness in the
speaker verification task and their natural ability to decouple
speaker and non-speaker variability. PSVM achieves the de-
coupling implicitly, through an objective function that naturally
tends to identify speaker-rich subspaces in its input space. The
TPSDA model, on the other hand, can include multiple explicit
speaker and channel variability factors, although in our experi-
ments we employed only a single speaker subspace.
To further reduce the impact of language information, we
also explored an additional pre-processing step that employs
Linear Discriminant Analysis (LDA) to project away the domi-
nant language directions. In particular, we compute a discrim-
inant language subspace, whose dimensionality is tuned on the
development set, and project the embedding onto its comple-
ment.
2.4. Score level
Lastly, we attempted language compensation at the score level
by using a language comparison score as a quality mea-
sure [22]. The motivation is that language similarity may affect
the speaker verification score to some extent, which therefore
should be reduced in same- or similar-language trials compared
to different-language trials. Language comparison scores were
obtained by training a ResNet18 [23] language classifier on the
TidyV oiceX training data, extracting embeddings from the de-
velopment data, and scoring the trials using cosine similarity
with mean subtraction.
3. Experimental setup
3.1. Training data
Although the TidyV oiceX training data includes examples from
3666 speakers, the total duration is only 370 h. Therefore, we
sought to expand the training data while maintaining multilin-
guality. We opted for two additional datasets: NIST SRE CTS
Superset [24] and V oxCeleb2 dev [1]. Despite CTS Superset
comprising telephony data, its merit lies in the number of speak-
ers (6867) and duration (6193 h). Moreover, it provides infor-
mation on 52 spoken languages, including multilingual speak-
ers. To ensure compatibility with our target domain, CTS data
was upsampled to 16 kHz. V oxCeleb2 dev is a standard dataset
in the SV community. Not only does it contain utterances of
5994 speakers totaling 2369 h of speech, but it also comprises
multiple languages. However, since V oxCeleb2 language meta-
data is not available, we employ language pseudo-labels 1 ob-
tained using an ECAPA-TDNN trained on V oxLingua107 [25]
when training under the language compensation strategy.
3.2. Extractor architectures
w2v-BERT 2.0 with MFAAs one of the front-ends, we em-
ployed w2v-BERT 2.0 [26] with layer adapters [27] and MFA
(Multi-scale Feature Aggregation) embedding extractor [28] as
presented in [29]. The following reasons motivated our choice.
First, the w2v-BERT 2.0 encoder was pre-trained on 4.5 M
hours of speech spanning 143 languages in a self-supervised
fashion. Second, Li et al. [29] showed that a strong speaker
embedding extractor can be built by appending the w2v-BERT
2.0 encoder with layer adapters and the MFA module and even-
tually fine-tuning with a large-margin strategy (LMFT) [30] on
V oxCeleb2 data. The LMFT version, available online2, served
as our starting point, which we adapted.
Since MFA was pre-trained on V oxCeleb2 and V oxBlink2,
only the weights representing TidyV oice speaker prototypes
were initialized from scratch. We updated them with a 5×
higher learning rate than the remaining weights, which were
optimized with a learning rate scheduled from10 −3 to5·10 −5
during 5 epochs using SGD. ArcFace employed a margin of 0.3
and a scale of 32. Input segments were 2 s long.
SimAMResNetWe explored ResNet-based architectures
as part of our front-ends. In particular, we evaluated SimAM-
ResNet100, a larger version of the baseline SimAMResNet34,
in which Simple Attention Modules (SimAM) [31] are inserted
into ResNet blocks to enhance feature representation. In ad-
dition, Attentive Statistics Pooling (AST) [32] is used instead
of conventional temporal statistics pooling to better aggregate
speaker information at the utterance level. We used a V oxBlink2
pre-trained model2 and, as detailed in Section 4.1, explored dif-
ferent data configurations during 6 epochs of LMFT, using a
learning rate decaying exponentially from5·10 −5 to1·10 −5.
We employed a segment duration of 6 s and exponentially up-
dated the margin from 0 to 0.3 over the first 3 epochs, as using
a fixed higher margin led to worse results on the tv26 dev set.
When experimenting with DSU, we trained six model vari-
ants by inserting the DSU module at different positions, includ-
ing after the initial convolution and after each ResNet stage.
During inference, the DSU module is disabled. We selected the
variant that performed best on the development set.

3.3. Back-end model
The PSVM, Spherical-Gaussian TPSDA (SG-TPSDA), and
the LDA complement projection models were trained on the
TidyV oiceX training dataset. Both the LDA and SG-TPSDA
subspace dimensions were tuned on the development set us-
ing the embeddings from networks that do not include lan-
guage compensation. The LDA space dimension was set to 15,
whereas the SG-TPSDA speaker subspace dimension was set to
120. For PSVM, we scaled the “default” regularizer employed
in [20] by a factor of 5, based on the results on the development
set. PSVM embeddings were length-normalized, whereas the
SG-TPSDA model did not use length normalization.
4. Experiments
4.1. Front-end training data
We present the results obtained with different fine-tuning data
in Table 2. We primarily focus on tv26 dev, but we also com-
plement the analysis with V oxCeleb1-hard (V ox1-H) for better
insights. Intuitively, fine-tuning on the TidyV oiceX training set
improves the performance of both our front-ends on tv26 dev
while degrading results on V ox1-H due to a mismatch in lan-
guages and domain (read vs. spontaneous speech) compared
to the pre-trained versions. The introduction of V oxCeleb2 dev
caused mixed outcomes. It substantially improved SimAMRes-
Net100’s performance on tv26 dev but degraded that of w2v-
BERT 2.0 with MFA. We hypothesize that the former benefited
from the increase in the number of training speakers, whereas
the latter model, pre-trained on massively multilingual data, de-
graded due to fine-tuning on predominantly English data. The
results on V ox1-H are consistent, showing the benefit of in-
domain fine-tuning data. CTS Superset, although belonging
to a different domain, further improved SimAMResNet100 on
tv26 dev, highlighting the importance of both its extended dura-
tion and the diversity of multilingual speakers. For w2v-BERT
2.0 with MFA, its multilingualism just helped the model com-
pared to an unfavorable T+V combination. Despite the size of
CTS Superset, we observe only a negligible but consistent dif-
ference on V ox1-H.
4.2. Front-end-level language compensation
In Table 1, we present the impact of the front-end-level tech-
niques targeting language agnosticity described in Section 2.
Based on the results on the tv26 dev set, we conclude that
both gradient reversal and DSU provide a performance boost
for SimAMResNet100, with GRL being overall superior. Im-
portantly, gradient reversal and DSU can be applied simultane-
ously. Our results reveal the complementarity of the two tech-
niques, as the combination outperforms either method alone.

trials. In order to show the effect of our techniques, it is in-

different language (imp̸=), 2) target trials with the same lan-
guage and impostor trials with the same language, 3) target tri-
als with a different language and impostor trials with a different
language, and 4) target trials with a different language and im-
postor trials with the same language. The results on the subsets
reveal the behavior of our initial models (without language com-

differ from those with the GRL technique. We note that we ex-

BERT 2.0. Due to a negligible effect, we do not present the
results here. We intend to investigate it further in the future. We
also stress that our analysis was performed using the tv26 dev

ability of labels for the evaluation trial lists at the time of sub-

Table 1 also shows that MinDCF with a target trial prob-
ability of 0.01 defined by the challenge approaches 1 for trial
sublists that contain impostor trials with the same language. To
explain this behavior, we show the distribution of scores across
all trial sublists in Figure 2. Subfigures 2b and 2d depict the
second mode of the non-target score distribution, which affects
the MinDCF values, suggesting potential issues with some of
the labels. Figure 2 also shows favorable changes in distribu-
tions after incorporating GRL, which are more pronounced for
the non-target trials.
4.3. Back-end-level language compensation and submission
Table 3 presents, among other results, those for back-end-
level language compensation. System 1 comprises SimAM
ResNet100 + PSVM (with length-normalized embeddings), and
system 2 combines w2v-BERT 2.0 w/ MFA + SG-TPSDA
(without length normalization). The two models can be com-
pared with models 1 and 5 (with no compensation) in Table 1,
respectively. The comparison shows the value of the back-ends.
Table 3 also shows systems that combine both embedding-
level and back-end-level language compensation. Rows with
models 4 through 7 present results for the same back-ends
trained with front-end-level language-compensated embed-
dings. We can observe that the back-end-level compensation is,
in this case, much less effective — for both front-ends, good
results are achieved only when the number of LDA dimen-
sions is drastically reduced, and for the w2v-BERT 2.0 embed-
dings, we still observe a degradation relative to cosine scoring
on the development set. On the other hand, we can observe that
language-compensated back-ends trained on the original, non-
language-compensated embeddings still achieve the best results
on the development set. This suggests that while front-end-level
compensation is indeed effective, it may still incur a loss of
speaker information that could be effectively extracted by ad-
hoc back-end models.
Finally, Table 3 summarizes the results of our final submis-
sion to the challenge. It includes back-end level language com-
pensation and consists of the fusion of systems 1 and 2. The
fusion weights have been estimated on the development set by
means of prior-weighted logistic regression [33, 34], with a tar-
get prior set to 0.5. The best single system (2) and the fusion
(3) consistently outperform the baseline on all the trial lists.
4.4. Score-level language compensation
To analyze the impact of score-level language compensation,
we divided the development set randomly into two parts with
non-overlapping speakers, each having 100k trials. We used
the first part for mean subtraction before cosine scoring and
to train a logistic regression calibration model with language-
comparison scores as quality measures. For system 5 in Table 1
(which does not include language compensation), this improved
the EER on the second part from1.28%to1.10%, while for
system 6 (which uses adversarial language compensation) the
improvement was negligible (from0.79%to0.78%EER).
5. Conclusion
This paper focused on addressing the harmful effects of lan-
guage variability in SV trials, a problem brought to the commu-
nity’s attention by the TidyV oice 2026 Challenge.
We evaluated multiple pre-trained front-ends and high-
lighted the importance of appropriate fine-tuning data. En-
forcing language-invariant embeddings via a gradient reversal
in a language classifier significantly improved performance,
while comparable gains were achieved by combining language-
entangled embeddings with suitable back-end models. We also
demonstrated improvements using score-level compensation by
introducing language-comparison scores.
