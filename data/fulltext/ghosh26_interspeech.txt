Abstract
Speech anonymisation aims to conceal the speaker’s identity
while retaining task-relevant information. Existing methods
struggle with acoustic patterns rarely seen during training, such
as dysfluencies in pathological speech. This fragility arises from
purely data-driven spectral envelope estimation without physical
constraints. We introduce Phy-VC, a voice conversion method that
enforces articulatory-acoustic coupling as an inductive bias through
a differentiable vocal tract resonance model. Trained on standard
speech, Phy-VC improves intelligibility, prosody, and naturalness
over state-of-the-art baselines on diverse speech patterns, including
stuttering, dementia, elderly, and emotion datasets, while achieving
effective anonymisation. Expert assessment confirms significant
preservation of clinically relevant speech attributes. Further, the
articulatory representation enables interpretable, systematic control
of speaker variation.

1. Introduction
Speech-based digital biomarkers are increasingly used for health
assessment [1], particularly in neurological conditions such as
dementia [2, 3] and affective monitoring [4, 5]. Progress in this
area requires sharing clinical speech data. However, speech carries
intrinsic speaker identity cues and is susceptible to voice cloning
and re-identification attacks [6]. Speech anonymisation addresses
this by removing the sensitive identity cues while retaining linguistic
and other acoustic features relevant for downstream tasks. In clinical
settings, anonymisation must preserve diagnostic cues, such as
dysfluencies, unnatural pauses, and voice quality attributes (e.g.,
tremor). However, existing methods often alter the degree of these
clinical attributes or generalise poorly to atypical articulation in
pathological and elderly speech [7].
V oice conversion (VC) has emerged as a practical anonymi-
sation strategy [8], concealing the source speaker’s identity by trans-
forming their speech to resemble atargetspeaker while preserving
linguistic content. Earlier non-neural methods, including Gaussian
mixture model-based methods [9, 10], and signal processing tech-
niques such as McAdams coefficient shifting [11], achieved limited
speaker similarity and naturalness. Consequently, the field largely
gravitated towards data-driven deep learning (DL) methods [8].
Among the DL approaches, one family learns speaker em-
beddings and conditions the synthesised speech on them to trans-
form timbre between the source and target. V ariational autoencoder
(V AE)-based methods [12, 13] learn to disentangle speaker identity

tends to penalise encoding of rapid, irregular acoustic events. This of-
ten leads to over-smoothed outputs [14], which can suppress phona-
tion irregularities important in clinical speech. Generative adversarial
network (GAN)-based methods [15] improve naturalness through
speaker-conditioned adversarial losses, but risk leaking the target
speaker’s emotion [16], which is problematic when source emotion
is a diagnostic signal. Emo-StarGAN [17] addresses this by penalis-
ing energy and spectral deviations from the source, while EASY [18]
disentangles speaker, content, and emotion through domain-
adversarial training. Diffusion-based VC [19] improves fidelity,
but its iterative denoising remains computationally expensive for
real-time clinical use. A separate paradigm, KNN-VC [20], replaces
learned transformation withK-nearest-neighbour (KNN) mapping,
where self-supervised features (WavLM [21]) from the source are
matched directly to a target speaker’s feature pool. Critically, most
of these frameworks rely on a separate neural vocoder (e.g., HiFi-
GAN [22]), a pain point for atypical speech. V ocoders trained on
standard data often suppress diagnostic markers, while training on
clinical data conflates pathological cues with recording noise [7].
Beyond feature-space transformation and mapping-based
paradigms, differentiable digital signal processing (DDSP) [23]
integrates interpretable digital signal processing components, such
as oscillators and filters, into neural network training. This is
particularly beneficial for pathological speech, where parametrising
excitation and spectral shaping separately may help to preserve
irregular phonation patterns that black-box vocoders tend to nor-
malise. DDSP-QbE [7] is a mapping-based VC framework which
adapts the DDSP framework for atypical-speech anonymisation.
It uses a subtractive synthesiser [24], which decomposes speech
into harmonic and stochastic components. However, it predicts
spectral shaping directly without physical constraints, which may
lead to implausible resonance drift in difficult cases, such as syllable
repetitions in stuttering and voice instability in elderly speech.
Thus, we proposePhy-VC 1, which integrates physical
principles of speech production through aphysics-informed
vocal tract bottleneck. In this framework, synthesis is driven by
articulatory-to-acoustic modelling rather than unconstrained spectral
estimation, motivated by the physical principle that articulatory
gestures (e.g., jaw and tongue movements) shape a vocal tract with
coupled resonances. To the best of our knowledge, Phy-VC is the
first VC method to incorporate a differentiable vocal tract geometry
model for speech anonymisation, particularly for pathological
speech. In addition, we introduce target-pool prosodic feature

2. Method
In this work, we build upon DDSP-QbE [7], which was designed for
anonymisation of pathological and elderly speech. Figure 1 presents
the complete Phy-VC architecture, where the red-shaded blocks
highlight the proposed additions over DDSP-QbE. We design the
physics module to be directly compatible with neural vocoders.
DDSP-QbE performs voice conversion in three stages. First,
phoneticWphon and emotionWemo representations are extracted us-
ing the 6th and 12th layers of WavLM respectively. Second,Wphon is
matched to the target speaker’s phonetic poolPphon (built from 6th-
layer features) through KNN, to produceˆWphon that carries source
content in the target speaker’s voice [7, 20]. Unlike DDSP-QbE,
which passesWemo directly to the Fusion module, we additionally
match it to the emotion poolPemo, built from 12th-layer features from
the target speaker’s utterances. This prevents source-speaker leakage
through the prosodic features. Finally, the Fusion module processes
ˆWemo and ˆWphon through separate Prosody and Phonetic Encoders,
whose outputs are summed and passed to a Conformer [25] that pre-
dicts fundamental frequencyˆF0, harmonic (Ψh) and noise (Ψs) filter
coefficients, driving a differentiable subtractive synthesiser [24].
During training, the source and target are the same speaker
(self-reconstruction). At inference, matching to a different speaker’s
pool performs voice conversion.
2.1. Physics-Informed Bottleneck
We introduce a physics-informed bottleneck (refer to Figure1) that
replaces the unconstrained spectral envelope with one grounded in
vocal tract acoustics, receivingˆWphon from the mapping stage and
producing the spectral filterE(f,t) for synthesis. Figure 2 details
each stage: articulatory prediction (A), vocal tract geometry (B),
formant extraction (C), and filter construction (D).
2.1.1. Articulatory Prediction
In the first stage, we predict eight control trajectoriesα(t)∈[−1,1]8,
from the mapped phonetic features ˆWphon, following Maeda’s
low-dimensional articulatory model [26, 27]. Each dimensionαi(t)
corresponds to a dominant articulatory degree of freedom: jaw
opening (α0), tongue-body position (α1), tongue-body shape (α2),
tongue tip (α3), lip aperture (α4), lip protrusion (α5), larynx height
(α6), and velopharyngeal port (α7). The controls α0−5 capture
segmental articulatory gestures [27]. In contrast,α6 controls vocal

reflect global anatomical and resonance characteristics that vary
more slowly than phoneme-level articulation. [28, 29].
To predict α(t) from ˆWphon, we first smooth it with a
Conv1D layer to reduce jitter from KNN matching, then apply
self-attention to capture long-range articulatory dependencies. A
feedforward network (MLP) then projects to 8 dimensions, with
the outputs passed throughtanh to yield α(t)∈[−1,1]8, where
αi=0 represents a neutral configuration and±1 the extremes (e.g.,
anterior/posterior tongue).

3. Experiment Protocol
3.1. Baselines
We evaluate our method against three representative baselines:
(i) Emo-StarGAN [17] (denoted asEmo), an emotion-preserving
GAN-based method; (ii) KNN-VC [20], a mapping-based approach;
and (iii) DDSP-QbE [7], the direct predecessor to Phy-VC and
the state-of-the-art on pathological speech anonymisation. For
a fair comparison, we also provide DDSP-QbE with mapped
prosodic features ˆWemo (using the same target pool), so that
performance differences reflect the physics module rather than the
feature-mapping strategy.
3.2. Datasets and Splits
Datasets:We use 5 English speech corpora, all resampled to16 kHz:
• LibriSpeech[38]: Read speech from audiobooks. We use the
train-clean-100 subset.
• ESD[ 39]: Emotional speech with five classes (neutral, happy,
sad, angry, surprise).
• ADReSSo[ 40]: Elderly speech from healthy controls and
dementia-diagnosed speakers. We filter silent, multi-speaker
(per provided annotations), and low-quality recordings by manual
inspection to remove remaining noisy clips, yielding 2,729
utterances from 293 speakers.
• Sep-28k[41] andFluency Bank[ 42]: Both datasets comprise
stuttered speech, and have been annotated for five dysfluency
types: prolongations, blocks, interjections, word repetitions and
sound repetitions. A sample is labelled dysfluent only if at least
two annotators agree. Blocks co-annotated as ‘Natural Pause’ by
two or more annotators are excluded. Recordings containing both
host and guest audio are discarded to avoid confounding equal
error rate (EER) computation. Speaker identities are obtained
from the extended dataset [43] yielding 16,135 recordings from
419 speakers.

• VCTK[ 44]: Multi-accent read speech, used only for target
speakers (Scottish and Welsh accents) to evaluate cross-accent
robustness.
We definestandard(SD) speakers as those drawn from LibriSpeech
and ESD, which we treat as neurotypical with no diagnosed speech,
language, or cognitive impairment, in contrast to the clinical
populations in SEP-28k, Fluency Bank, and ADReSSo.
T raining and Evaluation:All models are trained and evaluated on
identical train/validation/test splits for fair comparison. Speaker and
target selection are randomised with gender balancing. All models
are trained exclusively on standard (non-pathological) speech
comprising 50 speakers from LibriSpeech (≈15 h) and 5 speakers
from ESD (≈8 h , all emotions), with an 80/20 train/validation
split. This design intentionally excludes clinical data to evaluate
out-of-domain robustness to pathological speech, reflecting realistic
deployment scenarios where clinical corpora are scarce.
