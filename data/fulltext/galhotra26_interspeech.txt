Abstract
Infant distress vocalizations, includingcryingandfussing, func-
tion as graded signals that communicate infants’ needs to care-
givers. Most prior distress classifiers are trained on con-
trolled data and fail to generalize to noisier everyday record-
ings. However, models trained on real-world data show lower
accuracy (F1 = 0.61–0.66) and no published models or public
datasets distinguish graded fuss vs. cry vocalizations in real-
world recordings. Binary classifiers collapse distinct distress
states, discarding information needed to model caregiver re-
sponses that vary with distress level. We relabel an existing
real-world corpus to release the first dataset annotated for cry,
fuss, and non-distress from continuous daylong child-worn au-
dio, and establish new benchmarks: our best binary classifier
achieves macro F1 = 0.803 and our ternary classifier 0.624.
Cross-dataset tests show lab-trained models fail in real-world
conditions, whereas ours generalize more robustly.
Index Terms: infant vocalization, cry detection, fuss detec-
tion, real-world dataset, computational paralinguistics, machine
learning, audio classification
1. Introduction
Infant distress detection has been formally recognized as a chal-
lenge in speech and audio processing, with two dedicated Cry-
ing and Baby Sound sub-challenges at prior INTERSPEECH
meetings [1, 2]. While these events advanced algorithmic de-
velopment, the datasets used in these challenges were collected
in controlled conditions or pre-segmented to reduce task com-
plexity. Models trained on such “clean” data have been shown
to degrade sharply under real-world conditions, where variabil-
ity and complexity are pervasive [3, 4, 5].
Algorithms trained directly on naturalistic recordings show
improved robustness for binary classification of distress versus
non-distress. However, even the best-performing models have
modest accuracy in real-world recordings (e.g., F1≈0.61 on
deBarbaroCry [5]), underscoring the difficulty of modeling dis-
tress in daylong home audio. Strengthening real-world binary
detection is therefore essential for scalable, ecologically valid
research and intervention.
A second fundamental challenge in distress detection is that
infant distress is not a binary phenomenon. Infants communi-
cate throughgraded distress vocalizationsthat differ in arousal
and urgency. These signals can be reliably distinguished ascry-
ingorfussing. Crying consists of loud, rhythmic vocalizations
linked by brief inspiratory sounds and signal urgent needs re-
quiring immediate caregiver response. Fussing is lower in in-
tensity, less rhythmic, and marked by intermittent vocalizations

separated by longer gaps, signaling milder discomfort or a bid
for interaction. Importantly, high quality caregiver responses
calibrate to distress intensity, a response pattern central to much
developmental theory and linked to a number of positive devel-
opmental outcomes [6, 7, 8]. Collapsing fussing and crying into
a single category thus obscures meaningful variation in distress
intensity that is central to modeling caregiver responsiveness
and ongoing efforts to develop automated measures of caregiv-
ing quality [9, 10], a key target for early intervention [11].
To our knowledge, no published models perform three-way
classification ofcry,fuss, andnon-distressin real-world data,
largely because no publicly available dataset has supported this
task. Addressing both challenges—strengthening binary detec-
tion and extending classification to graded distress—is there-
fore necessary to advance ecologically valid infant vocalization
modeling. We address these needs by augmenting the pub-
licly available deBarbaroCry dataset with new annotations dis-
tinguishing cry, fuss, and non-distress in daylong, infant-worn
home recordings, and by leveraging this dataset to develop a
more robust binary distress classifier. These annotations estab-
lish the first naturalistic benchmark for multi-class infant dis-
tress classification and expose substantial generalization gaps
between lab-trained and real-world models, underscoring the
necessity of ecologically valid training data.

2. Related Work
Work in infant distress detection has primarily relied on datasets
collected in controlled environments such as hospitals or labo-
ratory settings, where high reported accuracies (80–95%) are
common [2, 12, 13]. However, these evaluations occur under
limited acoustic variability. When transferred to naturalistic
home recordings—characterized by background noise and over-
lapping speech—performance often degrades substantially [5].
To improve ecological validity, naturalistic child-worn cor-
pora such as deBarbaroCry [5] provide training data collected
in real-world home environments. Although models trained on
such data typically achieve lower absolute performance than
those trained on controlled corpora, they generalize more ef-
fectively to real-world deployment conditions. For example, a
cry detection model trained on the IL-CRIED laboratory cor-
pus achieved F1 = 0.236 on real-world data, whereas training
directly on deBarbaroCry yielded F1 = 0.613 [5].
Earlier models relied on handcrafted acoustic features such
as F0 and MFCCs combined with classical classifiers [14, 15].
More recently, convolutional neural networks (CNNs) have im-
proved performance by learning richer temporal–spectral repre-
sentations [16, 17]. Transfer learning has further advanced this
line of work: while image-pretrained networks such as VGG
and AlexNet have been adapted for cry detection [5, 18], mod-
els pretrained on large-scale acoustic corpora - such as Y AMNet
[19]-provide more domain-aligned representations.
Despite improvements in distress detection accuracy, ex-
isting work focuses almost exclusively on binary distress de-
tection, and no publicly available corpus distinguishes fussing
from crying in naturalistic audio. We address this gap by intro-
ducing the deBarbaroFussCry corpus and evaluating traditional,
deep, and hybrid modeling approaches under both binary and
graded classification settings.
3. Dataset
3.1. Source corpus
Our work builds on the deBarbaroCry corpus, derived from 742
hours of infant-worn daylong recordings collected in naturalis-
tic home environments using LENA devices [20]. These record-
ings capture substantial real-world variability, including over-
lapping speech, household noise, and clothing artifacts.
From the full corpus, LENA’s automated detection of in-
fant non-speech vocalizations identified 66 hours of candidate
segments likely to contain infant distress. These segments were
manually verified and released with binary labels. Of the 66
hours, 7.9 hours were confirmed as infant distress, while the
remaining segments were labeled as non-distress. In the orig-
inal release, all distress vocalizations—including both crying
and fussing—were grouped under a singledistresscategory. As
a result, the dataset supports only binary classification.
3.2. Extension to fuss vs. cry annotations
We augmented the 7.9-hour validated distress subset of deBar-
baroCry with annotations distinguishingfussingfromcrying.
Trained research assistants coded three classes:
•Crying:loud, potentially sustained, rhythmic negative vo-
calizations linked by brief, audible inspiratory sounds
•Fussing:lower intensity, less rhythmic intermittent negative
vocalizations separated by longer gaps between sounds
•Non-distress:all other infant vocalizations (e.g., babbling,
vegetative sounds) as well as background audio.
To maintain consistency with prior work coding distress vo-
calizations [21], cry vocalizations were required to have a min-
imum duration of 3 seconds and were merged if separated by
less than 5 seconds. Because fuss vocalizations are typically
shorter and less rhythmically structured, no minimum duration
criterion was imposed. Inter-rater reliability was assessed on a
72-hour subsample of unfiltered real-world recordings (distinct
from the 66-hour candidate set), spanning all three categories.
Three-way agreement was strong (κ= 0.847), with binary dis-
tress agreement atκ= 0.78[22].
3.3. Final release
The resulting corpus, named thedeBarbaroFussCrydataset,
consists of the 66 hours of manually verified candidate seg-
ments from deBarbaroCry, now relabeled for ternary classifica-
tion. Within these 66 hours, 7.9 hours contain validated infant
distress, subdivided into 4.75 hours of fussing and 3.15 hours of
crying. The remaining 58.1 hours consist of non-distress seg-
ments. The corpus is publicly available at HomeBank.2
4. Methodology
We structured our experiments in two stages. Prior work es-
tablished the feasibility of real-world binary distress detection,
but performance remained constrained by substantial acoustic
variability in daylong recordings. We therefore revisited the bi-
nary task (distress vs. non-distress) to strengthen robustness un-
der naturalistic conditions. Because graded classification pre-
supposes reliable detection of distress events, establishing a
stronger binary foundation was a necessary precursor to ternary
modeling. We then extended the best-performing binary frame-
work to the ternary task (fuss, cry, non-distress).
4.1. Preprocessing and evaluation
All models were trained on deBarbaroFussCry (Section 3). For
binary classification, fuss and cry were merged into a single
distresslabel. For ternary classification, the labels (fuss, cry,
non-distress) were used without modification. To determine in-
put duration, we compared 1-s windows, 5-s windows, and 5-s
windows with 4-s overlap using Gradient Boosting. Overlap-
ping 5-s segments performed best (F1 = 0.676; Table 1), balanc-
ing temporal precision with acoustic context, and were there-
fore used for all experiments. Class imbalance was addressed
through undersampling, and performance was evaluated using
Leave-One-Participant-Out Cross-Validation (LOPO-CV).

We evaluated three modeling paradigms: (1) traditional ma-
chine learning baselines, (2) convolutional neural networks, and
(3) transfer learning and hybrid models leveraging pre-trained
acoustic embeddings. Model–representation pairings were cho-
sen to systematically examine how classifier performance de-
pends on feature representation, rather than to exhaustively enu-
merate all architectural combinations.
Traditional baselines.Eight classifiers (Table 2) were im-
plemented using scikit-learn [23] and XGBoost [24]. Using
pyAudioAnalysis [25], we extracted 34 standard acoustic de-
scriptors widely used in speech and affect classification (e.g.,
MFCCs, zero-crossing rate, short-term energy) and summarized
each 5-s segment with mean, median, and SD, yielding a 102-
dimensional input vector.
CNNs from scratch.CNNs with 2–4 convolutional lay-
ers (ReLU + max-pooling) were trained with Adam [26] (lr =
0.001, batch = 32, 20 epochs). A final dense layer used sigmoid
(binary) [27]. Network depth and training duration were con-
strained to reflect the low-resource nature of labeled audio and
to mitigate overfitting given dataset size.
Transfer learning and hybrid models.To leverage large-
scale acoustic knowledge, we used Y AMNet, a 14-layer CNN
pre-trained on AudioSet [19], which includes a dedicatedcry
class. We evaluated frozen, fine-tuned, and MLP-based con-
figurations using the 1,024-dimensional Y AMNet embeddings.
Prior work on the deBarbaroCry corpus showed that margin-
based classifiers -particularly an RBF-kernel SVM - perform
strongly when trained on deep spectral features [5]. Be-
cause Y AMNet embeddings similarly provide high-dimensional
learned acoustic representations, we evaluated SVM as a prin-
cipled classifier for these features. To mitigate redundancy and
reduce overfitting in the 1,024-dimensional embedding space
under limited labeled data, we applied PCA (300 components
retaining 90% variance) prior to classification. For comparison,
we also paired Y AMNet embeddings with Gradient Boosting,
a strong traditional classifier identified during baseline evalua-
tion on handcrafted features. Among the evaluated approaches,
Y AMNet+SVM achieved the strongest binary performance and
was therefore extended to ternary classification using a one-vs-
one strategy [28].
5. Experiments and Results
5.1. Experimental setup
We report macro-averaged F1 as the primary evaluation metric
to account for class imbalance and to ensure consistency across
binary, ternary, and cross-dataset evaluations. For binary classi-
fication, distress-class precision, recall, and F1 are additionally
reported when comparing against prior work. All accuracy met-
rics were estimated using Leave-One-Participant-Out Cross-
Validation (LOPO-CV) to ensure subject-independent evalua-
tion.
5.2. Traditional machine learning baselines
We first established baselines using eight traditional classifiers
(Table 2). Gradient Boosting achieved the strongest perfor-
mance (F1 = 0.676), outperforming the other classifiers, and
was therefore carried forward into the hybrid models. SVMs
were not evaluated as standalone baselines but were incorpo-
rated within the hybrid models as described above.
5.3. Deep learning results
We next evaluated deep learning models on the deBarbaroFuss-
Cry dataset (Table 2). Fine-tuning Y AMNet improved perfor-
mance substantially over frozen embeddings (F1 = 0.386 vs.
0.275), indicating the benefit of adapting pretrained acoustic
representations to naturalistic infant audio.
Shallow CNNs trained directly on log-mel spectrograms
achieved higher overall performance, with the best 3-layer
model reaching F1 = 0.592. Increasing depth beyond three lay-
ers did not yield further gains, suggesting diminishing returns
under limited labeled data.

5.4. Hybrid model results
For binary classification, we report precision, recall, and F1
for the distress (positive) class to facilitate comparison with
prior work, alongside macro F1 as the primary summary metric.
Among the traditional classifiers, Gradient Boosting (GB) was
the strongest baseline (F1 = 0.676) and therefore served as the
primary comparison point for hybrid models. Combining Y AM-
Net embeddings with GB yielded a modest improvement (F1 =
0.761), indicating that pretrained acoustic features provide com-
plementary information to handcrafted descriptors. Replacing
GB with an RBF-kernel Support Vector Machine (SVM) fur-
ther improved performance, achieving the strongest binary re-
sults overall (F1 = 0.803). Consistent with prior findings that
margin-based classifiers perform well on learned representa-
tions, SVM more effectively leveraged the high-dimensional
structure of Y AMNet embeddings to model non-linear decision
boundaries. Table 3 presents these results.
5.5. Extension to ternary classification
Given its success on the binary task, we extended the Y AM-
Net+SVM hybrid pipeline to ternary classification (fuss,cry,
non-distress). Using a one-vs-one strategy with majority vot-
ing, the model achieved macro F1 = 0.624, far above random
chance (0.33; see also Table 3), with per-class performance (av-
eraged across LOPO folds) as follows:fuss(P = 0.328, R =
0.512, F1 = 0.398),cry(P = 0.539, R = 0.618, F1 = 0.582), and
non-distress(P = 0.939, R = 0.841, F1 = 0.895).

6. Real-World vs. In-Lab Training Data
To evaluate ecological validity, we conducted cross-dataset ex-
periments using our real-world dataset (deBarbaroFussCry)
and theINTERSPEECH 2018 (CRIED)challenge corpora.
When trained on INTERSPEECH data and evaluated on deBar-
baroFussCry, models exhibited substantial performance degra-
dation, highlighting the difficulty of transferring from con-
trolled laboratory recordings to naturalistic home environments.
Conversely, models trained on deBarbaroFussCry general-
ized more effectively to the INTERSPEECH test set, indicating
that training on ecologically valid audio better equips models
to handle cross-domain variability (Table 4). This asymmetry
underscores our central finding: training on noisy, ecologically
valid infant audio yields models that are more robust to domain
shift and better suited for real-world deployment.

7. Discussion
7.1. Overall performance
The combined Y AMNet+SVM model achieved the strongest
performance on the binary task, reaching a macro F1 of 0.803
under LOPO-CV , substantially improving upon previously re-
ported real-world baselines. When extended to the ternary set-
ting (fuss, cry, non-distress), the model achieved a macro F1 of
0.624, establishing the first benchmark for graded infant distress
classification in naturalistic home recordings.
These findings show that strengthening binary distress de-
tection provides a viable foundation for graded modeling. At
the same time, the reduced performance in the ternary setting
highlights the intrinsic difficulty of distinguishing fussing from
crying, likely due to their acoustic overlap and variability. To-
gether, the results suggest that pretrained acoustic embeddings
combined with margin-based classifiers offer a robust approach
for modeling infant distress in ecologically valid settings.
7.2. Comparison with prior work
Our model outperformed real-world baselines and far exceeded
LENA (F1 = 0.17) [22], which exhibits strong precision bias.
Compared to controlled-setting benchmarks like CRIED [2]
(UAR 75.6%), our cross-dataset experiments confirm that lab-
trained models generalize poorly to naturalistic conditions,
while models trained on our dataset transfer more effectively
across domains. We note, however, that improved cross-dataset
performance may partly reflect differences in dataset size and
composition rather than ecological validity alone, and we treat
these as confounds rather than controlled factors. Our testing
model uses the LOPO-CV method, which enhances generaliz-
ability across unseen data, in contrast to models that use fixed
training and testing subsets [29]. This approach allows our
model to reliably handle the unpredictability of real-world en-
vironments.
7.3. Impact of model architecture
Traditional classifiers provided a useful baseline but were less
effective in handling the acoustic variability of infant vocal-
izations. Deep learning (Y AMNet+MLP) improved perfor-
mance by leveraging pre-trained acoustic embeddings, which
capture richer temporal and spectral features. The hybrid Y AM-
Net+SVM pipeline performed best overall, likely because PCA
regularization reduced noise while SVM effectively modeled
non-linear decision boundaries in limited-data conditions. This
allowed the system to make better use of Y AMNet’s informative
features without overfitting.
For ternary classification, performance was lower than in
the binary task. This is expected given both class imbalance
(4.75 hours fuss vs. 3.15 hours cry) and the subtle acoustic
overlap between fussing and crying. Fussing is inherently less
stereotyped than crying and is more easily masked by environ-
mental noise, making it a harder category for automated sys-
tems. Nonetheless, a macro F1 score of 0.624 is well above
chance (0.33) and represents meaningful progress toward fine-
grained infant distress detection.
8. Limitations
Our ternary results highlight the difficulty of distinguishing fuss
from cry. Fuss was the most difficult class to classify, with rel-
atively low precision and F1 despite moderate recall, suggest-
ing frequent false positives for fuss. This pattern likely reflects
both class imbalance and acoustic overlap among fuss, cry, and
non-distress vocalizations, and indicates that additional work is
needed before automated models can reliably separate graded
distress states in naturalistic recordings. Future work should
explore more advanced architectures to better capture these dif-
ferences or consider incorporating additional acoustic parame-
ters such asF 0 and nonlinear phenomena that have been linked
to gradations in pain-related distress [30, 31]. Finally, as is stan-
dard in LOPO-CV evaluation, our reported metrics also reflect
participant-level variation inherent to the cross-validation pro-
tocol.
9. Conclusion
We release the first real-world dataset annotated for cry, fuss,
and non-distress from naturalistic infant audio. Our Y AM-
Net+SVM model achieves state-of-the-art binary classification
(macro F1 = 0.803) and establishes the first ternary baseline
(macro F1 = 0.624). Cross-dataset experiments confirm that
training on ecologically valid data yields more robust and trans-
ferable models than lab-only training. Both dataset and code are
publicly available to support future infant vocalization research.
