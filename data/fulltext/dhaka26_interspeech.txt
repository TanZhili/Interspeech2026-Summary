Generalize to Nonstandard Speech?

Abstract
The best ASR models on the Hugging Face Open ASR Leader-
board have 5–7% WER on standard benchmarks, yet their relia-
bility on nonstandard speech remains uncertain. We present the
first leaderboard-anchored evaluation of leading open models
spanningencoder–decoder(Whisper, CrisperWhisper),trans-
ducer(Parakeet),speech-augmented LM(Canary), andspeech-
aware LLM(Granite) architectures on stuttered speech and
dysarthric speech. Using curated subsets of FluencyBank, SEP-
28k, and the UIUC Speech Accessibility Project dataset, we
evaluated transcripts with WER, CER, F1 and BERTScore met-
rics. The average WER increases 2–5×over leaderboard re-
sults, with extreme cases reaching 24×inflation from hallu-
cination bursts. Overall, Whisper-Large-v3 generalizes most
robustly, while Parakeet shows complementary strengths on
dysarthric speech. The results expose a substantial general-
ization gap and underscore the urgent need for accessibility-
focused ASR evaluation.
Index Terms: Accessibility, automatic speech recognition,
dysarthria, speech differences, stuttering
1. Introduction
Automatic speech recognition (ASR) models that top the Hug-
ging Face Open ASR Leaderboard [1] achieve impressively low
average word error rates (WERs)—often below 7%—on main-
stream benchmarks such as LibriSpeech. Yet these gains do
not necessarily generalize to nonstandard speech. Prior work
shows that WER can be two to four times higher on stuttered
speech [2], and that dysarthric speech also remains a persistent
challenge despite progress in corpus development [3]. Stut-
tering introduces involuntary disfluencies such as repetitions
(e.g., “h-h-hello”), prolongations (e.g., “hhhhello”), and blocks
(e.g., “—hello”) [4], while dysarthria, often resulting from con-
ditions such as Parkinson’s disease, cerebral palsy, or stroke,
leads to slurred or slow articulation and altered prosody [3].
These differences cause mainstream ASR systems to misrec-
ognize speech, reducing accessibility for everyday applications.
Related Work.Efforts to improve ASR for speech differ-
ences span several directions. Corpora such as the Speech Ac-
cessibility Project [3], Project Euphonia [5], FluencyBank [6],
and SEP-28k [7] provide resources covering dysarthria, stutter-
ing, and other conditions. Model adaptation approaches include
personalized systems [8, 9, 10], user-centered studies [11], and
reinforcement learning [12]. Techniques such as parameter tun-
ing, data augmentation, and fine-tuning have also been proposed
to improve robustness [10, 13, 14]. Early critiques highlighted
disparities in commercial ASR [15], which persist in recent
analyses [2]. However, most prior work addresses a single con-
dition or model family, leaving open how today’s leaderboard-
ranked systems perform across diverse speech patterns.
Contributions.To address this gap, we make four contri-
butions:1. Leaderboard-anchored evaluation:Evaluation of
top open models from the Hugging Face Open ASR Leader-
board, selected as high-performing representatives of major
families—encoder–decoder(Whisper, CrisperWhisper),trans-
ducer(Parakeet-TDT-0.6B-v2),speech-augmented language
model(Canary-Qwen-2.5B), andspeech-aware LLM(Granite-
Speech-3.3-8B).2. Lexical and semantic analysis:Assess-
ment using lexical metrics (WER, CER, F1) and semantic sim-
ilarity (BERTScore F1) to capture both lexical accuracy and
meaning preservation.3. Quantification of the generaliza-
tion gap:Demonstration that leaderboard average WERs of 5–
7% on standard benchmarks inflate by 2–5×on nonstandard
speech, with extreme cases up to 24×.4. Error analysis and
guidance:Examination of hallucination patterns and error pro-
files, highlighting architecture-specific weaknesses and offering
practitioner guidance for inclusive deployment.
2. Methods
2.1. Datasets
We evaluate ASR models on three corpora of stuttered and
dysarthric speech. Figure 1 shows utterance duration distribu-
tions across corpora after 1.5×IQR outlier removal.
UIUC Speech Accessibility Project (SAP).We use the
UIUC SAP dataset [3], a large-scale corpus designed to advance
research in dysarthric speech recognition. It comprises record-
ings from over 580 speakers, totaling more than 501 hours of
speech across diverse English dialects (U.S., Canada, Puerto
Rico). Participants represent five major etiologies associated
with dysarthria: Parkinson’s disease, Down syndrome, amy-
otrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS), cerebral palsy, and stroke. Ev-
ery utterance is accompanied by metadata, such as the original
prompt text, a description of its category (e.g., Digital Assistant
Commands, Novel Sentences, Spontaneous Prompts), and the
expected transcription.
FluencyBank (FB).The FluencyBank dataset [6] contains
video and audio recordings of individuals who stutter in two
contexts: reading passages and interviews. The dataset in-
cludes 7 reading sessions and 12 interviews from 12 partici-
pants, with transcripts prepared in CHAT format [6]. Speech
recordings are segmented into utterances—sentences or sen-
tence fragments spoken by a single individual—and annotated

2.2. Large, Pre-Trained ASR Models
We evaluate five state-of-the-art ASR systems, selected as top-
ranked open models on the Hugging Face Open ASR Leader-
board and spanning four major architecture families.
Encoder–decoder (seq2seq).Whisper-Large-v3[16] is an
encoder–decoder Transformer trained at scale on multilingual
and multitask supervised data. We used the large-v3 checkpoint
with beam search disabled.CrisperWhisper[17] is a Whisper
variant fine-tuned on carefully corrected transcripts to reduce
hallucinations and improve verbatim alignment.
Transducer.Parakeet-TDT-0.6B-v2[18] is a transformer-
based transducer with a FastConformer encoder and a Token-
and-Duration Transducer (TDT) decoder, optimized for effi-
cient GPU decoding. We used the 0.6B parameter version with
stereo-to-mono conversion and a reduced batch size for memory
constraints.
Speech-augmented language model (SALM).Canary-
Qwen-2.5Bis a SALM that combines a FastConformer encoder
from NVIDIA’s Canary line [19] with a Qwen3-1.7B Trans-
former decoder. Trained on approximately 234k hours of pub-
licly available English speech, it is designed to operate both in
ASR mode for transcription and in LLM mode for text-only
tasks such as summarization or Q&A.
Speech-aware LLM.Granite-Speech-3.3-8B[20] is IBM’s
speech-aware large language model, built by modality-aligning
the Granite-3.3-8B-Instruct base to speech using public au-
dio–text corpora and synthetic data. For this study, we used
the 3.3-8B parameter variant, which incorporates parameter-
efficient fine-tuning (PEFT) adapters, with a fixed prompt to
enforce transcription-only behavior.
2.3. Unified Preprocessing and Inference Setup
To achieve a fair comparison, all models used a common pre-
processing and inference pipeline. Audio inputs were resam-
pled to 16 kHz and converted to single-channel (mono). Mod-
els using Hugging Face pipelines (Whisper-Large-v3, Crisper-
Whisper) converted the raw waveform into log-Mel spectro-
grams via each model’s feature extractor and processor. For the
NeMo-based models (Parakeet-TDT-0.6B-v2, Canary-Qwen-
2.5B), additional steps were required: Parakeet used a dedicated
stereo-to-mono conversion via temporary files, and Canary used
its own SALM interface and audio locator tags. Granite-
Speech-3.3-8B was instantiated with PEFT and run through
its custom Auto-Processor. For audio longer than 30 s, we
segmented the signal into overlapping 30 s windows, preserv-
ing each model’s context window and maintaining transcrip-
tion continuity across segment boundaries. Reference and pre-
dicted transcripts were normalized by lowercasing, removing
punctuation, and stripping disfluency (CHAT) codes. Because
this canonicalization maps disfluent productions onto their flu-
ent lexical targets, it disadvantages verbatim-oriented models
such as CrisperWhisper, whose elevated WER partly reflects a
metric artifact rather than a transcription failure.
All models used greedy decoding (beam size = 1) to isolate
each architecture’s intrinsic performance and remove variability
from search strategies. For multi-task models, we restricted the
configuration to transcription only. Granite-Speech-3.3-8B was
additionally given a system prompt discouraging instruction-
following and constraining output to verbatim transcription.
This unified setup ensured that performance differences re-
flect model robustness rather than variations in preprocessing or
inference settings.

3. Results and Discussion
Whisper-Large-v3 has the best overall performance in our eval-
uation suite (Table 2). Whisper achieves the best lexical F1
(0.83) and BERTScore (0.93), while Parakeet attains the low-
est CER (0.11) on dysarthric speech. These performance differ-
ences are consistent with known architectural properties, though
our black-box comparison cannot establish causation and we
present the following as hypotheses. Whisper’s encoder–
decoder, which reduces audio to 30-second contexts, may lose
fine-grained phonetic detail in severe dysarthria, whereas Para-
keet’s transducer allows frame-synchronous prediction that can
better preserve the temporal alignment of systematic articula-
tory deviations. Canary’s decoder-only language backbone re-
lies on auto-regressive prediction and may collapse as acoustic
quality degrades, while Granite degrades far more severely—
well beyond the 2–5×seen for the other models—revealing a
significant generalization gap. This 2–5×degradation is con-
servative: some utterances exceed 24×WER inflation, espe-
cially for Granite, where semantic grounding fails catastrophi-
cally.

3.1. Stuttered Speech
Whisper performs best on the stuttered-speech datasets, which
we attribute to two factors. First, its large-scale training on di-
verse web audio exposes it to abundant naturally occurring dis-
fluencies. Second, its multi-head self-attention may help sus-
tain attention across longer disfluent sections. Whisper scores
the lowest WER on FluencyBank (Table 3) both in the reading
(0.165) and the interview (0.208) activities, which is an 18–28%
difference between Whisper and Parakeet. This gap widens on
SEP-28k, which contains multiple disfluency types (prolonga-
tions, blocks, repetitions), where Whisper achieves a 33% rela-
tive WER reduction over Parakeet. Whisper’s full-context at-
tention integrates information across entire disfluent regions,
whereas Parakeet’s transducer enforces monotonic alignment
that commits early to suboptimal token boundaries and prevents
re-evaluation of local evidence. Surprisingly, Canary’s WER
on reading (0.458) is much higher than on interviews (0.292),
suggesting that its causal language model leans on the conver-
sational context and redundancy of spontaneous speech to off-
set acoustic uncertainty—support that is weaker in isolated read
passages.

Granite has the worst results across all conditions, with
catastrophic errors on ALS (5.58 WER) and digital-assistant
commands (3.44 WER). Statistical filtering (Table 6) reveals
systematic failure modes driven by extreme outliers. Represen-
tative cases are shown in Table 7: hallucinated repetitions (149
occurrences of “snore” for the target word “Snooze.”), collapsed
phone numbers, and runaway generation under atypical acoustic
input. The 39.64 WER on non-spontaneous prompts indicates
complete breakdown: on long read speech, Granite enters a sus-
tained hallucination state from which it cannot recover. Even
after applying heavy filtering to eliminate the outliers, Granite’s
0.992 WER exceeds all other models’ unfiltered overall WER,
showing systematic architectural incompatibility.
These failures likely stem from architectural decisions:
Granite couples acoustic representations to a frozen language
model through adapters. For atypical acoustic input, language
model priors dominate, leading to runaway generation. This
shows that scale alone does not ensure robustness: models with
tighter acoustic-linguistic coupling, such as Whisper and Para-
keet, are more promising starting points for fine-tuning. The ob-
served error patterns—repetition, digit collapse, and wake-word
fragmentation—indicate that Granite’s adapter mechanism can-
not ensure acoustic grounding for inputs outside its training dis-
tribution.
4. Conclusion and Future Work
Whisper-Large-v3 generalizes most robustly. Whisper and
Parakeet show the clearest complementary strengths: Whisper
leads on stuttered speech and semantically predictable condi-
tions such as Parkinson’s, while Parakeet achieves the lowest
CER on dysarthric speech and the best WER on cerebral palsy,
Down syndrome, and stroke. We attribute these differences
to fundamental design choices: Whisper’s cross-attention in-
corporates semantic context to fill in missing acoustics; Para-
keet’s transducer maintains the temporal accuracy essential for
systematic articulatory deviations; Canary’s decoder-only lan-
guage backbone exploits causal language modeling; and Gran-
ite’s adapter-based fusion is prone to attention collapse under
atypical conditions. Most importantly, systems with strong per-
formance on standard benchmarks perform poorly on nonstan-
dard speech, with extreme cases showing severe error inflation.
These differences directly affect people who depend on ASR
in their everyday communication: voice assistants fail to act on
commands, dictation systems fail to produce usable text, and
communication devices fail to capture intent. Whereas some
conditions still allow fairly predictable voice control despite er-
rors, others cause catastrophic breakdowns that render voice in-
terfaces unusable. This accessibility hierarchy, in which some
speech differences are served far better than others, exposes
evaluation practices that capture average but not worst-case per-
formance, underscoring the need for more diverse evaluation
suites and deployment safeguards.
Based on these insights, we identify three complementary
directions for future work. First, Whisper, Parakeet, and Canary
could be fine-tuned per condition on dysarthric speech using
parameter-efficient personalization with limited data. Second,
acoustic features indicative of the dysarthria type can be used in
a mixture-of-experts architecture to route utterances to the best
model, without having to cover all types of dysarthria in a sin-
gle model. Third, more representative training corpora spanning
conditions, severities, and task types would improve both eval-
uation and fine-tuning of foundation models. Together, these
directions chart a path from today’s accessibility hierarchies to-
ward universal design.
