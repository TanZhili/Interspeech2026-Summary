Abstract
Building upon a prosodic speech corpus collected through a
web-based interactive game, this paper examines its value for
analyzing biometric speaker attributes, such as gender and age,
and for LLM-level representation analysis. The corpus was de-
signed so that players serve as both performers and annotators,
creating a self-validating mechanism in which each utterance
is evaluated through independent human judgments. This de-
sign enables systematic assessment of annotation consistency
and label robustness. We evaluate the corpus through emo-
tion recognition and probing of LLM-aligned speech represen-
tations. Results show that prosodic embeddings encode stable
affective patterns while emotional attributes remain identifiable
in intermediate representations, raising privacy considerations.

1. Introduction
With the rise of speech models, the demand for high-quality
speech datasets has increased. Emotional speech datasets
present additional methodological challenges, as they must cap-
ture controlled and recognizable affective states. Data col-
lection strategies vary—naturalistic recordings (e.g., CMU-
MOSEAS [1]), elicited speech (e.g., HUMAINE [2]), or pro-
fessional acting (e.g., RA VDESS [3])—each balancing ecolog-
ical validity and experimental control. Dataset scope, includ-
ing the range of emotions, participant diversity, and linguistic
coverage, further affects model robustness and cross-linguistic
applicability. The following section outlines key challenges in
constructing such resources.
Annotators: Most emotional speech corpora rely on small
groups of raters who label recordings into predefined categories.
Reliability depends on annotator number and diversity, with
larger pools reducing bias and improving consensus. However,
expanding annotation is costly and time-consuming, leading to
trade-offs between quality and practical constraints, and often
limiting statistical robustness.
Limited variety of speakers: Many emotional speech datasets
rely on a small group of actors, limiting demographic variabil-
ity and potentially biasing models toward specific vocal profiles.
Increasing diversity in gender, age, regional origin, and linguis-
tic background can improve naturalness and model generaliza-
tion.
Linguistic diversity: As noted earlier, most emotional speech
datasets concentrate on widely studied languages, limiting the
cross-linguistic robustness of emotion recognition systems. Ex-
panding data collection to underrepresented languages would

reduce cultural and language-specific biases in training.
Utterance length: Most emotional speech datasets con-
tain short phrases, often only a few words, with utterance
length typically chosen without a clear methodological ratio-
nale. A more systematic design—from single words to longer
phrases—would enable controlled acoustic comparisons and
clearer analysis of how prosodic features vary across speech du-
rations.
Absence of context: Actor-based emotional speech datasets
(i.e., not extracted from films or TV) typically contain words
or short phrases repeated with different emotional labels, often
produced without contextual grounding. Providing specific sce-
narios can encourage more natural performances and improve
the suitability of such recordings for training emotion recogni-
tion systems intended for real-world use.
Taken together, these challenges highlight the need for
emotional speech datasets that combine methodological control
with greater diversity and contextual realism. The dataset in-
troduced in this work addresses these limitations directly. An-
notation reliability is improved through a large and diverse par-
ticipant pool, while speaker diversity is increased by allowing
contributions from many speakers rather than a small group
of actors. The dataset includes multiple languages, support-
ing cross-linguistic analysis, and systematically varies utterance
length from single words to longer phrases to enable controlled
study of prosodic cues across different speech durations. Fi-
nally, recordings are produced within explicit contextual sce-
narios, encouraging more natural emotional performances.
2. A New Dynamic Dataset
Inspired by earlier studies (e.g. [4] and [5]) that have success-
fully employed crowdsourcing for large-scale data collection,
we set out to develop a dataset that addresses the key limitations
outlined above, particularly with respect to contextual richness
and speaker diversity. To achieve this, we adopted an online
game-based framework for data gathering. This design follows
the Games With A Purpose (GW AP) paradigm introduced by
Luis von Ahn [6], which integrates data annotation into engag-
ing user tasks. Similar strategies have been effectively imple-
mented in projects such as DALI by Poesio et al.[7], demon-
strating the potential of game-based environments for collecting
high-quality linguistic data on a large scale.
2.1. Game Design
The concept of the game Actor’s Challenge (AC) is rooted in the
Stanislavskian acting method as discussed by Roman Jakobson
in “Linguistics and Poetics [8],” which illustrates how varia-
tions in prosodic expression alone can convey distinct emotional
meanings independent of lexical content.

The AC game is designed around neutral target phrases that
can be interpreted differently depending on context. To study
emotional prosody, we select linguistically simple expressions
with minimal inherent affective bias, such as “It’s a cappuc-
cino,” which can convey different emotions depending on the
situation. For attitudinal prosody, we include sentences like
“Kevin isn’t drinking because he is unhappy,” which allow in-
vestigation of focus, emphasis, or interpretive ambiguity.
For each target phrase, we construct multiple distinct con-
texts that specify the intended communicative situation. These
contexts guide participants in producing the utterance with the
appropriate emotional or attitudinal nuance, enabling system-
atic comparison of how prosody varies while lexical content re-
mains constant. We then design multiple discriminating con-
texts for each target phrase. These contexts establish the com-
municative setting in which the utterance is to be produced,
guiding participants toward the intended emotional or attitudi-
nal interpretation. For example, the phrase “It’s a cappuccino”
may be embedded in a scenario where the speaker has repeat-
edly clarified their order and reacts with frustration upon receiv-
ing the wrong drink. Similarly, the sentence about Kevin may
be framed within a description emphasizing habitual drinking
regardless of mood. By situating each target within a specific
scenario, participants are encouraged to modulate their prosody
in line with clearly defined emotional or interpretive cues.
Upon registration, participants provide basic demographic
information (e.g., age, gender, region, native language, and
preferred game language) and sign an informed consent form
stating that their anonymized recordings will be released under
a Creative Commons Attribution-ShareAlike 4.0 license. This
procedure ensures ethical compliance and transparency in data
use. Within the game, players alternate between two roles:
Auditioners, who produce target phrases in specified contexts,
andCasting Directors, who evaluate others’ performances.
This structured role rotation increases engagement while
enabling both data production and validation by the same
participant sample.
Audition:
In the Auditioner role, participants read a randomly assigned
context or stage cue and record the target phrase accordingly.
They may re-record and review their performance until satis-
fied before submitting it. Each phrase appears in two to four
different contexts, allowing exploration of varied emotional or
situational interpretations. After completing all contexts for
a given target, players proceed to additional audition rounds.
Following several sessions, they are automatically switched to
Casting mode.
Casting:
In the Casting Director role, participants listen to recordings
produced by other players and attempt to match each utterance
to its intended context. After selecting the most appropriate
context, they rate the performance on a 1–5 Likert scale,
assessing its quality and naturalness. This process serves
as a built-in validation mechanism for the recorded data.
They also monitor audio quality, flagging recordings with
technical issues, incorrect wording, inappropriate content, or
cues that make the task trivial. Reported cases are reviewed,
and repeated violations may lead to warnings or exclusion to
preserve dataset integrity. An additional feature allows users
to mark previously heard voices, generating auxiliary data for
speaker recognition research (e.g.[9]).
Overall, this dual-role structure integrates data collection
and evaluation within the same participant pool, promoting con-
tinuous quality control and contributing to a rigorously vetted
dataset.
2.2. Scoring System
The game targets users interested in practicing acting or emo-
tion recognition skills. Actors earn points based on how accu-
rately Casting Directors match their recordings to the intended
contexts, with scores weighted by the number of possible alter-
natives. Casting Directors receive points for correctly identi-
fying the intended context of each recording. Scores from both
roles are combined into an overall ranking displayed on a public
leaderboard. A system of progressive titles, ranging from be-
ginner to advanced levels, further incentivizes engagement and
sustained participation.
2.3. Collected Data and challenges
After a beta phase with structured user feedback, the platform 1
is now promoted through direct outreach and social media. To
date, the game has attracted 240 registered users (72 English,
61 Italian, 15 German, and 92 French), producing 1,018 com-
pleted recordings and 1,489 castings. Feedback has been largely
positive, although engagement tends to decline over time due
to the effort required for auditions, the complexity of contex-
tual prompts, and other practical constraints. Ongoing im-
provements focus on refining prompts, expanding outreach (in-
cluding to universities and acting schools), and adding Rus-
sian and Farsi before the public release of the fully anonymized
dataset. The current corpus primarily targets emotional prosody,
with recordings covering seven classes—joy, fear, anger, dis-
gust, surprise, sadness, and neutral—while attitudinal prosody
is presently less represented, as the initial development and ex-
periments have focused on emotional expression. We have also
been able to collect preliminary observations regarding agree-
ment patterns during the casting phase. In particular, the distri-
bution of votes across emotional categories provides an initial
indication of which emotions or scenarios are more consistently
recognized and which appear more challenging for participants
to judge. Future work will focus on increasing the size and
demographic balance of the corpus, collecting participant-level
engagement and self-assessment measures, improving the de-
sign and documentation of elicitation prompts, and establishing
stronger validation protocols through comparisons with profes-
sional actors and existing emotional speech corpora.
3. Dataset Implementation
To assess the usability of the AC corpus for downstream com-
putational tasks, we implemented a series of controlled ex-
periments focusing on automatic speech emotion recognition
(ASER) and privacy-related analyses. These experiments were
designed to evaluate whether the prosodic information cap-
tured through the game-based framework is recoverable by con-
temporary representation models. We adopted a standardized
embedding-based pipeline in which pretrained speech repre-
sentations are extracted and used to train lightweight classi-
fiers under speaker-independent evaluation protocols. This im-
plementation serves two purposes: first, to establish a repro-
ducible baseline for ASER performance on AC in comparison
with established corpora; and second, to examine whether af-
fective attributes remain identifiable in the learned representa-

3.1. Automatic Speech Emotion Recognition
To evaluate the usability of the AC corpus for automatic speech
emotion recognition (ASER), we implemented a standardized
embedding-based pipeline using the pretrained emotion2vec,
[10] base model as a frozen feature extractor, which is a self-
supervised model trained with utterance- and frame-level objec-
tives via online distillation to learn transferable affective speech
representations. Audio was resampled to 16 kHz and embed-
dings were extracted using the ModelScope inference pipeline.
When frame-level representations were returned, they were ag-
gregated into fixed-dimensional utterance vectors using mean
pooling, mean–standard deviation concatenation, or parameter-
free attention pooling.
The resulting embeddings were used to train lightweight
classifiers (logistic regression or a two-layer MLP) with feature
standardization. We evaluated 7-, 6-, and 4-class configurations
(with the 4-class setting used for cross-corpus comparison)
and applied both random and speaker-disjoint splits, with up
to 5-fold cross-validation where data permitted. The identical
embedding–classifier pipeline was applied to RA VDESS [3]
for English and Emozionalmente [11] for Italian, as well as in
cross-corpus transfer experiments (e.g., training on RA VDESS
and evaluating on AC, ensuring that performance differences
primarily reflect dataset characteristics rather than architectural
variation.

Ecological Considerations:
The performance difference between AC and canonical acted
corpora likely reflects differences in recording design and
production style. RA VDESS contains studio-recorded, profes-
sionally acted, and highly prototypical emotional portrayals.
In contrast, AC recordings are produced by non-professional
speakers under contextual prompts, leading to greater prosodic
variability and more natural affective expression.
Lower ASER accuracy on AC should therefore not be in-
terpreted as reduced dataset quality, but rather as a consequence
of its more diverse and contextually grounded emotional real-
izations, which present a more challenging recognition setting.
3.2. Privacy leakage
Recent studies show that intermediate representations of large
speech and multimodal models may encode sensitive speaker
attributes beyond the primary task objective [12], [13], raising
concerns about unintended disclosure of demographic or bio-
metric information. Using the AC corpus, we examine privacy
leakage across stages of a speech–LLM pipeline. While sim-
ilar analyses could be conducted on other datasets, AC is par-
ticularly suitable because it combines multilingual recordings,
diverse non-professional speakers, contextualized prompts, and
demographic metadata. Our implementation follows the SLAM
architecture [14], originally proposed for automatic speech
recognition (ASR), which links a pretrained speech encoder, a
projection module, and a large language model. Encoder rep-
resentations are mapped into the language-model embedding
space through a lightweight projector while backbone com-
ponents remain frozen. We first evaluate automatic speech
emotion recognition to determine whether affective informa-
tion remains recoverable at successive stages, and then extend
the analysis to speaker age prediction to assess whether demo-
graphic attributes are likewise encoded in the representations.

embeddings extracted at different processing stages, we
estimate the extent to which emotional cues persist in the
model’s internal representations.

4. Conclusion and future work
This work introduced a prosodic speech corpus collected
through a game-based framework designed to address limita-
tions of traditional emotional speech datasets. By integrating
data production and evaluation within the same participant pool,
the AC platform enables large-scale collection of emotionally
expressive speech while providing built-in validation through
human judgments.
Evaluation with a standardized embedding-based ASER
pipeline shows that AC forms a more challenging benchmark
for automatic speech emotion recognition than canonical acted
corpora, reflecting the greater variability and contextual realism
of the recordings. Representation probing further demonstrates
that affective information and age remain recoverable in inter-
mediate stages of the speech–LLM pipeline, indicating poten-
tial privacy leakage.
These findings highlight the dual value of AC as both a re-
source for studying prosodic expression and a testbed for ana-
lyzing modern speech–LLM architectures. Future work could
extend this analysis to additional biometric attributes such as
speaker verification and accent, enabling broader investiga-
tion of how sensitive speaker characteristics propagate through
speech representations.
