Perception of English /iː/–/ɪ/ by Japanese Listeners under Silent-Centre and 
Devoiced Vowel Conditions  
Shinichi Tokuma  
Department of English, Faculty of Commerce, Chuo University, Japan 
tokuma.004@g.chuo-u.ac.jp 
 
 
Abstract 
This study examined Japanese listeners’ perception of the 
English /iː/ –/ɪ/ contrast under ‘silent-centre’ and devoiced 
vowel conditions, extending previous ‘silent-centre’ research 
by incorporating vowel devoicing and aspiration. The results 
show that L2 vowel perception is strongly shaped by L1 
Japanese phonological knowledge, particularly duration -based 
effects and clipping -induced effects linked to gemination. 
Contrary to prediction, stimuli with high -frequency 
components of the devoiced vowel  and the pr esence of 
aspiration did not improve identification accuracy. Instead, 
Japanese listeners with lower English proficiency appeared not 
to exploit spectral information associated with devoicing or 
aspiration, which is consistent with earlier findings. 
Index Terms : L2 speech perception , vowel devoicing, 
influence of L1 
1. Introduction 
An important study that demonstrated that the perception of 
vowels relies more heavily on their inherent duration and 
dynamic spectral change  is the ‘silent -centre’ (SC) vowel 
paradigm, proposed by Strange and her colleagues (e.g. [1]). In 
this paradigm, L1 listeners identify English vowels in /CVC/ 
sequences where the central part of the vowel is omitted, while 
its duration remains intact. Their findings reveal that listeners 
can still identify SC vowels accurately, even without the vowel 
nucleus. This rai ses the question of whether L2 listeners 
perceive SC vowels in the same way, since L2 speech 
perception often depends on acoustic cues that differ from those 
used by L1 listeners (e.g. Japanese listeners’ perception of 
English /l/–/r/ [2]). 
L2 perception of SC vowels has been examined in earlier 
work. Rogers and Lopez [ 3], using an SC paradigm with 
American English vowels, showed that late Spanish L2 learners 
(age of onset  ≥ 18) consistently performed worse than L1 
listeners, whereas early learners (age of onset ≤ 12) matched L1 
performance for whole-word and 40ms preserved syllables but 
were significantly less accurate for silent -centre stimuli. 
Similarly, Schwartz et al. [ 4] found that Polish L2 listeners ’ 
perception of British English SC vowels varied wi th 
proficiency: higher proficiency listeners relied more on 
dynamic formant information (vowel-inherent spectral change), 
resulting in greater identification accuracy.  Tokuma [5] also 
examined Japanese listeners’ perception of the English vowels 
/iː/, /ɪ/ and /e/ under the SC paradigm, using the methodology 
adapted from [3]. The results indicate that perception of /iː/ and 
/ɪ/ is strongly constrained by the Japanese phonological system, 
particularly its reliance on vowel length and consonant 
gemination, and that this native -language influence outweighs 
any perceptual effects of duration neutralisation, despite 
occasional vowel -specific confusions.  Among these previous 
studies, Tokuma [5]’s results are of particular interest in that 
they clearly reflect the influence of the listeners’ native 
language, Japanese. 
Extending Tokuma [5]’s approach, the present study 
examines the perceptual effect of  vowel devoicing, which has 
not been considered in earlier SC studies. In Japanese, vowel 
devoicing occurs in some specific phonetic environments, and 
when SC stimuli eliminate the steady-state portion of the vowel, 
the resulting absence of energy resemble devoiced vowels, and 
that may influence the perception of Japanese listeners.  
However, two issues arise from the experimental setup . 
First, the consonantal environments used in previous SC studies 
including [1,3,4,5] do not trigger vowel devoicing in Japanese  
as devoicing is restricted to environments where a high vowel 
is flanked with voiceless consonants.  Second, the acoustic 
properties of Japanese vowel devoicing are not identical  to 
those of SC vowels. Japanese vowel devoicing, contrary to what 
some research such as [6] claims, does not entail the complete 
absence of (part of) vowels as in the SC vowel paradigm. High-
frequency components of the vowels persist: for example, Su 
[7] identified spectral components in the upper frequency range 
even when periodic glottal pulses were absent, and also Figure 
1 in Yoshida [8] shows the existence of aperiodic components 
in the waveforms of devoiced vowels. 
To address these issues, the present study further develops 
Tokuma [5]’s approach by creating English stimuli in phonetic 
environments that would induce vowel devoicing in Japanese , 
i.e. /CVC/ where /C/ is a voiceless plosive/fricative and /V/ is a 
high vowel. By examining how Japanese L2 listeners respond 
to such stimuli, this study aims to clarify how native Japanese 
devoicing patterns influence the perception of English SC 
vowels. 
    Using voiceless plosives in the /CVC/ stimuli introduces 
an additional issue, as the transition from the initial /C/ to /V/ 
naturally involves aspiration in English. This raises another 
question: whether aspiration plays any role in devoiced vowel 
perception due to the parallel acoustic features between vowel 
devoicing and aspiration following voiceless plosives: the voice 
bar is absent but high-frequency components remain.  
Previous research on the acoustic characteristics of 
aspiration in Japanese proposes its potential influence on the 
perception of the following vowel. For example, Yoshida [8] 
mentions that when a vowel following /p/ undergoes devoicing 
in Japanese, the remnants of the plosive (i.e. the aspiration 
phase) prevail acoustically [ 8: p9]. Yoshida [8] furthermore 
refers to Maekawa [9], which suggests that, in the articulation

Overall, to investigate these issues, an experiment using 
English /CVC/ syllables where /C/ is a voiceless consonant was 
designed to accurately reflect phonetic environments in which 
vowel devoicing naturally  occurs in Japanese. Two types of 
stimulus manipulation were used: the silent-centre type (SC), 
characterised by the complete removal of the vowel nucleus to 
create silence, as used in previous studies ; and the devoiced 
vowel type (DEV), with high -frequency components of the 
devoiced vowel. DEV stimuli were used to examine how the 
presence or absence of residual acoustic features affects vowel 
perception. The SC stimuli were further manipulated to 
examine the potential role of aspiration as a perceptual cue. 
The present study examines three research hypotheses: (1) 
in line with Tokuma  [5]’s findings, strong L1 Japanese 
influence is expected in the L2 English perception - vowel 
clipping to induce a perceptual bias toward /i/ , as well as 
Japanese listeners’ reliance on vowel duration as a primary cue 
in vowel identification; (2) identification rates are predicted to 
differ between SC and DEV stimuli, with DEV stimuli expected 
to show higher identification rates than SC stimuli, given that 
vowel devoicing is common in Japanese; and (3) since listeners 
are known to integrate aspiration into the perception of the 
following vowel, stimuli containing aspiration are predicted to 
be identified more accurately.   
2. Experiment  
2.1. Materials 
As in Tokuma [5], the stimuli of this experiment consist of 
American English /CVC/ syllables where the vowel is /i:/ and 
/I/, specifically the words ‘ teat/tit’ for SC stimuli and 
‘peace/piss’ for DEV stimuli. These words were selected 
because their initial and final consonants are both voiceless, the 
phonetic environment where vowel devoicing frequently occurs 
in Japanese. Furthermore, according to [13], which ranks 
phonotactic environments for vowel devoicing from A to E, 
based on their likelihood of eliciting the phenomenon, the word 
‘titto’ (Japanised ‘tit’) can be categorised as rank A (highly 
likely), whereas the word ‘pis u’ (Japanised ‘piss’) as rank B 
(very likely). The vowel s /i:/ and /I/ were selected since it has 
been reported (e.g.  Nozawa & Weyland  [14]) that Japanese 
listeners of English show great perc eptual confusion between 
/i:/ and /I/ even in ordinary listening environments.  
A native speaker of General American English (male, in 
his 50s, with no speech impairments) participated in the 
recording session and was asked to read the target /CVC/ words 
at a comfortable speaking rate within a frame sentence printed 
on a sheet: “The word is _____, and repeat _____ constantly. ” 
The second of the two tokens in the sentence was selected for 
manipulation, since its unstressed position simulates the context 
of Japanese vowel devoicing, which typically occurs in  
unaccented syllables. The speaker completed two readings of 
the sheet in each session, and the session was repeated, resulting 
in four tokens of each /CVC/ type being recorded.  
The left-channel monaural recording was made in a quiet 
room with a Sony Electret Condenser Microphone ECM -
PCV80U attached to a Windows laptop PC, and the sound was 
transferred to the PC by Praat (ver. 6.4.46) [15], at a sampling 
frequency of 44,100 Hz. 
One optimal token for each /CVC/ type was selected from 
the four recorded tokens based on spectral analysis and auditory 
impression. As in  Tokuma [5], the target /CVC/ tokens were 
excised from their frame sentences and manipulated in Praat to 
create SC/DEV vowels.  
For SC stimuli, the vowel onset and offset were identified 
using waveform and spectrographic analysis, and four /VC/ 
transitions were generated with vowel durations of 10, 20, 30, 
and 40ms. For each duration condition, two /CV/ transition 
types were created. In one type, the aspiration portion was 
manipulated (Aspiration Edited : AE). In the other, the 
aspiration portion was preserved and the subsequent vocal 
portion was manipulated (Aspiration Not Edited : ANE). Each 
type was then combined with the four /VC/ transitions, yielding 
four AE token types and four ANE token types. This design 
enabled an investigation of the perceptual influence of 
aspiration.  
To make SC syllables, for the word ‘ teat/tit’, the central 
portion of the vowel was removed by adjusting the intensity tier. 
To smooth transitions, a 2ms linear ramp filter, as described in  
[3,5], was applied to minimise clipping effects. The duration 
from the onset of the initial C and the offset of final C for ‘tit’ 
was 224.7ms, and 263.1ms for ‘teat’.  
In contrast, for DEV tokens, in the word ‘peace/piss’, the 
central portion of the vowel was removed and replaced with an 
extension of the final 20ms segment of aspiration. The original 
duration of the vowel /ɪ/ was 94.0ms for ‘piss’ and 128.3ms for 
‘peace’, and the duration was varied in 20ms steps. This created 
a six-stimulus continuum of voiced segments: original, 80ms, 
60ms, 40ms 20ms and 0ms.

2.2. Participants 
Fifteen Japanese students from Chuo University in Japan, aged 
between 18 and 22, participated in the experiment. None of the 
participants had prior experience of living or studying abroad, 
nor did they have any hearing impairments. All had studied 
English for six to seven years as part of their formal education. 
Their mean TOEIC List ening and Reading score, converted 
from an English placement test, was 665, which aligns with an 
estimated English proficiency level of CEFR B1 based on both 
this score and info rmal evaluation. Participants were provided 
with a small monetary compensation for their participation. 
2.3. Procedure 
The experiment was conducted in a quiet language 
laboratory at the participants’ university. Each participant sat at 
a laptop and wore covered -ear headphones. They listened to 
stimulus tokens and selected the target word displayed on the 
screen using a mouse, ‘teat’ - ‘tit’ or ‘piss’ - ‘peace’ provided 
as options. Word positions on the screen, as well as the order of 
stimulus presentation , were randomised. The p articipants 
adjusted the sound level to their comfort . The experiment was 
fully controlled by Praat with Experiment MFC objects.  The 
total number of stimulus presentations was: 30 (token types) x 
2 (screen positions) x 2 (repetitions) = 120. 
Before the experiment, a task-familiarisation session was 
held, and a trial session consisting of eight test stimuli followed, 
during which any questions were addressed. In the main session, 
short breaks were inserted after every ten stimulus presentations. 
At the end of the experiment, the general purpose was explained 
to the participants, but no individual feedback was provided.

4. Discussion 
Based on the results, it can be hypothesised that the perception 
of the English /iː/–/ɪ/ contrast by Japanese listeners is shaped by 
the interaction of three perceptual effects:  
Effect 1 – (noted by Morrison  [16]) generally 
Japanese listeners tend to perceive English /iː/–/ɪ/ tokens as /iː/.  
Effect 2 – (reported by Tokuma  [5]) shorter vowel 
duration evokes /ɪ/ identification.  
Effect 3 – (also reported by Tokuma [5]) the SC token 
construction produces a strong auditory impression of vowel 
clipping, which causes preference for /ɪ/ in SC , as many  
Japanese loanwords from English /CVC/ with / ɪ/ are adapted 
with gemination and a short /i/ (e.g. ‘ kick’ → kikku), and 
Japanese consonantal geminates are known to create an 
auditory impression of vowel clipping [17]. 
These perceptual effects account for the observed patterns 
across stimulus types. In full  tokens of Figures 4 -7, the 
participants overwhelmingly selected /iː/, reflecting solely 
Effect 1, as neither duration reduction nor clipping was present.   
In the 0–40ms tokens of Figure s 4 & 5 , AE tokens 
demonstrate a  strong perceptual preference  for /ɪ/ due to  the 
addition of Effects 2 and 3. In contrast, ANE token show a more 
complex pattern: in these Figures, the stimuli of 40ms and 30ms 
show perceptual preference for /iː/, whereas 20ms and 10ms 
favoured /ɪ/. This apparent discrepancy between AE and ANE 
can be accounted for by the acoustic property of both stimuli: 
in the case of AE, the vowel transition contained less than 40ms 
of voiced material, as the edited portion of /CVC/ onset 
consisted of aspiration and therefore did not seem to contribute 
to vowel duration perception. In the case of ANE , both of the 
edited transition remained voiced, effectively doubling the total 
voiced duration (e.g. 2 0ms + 20ms = 40ms). This implies that 
40ms functions somehow as a perceptual threshold at which 
Effects 2 & 3 begin to exert a stronger influence. 
In Figures 6 & 7 for ‘peace/piss’, Effect 3 is absent since 
English loanwords /pVs/ do not evoke gemination in Japanese, 
and listeners were influenced by the interaction solely of Effects 
1 & 2. In Figure 7, as voiced duration shortened, the duration -
based /ɪ/ bias strengthened, and hence increased the /ɪ/ choices. 
Figure 6 for ‘piss’, however, shows a different pattern – the 
identification curve levels off, suggesting weaker Effect 2 than 
observed in Figure 7. However, the curve flattens around 60%, 
rather than the expected midpoint, suggesting Effect 2 exerting 
a slightly stronger influence than Effect 1. This unique pattern 
of ‘piss’ should be investigated in future research.  
One possible interpretation of the data would be that 
Effects 2 & 3 exert influences in a different manner. Effect 2 
alone cannot account for th e flattened curve in /ɪ/ responses in 
Figure 6.  Effect 2 appears to apply generally  to shortened 
stimuli, whereas Effect 3 is strongly triggered by the 40ms 
voiced vowel part. This can be shown in t he higher 
identification accuracy for  AE 40ms /  ANE 20ms ‘tit’ tokens 
(Effect 3 activated) in Figure 4 than 40ms ‘piss’ tokens (Effect 
3 not activated) in Figure 6.  However, this needs further 
verification.  
These findings are now discussed with reference to the 
research hypotheses outlined in the Introductio n. First, as 
predicted, strong L1 Japanese influence was observed, 
manifested in both duration -based perceptual effect (Effect 2) 
and clipping-induced effect (Effect 3), with the latter possibly 
triggered once voiced duration falls below approximately 40ms. 
Second, identification rates differed between SC and DEV 
tokens. Although DEV tokens were expected to yield higher 
identification accuracy due to their similarity to Japanese vowel 
devoicing, accuracy was in fact slightly lower. This may be 
because the listeners (CEFR B1) did not utilise spectral 
information in the devoiced vowels in DEV condition, like the 
lower proficiency (CEFR B1, contrasted with a higher C1/C2 
group) participants in Schwartz et al. [4]  Turning to the third 
hypothesis, contrary to prediction, the identification rate was 
lower for the ANE tokens than for the AE tokens. As with the 
second h ypothesis, this indicates that the listeners did not 
effectively utilise the available spectral information , a pattern 
again consistent with the findings of Schwartz et al. [4].  
  
5. Conclusions 
This study examined Japanese listeners’ perception of the 
English /iː/ –/ɪ/ contrast under SC and DEV conditions, 
extending previous SC research by incorporating vowel 
devoicing and aspiration. The results show that L2 vowel 
perception is strongly shaped by L1 Japanese phonological 
knowledge, particularly duration -based effects and clipping -
induced effects linked to gemination. Contrary to prediction, 
DEV stimuli and the presence of aspiration did not improve 
identification accuracy. Instead, listeners appea red not to 
exploit spectral information associated with devoicing or 
aspiration, consistent with earlier findings for lower -
proficiency L2 listeners. Overall, the results highlight the 
importance of L1 -specific phonological processes in shaping 
SC vowel perception and point to the need for further research.
