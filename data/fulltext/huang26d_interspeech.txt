Abstract
Smooth turn-taking is essential for natural interaction in face-
to-face spoken dialogue. However, reactive models often lag
and fail to account for asynchronous pre-speech motion on-
sets across body parts. Our work presents a systematic analysis
to determine the effect of continuous kinematics on predictive
lead times as one of the first to adopt this perspective. Specif-
ically, we evaluate a Transformer on InterAct 3D skeletal data
by varying observation windows and lead times across diverse
scenarios. Moreover, our results indicate that intent operates
on independent timelines where upper-body signals enable sta-
ble predictions up to 3.0s prior to speech, while turn-claiming
involves a 1.5s preparatory build-up versus the explosive burst
of floor-holding. This work brings initial insights into asyn-
chronous multimodal coordination as a core governing principle
of proactive turn-taking negotiation.
Index Terms: dyadic interaction, turn-taking, motion cues
1. Introduction
In face-to-face spoken dialogue, smooth turn-taking is essential
for natural and socially appropriate interaction [1]. Interlocu-
tors must continuously negotiate floor ownership [2] to deter-
mine exactly when a turn should end and how the next should
seamlessly begin. This applies to both human conversations and
emerging human-agent applications [3–5]. Historically, com-
putational models frame these transitions as a reactive acoustic
boundary detection task. They rely on acoustic and linguistic
cues [6]. However, this speech-centric approach inherently lags
behind human intention [7]. It struggles to resolve ambiguous
behaviors like overlapping speech or short pauses. This limi-
tation stems from a fundamental mismatch. In reality, natural
interaction is a multimodal negotiation of speech and motion.
Speech rarely occurs in isolation [8]. Interlocutors physically
prepare for speech well before vocalization occurs [9,10]. Thus,
full-body motion acts as a proactive telegraph of communicative
intent that resolves these acoustic ambiguities [11].
Many computational works simply append visual cues like
gaze or facial expressions as additional input features to im-
prove turn prediction [12–15]. Instead of modeling continu-
ous kinematic trajectories, these approaches typically rely on
abstracted statistical features or discrete action labels such as
mutual gaze, head nods, or specific gesture types [16–18]. Al-
though validating the predictive value of visual signals, they
treat them as abstract, synchronized data streams. They fail
to precisely quantify the underlying physical kinematics. Fur-
thermore, prior research largely overlooks temporal dynamics
across the entire body. It fails to investigate whether differ-
ent body parts possess distinct pre-speech motion onsets dur-
ing conversational negotiation. Without tracking these distinct
timelines, the physical mechanisms of early turn-taking prepa-
ration remain largely unexplored.
Inspired by this gap, we investigate turn-taking as a full-
body multimodal negotiation. We hypothesize that turn-taking
is proactively negotiated through asynchronous pre-speech mo-
tion onsets across different body parts, rather than decided at
vocalization. Drawing on the physiological preparatory phase
of speech [10, 19], we argue that movement preparation times
vary inherently between modalities. Furthermore, we hypoth-
esize that these independent predictive lead times dynamically
adapt to the conversational context. Specifically, the duration of
anticipatory movement differs starkly based on the intent of the
speaker [20]. Proactively claiming a turn requires a prolonged
early kinetic preparation. On the other hand, defending (hold-
ing) the floor triggers a delayed kinetic timeline closer to speech
onset. Capturing these subtle dynamics requires a new compu-
tational perspective. We must analyze continuous skeletal kine-
matics to pinpoint exactly when each body part manifests com-
municative intent. By systematically shifting the predictive time
horizon, we can locate the precise physical temporal parame-
ters of multimodal negotiation. To empirically validate these
hypotheses and quantify the limits of early-warning signals, we
formulate the following research questions:
• RQ1: Do asynchronous pre-speech motion onsets vary by
modality, with upper-body preceding distal joints?
• RQ2: How do predictive lead times differ between floor-
claiming and floor-holding intents?
To respond to these questions, we investigate floor-claiming
versus floor-holding dynamics on a 3D skeletal corpus, namely
the InterAct dataset. We evaluate a Transformer model across
diverse scenarios involving both silent gaps and overlapping
speech. Our method systematically varies observation windows
and predictive lead times to isolate pre-speech signals. As one
of the first to provide such biomechanical evidence, our analysis
indicates that intent operates on independent timelines across
body parts. Specifically, our results suggest that: 1) a modality-
specific hierarchy exists, where upper-body kinematics sustain
predictions up to 3.0s prior to speech; and 2) lead times are fun-
damentally intent-driven. By capturing preparatory movements
1.5s before vocal onset, we can identify turn-claiming intent
through an accumulation of motion diversity. Claiming a turn
involves a 1.5s preparatory accumulation of motion diversity. In
contrast, holding the floor triggers an immediate explosive burst
at the speech collision boundary. These findings prove that in-
tent is kinematically encoded well before vocalization. They
establish asynchronous multimodal coordination as a core turn-
taking principle.

2. Dataset and Data Processing
We utilize the InterAct dataset [21], which provides 8.3 hours
of high-fidelity 3D skeletal data across 241 semi-structured
dyadic scenarios involving 7 participants. Unlike multiparty or
linguistic-focused datasets [4, 22], InterAct offers continuous
full-body motion capture, making it uniquely suited for investi-
gating pre-speech kinematic cues in two-party dialogues.
2.1. Utterance and Turn Definitions
Following [23], we apply Pyannote [24] for speaker diarization
(0.1s min silence; 0.15s merge threshold). We define a tran-
sition as a Shift (speaker change) or a Hold (floor retention).
Using a 0.3s pause threshold [18] and the Floor-Transfer Offset
(FTO) [25], interactions are categorized into four cases (Fig. 1):
•Case A (Hold w/ Pause):Floor retention following silence.
•Case B (Shift w/ Pause):Turn-taking following a gap.
•Case C (Shift w/o Pause):Immediate turn-taking or over-
lapping speech.
•Case D (Hold w/o Pause):Continuous speech without sig-
nificant pauses.
After excluding ambiguous segments and short backchannels,
the processed dataset contains 2,439 shifts and 1,505 holds,
with detailed case-wise statistics summarized in Table 1.
2.2. Kinematic Pre-processing and Synchronization
The motion data consists of 60-joint skeletal hierarchies in Bio-
vision Hierarchy (BVH) format. We downsample the original
120 fps recordings to 30 fps to align with computational con-
straints. To ensure acoustic-kinematic synchrony, audio times-
tamps are quantized to the nearest 0.1s. At 30 fps, this ensures
a deterministic mapping where each 0.1s audio interval corre-
sponds to exactly 3 motion frames. All motion clips are subse-
quently aligned to these diarized speech boundaries to analyze
the temporal precedence of kinematics relative to vocalization.

3.3. Optimal Observation Horizon
To validate our statistically derived temporal distribution, we
conduct a probing experiment using a state-of-the-art turn-
taking prediction model [14]. Detailed experimental setups are
deferred to Section 4.1. We evaluate the predictive power of
the FullBody, UpperBody, Hands, and Head across six progres-
sively expanding temporal windows, denoted asW L(t)with
L∈{0.5,1.0,...,3.0}seconds. For brevity, we refer to these
windows asW L in this section. Figure 2 illustrates the F1-score
(Shift) trajectories for each spatial region. The results reveal a
distinct and highly specialized kinematic hierarchy:
First, UpperBody consistently emerges as the most robust
comprehensive predictor, peaking at an F1-Shift of 76.81% un-
derW 0.5 and maintaining high efficacy atW 2.0 (75.97%). This
strongly suggests that integrating UpperBody posture with dis-
tal hand articulations provides the most stable anticipatory sig-
nal. While the importance of UpperBody and hand gestures
in communication has been broadly acknowledged [10], to the
best of our knowledge, we are among the first to quantita-
tively demonstrate that this specific modality combination sig-
nificantly outperforms isolated joint groups or the entire full-
body skeleton in predictive tasks.
Second, a striking observation is the drastic performance
degradation of the Head modality over time. While the
Head provides a strong immediate signal atW 0.5 (F1-Shift =
65.22%), expanding the window toW 3.0 causes a sharp de-
cline to 54.16%. This perfectly corroborates our prior statis-
tical finding: head-driven preparatory movements are heavily
concentrated in the immediate 0.5–0.6s pre-speech window. As
the window expands, continuous listening behaviors (such as
backchanneling nods) are inevitably introduced as confound-
ing noise, causing the model to collapse. While head cues are
known for rapid synchronization [27], this severe temporal sen-
sitivity is a novel empirical finding.
Finally, our results challenge the intuitive assumption that
providing a longer historical context strictly improves predic-
tion. For the Full Body trajectory, performance reaches its peak
atW 2.0 (F1-Shift = 75.16%) but noticeably degrades when ex-
tended toW 2.5 (70.68%). This inflection point proves that an
optimal truncation boundary is crucial. An overly extended ob-
servation window inadvertently captures the kinematic “tail” of
the previous utterance (co-speech gestures), which pollutes the
feature space and confuses the predictive model.
4. Asynchronous Intent-Driven Lead Times
Having established the optimal observation horizon, this section
investigates the anticipatory limits of our models by shifting the
prediction boundary further into the past. We systematically
evaluate how the required lead time adapts across diverse con-
versational contexts, uncovering a profound decision-time gap
between the early emergence of floor-claiming intents and the
delayed stabilization of floor-holding states. Our code can be
found on github repository1.
4.1. Task Formulation for Lead Time Prediction
We formulate turn-taking prediction as binary classification:
Shift(Cases B/C) versusHold(Cases A/D). Input sequences
consist of 3.0-second skeletal streams (90 frames at 30 fps), en-
coded via 6D continuous rotation matrix [28]. This represen-
tation ensures mathematical continuity and stable gradient de-
scent. Consistent with Section 3, all features are speaker-wise
Z-score normalized.To evaluate the prediction horizon across
body parts, we introduce a prediction lead timeτ, shifting the
fixed 3.0s observation window backward from speech onsett:
W3.0(t−τ) = [t−τ−3.0,t−τ]. We evaluate four conditions
τ∈{0.0,0.5,1.0,1.5}s:
•Baseline (τ= 0.0s):Evaluation at speech onset using the
immediate historyW 3.0(t).
•Proximal Anticipation (τ∈ {0.5,1.0}s):Capturing late-
stage distal signals (head/hands) and their transition to core
postural shifts.
•Distal Anticipation (τ= 1.5s):Targeting early intention-
building signals (e.g., UpperBody leaning) at the maximum
horizon before co-speech interference.

5. Conclusion and Future Work
This study demonstrates that communicative intent manifests
physically well before vocalization through asynchronous mo-
tion onsets. We identified independent timelines across modal-
ities: distal joints (head/hands) provide transient cues within a
0.5s window, while the upper body enables stable predictions
up to 3.0s prior to speech. Furthermore, these lead times are
highly intent-driven: claiming a turn involves a preparatory ki-
netic accumulation 1.5s before articulation, whereas holding the
floor triggers an immediate explosive kinetic burst exactly at
speech collision. Ultimately, this stark temporal contrast proves
intent accumulates kinematically long before words are spoken.
While this study establishes the distinct predictive horizons of
isolated modalities, human motion is inherently synergistic. Fu-
ture work will investigate how complex inter-modality synchro-
nization jointly influences turn-taking negotiation.
