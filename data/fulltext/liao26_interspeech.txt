Abstract
Speaker diarization identifies anonymous speakers, yet educa-
tional settings require role-based segregation (teacher vs. stu-
dent). Generic models struggle here due to data scarcity and
domain shifts. We propose a Mean Teacher-based Role-Aware
Semi-Supervised Domain Adaptation framework using our pro-
posed TSSD, a semi-supervised dataset. By exploiting unla-
beled target-domain data, our method aligns generic acoustic
priors with classroom distributions. To drive this adaptation,
we propose Role-Aware Union Loss that reformulates the task
from identity distinction to role detection, enabling fine-grained
separation via coarse supervision to resolve overlapping ambi-
guity without explicit IDs. we also adopt PIT-MSE consistency
loss to enforce robust student-teacher model alignment invariant
to speaker ordering. Experiments show our method disentan-
gles complex acoustic scenes into distinct role-based streams,
offering a scalable solution for “many-to-one” mapping in con-
versational analysis.
Index Terms: Speaker Diarization, Role-Aware Union Loss,
Semi-Supervised Domain Adaptation, Mean Teacher
1. Introduction
Speaker diarization, the task of identifying “who spoke when”,
has long been dominated by clustering-based methods repre-
sented by VBx [1]. Subsequently, End-to-End Neural Diariza-
tion (EEND) [2, 3, 4, 5] emerged. Using Permutation-Invariant
Training (PIT) to output multi-channel activity, EEND makes
significant progress in handling overlapping speech and multi-
speaker scenarios. However, EEND remains computationally
costly for long recordings. To address it, End-to-End Neu-
ral Diarization with Vector Clustering (EEND-VC) [6, 7] com-
bines local EEND for overlap detection with vector clustering
for long-term speaker association. Building on this, PyAnnote
[8, 9] standardizes a segmentation-clustering pipeline, achiev-
ing robust performance. DSE-CBM [10] leverages a frozen
WavLM [11] and ConBiMamba [12, 13, 14, 15, 16] within
PyAnnote, and introduces explicitly modeling speaker change
points, achieving SOTA on multiple generic benchmarks.
Despite these advances, applying generic models to educa-
tional settings reveals fundamental limitations. Previously, Xu
et al. observed a significant performance degradation when ap-
plying PyAnnote to child-adult dyadic interaction [17]. Given
the acoustic similarities, performance in teacher-student set-
tings is likely to be similarly compromised. The first major
challenge lies in the severe domain gap and data scarcity. Un-
like standard meeting or reading datasets, real-world classrooms

feature strong far-field reverberation and complex noise, cre-
ating a mismatch that hinders direct generalization. Further-
more, privacy concerns and high annotation costs make acquir-
ing large-scale in-domain labels impractical. To bridge this
gap, we curate TSSD, a real-world teacher-student diarization
dataset. Building on this, we propose a Role-Aware Semi-
Supervised Domain Adaptation framework based on the Mean
Teacher [18] paradigm. By leveraging abundant unlabeled data,
this approach aligns synthetic priors with classroom distribu-
tions, ensuring robust adaptation in low-resource environments.
Secondly, a more critical challenge stems from the funda-
mental mismatch of reducing distinct identities to social roles.
Unlike generic tasks that distinguish individuals, educational
analysis abstracts voices into binary categories (Teacher vs.
Student). This creates a many-to-one” mapping where the col-
lective student” label obscures individual overlaps. Traditional
models fail to decouple these sources without fine-grained iden-
tity supervision. To resolve this, we propose Role-Aware Union
Loss. It reformulates the task by treating the “student” label as
a logical union of latent streams, inducing spontaneous channel
specialization to disentangle overlaps without explicit IDs. Ad-
ditionally, to stabilize this weakly supervised process, we adopt
PIT-MSE [19] consistency constraint to enforce permutation-
invariant alignment between teacher and student models. This
suppresses the mode collapse issue common in semi-supervised
training, ensuring convergence stability.
While tailored for education, this framework establishes
a transferable paradigm for handling semantic ambiguity and
overlapping speech in broader role-based conversational analy-
sis. In summary, our main contributions are:
• We release the TSSD dataset, a challenging real-world Man-
darin dataset annotated for teacher-student speaker diariza-
tion. TSSD fills a critical gap in educational speech analysis.
• We propose a Role-Aware Semi-Supervised Domain Adapta-
tion framework based on the Mean Teacher [18] paradigm. It
leverages abundant unlabeled data to transfer generic acous-
tic priors, mitigating the scarcity of in-domain annotations.
• We propose Role-Aware Union Loss to resolve coarse label
ambiguity. It decomposes the “student” label into a logical
union of latent streams, inducing channel specialization. This
enables fine-grained separation without explicit IDs, facilitat-
ing downstream clustering. We also incorporate PIT-MSE to
enforce permutation-invariant consistency for robust student-
teacher model alignment during semi-supervised adaptation.
• Results demonstrate a superior Diarization Error Rate (DER)
of 16.95%, proving that our method can implicitly disentan-
gle complex acoustic scenes into distinct role-based streams.

The Role-Aware Union Loss forces the model to dynam-

different time steps. As training progresses, this competition
evolves into stable Channel Specialization: the model learns to
anchor different student sources to fixed, non-interfering chan-
nels, thereby achieving Implicit Disentanglement within the
“student” class using only coarse-grained supervision.
2.3. Consistency Regularization via PIT-MSE
Standard Mean Teacher [18] employs MSE as the consistency
loss, assuming a fixed correspondence between the output di-
mensions of the Student model and Teacher model. However,
speaker diarization suffers permutation ambiguity—the model
may output the same speaker in different channels across differ-
ent evaluations (e.g., Student model assigns Speaker A to Chan-
nel 1, while Teacher model assigns Speaker A to Channel 2).
Under such conditions, a naive MSE would force the model to
minimize the difference between mismatched speakers, leading
to optimization conflicts or mode collapse. To address this, we
apply PIT-MSE [19] to the consistency constraint:

3. Experiments
3.1. Datasets
We categorize our data into source domain for pre-training and
target domain for adaptation.

1) Pre-training Data:To endow the model with gen-
eral speaker diarization capabilities, we utilized a comprehen-
sive collection of datasets containing both simulated and real-
world recordings. Following the method proposed in EEND
[2, 3, 4, 5], we constructed LibriSpeech-based [20] simulated
datasets for 1 to 4 speakers, totaling 3536.25 hours. For
real-world data, we aggregated eight public datasets. Specifi-
cally, we incorporated AISHELL-4 [21], AliMeeting [22], and
MagicData-RAMC [23] to introduce Mandarin-specific acous-
tic priors similar to the target classroom scenario. Meanwhile,
we also included AMI (Channel 1) [24, 25], V oxConverse [26],
MSDWild [27], A V A-A VD [28], and Ego4d [29] to enhance the
model’s robustness against complex background noise and vari-
able environments. Following [10], we combined all the train-
ing and validation sets into a compound dataset for pre-training.
2) Target Domain Data:We collected TSSD, a real-world
teacher-student diarization dataset with 45 annotated sessions,
with detailed statistics provided in Table 1. All annotations
must strictly split any pause exceeding 0.3s, with boundary pre-
cision of 0.1s. As shown in Table 1, TSSD presents challenging
characteristics: teacher dominance, frequent speaker changes,
and short student turns. This high-frequency and fragmented
interaction, combined with 3.44% teacher-student speech over-
lap and reverberation, constitutes representative target domain
data. Additionally, 174 unlabeled audio files totaling 110.20
hours are utilized for unsupervised consistency learning.

3.2. Baseline system
Our system builds upon DSE-CBM [10], which applies ConBi-
Mamba [16] into the PyAnnote [8, 9] pipeline to achieve precise
speech boundary detection. Adopting the original configura-
tion, the encoder utilizes a stack of 7 ConBiMamba layers with
feature fusion across the last three layers. Audio recordings are
segmented into 20s chunks, with the model configured for a 4-
channel output and a maximum of 2 simultaneous speakers.
3.3. Implementation details
1) Pre-training:We pre-train DSE-CBM [10] on the compound
dataset (Section 3.1). Training uses a batch size of16and the
C-AdamW [30] optimizer. The learning rate linearly warms up
to1×10−4 over5epochs ,then halves upon2epochs validation
plateaus (min1×10 −6). Training terminates after10epochs
of no improvement (max50epochs).
2) Semi-Supervised Domain Adaptation:We fine-tune on
TSSD using the Mean Teacher [18] framework, initializing both
student and teacher models from the final pre-training check-
point. Teacher model updates via EMA with a decay factor
α= 0.999. To enforce invariance, we apply asymmetric aug-
mentation strategy: teacher model receives mild gain pertur-
bation, while student model undergoes a strong augmentation
including pitch shifting, band-pass filtering, reverberation [31],
and environmental noise [32]. Augmentation intensity follows
a curriculum schedule, ramping up over the training process to
stabilize convergence. We employ PIT-MSE [19] as the consis-
tency loss. Optimization follows pre-training policies, but with
an initial learning rate1×10 −5 for a maximum of40epochs.
3) Inference:We average the teacher model weights from
the last three epochs of the domain adaptation stage for in-
ference. Local speaker embeddings are extracted using the
ECAPA-TDNN [33] implemented in the SpeechBrain toolkit
[34]. Agglomerative Hierarchical Clustering [35] with centroid
linkage is applied to group these embeddings. We fix the clus-
tering threshold at0.75and the minimum cluster size at30.
4. Results and Analysis
4.1. Main Results and Ablations
Table 2 presents the performance comparison on the TSSD test
set. Our analysis of the experimental results is as follows.
1) Comparison with Open-Source Systems:Section I of
Table 2 indicates that off-the-shelf systems struggle in this chal-
lenging classroom environment. The PyAnnote Community-1
[8, 9] (Row A) yields a high DER of 34.88%. Even the robust
pipeline combining PyAnnote Segmentation [8, 9] with VBx [1]
clustering (Row B) achieves only a DER of 26.45%. In contrast,
our proposed method (Exp. 7) achieves a DER of 16.95%. This
significant gap highlights the necessity of domain adaptation for
teacher-student speaker diarization tasks.
2) Effectiveness of Supervised Baselines:Section II of Ta-
ble 2 evaluates the impact of the initialization strategies. Due
to domain shift, directly applying the pre-trained DSE-CBM
[10] without fine-tuning (Exp. 2) results in poor performance
(30.35% DER). Although training the 2-channel model from
scratch (Exp. 1) improves the DER to 22.42%, the best super-
vised performance is achieved by fine-tuning the pre-trained 4-
channel model (Exp. 3), reaching a DER of 21.42%. This con-
firms that transferring acoustic features from large-scale pre-
training provides a solid initialization for the task.
3) Ablation Study on Semi-Supervised Components:Sec-
tion III of Table 2 investigates the impact of specific loss adap-
tations integrated into the Mean Teacher [18] framework. Ini-
tially, simply applying the Mean Teacher framework (Exp. 4)
reduces the DER from 21.42% (Exp. 3) to 19.77%, confirm-
ing the utility of unlabeled data. Building on this, replacing the
PIT segmentation loss [2] with Role-Aware Union Loss (Exp.
5) addresses the semantic ambiguity in overlapping speech. By
reframing the objective from “separating individuals” to “de-
tecting roles,” this modification reduces the Speaker Confu-
sion rate from 5.20% (Exp. 4) to 2.70% (Exp. 5), corrobo-
rating the qualitative analysis in Section 4.3. Separately, Exp.
6 introduces PIT-MSE [19] to replace MSE as the consistency
loss. Since speaker diarization is permutation-invariant, rigid
channel-wise constraints are suboptimal; PIT-MSE resolves this
by enabling permutation-aware alignment between student and
teacher predictions. This valid consistency constraint refines
decision boundaries, lowering the DER to 17.80%. Ultimately,
combining both strategies (Exp. 7) yields the best performance
with a DER of 16.95%. This demonstrates the complementary
nature of the proposed components: Role-Aware Union Loss
resolves label ambiguity in the segmentation branch, while PIT-
MSE enforces robust alignment in the consistency branch.

4.2. Impact of Labeled-to-Unlabeled Ratio
To investigate the sensitivity of our framework to the volume
of unlabeled data, we vary the labeled-to-unlabeled ratior L/U
within the range of{0.25,0.5,1.0,1.5,2.0,3.0}. As illustrated
in Figure 2, the model achieves the lowest DER atrL/U = 1.0.
Notably, at the lower extreme, performance degrades signifi-
cantly. We attribute this to supervision signal dilution: the
scarcity of labeled samples in each batch leads to unstable gra-
dients, preventing the teacher model from learning reliable rep-
resentations. Conversely, an excessive ratio also hampers per-
formance, likely because insufficient unlabeled samples weaken
consistency regularization and target-domain adaptation.

5. Conclusion
In this work, we addressed the challenge of role-based diariza-
tion for teacher and student in educational scenarios by collect-
ing a new dataset, TSSD. We proposed a Role-Aware Semi-
Supervised Domain Adaptation framework that mitigates anno-
tation scarcity via the Mean Teacher paradigm. Specifically, our
proposed Role-Aware Union Loss redefines the segmentation
objective to resolve overlapping ambiguity, while the adopted
PIT-MSE ensures robust student-teacher model alignment. Ex-
perimental results validate the framework’s effectiveness in dis-
entangling complex acoustic environments.
