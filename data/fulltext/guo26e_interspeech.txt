Abstract
Despite extensive research on textual and visual disambigua-
tion, disambiguation through speech (DTS) remains underex-
plored. This is largely due to the lack of high-quality datasets
that pair spoken sentences with richly ambiguous text. To ad-
dress this gap, we present DEBATE, a unique public Chinese
speech-text dataset designed to study how speech cues and pat-
terns such as pronunciation, pauses, stress, and intonation can
help resolve textual ambiguity and reveal a speaker’s true in-
tent. DEBATE contains 1,001 carefully selected ambiguous ut-
terances, each recorded by 10 native speakers to capture diverse
spoken disambiguations. We detail the rigorous data pipeline
and benchmark threelarge speech language models, revealing a
significant gap between machine and human intent understand-
ing. DEBATE represents the first effort of its kind and offers a
foundation for building similar DTS datasets across languages
and cultures.
Index Terms: speech-based disambiguation, Chinese speech
dataset, large speech-language models
1. Introduction
Despite advances in Large Language Models (LLMs), certain
forms of ambiguity remain challenging to resolve [1].Am-
biguity arises naturally in human languagewhen words or
phrases carry multiple possible interpretations.
For example, Mandarin Chinese, an isolating language,
conveys grammatical relationships mainly through semantic
context [2], unlike synthetic languages that use morphological
inflections and complex conjugation [3]. While this structural
flexibility enriches expression, it also makes Mandarin more
susceptible to syntactic and semantic ambiguities, particularly
in written form [4].
Ambiguity can sometimes be a source of humor, but most
real-world communication demands clarity and precision to
avoid misunderstanding [5]. Disambiguation has long been cen-
tral in Chinese NLP research [6, 7, 8]. A variety of text-based
corpora have been developed, and computational models have
been proposed to resolve these ambiguities through techniques
such as sentence rewriting or contextual reasoning [8, 4, 9].
However, most existing efforts focus solely on textual input, ig-
noring other communicative modalities that humans naturally
rely on for clarity.

Meanwhile, recent advancements in speech and audio un-
derstanding and their integration with LLMs have enabled
audio-based understanding to play a more significant role in
multimodal AI systems [10, 11, 12, 13]. In natural spoken com-
munication, some ambiguities in written Chinese can be ef-
fortlessly resolved. We categorise such speech-based disam-
biguation cues into three main types: (1)Pronunciation Dis-
ambiguation: Some Chinese characters have multiple pronun-
ciations, which can convey different meanings [14, 15]. When
spoken, pronunciation naturally clarifies the intended word.
(2)Prosodic Pausing: Although written Chinese does not use
spaces to separate words, native speakers often pause between
semantically distinct word groups during speech, providing
cues for proper segmentation and interpretation [16]. (3)Stress
and Intonation: While absent in written text, speakers may
use stress and intonation to highlight intent and clarify mean-
ing during oral communication. Despite the growing avail-
ability of text-only ambiguity corpora and various speech-text
paired datasets, to the best of our knowledge, there is no ex-
isting dataset specifically designed to study how speech cues
naturally disambiguate textual ambiguity in Mandarin. To ad-
dress this gap, we introduceDEBA TE: a novel speech-text cor-
pus aimed atDisentangling Textual Ambiguity in Mandarin
through Speech. The DEBATE dataset is designed to evalu-
ate and train models on their ability of disambiguation through
speech (DTS). Our main contributions are as follows:
• We present DEBATE, a publicly available Mandarin speech-
text corpus specifically designed to facilitate research on dis-
ambiguation through speech. The corpus includes1 k+text
entries paired with10 k+audio samples (9.66hours).
• We established a structured data collection framework, out-
lining each step involved in constructing the database, includ-
ing the development of the textual corpus and audio recording
setups, to ensure both diversity and quality.
• We benchmark three large speech-language models. Our re-
sults show that these models still struggle to effectively lever-
age acoustic information for understanding true intent, per-
forming much worse than human participants on DTS.

3. DEBA TE dataset
We will provide a detailed description of the speech-based dis-
ambiguation scenarios addressed by DEBATE, as well as the
detailed process for constructing the dataset.
3.1. Dataset overview
Ambiguities in Chinese can be divided into those that can be
resolved from text and those that require speech cues such as
pronunciation, intonation, or pauses for disambiguation. The
DEBATE dataset focuses primarily on the latter, where surface-
level textual content is ambiguous but prosodic and phonetic in-
formation clarifies meaning. Specifically, DEBATE covers three
representative types of speech-assisted disambiguation scenar-
ios, categorized by the speech cues used to resolve ambiguity,
as outlined below. We treat these three ambiguity types as three
benchmark tasks throughout the paper:T P ronu,T P ause and
TStres.
First,polyphonic character ambiguityis a common phe-
nomenon in Chinese, where a single character may have mul-
tiple pronunciations and corresponding meanings. For instance,
in the left example of Figure 1, the character “重” may mean
a serious tone or repeated speech, depending on pronunciation.
In written form without sufficient context, determining the in-
tended meaning is difficult. However, the speech clearly reveals
the correct pronunciation, allowing the listener to resolve the
ambiguity accurately.
Second,structural ambiguityin Chinese arises from the
lack of explicit word boundaries, where different segmentations
lead to different meanings. As shown in the middle example of
Figure 1, the same sentence can be parsed to suggest that the ap-
ple is tasty or not, depending on how it is segmented. However,
prosodic pauses in speech provide cues to sentence boundaries,
where pause position and duration help infer structure and re-
duce syntactic ambiguity.
Third,focus ambiguitymay arise from shifts in semantic
emphasis, often signaled by stress or intonation. In Chinese,
shifting the position of stress within a sentence can significantly
alter its meaning. For example, in the third pair of sentences
shown in Figure 1, when the stress falls on “想起来了”, it indi-
cates that the speaker has recalled something. In contrast, when
the stress is placed on “起来” , it suggests an intention to physi-
cally get up. Such semantic ambiguities caused by stress place-
ment are often difficult to detect in text, but in spoken communi-
cation, speakers typically clarify the intended meaning through
variations in pitch or intensity.
In summary, DEBATE targets ambiguity types that are ob-

3.2. Data generation pipeline
The overall pipeline is illustrated in Figure 2, comprising three
main stages. We depict each stage in the following.
(1) Raw Text Gathering and Annotation:We constructed
a Chinese text dataset tailored for research on speech-based dis-
ambiguation using three sources: open-source ambiguity cor-
pora on GitHub, social media posts with ambiguous expressions
(queried by keywords such as “ambiguity”, “phrase segmenta-
tion”, and “stress”), and structurally complex and ambiguous
examples from civil service exam verbal reasoning tasks.
After collecting the text, we manually annotated samples
into three ambiguity types:polyphonic character ambiguity,
structural ambiguity, andfocus ambiguity, excluding others. To
enrich coverage and syntactic diversity, we employed large lan-
guage models (LLMs) to generate additional sentences, all of
which were human-verified for grammaticality, logical consis-
tency, and correct ambiguity type. Sentences with inappropriate
or sensitive content were removed to ensure ethical compliance.
This process yielded200,401, and400samples for the three
types, respectively.
Next, we applied three prosodic-level strategies to sys-
tematically address the three types of ambiguity.For poly-
phonic character ambiguity, we annotated pronunciations to
clarify meaning.For structural ambiguity, we used “/” to mark
prosodic boundaries.For focus ambiguity, We annotated the
words requiring stress in sentences to highlight their pragmatic
focus and help listeners accurately identify the speaker’s inten-
tion and communicative emphasis.
To further enhance the usability of this dataset in semantic
understanding and downstream evaluation tasks, we generated
semantic annotations for each sentence. These annotations were
produced using LLMs, generating multiple candidate explana-
tions per sentence. We then conducted human review to eval-
uate the fluency, semantic accuracy, and logical consistency of
each candidate. Only the most optimal explanation per disam-
biguated sentence was retained.
(2) Speech Data Recording:To ensure diversity and qual-
ity control, we recruited ten volunteers (eight young adults and
two elderly, gender-balanced 5:5) for the audio recording task,
ensuring demographic representativeness in both age and gen-
der. All participants received detailed instructions on prosodic
features, including stress placement, intonation patterns, and
pause locations according to different task types. different task
types.
The recordings were conducted using participants’ own
devices, primarily consisting of smartphone microphones and
built-in laptop microphones, to closely mimic real-world de-
ployment scenarios of speech models and enhance the dataset’s
ecological validity and generalizability. To control recording
quality, each participant submitted ten test recordings, which
were manually reviewed for equipment performance and en-
vironmental conditions. Only those meeting the standards pro-
ceeded to the full recording task.
During the formal recording sessions, we implemented a
two-person collaborative mechanism: one individual performed
the recording while the other monitored in real time, listening
for issues such as mispronunciations or omissions. If any issues
were noticed, immediate feedback was provided and the utter-
ance was re-recorded. This approach helped improve both the
accuracy and consistency of the recordings, effectively prevent-
ing the accumulation of low-quality data.

3.3. Dataset statistics
In total, the DEBATE dataset comprises approximately 9.66
hours of high-quality speech data spanning a variety of disam-
biguation task scenarios. Detailed descriptions of dataset struc-
ture, task distribution, and quality evaluation metrics can be
found in Table 1. In particular, individual recordings range from
1.15 seconds to 11.80 seconds in duration, and the corpus ex-
hibits strong diversity and representativeness in content. These
characteristics make DEBATE a robust and valuable asset for
future research and applications.

BATE dataset, we benchmarked several representative Large
Speech Language Models (LSLMs). The primary objective is
to assess how well these models can interpret semantically am-

4.2. Results and analysis
We report performance in terms of accuracy and macro-F1. Ta-
ble 2 summarises the performance of three selected LSLMs
across different types of semantic disambiguation tasks. Over-
all, Gemini-2.0-Flash and Qwen2.5-Omni demonstrated com-
paratively strong performance, exhibiting competitive capabil-
ities across tasks, whereas Qwen2-Audio showed relatively
weaker results. This performance gap can be partially attributed
to the more powerful underlying LLM architectures and the
larger-scale training corpora used by the former two models.
In terms of specific task performance, all three models
demonstrated generally decent results in polyphone disam-
biguation (TP ronu) and sentence structure understanding tasks
(TP ause). Notably, in the sentence structure task, the model out-
puts were more stable and consistent, indicating that current
LSLMs possess the foundational capabilities to detect intra-
sentence pauses. This suggests that these models can, to a cer-
tain extent, perceive hierarchical sentence structures and infer
content that aligns more closely with the intended semantic
meaning.
However, in the stress understanding task, all models per-
formed poorly, with Qwen2-Audio in particular producing re-
sults that were nearly random. This highlights a clear limita-
tion in the models’ ability to capture finer prosodic features in
speech, such as stress patterns and pitch variations. Compared
to pauses, which are relatively explicit and have clear tempo-
ral boundaries, stress involves more complex acoustic varia-
tions within the speech signal. Accurate recognition of speaker
emphasis at the semantic level thus requires a higher degree
of prosodic modelling capability. Moreover, current pre-trained
LSLMs are predominantly trained on large-scale, automatically
transcribed corpora, in which prosodic features like stress are
rarely annotated. This further constrains the models’ ability to
learn and generalise such nuanced characteristics.

5. Conclusion
We introduce DEBATE, a publicly available Mandarin speech-
text corpus specifically designed for evaluating speech-based
semantic disambiguation. Using it, we evaluated current large-
scale speech-language models (LSLMs), including represen-
tative models from the Qwen and Gemini families. Our re-
sults suggest that current LSLMs exhibit only modest ability in
speech-based disambiguation, achieving best accuracy of about
58–68% across all tasks and models, which remains far below
human performance. This highlights the necessity of further
improvements in acoustic modelling within LSLMs. Beyond
benchmarking semantic understanding, DEBATE can also sup-
port text-to-speech research on homograph disambiguation and
prosodic stress rendering. In future work, we will further use
DEBATE to evaluate more challenging open-ended and interac-
tive speech disambiguation protocols.
