Abstract
Cross-speaker analyses of electromagnetic articulography
(EMA) are confounded by vocal-tract morphology. We present
an auditable palate-referenced normalization combining palate-
length scaling with a pharyngometry-derived oral landmark
anchoring a low-parameter anterior–posterior warp. In Ger-
man diadochokinetic (DDK) sequences, interspeaker disper-
sion of multisensor tongue trajectories falls from 31.28 mm to
26.36 mm (scaling) and 26.13 mm (scaling+warp; 16.5% to-
tal; most from scaling). Yet leave-one-speaker-out ridge regres-
sion does not improve F1/F2 prediction from EMA for DDK or
sustained vowels (Hz, Bark, tract-length-normalized). Speaker
identification remains high for absolute positions but drops af-
ter removing static offsets, indicating residual speaker structure
beyond a one-dimensional warp. The method improves geo-
metric comparability but not speaker-independent articulatory–
acoustic mapping.

1. Introduction
Electromagnetic articulography (EMA) tracks articulator mo-
tion with millisecond resolution and supports analyses from
phonetics to articulatory modeling [1, 2]. A recurring goal is
cross-speakercomparison: relating kinematics across speak-
ers or building speaker-independent articulatory–acoustic map-
pings. Spatial comparison is confounded by vocal-tract mor-
phology and by the indirect link between EMA coordinates and
anatomy, even after standard head correction and palate-based
coordinate definitions [3, 4].
Standard EMA processing mitigates part of this by rigid
head-movement correction and by defining a speaker-specific
coordinate system using dental references and/or a palate trace
[5, 2]. These steps substantially improve within-speaker inter-
pretability, but they do not eliminate morphology-driven off-
sets across speakers. Moreover, speakers can preserve acoustic
contrasts with different articulatory configurations, so residual
cross-speaker differences after standard preprocessing can re-
flect both anatomy and strategy [6, 7, 8]. This motivates nor-
malization procedures that are explicit about what anatomical
factor is being corrected and that remain interpretable in pho-
netic analyses.
Hard-palate morphology in particular correlates with lin-
gual articulation and articulatory working space [9, 10, 11, 12].
Existing cross-speaker normalizations range from global simi-

[15]. In speech-technology contexts, related approaches have
been used for across-speaker normalization in silent-speech
recognition and articulatory modeling [16, 17]. At the other
end of the spectrum, anatomy-rich co-registration with imaging
(e.g., real-time MRI) can provide anatomically grounded align-
ment but requires specialized resources and still depends on
landmark choices and co-registration assumptions [18]. Global
transforms are attractive because they are simple and stable;
more flexible mappings can capture spatially varying differ-
ences but require careful anchoring and raise interpretability
questions. Accordingly, we restrict the nonrigid component to
a single anchored degree of freedom to preserve interpretability
and auditability.
We propose a low-burden anatomical anchor:acoustic
pharyngometry, which yields distance-indexed area functions
of the upper airway and has shown good test–retest reliabil-
ity for several oral and pharyngeal measures [19, 20]. It has
also been used as a noninvasive tool in epidemiologic studies
[21]. From the pharyngometry trace we extract an oral dis-
tance landmark defined relative to the oral–pharyngeal junc-
tion (OPJ), and combined with an EMA palate trace this land-
mark anchors a low-parameter anterior–posterior (A–P) map-
ping grounded in independently measured quantities (palate
length and the relative oral landmark), so the normalization
can be inspected with simple quality-control (QC) overlays.
Using a German multimodal dataset with EMA, audio, and
an anatomical subset [22], we test whether the proposed nor-
malization (i) reduces between-speaker dispersion for tightly
matched DDK trajectories (geometric comparability), (ii) im-
proves leave-one-speaker-out (LOSO) articulatory-to-formant
prediction (speaker-independent mapping), and (iii) attenu-
ates speaker-identifiable structure. We present an auditable
palate-referenced EMA normalization (uniform palate-length
scaling plus a pharyngometry-anchored, piecewise A–P warp)
and evaluate scaling versus warping separately using disper-
sion and LOSO articulatory–acoustic prediction, complemented
by speaker-identification controls that quantify what speaker-
specific structure persists after normalization.

and audio (48 kHz) for 29 German-speaking adults; 18 also
have acoustic pharyngometry [22]. Reported analyses require
valid EMA palate traces and pharyngometry landmarks, yield-

3. Results
3.1. Reduced dispersion for matched DDK trajectories
Scale-only normalization reduced mean between-speaker dis-
persion from 31.28 mm to 26.36 mm (15.7%;N= 14;
Fig. 2A). Adding the pharyngometry-anchored A–P warp
yielded a smaller additional reduction to 26.13 mm (total
16.5%; 0.7% beyond scaling; Fig. 2B); because only A–P is
warped, effects are diluted in the 6D (TT/TB/TD×A–P/S–I)
distance metric, but the ordering held across the cycle.
3.2. LOSO articulatory-to-acoustic mapping does not im-
prove
Despite improved geometric alignment, adding the non-uniform
warp does not improve LOSO formant prediction from EMA
for DDK: correlations are lower than scale-only across target
modes (Table 1), with a larger degradation for F1 than for F2.
The drop occurs for most held-out speakers (Fig. 3) and per-
sists in separate habitual/fast-rate analyses and under alterna-
tive voiced-frame filtering. Using palate-relative constriction
predictors yields similar performance and does not reverse the
ordering (last row). Sustained-vowel mapping shows the same
pattern (F1: 0.630→0.627; F2: 0.677→0.668;N= 14), and
neither target VTLN nor within-fold anatomy residualization
reverses the DDK result.
3.3. Residual speaker-specific structure
Speaker identification from absolute trajectories remains high
and increases after normalization (baseline 0.751; scale-only
0.800; scale+warp 0.902;N= 14), but drops strongly after
mean-centering and is near chance for velocities (Table 2), in-
dicating that decodable speaker identity is dominated by static
positional offsets not removed by a 1D A–P warp.

4. Discussion
A pharyngometry-anchored, auditable A–P warp combined
with palate-length scaling reduces between-speaker dispersion
for tightly matched DDK trajectories. The pairwise view makes
the decomposition transparent: uniform scaling accounts for
most of the reduction in the 6D tongue-trajectory dispersion
metric, whereas the additional A–P warp provides only a small
mean improvement beyond scaling and can increase dispersion
for some speaker pairs. This modest beyond-scaling effect is
interpretable rather than problematic: the warp acts only along
A–P, but dispersion is computed in a 6D space (TT/TB/TD×
A–P/S–I), so any A–P-only improvement is diluted by dimen-

habitual/fast-rate analyses and under our voiced-frame filtering,
suggesting that increased spatial overlap alone is insufficient for

Pharyngometry is an indirect acoustic-reflection measure-
ment [21, 23] and can be posture- and protocol-sensitive [20]:
it is a static acoustic-reflection procedure, depends on measure-
ment configuration, and yields distance landmarks rather than
direct midsagittal geometry. Using the proportional landmark
upeak helps reduce constant offsets, but a single landmark can-
not capture differences in palate curvature or pharyngeal shape,
andL pal may still contain noise from posterior trace termi-
nation despite envelope fitting. Nevertheless, its low burden
and demonstrated reliability make it attractive as an additional
anatomical constraint in multimodal datasets [19].
An immediate extension is to retain the same auditable phi-
losophy but add constraints: for example, incorporate palate-
shape descriptors beyond A–P extent, or move to a constrained
2D warping family anchored by multiple stable landmarks,
potentially borrowing from more flexible alternatives (e.g.,
Procrustes-style similarity transforms or TPS warps) while
keeping the mapping explicitly anchored and QC-able [15].
More broadly, if the goal is speaker-invariant articulation–
acoustics mapping rather than geometric comparability, normal-
ization may need to be paired with explicit anatomical modeling
of the acoustic targets.
In practice, we recommend reporting scale-only coordi-
nates as the primary geometric normalization and treating the
pharyngometry-anchored non-uniform A–P warp as an op-
tional, auditable refinement. Because the warp can degrade
cross-speaker formant prediction, downstream tasks should be
validated explicitly rather than assuming that improved spatial
overlap implies a more speaker-invariant articulatory–acoustic
mapping.
5. Conclusion
We introduced an auditable normalization for palate-referenced
EMA anchored in a palate-length proxy and a pharyngometry-
derived oral landmark. The method reduces cross-speaker dis-
persion in DDK tongue trajectories but does not improve LOSO
formant prediction for DDK or sustained vowels. The results
position the approach as a practical geometric normalization
for cross-speaker articulatory comparison and motivate richer
anatomy-aware constraints for speaker-invariant articulatory-
to-acoustic modeling.
