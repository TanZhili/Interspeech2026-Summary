Structured Prompting vs. Self-Training for Audio Reasoning Under Limited
Data and Compute: Lessons from Interspeech Audio Reasoning Challenge
2026
Sujit Noronha1, Steven Au1, Kaushlendra Tripathi1
1 Independent Researcher

Abstract
This study consists of our approaches to the Interspeech 2026
Audio Reasoning challenge evaluating chain-of-thought-based
reasoning traces on the Multi-Modal Audio Reasoning bench-
mark (MMAR). For audio reasoning with limited data and com-
pute, practitioners and researchers must choose among various
strategies ranging from prompt engineering at no cost to self-
training requiring substantial compute. We compare these on
the state-of-the-art Qwen3-Omni 30B model across the 1000
audio questions spanning four reasoning categories and 16 sub-
categories. We evaluate three strategies which include struc-
tured prompt engineering through iterative error analysis, au-
tomated prompt optimization using DSPy MIPROv2 and Rein-
forced Self Training (ReST) with qLORA finetuning. Our struc-
tured prompting approach helped achieve an accuracy increase
of 5.5% compared to baseline, while ReST based training led to
a decrease in performance compared to baseline.
Index Terms: audio reasoning, prompt engineering, self-
training, resource-efficient learning, multimodal models
1. Introduction
Large audio language models such as Gemini [1], GPT-4o [2],
Qwen-Audio [3], Audio Flamingo 2 [4], and SALMONN [5]
show that a single model can support diverse audio tasks, in-
cluding speech recognition [6], audio captioning, and event
detection [7]. Beyond transcription, these systems must in-
fer both low-level acoustic cues and higher-level semantics and
cultural context, which is essential in applications where faith-
ful audio understanding is critical. However, while chain-of-
thought (CoT) prompting improves reasoning in text-only set-
tings [8, 9, 10], extending CoT to audio reasoning remains
constrained by the limited availability of high-quality audio-
specific reasoning traces [11]. This scarcity makes it difficult
to decide whether progress should come primarily from better
prompts or from weight updates when compute and labeled su-
pervision are limited.
The 2026 Interspeech Audio Reasoning Challenge [12] tar-
gets this gap by evaluating end-to-end audio reasoning in the
Single Model Track. The benchmark consists of 1,000 four-
choice questions spanning four reasoning categories and 16 sub-
categories. In this paper, we present our submission and empir-
ical findings for the Single Model Track.
Our methods build on Qwen3-Omni [13], a 30B-parameter
multimodal model. To study the trade-offs between per-
formance and resource requirements, we compare three ap-
proaches: (i)Structured Prompt Engineering, which injects
domain knowledge via iterative error analysis with no train-
ing cost; (ii)Automated Prompt Optimizationusing DSPy
MIPROv2 [14, 15, 16], which searches over prompt candi-
dates using development feedback; and (iii)Reinforced Self-
Training (ReST)[17] with qLoRA fine-tuning [18], which gen-
erates synthetic rationales and updates model weights using fil-
tered correct solutions. Our contributions are:
• A system based on structured CoT prompting that achieves
72.6% accuracy (+5.5% over baseline).
• A comparison of three improvement strategies at different re-
source cost points, showing that prompt design outperforms
both automated optimization and self-training.
• A subcategory-level analysis on MMAR showing that Struc-
tured Reasoning improves 13 of 16 subcategories, while
ReST and MIPROv2 exhibit narrower gains and broader re-
gressions under the same evaluation setting.
2. Methodology
2.1. Approach 1: Reinforced Self-Training (ReST)
The process of Fine-tuning audio reasoning models requires
high-quality reasoning traces, which are expensive to develop
and have limited availability. Preference-based methods (e.g.,
DPO [19], GRPO [20]) typically require annotated rationales,
which are scarce in the audio domain. Reinforced Self-Training
(ReST) mitigates this by generating candidate rationales with
the model itself and training only on solutions verified by an-
swer correctness, which matches the supervision available in
MMAR (question–answer pairs).
Our adaptation replaces ReST’s learned continuous reward
model with a discrete correctness filter organized into difficulty-
basedlearning zones. This is closely related in spirit to STaR
[21], which bootstraps reasoning by re-training on the model’s
own correct rationales.
Phase 1: Candidate generation.We combine MMAR train-
ing questions with auxiliary data targeting specific weaknesses
(MusicBench [22] for music theory and CountingQA [23] for
statistics). For each question, we sample 16 diverse rationales
with temperature 0.6 and produce a final answer within special-
ized<think>tags for CoT.
Phase 2: Learning-zone filtering.We score candidates by
exact-match answer correctness and compute per-question suc-
cess rates across the 16 samples. We train only onlearning-zone
questions (26–75% success), selecting up to three correct ratio-
nales per question (Table 1).
Phase 3: Supervised fine-tuning.We fine-tune with qLoRA
(NF4 4-bit) and LoRA adapters on attention and MoE ex-
pert modules. Training runs for one epoch per iteration with
a decayed learning-rate schedule; after each iteration, LoRA
weights are merged into the base model before the next gen-
eration cycle. Table 2 lists hyperparameters.

2.2. Approach 2: Automated Prompt Optimization
We evaluate automated prompt search using DSPy
MIPROv2 [14]. Using a stratified train/dev split of MMAR and
anAudioMCQsignature, MIPROv2 achieves 63.3% accuracy,
3.8 points below the baseline. Qualitatively, optimized prompts
tended to omit the task-specific constraints surfaced by manual
error analysis (e.g., explicit counting rules, emotion cues, and
anti-looping constraints).
2.3. Approach 3: Structured Prompt Engineering
We analyze the baseline results to check the model reasoning
traces to understand failure patterns and then add target instruc-
tions and re-evaluate. This produced four prompt versions dur-
ing the iterations, each solving a specific failure in the reasoning
trace.
2.3.1. Expert Analyst
We observe three dominant failure modes: (i) counting errors
from merging overlapping events, (ii) a positivity bias in emo-
tion/intent classification, and (iii) subjective comparisons driven
by preference rather than acoustic criteria. The prompt enforces
event-by-event counting, explicit consideration of negative af-
fect (e.g., sarcasm/frustration), and objective comparison crite-
ria, improving accuracy to 68.3% (+1.2).
2.3.2. Category-Aware
To reduce strategy mismatch across question types, this
prompt first predicts a coarse type (counting, music/instru-
ments, timing/sequence, spatial, emotion/intent, anomaly, cul-
tural, counterfactual) and then applies type-specific guidance.
The longer prompt increases occasional misclassifications and
yields 68.1% (+1.0).
System Prompt: Structured Reasoning
You are an expert audio analyst.
REASONING FORMAT— Structure your thinking as
follows:
1.HEARD: Describe exactly what you hear (e.g., “a
male voice says ‘come in’ ”).
2.ANALYSIS: Connect observations to the question;
eliminate contradictory choices.
3.ANSWER: State which choice fits and why in one
sentence.
RULES:
•Specificity: Cite exact sounds, words, or musical ele-
ments.
•Conciseness: State points once; do not repeat or
second-guess.
•Counting: Number each event sequentially (1, 2, 3...).
•Emotions: Judge by tone and prosody, not just literal
words.
•Commitment: Avoid self-correction (no “Wait” or
“Actually”).
OUTPUT FORMAT: Respond with exactly one line:
ANSWER: [exact choice text]

2.3.3. Targeted Hints
Instead of broad coverage, we add hints only for the weakest
subcategories: music & instruments (timbre/melody/rhythm/in-
strumentation), correlation vs. causation, and spatial position-
ing (left/right/center, distance). This yields 67.4% (+0.3).
2.3.4. Structured Reasoning
The Structured Reasoning variant achieved our highest accu-
racy of 72.6%, representing a 5.5% improvement over the base-
line. This approach succeeds by enforcing a linear cognitive
workflow that separates sensory grounding from logical deduc-
tion. The prompt contains a three stage sequence consisting
of a HEARDstage for a perceptual description, ANALYSISfor
evidence synthesis, and ANSWERfor final commitment. This
structure effectively reduces the overthinking loops frequently
observed in large-scale audio models.
By forbidding terms such as “Wait” or “Actually,” the
prompt establishes a decisiveness heuristic. This prevents the
model from overwriting correct initial perceptions with halluci-
nated corrections during extended chain-of-thought processing.
As a result, the model maintains higher fidelity to the original
audio evidence throughout the reasoning path. The full system
prompt is detailed in Figure 1.

3. Experiments
3.1. Dataset
We evaluate Qwen3-Omni 30B [13] on the Multi-Modal Au-
dio Reasoning (MMAR) benchmark [25], with 1,000 multiple-
choice questions across diverse audio samples. Each question
provides four answer choices and is categorized into one of 16
subcategories organized across four reasoning layers:Signal
(acoustic quality, anomaly detection, audio difference analysis),
Perception(environmental perception, counting, music theory,
correlation, temporal, spatial analysis),Semantic(content anal-
ysis, emotion & intention, speaker analysis), andCultural(cul-
ture of speaker, professional knowledge, imagination, aesthetic
evaluation). This helps evaluate reasoning effectively and dives
deeper into the low-level signal properties to the high-level cul-
tural understanding.
3.2. Evaluation Metric
We report accuracy as the percentage of correctly answered
questions out of 1,000 as per-subcategory breakdowns to ana-
lyze where each approach succeeds and fails.
4. Results and Analysis
4.1. ReST Training Statistics
From candidate generation across 4,153 training problems (16
samples each), the model produced 66,448 candidates with
43,182 correct (65.0%). Of all problems, 3,669 had at least one
correct solution, but only 889 (21.4%) fell in the learning zone,
yielding 4,361 training samples, a limited quantity for improv-
ing a 30B parameter model.
4.2. Overall Accuracy
In Table 3, Structured Reasoning achieves a +5.5% improve-
ment over baseline at no training cost. Even relative to the 4-bit
baseline (65.7%), ReST shows only a 1.0% gap, indicating that
self-training with limited labeled data did not improve perfor-
mance in this setting. MIPROv2 automated optimization per-
formed worst at 63.3%.
4.3. Subcategory Analysis
Table 4 provides a granular breakdown across the 16 subcat-
egories, highlighting the performance trade-offs between each
approach:
•Broad Efficacy of Structured Reasoning:This approach
achieves best or tied-best accuracy among our evaluated
methods in 9 of 16 subcategories. Improvements are most
pronounced inCorrelation Analysis(+12.0%),Counting &
Statistics(+13.1%),Professional Knowledge(+8.4%), and
Content Analysis(+3.9%).
•Specialized Gains and Regressions in ReST:ReST outper-
forms the baseline in only six subcategories. While it yields
gains inAesthetic Evaluation(+8.9%) andSpeaker Analysis
(77.1%), these are offset by significant degradation inMusic
Theory(−15.4%),Culture of Speaker(−8.3%), andEmo-
tion & Intention(−8.4%).
•Inconsistency of MIPROv2:Although MIPROv2 achieves
peak performance inSpatial Analysis(86.7%) andImagina-
tion(80.0%), it negatively impacts most other domains, with
notable regressions inCulture of Speaker(−11.6%) andCor-
relation Analysis(−12.0%).
5. Discussion
5.1. Resource Efficiency of Structured Prompting
At zero compute cost, Structured Reasoning achieves the largest
accuracy gain (+5.5%). While ReST-based fine-tuning neces-
sitates substantial GPU hours for candidate generation, filter-
ing, and iterative fine-tuning, it results in subpar accuracy when
compared with the 4-bit baseline. This shows that when data is
limited, compute-intensive training might not yield the expected
results for models with strong existing audio capabilities.
5.2. Analysis of Self-Training Failure
The suboptimal performance of ReST can be attributed to three
primary factors:
•Sparse Learning Zone Coverage:Only 889 problems
(21.4%) fell within the ”learning zone,” yielding a mere 4,361
training samples. This data volume is insufficient to shift the
behavior of a 30B-parameter model across 16 diverse subcat-
egories.
•Distributional Mismatch:Auxiliary datasets (CountingQA,
MusicBench) exhibited poor transferability to the MMAR
distribution. The most significant regression occurred in Mu-
sic Theory (−15.4%) suggesting misalignment between the
auxiliary tasks and the target evaluations.
•Activation vs. Acquisition:Qwen3-Omni possesses latent
domain knowledge for audio reasoning. While Structured
Reasoning effectively surfaces this knowledge via explicit
scaffolding, fine-tuning on a sparse, potentially noisy signal
risks overwriting these pre-existing representations.
5.3. Structured Prompting vs. Adding Rules
The prompt evolution highlights an important design princi-
ple: restructuring the reasoning format is more effective than
adding specific rules for each domain. Expert Analyst and Tar-
geted Hints provided clear guidance (e.g., counting guidelines
and emotion cues) for weak MMAR subcategories. However,

5.4. Limitations
Several factors constrain the scope and generalization of this
study. Our evaluation was conducted exclusively on the MMAR
benchmark, limited to a single base model of Qwen3-Omni
30B. The sample size of 1,000 problems may not fully rep-
resent the broader diversity of audio reasoning tasks. This is
particularly evident in subcategories such as Aesthetic Evalua-
tion and Audio Difference Analysis, where the limited count of
eight samples per category introduces potential statistical noise.
The fine-tuning methodology of computational constraints re-
stricted our implementation to one epoch, leaving the impact of
extended training cycles unexplored. A precision discrepancy
exists between our experimental branches. While prompt en-
gineering utilized 16-bit (bfloat16) inference, the ReST experi-
ments employed 4-bit quantization. Although the inclusion of a
4-bit baseline facilitates a more equitable comparison, this dif-
ference in numerical precision remains a confounding variable
in the comparative analysis.
6. Conclusion
We compared three approaches for improving audio reason-
ing under limited data and compute: Structured Prompt En-
gineering, Automated Prompt optimization, and Reinforced
Self-Training. Structured Reasoning reaches the best accuracy
(72.6%, +5.5% over baseline) at no training cost, while ReST
(64.7%) and MIPROv2 (63.3%) both lower performance de-
spite considerable compute investment.
Subcategory analysis shows that structured prompting im-
proves performance broadly across 13 of 16 subcategories.
While self-training achieves narrow gains on a few subcate-
gories at the cost of widespread regression. For practition-
ers working under resource constraints on new audio reasoning
tasks, our results point to a clear strategy: invest in systematic
error analysis and structured prompt design before turning to
fine-tuning.
Future work should evaluate cross-benchmark and cross-
model transfer of the Structured Reasoning scaffold, in-
cluding whether the same HEARD→ANALYSIS→ANSWER
format improves other audio reasoning benchmarks without
benchmark-specific edits. Another direction is to explore hy-
brid self-training strategies that selectively apply ReST to sub-
categories where it shows gains, while testing whether more ex-
tensive learning-zone coverage can make self-training more re-
liable than uniform fine-tuning across the full task distribution.
