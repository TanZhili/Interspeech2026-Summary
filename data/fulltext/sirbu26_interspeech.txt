Rule-Based, Neural, and Large Language Model Approaches

Abstract
Inverse Text Normalization (ITN) converts spoken-
form automatic speech recognition transcripts into stan-
dardized written text and is a critical post-processing step
in speech systems. We present a comparative study of
ITN in Romanian, evaluating rule-based grammars, neu-
ral sequence-to-sequence models, fine-tuned multilingual
transformers, and large language models under a unified
evaluation framework that separates copy and normaliza-
tion errors. Experiments on 24k manually annotated spo-
ken–written pairs, including out-of-distribution evaluation,
reveal distinct trade-offs. Deterministic grammars provide
high precision and efficiency but limited coverage, while
neural models benefit from pretraining yet exhibit numer-
ical instability. Few-shot LLM prompting achieves near-
human accuracy and stronger cross-domain generalization
at higher computational cost. Our results clarify accu-
racy–efficiency trade-offs and provide practical guidance
for deploying ITN in speech pipelines.
Index Terms: Inverse Text Normalization, Automatic
Speech Recognition, Rule-Based Systems, Large Language
Models, Domain Generalization
1. Introduction
Modern Automatic Speech Recognition (ASR) systems
achieve high lexical accuracy, yet their outputs often pre-
serve spoken-form realizations of numbers, dates, and other
semiotic expressions (e.g., “one hundred euro” instead of
“100 EUR”). While acceptable in raw transcripts, such
forms are inconsistent with written conventions and can
hinder readability, subtitle generation, search, and down-
stream language processing. Converting spoken-form tran-
scripts into standardized written text is, therefore, a critical
post-processing component in practical speech pipelines.
Inverse Text Normalization(ITN) performs this trans-
formation by mapping spoken-style text into canonical
written form. Unlike Text Normalization (TN), which sup-
ports text-to-speech systems by generating spoken realiza-
tions, ITN operates downstream of ASR and must pre-
serve lexical context while accurately transforming numer-
ical and semiotic expressions. Existing ITN solutions range
from deterministic weighted finite-state grammars [1, 2, 3]
to hybrid and neural sequence models and transformer-
based taggers [4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9]. Recent work has shown
that instruction-tuned large language models (LLMs) can
perform spoken-to-written conversion through in-context
learning without task-specific fine-tuning [10]. Such ap-
proaches suggest that pretrained models may implicitly en-

Romanian provides an informative case study, as it has

practical questions for deployment: When are deterministic
grammars sufficient? Do neural models benefit from mul-
tilingual pretraining? Can large language models (LLMs)
generalize reliably under domain shift?
In this work, we present the first empirical study of
ITN in Romanian, comparing rule-based grammars, neu-
ral sequence-to-sequence models, fine-tuned multilingual
transformers, direct few-shot large language models, and an
agentic tool-augmented LLM pipeline under a unified eval-
uation framework that separates copy and normalization er-
rors. To enable reproducible comparison, we develop a lin-
guistically grounded Romanian ITN guide and construct a
standardized benchmark of 24,753 manually annotated spo-
ken–written pairs derived from broadcast news, comple-
mented by out-of-distribution (OOD) evaluation spanning
audiobooks, children’s stories, film dialog, and conversa-
tional podcasts covering both narrative and spontaneous
speech styles. In addition to developing new resources, we
highlight the accuracy-efficiency trade-offs and offer practi-
cal guidance for selecting ITN solutions in speech systems.

2. Experimental Setting
2.1. Corpus Construction and Domain Coverage
The corpus is derived from RO-N3WS, a Romanian ASR
benchmark containing over 126 hours of broadcast news
speech fromProTV NewsandObservator News[14].
Broadcast news was chosen because it is rich in numeri-
cal, temporal, and institutional expressions relevant to ITN,
while providing stylistic diversity across channels. For
OOD evaluation, we use a held-out RO-N3WS subset con-
taining literary audiobooks, children’s stories, film dialog,
and conversational podcasts. These domains introduce nar-
rative, informal, and spontaneous speech styles, enabling
evaluation under distribution shift. Sentences containing
numerical or symbolic expressions were extracted using
regular expressions and thespaCy[15] 1 NLP toolkit.
Broadcast news samples were used for training, validation,
and in-domain testing, while the OOD subset was reserved
exclusively for robustness evaluation.
2.2. Annotation Protocol for Romanian ITN
To ensure consistent annotation and evaluation, we define
a normalization protocol covering eight categories:cardi-
nals, ordinals, currencies, units of measure, dates and time,
percentages, half expressions,andspecial constructions.
Each category specifies how spoken forms are converted
into standardized written representations.
Beyond simple substitutions, the annotation guide ad-
dresses Romanian-specific linguistic phenomena: (i) in-
flectional agreement between numbers and nouns (e.g.,doi
copii“two children” vs.dou ˘a persoane“two persons”); (ii)
the use of Roman numerals for centuries and formal titles
(e.g.,secolul XIX“the 19th century”); and (iii) institutional
naming conventions (e.g.,Pilonul III“Pillar III”,Legea
nr. 95/2006“Law no. 95/2006”). The rules favor concise
numeric forms whenever readability is preserved, yielding
consistency across news and administrative registers. The
full guide, dataset, and evaluation scripts are publicly avail-
able at:https://github.com/RoITN.
Three annotators, native speakers, applied the Ro-
manian ITN Guide within theDoccanoannotation inter-
face [16]. To guarantee consistency, all annotations were
verified in two passes: a first reviewer normalized punc-
tuation and spacing, and a second reviewer validated the
final outputs. The resulting corpus contains24,753 spoken-
written pairs, covering all normalization categories. Each
entry stores a raw spoken-form sentence (‘text‘) and its
normalized counterpart (‘label‘). Table 1 reports the per-
category distribution. From the total of 24,753 instances,
1,988 examples(992 from ProTV and 996 from Observa-
tor) were reserved for testing, while the remaining22,365
exampleswere used for training and validation. The OOD
split of the corpus contains100sentences from each do-
main:audiobooks,children’s stories,film dialog, and con-
versational podcasts in a total 400-instance OOD test set.
The splits for training, validation, and testing were stratified
by category to preserve proportional representation.

forms may be linguistically acceptable. We adopt an evalu-

that should remain unchanged (copy spans or NI-WER)
and those requiring normalization (normalization spans or

I-WER). An alignment between the input, reference, and
model prediction is performed at the token level using
difflib.SequenceMatcher, which identifies substi-
tutions, deletions, and insertions between token sequences.
Based on this alignment, we report multiple complemen-
tary scores that jointly capture the overall accuracy, preser-
vation of unchanged tokens, and precision of normalization
actions: (1)Global WERare the standard word error rates
computed over the entire sentence (reference vs. predic-
tion), penalizing substitutions, deletions, and insertions; (2)
Copy WERare computed only on tokens that should remain
unchanged (copy spans), ignoring extra tokens in the pre-
diction, and so measure how faithfully the model preserves
unmodified parts of the sentence; (3)Norm WERfocus ex-
clusively on tokens that require normalization (normaliza-
tion spans), capturing the model’s ability to correctly trans-
form spoken expressions into their written form. To provide
a single summary score while retaining balanced sensitivity
to both preservation and transformation behavior, we report
aMean WER, defined as the unweighted average of Global,
Copy, and Normalization WER (see Figure 2).
3. Comparative Evaluation and Analysis
The experiments aim to provide an interpretable compar-
ison across symbolic, neural, and LLM-based paradigms
rather than to optimize any single model.
Grammar-Based ITN (NeMo / Pynini)is a determin-
istic baseline. We implemented ITN rules usingNeMo
Text Processing 1.1.0and thePynini 2.1.6
weighted finite-state transducer framework. Each normal-
ization category (see Table 1) is represented as a pair of
tagging and verbalization grammars, following the design
principles introduced in [3]. On average, the grammar-
based ITN system comprises about 15 normalization rules
per class, that were manually designed and implemented by
an expert Romanian native speaker, following the conven-
tions specified in the Romanian ITN Guide. The process
relied on expert linguistic knowledge of Romanian number
morphology and domain conventions, as well as familiar-
ity with Weighted Finite State Transducers composition and
debugging tools. Development required approximately 80
person-hours of iterative design, testing, and error analysis.
Figure 2 shows that grammar-based ITN remains
competitive, achieving low error rates (1.92% in-domain,
4.56% OOD) comparable to a fine-tuned 8B model, with-
out requiring dedicated inference hardware. This makes it
attractive for privacy-sensitive and edge deployments.
mT5-smallis a multilingual pre-trained model [17] on 101
languages. The model was fine-tuned for five epochs with
a learning rate of2×10 −4, batch size 16, and weight decay
0.01. The maximum input and output length were set to
128 tokens. All runs used the AdamW optimizer with linear
learning-rate scheduling and gradient-norm clipping at 1.0.

appropriate normalization tool for each span; 3. integrates
the transformed outputs back into the sentence structure.
This design ensures interpretability and allows fine-grained
tracking of which rule or transformation was applied.
From a cost perspective, the agentic system is substan-

lower performance than a high-quality deterministic gram-

4. Discussion
The OOD results in Table 2 highlight clear trade-offs across
systems under distribution shift. LLM Prompting achieves
the best overall performance, obtaining the lowest G-WER
(1.49%) and N-WER (5.51%), and comes closest to hu-
man performance (Mean WER 2.57 vs. 2.45). This in-
dicates strong generalization and robust normalization ca-
pability in heterogeneous domains. The grammar-based
NeMo/Pynini system performs particularly well on copy
spans (C-WER = 0.11%), confirming that deterministic
rules remain highly reliable when the task reduces to faith-
ful reproduction. However, its substantially higher N-WER
(11.62%) suggests limited flexibility when encountering
unseen or stylistically diverse normalization patterns. RoL-
LaMA exhibits balanced behavior but lags behind LLM
Prompting in both global and normalization accuracy, while
Agentic ITN shows competitive copy performance yet suf-
fers from a very high N-WER (16.27%), indicating insta-
bility in the normalization component under OOD condi-
tions. Error analysis revealed two dominant failure modes-
for the Agentic ITN. First, the LLM controller occasionally
fails to identify spans requiring normalization and therefore
does not invoke the appropriate tool. Second, after tool ex-
ecution, the LLM may introduce unintended modifications
when reintegrating the normalized spans into the final sen-
tence. These effects primarily impact normalization accu-

observed performance should not be interpreted as defini-

as a practical reference point reflecting the capabilities of
current large-scale pretrained models. The grammar-based
NeMo/Pynini implementation processes approximately 60
sentences/s on CPU in our experimental setup. In con-
trast, Agentic ITN achieves roughly 0.45 sentences/s, while
direct LLM prompting reaches approximately 0.21 sen-
tences/s. These measurements are hardware- and API-
dependent and should therefore be interpreted as indica-
tive rather than absolute benchmarks. Nevertheless, they
highlight the substantial trade-off between normalization
accuracy and inference efficiency. While LLM-based ap-
proaches achieve the best performance, grammar-based
ITN remains attractive for latency-sensitive deployments
due to its higher throughput and negligible operating cost.
4.1. Human Evaluation
Five different annotators, native speakers of Romanian, are
recruited and tasked to do ITN on the out-of-domain data,
based on our guideline. Human annotators achieve a Mean
WER of 2.45%, which serves as a practical upper bound
for Romanian ITN performance. Inter-annotator variation
remains low (Table 3). The mean inter-annotator WER of
1.65% and standard deviation 0.37 indicates a high degree
of alignment despite the presence of stylistic variation. Re-
maining discrepancies mostly reflect alternative valid nor-
malization choices or slight differences in applying the ITN
guidelines rather than genuine transcription errors. No-
tably, the best automatic system, LLM Prompting, performs
within 0.15 percentage points of the human average, indi-
cating near-human consistency in cross-domain normaliza-
tion. Overall, these results confirm that the task is well-
defined and reproducible, providing a reliable human refer-
ence against which to assess automatic systems.

5. Conclusions
We present the first empirical study of Romanian ITN, com-
paring rule-based, neural, transformer-based, and LLM ap-
proaches under a unified evaluation framework that sepa-
rates copy and normalization errors. We introduce a lin-
guistically grounded ITN guide and a benchmark of 24K
manually annotated spoken–written pairs, complemented
by OOD evaluation on audiobooks, children’s stories, film
dialog, and conversational podcasts. Our results show that
proprietary LLMs achieve near-human performance but in-
cur higher inference costs and require remote processing.
Fine-tuned open-weight models offer a competitive alter-
native when suitable hardware is available. Notably, a care-
fully engineered rule-based system achieves performance
comparable to open-weight models at a fraction of the in-
ference cost.
