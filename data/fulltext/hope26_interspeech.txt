Abstract
Speech-generating devices (SGDs) are essential tools for many
speech-disabled individuals, yet current systems often rein-
force normative assumptions about identity and communica-
tion. Prior research on gender expansive SGD users has
largely focused on gendered voice options, with less attention
to broader identity alignment and the technological conditions
that shape users’ agency. This study examines how gender
expansive SGD users experience power over their voices us-
ing thematic analysis of qualitative survey data. Three themes
emerged: (1) integration of SGDs into daily life, (2) identity
expression, and (3) power and agency. Participants expressed
the need for more responsive and reliable technology, inclusive
and customizable voices, and greater autonomy in defining how
their voices sound and are perceived. Ultimately, their perspec-
tives call for a reimagining of SGD systems as tools for reclaim-
ing the power of one’s voice and the ability to define and enact
one’s will through it.
Index Terms: nonbinary, identity alignment, assistive technol-
ogy, agency, human-computer interaction, voice sovereignty
1. Introduction
Gender expansive (i.e., transgender and nonbinary) people who
rely on speech-generating devices (SGDs) to communicate live
at the intersection of two marginalized groups. They are a het-
erogeneous group themselves, differing in the conditions that
bring them to assistive communication, in how they understand
and express their gender, and in their linguistic and cultural
backgrounds. This makes their lived experience a uniquely
valuable source of insight into assistive voice technology and
its design, yet prior work with this community has primarily ex-
amined the expression of gender-diverse identity. The present
study broadens this scope to what gender expansive SGD users
can tell us about technology, voice, and identity more broadly,
and about the power and agency they hold over their voices.
1.1. SGDs and technological integration
SGDs are a type of high-tech augmentative and alternative com-
munication (AAC) which produce synthetic voices that encode
linguistic messages dictated by users through symbols, pictures,
or typed messages using a keyboard. For people who use SGDs,
the synthetic voice a device produces is closely tied to their
identity and to how they are represented to others [1]. Quali-
tative research has shown that what users value in these systems
extends well beyond message transmission to the contextual
factors that shape communication and the personal significance
of how they are heard [2]. Moreover, because these devices

are “close to the body, inviting frequent interaction” they may
become embodied – that is, in this context, they may become
an integral part of one’s body [3, 4]. This has also been seen
in other assistive devices or tools, such as wheelchairs, where
neuroscientific evidence has shown wheelchair users may inte-
grate their wheelchairs into their body schema, becoming a full
extension of one’s self [5]. Because of their close and frequent
interactions with these devices, SGD users may indeed embody
their devices and the synthetic voice output from them as inves-
tigated in [6]. If the device is experienced as part of the body,
constraints on that device become constraints on the self.
1.2. Identity and voice
V oice encodes and indexes many features of identity [7, 8], such
as gender identity, regional or geographic identity, age, race,
educational background, ability or disability. Further, others’
speech-based assumptions about an individual’s identity reach
high agreement between listeners in less than a second [9]. Lis-
teners extend the same rapid judgments to synthetic voices, im-
pacting how SGD users themselves are perceived [1, 10, 11].
Recent AAC-user-led research on culturally and linguisti-
cally aligned synthetic voices reports that users want voices re-
flecting their identity but lack accessible ways to specify how
those voices should sound [11]. High-tech AAC systems can
support aspects of linguistic identity, including culturally rele-
vant vocabulary and some dialectal pronunciation, but they pro-
vide more limited support for morphosyntactic and pragmatic
variation (e.g., grammatical patterns, register, and intonation)
and frequently require substantial manual programming to im-
plement these features [12]. The Mid-Atlantic Gender Expan-
sive Speech (MAGES) corpus [13], one of the only functional
datasets for training gender expansive synthetic voices, lacks di-
alectal diversity; therefore, for nonbinary and transgender SGD
users outside the Mid-Atlantic United States, it is often insuffi-
cient to meet their linguistically diverse needs.
For some nonbinary individuals (i.e., those who do not align
exclusively with male or female identity), being unable to accu-
rately convey their gender identity may lead to an increase in
misgendering, which may exacerbate mental health issues [14].
Additionally, regardless of misgendering, everyone deserves a
voice that accurately reflects their gender identity. Recent work
has examined how nonbinary people who use SGDs may ac-
complish greater voice gender affirmation with their synthetic
voices (see [6, 15, 16]), highlighting the limitations of exist-
ing SGDs in supporting gender expansive identity expression.
These studies, however, focused on whether voices reflected the
participant’s gender identity, without examining how nonbinary
SGD users experience broader identity expression, technologi-
cal constraints, or their agency and power over their voices. In

1.3. Power, Agency, and Sovereignty
Agencyrefers to the capability of individuals to act intention-
ally and make a difference in the social world [17], while
poweris the chance that an individual in a social relationship
can achieve their own will even against the resistance of oth-
ers [18]. Applied to SGDs, agency can be understood as “the
ability for users to author and transmit messages enacting their
intentions” [19], and power as the ability to transmit those in-
tentions even when constrained by social expectations. These
concepts connect tosovereignty, a framework originating in In-
digenous thought that centers recognized authority over one’s
actions and conditions of participation [20]. Recent assistive
technology research extends this throughrelational sovereignty,
defined as the user’s authority to determine when to act indepen-
dently or interdependently and to define the goals and terms un-
der which technology is used [21]. From this perspective,voice
sovereigntyfor SGD users can be understood as the recognized
authority to define, control, and enact one’s voice in ways that
reflect one’s identity, intentions, and, when applied relationally,
chosen modes of interaction within social contexts.
1.4. The current study
AAC users are often left out of AAC research [22]. As [23]
states:“[AAC users] contributions to AAC research - via par-
ticipatory models that position us as equal partners - can, will,
and do make that research more meaningful.”This study invites
SGD users to participate in the first stage of participatory action
research [24] by conducting a needs assessment of the commu-
nity in an open-ended way, letting the community itself dictate
what the greatest needs of their community are. This will set
the stage for future research to be able to address real, emerging
concerns of the community without making assumptions about
what the community would like to be addressed. Following the
present research, researchers should continue to use a full par-
ticipatory action research approach inviting SGD users to be
part of research teams and involved in the recruitment, data col-
lection, data analysis, and research dissemination.

Three nonbinary SGD users over the age of 18 were recruited
from a mailing list of nonbinary SGD users who had previously
consented to be contacted for the purposes of research. Partici-
pants were compensated 20 USD for their participation.

2.2. Survey
Participants completed two anonymous surveys one week apart
via Google Forms. The initial survey asked for information con-
cerning each participant’s gender identity and SGD usage (see
section 2.1 above) and then presented a series of questions ask-
ing them to describe their daily experiences with their SGD and
how they can (or cannot) express their identity through their
SGDs. At the conclusion of the first survey, participants were
instructed to take a week to reflect on the questions as they went
about their daily life prior to completing the second survey. The
second survey was provided to participants one week later, and
included all questions except the demographic questions; this
was so that after a week of reflection, participants could add to
their responses in case of any new insights.
2.2.1. Survey questions
Listed below are the open-ended survey questions we asked par-
ticipants on both surveys.
Q1Describe an average day from the moment you get up to
the time you go to sleep, focusing on how you use your
SGD.
Q2Describe situations that you find challenging when using
your SGD.
Q3Are there times of day or types of situations where using
your SGD feels easier or harder?
Q4Do you prepare or rehearse things you want to say using
your SGD in advance? Why or why not?
Q5Does using your SGD limit your ability to express your
identity? If yes, please describe a time where this oc-
curred.
Q6How could your SGD device be improved to allow you to
express your identity more effectively?
Q7How well does the voice(s) you use on your SGD reflect
your identity?
Q8How would you customize your voice(s) on your SGD to
better fit your identity, if at all?

2.3. Analysis
We used an inductive thematic analysis approach [25] con-
ducted on responses to the semi-structured theoretically in-
formed survey questions. As a result, major themes identified
are heavily influenced by the survey questions themselves. To
look deeper into the specifics, we further divided themes identi-
fied into several subthemes where applicable. Quotes from sub-
themes are further divided into facilitators and barriers where
applicable.
3. Results
Three major themes emerged from the data: integration of
SGDs into daily life, identity expression, and a cross-cutting
theme of power and agency. Selected quotes are presented in
each theme below. Full counts in each subtheme are shown in
Figure 1.
3.1. Integration of SGDs into daily life
3.1.1. Physical integration
Participants reported that they kept their devices close to them
and frequently interacted with them, suggesting that these de-
vices become integrated into the concrete and physical realities
of day-to-day life.
Facilitators of physical integration:
“My iPad stays on me or nearby and I use it to communicate. ”
“Put on device (worn on strap). . . Device used as needed
throughout [the day]. ”
“My communication device or phone generally come with me
unless they are charging. ”
“[It’s easier to use my SGD] laying on my bed with my device
mounted. ”
Barriers to physical integration:
“Loud places. ”
“Anywhere where it’s too loud, or wet. ”
3.1.2. Cognitive and emotional integration
Participants described cognitive and emotional factors that fa-
cilitate or hinder communication in daily life.
Facilitators of cognitive and emotional integration:
“I use [my SGD] most often in the evenings, when my energy
is lower. ”
“[Scripting is] really important cognitively for me. ”
“Sometimes my communication device comes with me mostly
for comfort. ”
Barriers to cognitive and emotional integration:
“Extreme fatigue. . . makes it harder. ”
“Slow if I don’t [script]. ”
“Trying to answer questions. . . I end up really struggling. ”
“I have a lot of trauma associated with trying to force speech
and that makes it hard to use my device even though I need
to. ”
3.1.3. Social integration
Participants described that working with friends and those
who are understanding facilitates SGD integration while it was
harder with people who are not understanding.
Facilitators of social integration:
“[Using my SGD is] easier with partner or friends. ”
“When interacting with people who understand, [using my
SGD is] easier. ”
Barriers to social integration:
“People who don’t wait. ”
“Anything with speed being relevant. People try to rush me. ”
“Harder with family. ”
“People who don’t understand/accept AAC. ”
3.2. Identity expression
3.2.1. General/broad identity expression
Facilitators of general identity expression:Participants de-
scribed their SGD as enabling identity expression that was not
possible through speech alone.
“It usually helps me express my identity. ”
“Better than my mouth voice. ”
Barriers to general identity expression:Participants had to
sacrifice their expression of identity to sound intelligible.
“The voice I use doesn’t always feel like the best fit. ”
“Better than my mouth voice and still badly. ”
“I chose a voice based on intelligibility and not identity. ”
3.2.2. Gender identity expression
Facilitators of gender expression:Participants expressed a de-
sire for voices aligned with their gender identity, particularly
voices outside of binary gender norms.
“More gender neutral and queer voices. ”
“More gender options. ”
“Have something which people can’t immediately identify as
Male or Female. ”
“Make it gender weird. ”
Barriers to gender expression:Participants also described
technological limitations that prevented alignment between
their SGD voice and gender identity.
“I use the Acapella Scott voice pitched down, and TD Snap
didn’t used to allow changing pitch so I had to use a voice
that fit worse. ”

3.3. Power and agency
This major theme cuts across the other two themes and ties them
together. For both integration of SGDs into daily life and iden-
tity expression, the ability to enact one’s will despite others’
resistance frequently comes up for participants.
Facilitators of power and agency:Participants described re-
claiming ownership, autonomy, and control over their voice
through customizing their SGD. They intentionally shaped their
voice as an act of agency rather than simply accepting avail-
able options. Technology could support this agency by enabling
greater flexibility, customization, and choice.
“Its my voice and i want the power. ”
“I want something that is uncanny valley purposefully. ”
“Make it easier for me to have multiple options to swap be-
tween moment to moment. ”
Barriers to power and agency:Participants described ways
in which technological limitations constrained their ability to
act with autonomy. Social expectations of conversational speed
also constrained participants’ agency. Safety further influenced
one participant’s ability to enact their will using their voice.
“The inability to move quickly and the logistics [...] end up
limiting me. ”
“People who don’t wait [make it challenging to use my
SGD]. ”
“People try to rush me, that makes me struggle. ”
“Sometimes [it’s challenging to use my SGD] if I feel unsafe. ”
4. Discussion
This study presents an in-depth investigation into the current
needs of three nonbinary SGD users. While the sample is small,
the qualitative approach yields richer data, appropriate for an
investigation of a niche, under-researched community [23].
4.1. Facilitators and barriers of power in SGDs
We found evidence that SGDs facilitate the embodiment of
power in expanding the daily experiences and identity expres-
sion of nonbinary SGD users within this study. Participants
were thoughtful about what facilitates their lived experiences
and identity expression, while considering possibilities of what
could enhance this if they were given more power and agency,
and if those developing SGDs listened to their input in the fu-
ture. These current and hoped-for facilitators include faster and
more responsive SGDs, more gender options (including ones
subverting binary norms), and more dialectal options.
We found several areas where barriers to enacting one’s will
and expressing identity exist. Participants described prioritiz-
ing intelligibility over identity when selecting SGD voices, even
when those voices did not reflect their sense of self. One par-
ticipant explained that they “chose a voice based on intelligi-
bility and not identity”, illustrating how communicative effec-
tiveness often takes precedence over personal authenticity. This
echoes phenomenological accounts such as [26], where users
revert to more generic or standardized voices because they are
more readily understood, even when these feel less personal or
identity-aligned. Many barriers also occurred in social interac-
tions, reflecting limited relational sovereignty; participants of-
ten felt rushed and reported that others would not wait for them
to communicate.
4.2. Implications for future research and SGD design
Recent advances in text-to-speech (TTS) architectures show
that many features requested by our participants are now tech-
nically feasible. Systems including Parler-TTS [27], StyleTTS-
ZS [28], V oiceCraft [29], CosyV oice 3 [30], and IndexTTS2
[31] demonstrate complementary forms of control, including
natural-language prompting of speaker identity, style, accent,
and prosody; zero-shot speech editing; multilingual and dialec-
tal voice cloning in realistic speech conditions; pronunciation
inpainting; and duration/timing control with disentangled con-
trol of timbre and expressive style. Speaker interpolation meth-
ods [32] suggest ways users might explore continuous regions
of voice space rather than selecting only from fixed, discrete
voice categories. These capabilities map directly to needs iden-
tified in our study, including identity-aligned voices beyond bi-
nary gender categories, regional accents, flexibility in voice cus-
tomization, and conversational timing that supports turn-taking.
While state-of-the-art TTS systems can provide the tech-
nical capabilities needed for identity-aligned voices, they do
not yet operationalize identity-centered design in assistive con-
texts. Control mechanisms often rely on stereotyped or gen-
dered descriptors, dialect layers are not always independent of
timbre, and evaluation metrics remain focused on intelligibility
and similarity rather than ownership, identity alignment, or per-
ceived agency. In addition, practical concerns such as latency,
privacy, licensing feasibility, and consent workflows remain un-
derdeveloped in SGD deployment. These stakes grow sharper
as SGDs begin to incorporate LLMs that generate or complete
utterances: the more a device contributes to what is said, the
more critical it becomes to preserve the user’s agency and au-
thorship over their voice [33].
Future work should center the active involvement of SGD
users in the design and evaluation of communication technolo-
gies to support voice sovereignty [22]. This may include co-
design sessions with SGD users to prototype voices that reflect
gender expansive identities and regionally specific linguistic
features; collaborative exploration of interactional norms (e.g.,
training conversation partners to wait); and designing interfaces
that visually signal when a user is composing a message.
5. Conclusion
In [26], the author describes how devices running out of battery
can cause an instant and abrupt stop to the embodiment of the
device:“Any chance of continuing to meaningfully engage in
embodiment or hermeneutic relation with the device has been
abruptly interrupted by the device itself. It has become some-
thing other than the device that allows one to speak but rather
an other that demands I attend to its needs—in this case the
need for power.”Humans, too, have a need for power. It is up to
the linguists, speech technologists, speech scientists, and engi-
neers working on SGD research and development to finally, and
at once, recharge their work by ceding power to members of the
SGD community, especially those who are the most marginal-
ized within that community.
