Abstract
We propose LlaSep, an autoregressive approach for speaker
separation. LlaSep uses discrete audio tokens with a causal
language model to generatively separate mixed speech sig-
nals. Unlike existing methods that rely on continuous spec-
tral representations and task-specific components our system
operates entirely in the discrete token domain. LlaSep gen-
erates per-speaker codec token streams in a single decoding
pass, conditioned on the tokenized mixture and semantic fea-
tures from a pretrained speech encoder. LlaSep is trained via
supervised fine-tuning on ground-truth speaker tokens from a
large-scale synthetic multilingual conversation dataset spanning
seven languages and 15k hours. Evaluation on LibriCSS and
CallHome demonstrates competitive separation and diarization
performance and substantially higher audio quality compared
to previous baselines, demonstrating that token-level language
modeling is a viable paradigm for multi-speaker speech pro-
cessing.
Index Terms: speaker separation, diarization, multi-speaker,
audio language model
1. Introduction
Speaker separation, the task of recovering individual speech sig-
nals from a mixture, is a core problem in multi-speaker speech
processing. Real conversations are highly intermittent, alternat-
ing between silence, single-speaker regions, and overlapping
speech, the latter of which remains a major failure mode for
downstream tasks such as ASR [1]. Once clean per-speaker
streams are available, speaker attribution (i.e., diarization) can
be derived directly from the non-silent regions, making high-
quality separation an interesting alternative for robust multi-
speaker diarization and understanding.
Historically, separation pipelines have operated on con-
tinuous time-frequency representations, relying on special-
ized components such as mask estimation networks [2],
permutation-invariant objectives [3, 4], and clustering-based
post-processing [5]. Methods such as Conv-TasNet [2] and
SepFormer [4] have advanced the state-of-the-art on waveform-
level separation, but remain bound to continuous signal repre-
sentations and task-specific architectures.
Recent progress in neural audio codecs provides an alter-
native abstraction. Models such as SoundStream [6] and En-
Codec [7] learn to compress audio into discrete token sequences
via residual vector quantization, and current speech codecs
offer compact, high-fidelity tokenizations that make speech
amenable to large language model (LLM)-based sequence mod-
eling [8, 9]. These discrete representations have already en-

In this work, we cast multi-speaker separation as an autore-
gressive token generation problem. Given a tokenized mixture
of up toN=4speakers, a causal language model generates per-
speaker token streams in sequence, performing speaker sepa-
ration in a single decoding pass. The model is trained via su-
pervised fine-tuning on ground-truth per-speaker codec tokens
from a large-scale synthetic multilingual conversation dataset,
using a pretrained multilingual speech language model as the
backbone and semantic features from a frozen Whisper encoder
as conditioning.
Our contributions can be summarized as follows:
• We introduce LlaSep, an autoregressive speaker separation
model that operates entirely in the discrete token domain,
generating per-speaker codec streams from a single tokenized
mixture of up to 4 speakers.
• To train LlaSep, we build MLSEE-Conversation, a synthetic
dataset containing 15k hours of conversations built from the
MLS [16], Emilia [17], and EuroSpeech [18] datasets.
• To further research in autoregressive speaker separation, we
open-source the codebase, model checkpoint, and MLSEE-
Conversation dataset.
2. Related Work
2.1. Speaker Separation
Speaker separation has progressed from deep clustering [19]
and permutation invariant training (PIT) [20] to time-domain
methods such as Conv-TasNet [2] and its dual-path extension
DPRNN [21], as well as attention-based architectures such as
SepFormer [4]. On the diarization side, the field has moved
from clustering-based pipelines combining voice activity detec-
tion, speaker embeddings, and agglomerative clustering [22] to
end-to-end neural approaches that directly predict frame-level
speaker activities [23, 24]. Because separation and diariza-
tion share underlying structure, joint formulations such as TS-
SEP [25] have been explored, conditioning mask estimation on
speaker embeddings to produce both speaker activity estimates
and separated signals. However, all of these methods operate
on continuous spectral or waveform representations and rely
on task-specific components. More recently, TokenSplit [26]
demonstrated speech separation and recognition using discrete
codec tokens within a single encoder-decoder Transformer, and
TSELM [27] applied language models to target speaker extrac-

2.2. Neural Audio Codecs
Neural audio codecs learn to compress waveforms into discrete
token sequences via residual vector quantization (RVQ). Sound-
Stream [6] introduced a convolutional encoder-decoder with
RVQ paradigm, and EnCodec [7] extended it with improved
training objectives and a lightweight Transformer for further
compression. More recently, X-Codec [28] demonstrated that
integrating semantic features from a pretrained self-supervised
model such as WavLM [29] into the quantization pipeline yields
tokens that better preserve content information, making them
more suitable for downstream language modeling. Its succes-
sor, XCodec2 [30], provides a single-codebook tokenization
with a vocabulary of 65k entries at 50 tokens per second, bal-
ancing reconstruction fidelity with a compact token sequence
ideal for language models.
2.3. Language Models for Audio
The availability of high-quality discrete audio tokens has en-
abled language-model-based approaches to speech generation.
AudioLM [31] first showed that hierarchical token prediction
over semantic and acoustic tokens can generate coherent speech
continuations without any text supervision. V ALL-E [10] was
the first to cast text-to-speech as conditional codec language
modeling, demonstrating zero-shot speaker adaptation from a
3-second prompt, with V ALL-E X [32] extending this to cross-
lingual synthesis. LLaSA [30] simplified this further by using
a single-layer scalar quantization codec (XCodec2) and a stan-
dard Llama [33] architecture, showing that scaling both train-
ing and inference compute yields consistent improvements in
speech naturalness. Whisper [34], while not an audio genera-
tive model, provides robust multilingual speech representations
that have been widely adopted as conditioning features. Despite
these advances in single-stream generation, applying causal
language models to blind multi-speaker separation, where the
model must disentangle all overlapping sources from a sin-
gle tokenized mixture without enrollment, remains unexplored.
Our work bridges this gap by recasting speaker separation as
autoregressive token generation from a pretrained speech lan-
guage model.
3. Method
3.1. LlaSep Architecture
Overview.Our approach takes a multi-speaker audio mixture,
encodes it into discrete tokens as well as continuous Whisper
embeddings, and generates separated per-speaker token streams
using a causal language model (cf. Figure 1). The pipeline con-
sists of three components: (i) a discrete audio tokenizer, (ii) a
semantic conditioning module using Whisper, and (iii) an au-
toregressive language model.
Audio Tokenization.We use XCodec2 as the audio tokenizer.
Given a waveform sampled at 16 kHz, XCodec2 encodes it into
a sequence of integer tokens drawn from a vocabulary of size
V= 65 536at a framerate of 50 Hz. For an 8-second seg-
ment, this yields a sequence of 400 tokens per source. Both
the input mixture and individual speaker sources are tokenized
independently. The XCodec2 decoder reconstructs waveforms
from generated token sequences at inference time.
Semantic Conditioning.To provide semantic guidance for
separation, we extract embeddings from a Whisper-small en-
coder and project them into the hidden dimension of the audio-
language model via a learned linear projection:
hsem =W proj·Whisper(x)(1)
wherexdenotes the input audio signal. During training, the
Whisper encoder receives the sum of clean source signals. Dur-
ing inference, it receives the mixture signal directly.
Language Model Backbone.We use LLaSA-1B-
Multilingual [30], a causal decoder-only transformer, as
the backbone. Since LLaSA already operates over XCodec2
tokens, we only extend the embedding layer with special
speaker delimiter tokens that mark per-speaker output regions.
The Whisper projection embeddings are injected into the
sequence at the corresponding positions before the speaker
output regions. The model generates up toK= 4speaker
token streams autoregressively, conditioned on the mixture
tokens and semantic embeddings as a prefix.
Training: Supervised Fine-Tuning.The model is trained with
a standard cross-entropy loss on the ground-truth speaker token
sequences. The loss is computed only over the speaker output
tokens; mixture tokens and Whisper embeddings in the prefix
are masked from the loss:

3.2. MLSEE-Conversation Dataset
To train LlaSep we construct MLSEE-Conversation, a large-
scale synthetic multi-speaker conversation dataset derived from
three multilingual speech corpora: MLS [16], Emilia [17], and
EuroSpeech [18]. The dataset comprises approximately 6.9 mil-
lion 8-second conversation samples, each tokenized into a fixed-
length sequence of 400 XCodec2 tokens.
Source Data and Languages.The MLS dataset contributes the
large majority of source utterances (89.0%), with smaller contri-
butions from Emilia (7.5%) and EuroSpeech (3.5%). Seven lan-
guages are represented with roughly balanced coverage (cf. Fig-
ure 2). Conversations are predominantly monolingual (98.9%)
of samples have all speakers in the same language and the re-
maining 1.1% form multilingual mixtures.
Conversation Generation.Speakers are randomly sampled per
conversation, and each sample is synthesized according to one
of four generation methods that control conversational dynam-
ics:
•Normal(55.1%): Sequential turn-taking where each speaker
is conditioned on the previous speaker’s offset, with gaus-
sian distributed segment durations, producing naturally flow-
ing conversations with realistic turn transitions.
•Erlang(21.4%): Each speaker’s activity is sampled inde-
pendently with Erlang-distributed segment durations, without
conditioning on other speakers. This leads to more frequent
overlap and a more balanced distribution of up to 4 speaker
mixtures.
•Main+interrupts(16.6%): One dominant speaker with ran-
domly sampled interruptions from other speakers.
•Full overlap(6.9%): All speakers active simultaneously,
representing the most challenging separation scenario.

(69.3%), followed by 1 speaker (15.5%), 3 speakers (7.8%),

20 samples and compute the mean to account for nondetermin-

time-frequency masking (SepFormer, SepReformer) or MixIT-

then decoded, producing perceptually clean audio by construc-

reproduction of the source, but content fidelity depends entirely
on the codec’s reconstruction quality and the language model’s
token accuracy.
MLSEE-Conversations results.Tables 2 and 3 report results
on held-out synthetic conversations. LlaSep leads across all
metrics in both settings, though performance degrades with in-
creasing speaker count: DER rises from 28.11% (2 speakers)
to 43.79% (4 speakers). This is expected, as each additional
speaker stream lengthens the output sequence and compounds
error propagation during autoregressive decoding. Despite this
degradation, LlaSep still outperforms PixIT in the 4-speaker
setting, and unlike the mask-based baselines, does not require
architectural changes to handle variable speaker counts.

ble 4), PixIT achieves the second best DER (30.20%)
with LlaSep outperforms with (24.84%), additonally, LlaSep
produces substantially higher-quality separated audio (e.g.,
DNSMOS-OVRL: 3.09 vs. 2.26). That LlaSep leads on both
diarization accuracy and audio quality despite being trained
exclusively on synthetic data suggests strong domain trans-

speech. This is achieved using only synthetic training data,
without exposure to any real conversational speech, further un-
derscoring the quality of our synthetic dataset. Incorporating
more naturalistic turn-taking during data generation could fur-
ther widen this gap.
5. Conclusion
We presented LlaSep, an autoregressive speaker separation
model capable of achieving high perceptual separation qual-
ity and competitive diarization accuracy. A key practical ad-
vantage of the autoregressive formulation is that speaker count
is implicitly handled by the generation process, allowing it to
easily extend to an arbitrary number of speakers and avoid the
fixed-output limitation that can cause issues with mask-based
methods.
