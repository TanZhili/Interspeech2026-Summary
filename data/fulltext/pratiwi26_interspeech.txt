Adaptation to Room Acoustics in Understanding V ocoded Speech: A
Comparison Between Listeners With Varying Immersion Age
Epri Pratiwi1, C.T. Justine Hui1, Yusuke Hioka1
1 Acoustics and Vibration Research Centre,
Department of Mechanical and Mechatronics Engineering, University of Auckland, New Zealand
epri.pratiwi@auckland.ac.nz, justine.hui@auckland.ac.nz, yusuke.hioka@ieee.org
Abstract
This study investigates adaptation to room acoustics under
vocoded speech in listeners with varying immersion age. Adap-
tation was induced using a blocked presentation, with sentences
presented across the same room acoustic condition. Speech
intelligibility was assessed using a word identification task,
and listening effort by peak pupil dilation (PPD). Under non-
vocoded speech, intelligibility remained near ceiling across
environments, limiting observable adaptation, whereas under
vocoded speech it varied across sentences. In contrast, PPD
showed similar levels across immersion groups for both non-
vocoded and vocoded speech. Overall, the pattern of change
across successive sentences was comparable across immer-
sion groups. These findings suggest that although immersion
age may influence speech recognition when speech cues are
degraded, adaptation to consistent room acoustics may oper-
ate similarly across listeners with different immersion back-
grounds.
Index Terms: adaptation, room acoustics, vocoded speech, im-
mersion age
1. Introduction
Cochlear implants (CIs) restore access to sound and substan-
tially improve speech recognition for individuals with severe-
to-profound hearing loss. However, CI users often experience
difficulty understanding speech in everyday listening environ-
ments where reverberation is typically observed [1, 2]. Rever-
beration greatly reduces the modulations present in the temporal
envelopes of speech signal, making it challenging for CI users
to extract useful information about the speech (e.g. F0 modu-
lations, location of syllable/word boundaries) [3]. Because CI
processing primarily conveys temporal envelope cues across a
limited number of spectral channels [4, 5], even small amounts
of reverberation can disproportionately deteriorate speech intel-
ligibility for CI users [6].
Although reverberation degrades speech intelligibility, the
human auditory system can adapt to a reverberant listening en-
vironment through prior exposure, a phenomenon referred to as
room adaptation [7, 8]. A series of studies manipulating prior
room exposure through blocked (constant room acoustic) and
unblocked (varying room acoustic) presentation have shown
that speech recognition is higher when the acoustic environment
remains consistent across trials, whereas frequent room changes
disrupt performance [9]. This adaptation has been demonstrated
in normal-hearing (NH) listeners and has also been observed
in adult CI users [7, 10], who show higher intelligibility under
blocked presentation.
However, the impact of reverberation on speech perception
may also depend on the listener’s linguistic background. Re-
search on second-language (L2) speech perception has reported
that group differences between native and non-native listeners
are often small in anechoic conditions but become more pro-
nounced when the space is reverberant [11, 12]. Further studies
indicate that immersion age and proficiency contribute to per-
formance under adverse listening conditions [13, 14, 15]. In
addition, recent work has demonstrated that familiarity with a
specific language variety (e.g., New Zealand English), shapes
speech intelligibility across acoustic environments, under re-
verberation and varying spatial separation [16]. These findings
suggest that different levels of linguistic experience may lead to
different strategies for processing degraded speech.
Although listeners adapt efficiently to exploit information
from the preceding acoustic context [17], most existing ev-
idence is derived from native listeners. It remains unclear
whether similar adaptation occurs when speech is processed in
a later acquired language under CI processing. With contin-
ued globalisation bringing together speakers and listeners from
diverse language backgrounds in environments where their sec-
ond language is spoken, understanding how linguistic experi-
ence influences adaptation to degraded speech has become in-
creasingly relevant.
Previous studies on room adaptation have primarily exam-
ined behavioural measure such as speech intelligibility [7, 8].
While intelligibility reflects the accuracy with which listeners
recognise spoken material, it does not necessarily capture the
cognitive resources required to achieve that performance [18].
These cognitive demands are commonly referred to as listening
effort, defined as the mental resources allocated to understand
speech under challenging conditions [19]. Physiological mea-
sures such as pupillometry provide an objective index of lis-
tening effort, as pupil dilation increases with task-evoked men-
tal effort [18, 20, 21]. In addition, the cognitive resources re-
quired for speech comprehension may vary depending on listen-
ers’ linguistic experience, such as immersion age in the target
language. To our knowledge, no study has examined whether
changes in speech intelligibility following room adaptation un-
der CI processing are accompanied by corresponding changes in
listening effort across listeners with different immersion ages.
The present study investigates adaptation to room acoustics
under CI processing across listeners with different immersion
experiences. Using a blocked presentation, sentences were pre-
sented within a constant acoustic environment to induce adap-
tation. Speech intelligibility and listening effort were mea-
sured across sentences. Therefore, the present study aims to
investigate: (i) whether intelligibility gains following consis-
tent acoustic exposure are accompanied by reductions in the
pupil dilation response, and (ii) whether this relationship dif-
fers across listeners with varying levels of language familiarity
under CI processing.

2. Methods
2.1. Stimuli
The speech materials consisted of three-word English sen-
tences drawn from the simplified University of Canterbury
Auditory–Visual Matrix Sentence Test (UCAMST-P) [22].
UCAMST-P is based on an 18-word, 6 × 3 testing matrix, where
sentences follow a fixed structure: ”Quantity + Adjective + Ob-
ject”. These pseudo-sentences are randomly generated while
maintaining grammatical structure but remaining semantically
unpredictable.
Stimuli were presented in two speech types: non-vocoded
speech (unprocessed) and vocoded speech. V ocoded speech was
used to simulate CI processing. This approach allows investiga-
tion of the effects of CI processing without recruiting individ-
uals with CIs [23], thereby avoiding patient-specific confound-
ing factors such as variability in neural survival patterns [24].
The methods for vocoding was followed the method described
in a previous study [25]. The speech signals were decomposed
into eight frequency bands spanning 400–7000 Hz using FIR
band-pass filters, with cutoff frequencies spaced according to
the mel scale. Within each frequency band, the temporal enve-
lope was extracted using the Hilbert transform and used to mod-
ulate a white noise carrier. The modulated signals were subse-
quently filtered using the same band-pass filters and summed
across channels to produce the final vocoded stimulus.
2.2. Room Acoustics
Traditional CI studies have often simulated reverberation by
convolving speech with measured or simulated room impulse
responses (RIRs) and presenting the signal through the device’s
direct audio input [1, 2, 3]. Because direct audio input bypasses
the listener’s head-related transfer functions (HRTFs), this ap-
proach removes spatial cues and provides only a limited ap-
proximation of real listening conditions. To address these lim-
itations, we implemented a spatial sound reproduction system
[26] to recreate measured acoustic environments using an array
of loudspeakers. In this way, participants were immersed in the
reproduced sound field, enabling them to experience the spatial
acoustics of the original environment while utilising their own
HRTFs.

were first measured in representative environments using a 32-

2.3. Presentation
Participants were seated at the centre of the spherical loud-
speaker array in an anechoic chamber at the University of Auck-
land. The seat position was adjusted so that the centre of
the head aligned with the geometric centre of the loudspeaker
sphere. A computer monitor equipped with a Tobii Pro Spark
eye tracker (sampling rate: 60 Hz) was positioned approxi-
mately 60 cm in front of the participant. A calibration was con-
ducted prior to the experiment to ensure accurate eye tracking.
Consistent with previous studies [30], the luminance of the vi-
sual field was controlled to avoid extremes in pupil size driven
by the light reflex rather than cognitive load. Screen luminance
and ambient lighting were held constant throughout the experi-

ment, with ambient illumination measured at 80 lux.
Speech stimuli were presented using the 31-channel loud-
speaker system, calibrated to 60 dBA. Each trial began with
the presentation of a fixation cross (+) on the screen. Partici-
pants were instructed to maintain visual fixation throughout the
2-s pre-stimulus silent interval and during sentence presenta-
tion. A single sentence was then presented (Figure 1(a)), and
pupil diameter was recorded continuously from trial onset until
response initiation. Following stimulus offset, participants en-
tered their responses via a graphical user interface (GUI) (Fig-
ure 1(b)). Each sentence was presented once only, and no per-
formance feedback was provided.
Prior to the main experiment, participants completed a short
practice session to familiarise themselves with the task and re-
sponse procedure. The main experiment comprised 100 trials in
total, corresponding to ten sentences in each of the five acous-
tic environments for both speech types. A blocked presentation
design was employed to examine adaptation to consistent room
acoustics, consistent with established room adaptation studies
[7, 9, 10]. Within each block, all ten consecutive sentences were
rendered in the same acoustic environment, providing sustained
exposure to that room condition. The order of the five acoustic
environments was counterbalanced across participants to min-
imise order effects. Adaptation was analysed at the sentence
level (sentence order 1-10) to capture trial-by-trial changes with
increasing exposure. Speech intelligibility and pupil dilation
were recorded on each trial. Listeners were encouraged to guess
when unsure, and regular breaks were provided to minimise fa-
tigue. The procedure was approved by the University of Auck-
land Human Participants Ethics Committee (#22965).
2.4. Participants
A total of 51 participants (age range: 24–41 years) were re-
cruited and classified into three immersion groups based on
their exposure to New Zealand English (NZE): early immersed,
late immersed, and non-immersed.
• Early immersed listeners: listener who was born in New
Zealand or having immigrated before the age of 13 (n= 17,
28.0±5.7years old).
• Late immersed listeners: listener who had lived in New
Zealand for more than two year and used English in daily
communication (n= 14,39.0±6.7years old).
• Non-immersed listeners: listener with limited immersion in
NZE, having resided in New Zealand for less than a year and
primarily acquired English through formal education (n=
20,31.5±4.8years old).
All participants reported normal or corrected-to-normal vi-
sion and no history of neurological, hearing, visual, or lan-
guage impairments. The first language of the listeners in late-
immersed group included: Chinese Mandarin, German, Bahasa
Indonesia, Khmer, and Vietnamese.
2.5. Pupil data pre-processing
After data collection, pupil traces were pre-processed follow-
ing the previous study [31]. A pupil diameter deviating more
than 3 mm standard deviations from the mean pupil diameter
within a trial was classified as a blink. Blinks were removed
by a linear interpolation that started about 80 ms before and
ended 150 ms after the blinks. The de-blinked signals were
smoothed using a moving average filter with a symmetric rect-
angular window of 117 ms to remove high-frequency artefacts.
Pupil diameter was then averaged across the left and right eyes.
The averaged signal was then baseline-corrected by subtract-
ing the mean pupil diameter during the 2-s pre-stimulus inter-
val. The baseline-corrected data were normalised relative to the
pupil range [32]. The lower and upper limits of the individual’s
pupil range were defined as the 0.025 and 0.975 quantiles of the
data points within 2.5-s after the sentence onset. The normalisa-
tion involved a subtraction of the lower limit and a subsequent
division by the dynamic range. PPD calculated as the maximum
baseline-corrected pupil diameter within the analysis window
extending from sentence onset to stimulus offset [21, 33, 34].
2.6. Marking and Statistical Analysis
Participants’ responses were scored on a word-by-word ba-
sis, with the number of correctly identified words per sentence
recorded [22]. These scores were subsequently normalised to
range from 0 to 1 and are referred to as proportion correct
hereafter. Speech intelligibility data were analysed using lin-
ear mixed-effects models implemented in thelme4package [35]
in R [36]. Acoustic environment, speech type (non-vocoded,
vocoded), listener group (early-immersed, late-immersed, non-
immersed), and sentence order (1–10 within each blocked se-
quence) were included as fixed effects. Participant ID was in-
cluded as a random intercept. The model was compared us-
ing likelihood ratio tests, by including interactions between
multiple factors when they significantly improved model fit.
Post-hoc analysis for pairwise comparison was implemented by
emmeans package [37] with correction of p-values by Tukey
method.
3. Results and Discussion
3.1. Speech intelligibility analysis
A significant three-way interaction was observed between envi-
ronment, speech type, and sentence order (χ 2(36) = 146.57,
p<.001). In addition, a significant interaction between speech
type and immersion group was observed (χ 2(2) = 43.48,
p < .001). Interactions involving immersion group and sen-
tence order were not significant.
The present results show that speech intelligibility was
strongly affected by vocoder processing, with substantially
lower performance under vocoded speech compared with non-
vocoded speech. This finding is consistent with previous studies
demonstrating that CI processing substantially reduces the spec-
tral information available for speech recognition [38]. When
speech was vocoded, listeners therefore faced a listening condi-
tion that resembles the degraded signal experienced by CI users.
Importantly, the results further suggest that this disadvantage
may be exacerbated when listening occurs in a later-acquired
language. While previous research on CI users has primarily
focused on native listeners [7], relatively little work has exam-
ined how non-native listeners adapt to room acoustics under CI
processing. The present findings therefore highlight the poten-
tial challenges faced by CI users when listening in their second
language.
Despite these disadvantages, listeners demonstrated a clear
ability to adapt to the acoustic environment over successive sen-
tences. As illustrated in Fig. 2, intelligibility changed across
sentence order depending on both the acoustic environment and
whether speech was vocoded. Under vocoded speech, intelligi-
bility varied more strongly across acoustic environments, sug-
gesting that listeners relied more heavily on prior exposure to
the acoustic context [17] when the speech signal was degraded.

3.2. Listening effort analysis
A significant two-way interaction between speech type and
group was observed (χ 2(2) = 29.82,p <0.001), as well as
between environment and speech type (χ 2(4) = 32.60,p <
0.001). These results indicate that listening effort differed de-
pending on both speech processing condition and acoustic envi-
ronment. As shown in Fig. 3, vocoded speech generally elicited
larger pupil dilation responses than non-vocoded speech, con-
sistent with increased cognitive processing demands associated
with degraded speech [39]. The magnitude of PPD also varied
across immersion groups, which may reflect differences in how
listeners with varying levels of language experience cope with
degraded speech input.
The results also revealed a significant interaction between
environment and speech type, indicating that the influence of
room acoustics on listening effort depended on whether speech
was vocoded. As illustrated in Fig. 3, differences between
acoustic environments were more apparent under vocoded
speech, suggesting that room acoustics may have a greater in-
fluence on listening effort when speech cues are limited.
Despite these differences across conditions, PPD generally
decreased across successive sentences. This overall reduction in
PPD suggests that listeners became more efficient at processing
the speech over time, leading to reduced listening effort as the
acoustic context became more familiar. Importantly, because
interactions involving sentence order were not observed, this
reduction in listening effort appeared to occur broadly across
groups, environments, and speech types.

4. Conclusion
The present study examined how listeners with different lan-
guage immersion backgrounds adapt to room acoustics when
processing vocoded speech, considering speech intelligibility
and listening effort. To our knowledge, few studies have ex-
amined how listeners with later immersion backgrounds adapt
to room acoustics under CI processing speech in terms of intel-
ligibility, representing a key contribution of the present study.
The results showed that immersion age influenced intelligibility
under vocoded speech, indicating that listeners with later im-
mersion may experience greater difficulty when understanding
degraded speech signals. However, interactions involving im-
mersion group and sentence order were not observed, suggest-
ing that the pattern of adaptation across successive sentences
was comparable across immersion levels. Improvements in in-
telligibility across sentences were accompanied by reductions
in pupil dilation, suggesting reduced listening effort as listeners
became familiar with the acoustic environment. Importantly,
this pattern appeared similar across listeners with different lev-
els of language familiarity. In addition, the strong effect of
vocoder processing appeared to dominate over differences be-
tween acoustic environments.
A second contribution of the present study is the inclu-
sion of listening effort alongside intelligibility to examine how
listeners adapt to room acoustics under simulated CI process-
ing. Using pupillometry, changes in listening effort could be
tracked across successive sentences. The results revealed in-
teractions between speech type and immersion group, and be-
tween speech type and acoustic environment, indicating that
both language experience and room acoustics influenced listen-
ing effort. Together, these findings provide new insight into how
listeners with different language immersion backgrounds adapt
to acoustic environments under CI processing, and demonstrate
the value of combining behavioural and physiological measures
to better understand speech processing in adverse listening con-
ditions and its implications for CI listening environments.
