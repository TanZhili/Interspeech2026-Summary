Abstract
Automated depression screening is critical for alleviating medi-
cal resource shortages. While Large Language Models (LLMs)
advance risk assessment, current methods rely on costly fine-
tuning, risking overfitting. To address these challenges, we
propose Moot-Court, a training-free reasoning engine inspired
by Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO). By integrat-
ing clinical transcripts with textualized acoustic features, Moot-
Court prompts a frozen LLM to conduct a simulated court
debate, executing dialectical reasoning grounded in clinical
symptom networks. Furthermore, we introduce Codex, a dy-
namic, self-evolving experience library updated via a training-
free, reward-based feedback algorithm, explicitly constraining
the LLM’s reasoning trajectory to suppress hallucinations and
enforce accuracy. Experiments on DAIC-WOZ show Moot-
Court surpasses fine-tuned models, providing a robust, training-
efficient solution.
Index Terms: Depression detection, Multimodal learning,
Large language models, Training free, Dialectical reasoning
1. Introduction
Depressive disorders remain a major global public-health chal-
lenge. The Global Burden of Disease (GBD) 2019 study high-
lights depression as a leading cause of disability with no re-
duction in global burden since 1990 [1]. Despite this burden,
current practice still relies on questionnaire screening and clin-
ical interviews. However, questionnaire cutoffs often misalign
with structured diagnosis [2]. Moreover, detection rates in some
low- and middle-income country (LMIC) primary care settings
remain as low as 3.9%–7.0% [3]. These limitations necessi-
tate objective, scalable Automatic Depression Detection (ADD)
methods to support—not replace—clinical diagnosis.
In the realm of ADD, while early techniques like word-level
multimodal fusion and pretrained Transformers improved per-
formance on benchmarks like DAIC–WOZ [4, 5, 6, 7], they lack
clinical interpretability [8]. Furthermore, analyses reveal sus-
ceptibility to shortcut bias, creating a disconnect with medical
requirements for trustworthy explanations [9, 10].
While recent Large Language Models (LLMs) offer strong
interpretability for ADD by generating readable clinical evi-
dence in text-only settings[11, 12], these approaches inherently
discard critical non-verbal biomarkers. Conversely, extensive
ADD research demonstrates that acoustic cues and textual se-
mantics are complementary, and joint modeling is generally
more robust, directly motivating the integration of ”speech +
text” into LLM frameworks [4, 5, 6]. Although existing meth-
ods that inject speech into LLMs achieve performance gains,

they predominantly rely on parameter adaptation—such as in-
struction tuning or P-tuning—which incurs substantial training
costs [13, 14, 15]. However, such fine-tuning demands substan-
tial computational resources, making it prohibitive for standard
clinical environments. More critically, under the low-resource
constraints typical of clinical datasets, such weight-updating
paradigms are highly susceptible to severe overfitting [16, 17].
Beyond hardware and data constraints, fine-tuning inherently
suffers from ”model lock-in,” lacking the flexibility to transfer
learned patterns across different architectures.
To address this, we propose Moot-Court, a training-free
dialectical reasoning engine for ADD inspired by Training-
Free Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) [13], a cost-
effective paradigm that optimizes model behavior by distill-
ing experiential knowledge from group-based relative seman-
tic advantages without any gradient-based parameter updates.
However, unlike the original Training-Free GRPO, Moot-Court
adapts this paradigm to depression detection by framing the di-
agnostic process as a structured judicial proceeding.
Specifically, to bypass the multimodal fusion bottleneck
without the overfitting risks of fine-tuning, we verbalize acous-
tic features into textual descriptors to form a multimodal prompt
context without parameter updates. Furthermore, to resolve
clinical contradictions and provide a transparent decision path
that black-box models lack, we frame the diagnostic pro-
cess as a structured judicial proceeding over pathological net-
works, leveraging the psychopathology symptom-network [15]
to adopt a dialectical reasoning engine that explicitly quantifies
the diagnostic workflow. Finally, to overcome the static limi-
tations of traditional prompting, we implement a retrospective
critique mechanism that distills diagnostic insights into a self-
evolving experience library (Codex). By extracting high-quality
positive rules and negative traps, Codex explicitly constrains the
LLM’s reasoning trajectory, ensuring the model avoids past pit-
falls and follows verified clinical paths without weight updates.
Our main contributions are as follows:
• We introduce Moot-Court, a training-free framework for
multimodal depression detection that consistently outper-
forms fine-tuned models.
• We design a dialectical reasoning mechanism that, to the
best of our knowledge, represents the first integration of
symptom networks into an LLM-driven depression diagnos-
tic paradigm, thereby enabling a more explicitly quantifiable
and interpretable diagnostic process.
• We develop a dynamic Codex governed by a reward-based
feedback algorithm that constrains the LLM’s reasoning tra-
jectory to suppress hallucinations and enforce accuracy.

2. Methods
As illustrated in Figure 1, the proposed training-free Moot-
Court framework first integrates transcripts and acoustic
biomarkers into a diagnostic blueprint. Next, adversarial agents
perform dialectical reasoning: aProsecutorbuilds aProsecu-
torial Graph (G P )uncovering latent depressive links, while a
Defense Attorneyconstructs aDefense Graph (G D)offering
alternative explanations. Finally, a multi-agentJudgeensemble
then resolves these competing graphs, and aReflectordistills
the diagnostic insights into a self-evolvingCodex.
2.1. Multimodal Blueprinting
We propose a multimodal blueprinting mechanism that trans-
forms unaligned multimodal signals into a structured diagnos-
tic blueprint, defined asB={V L,V S,V P}. The process begins
with multimodal feature textualization, which converts raw au-
dio into a set of symbolic acoustic biomarkers(M) (detailed in
Section 3.2), whereM={m 1,m 2,...,m n}andm i represents
the symbolic acoustic features of thei-th utterance. Based on
M, theBis constructed hierarchically:
•Utterance-level Acoustic Markers (V L): To achieve seman-
tic alignment, each markerm i is appended to its correspond-
ing transcriptT i, forming the unified nodeV L,i = [Ti,m i].
•Session-level Statistical Trajectories (V S): This stage con-
structs a macro-portrait by applying a statistical operator
VS ={F slope(M),F dist(M),F inter(M)}, where temporal
trends (F slope) are derived via linear regression of acoustic
fluctuations over time; distributional metrics (F dist ) cover
the mean, standard deviation, and extrema across all turns;
and interactive proactivity (F inter) is quantified through the
interruption rate.
•Subject-level Descriptive Portraits (V P ): This stage utilizes
a large audio model to generate descriptions covering demo-
graphic estimates (e.g., gender, age) and overall prosodic fea-
tures.
Following textualization, an LLM performs knowledge-
guided node construction to extract discrete fact nodesV
grounded in Psychiatric Network Theory [15, 18], categorizing
them into three theoretical dimensions.
•External Field (V ext): Captures environmental triggers (e.g.,
job loss, interpersonal conflicts) that act as external forces
activating the symptom network.
•Internal State (V int): Identifies subjective symptom reports
(complaints) and positive states (well-being), distinguishing
core psychopathology from transient moods.
•Evidence Marker (V evid): Maps theMandV S into objec-
tive clinical evidence.
Formally, each extracted node is defined as a tuple
v={ID,Type,Label,Quote,Bio tags}, whereIDis
the unique identifier;Typedenotes the hierarchical category;
Labelrepresents the clinical descriptive tag;Quotecontains
the raw input evidence; andBio tagsanchors the correspond-
ing acoustic features.
2.2. Dialectical Reasoning via Adversarial Graphs
Upon extracting the fact node setV, the framework trans-
forms discrete nodes into two opposing graphs:Pathogenic
Graph (G P )and theContextual Graph (G D). This process
is driven by Prosecutor and Defense Attorney.
The Prosecutorexecutes a pathological edge scanning pro-
tocol to identify self-sustaining symptom networks. Instead of
treating symptoms in isolation, it employs the edge set defined
in Table 1 to reconstruct the pathological mechanism. Specif-
ically, it anchors bio-behavioral markers to clinical symptoms
(eind), then traces feedback loops (e refin) and cognitive distor-
tions (egen) to detect if the network has entered a hysteresis state.
Crucially, it identifies clinical paradoxes—such as ”Smiling De-
pression”—by detecting conflicting polarities between acoustic
features and textual reports via Contradiction Edges (econg).
The Defense Attorneyacts as a safety mechanism, per-

Leveraging the edge definitions in Table 1, it enforces a diag-
nostic hierarchy that prioritizes a ”Mania Veto” (eexcl) to strictly
rule out bipolar disorder history. Concurrently, it seeks to con-
textualize symptoms by mapping them to external life stressors
(ectx) or physiological alibis (eexp) , thereby distinguishing reac-
tive sadness from endogenous pathology. Additionally, it inte-
grates resilience factors (einhib) to account for the patient’s pos-
itive coping mechanisms.

2.3. Multi-agent Verdict and Retrospective Critique
In the decision-making phase, the system implements a mecha-
nism of parallel adjudication and collective reflection. For each
clinical case, the system initializeskindependent Judges, each
assigned a distinct sampling temperature to establish a logical
gradient in reasoning.
Prior to inference, high-quality experience is dynamically
retrieved from the Codex based onS(detailed in Section 2.4).
To prevent prompt overflow, each Judge conducts a secondary
screening for high task-relevance. Crucially, they retain the au-
thority to veto all retrieved priors for straightforward samples
to maintain inferential independence. The final selected experi-
ences are injected into the prompt as semantic priors.
Upon the conclusion of parallel adjudication, the system
categorizes the outcomes into three game-theoretic scenarios
based on the distribution of correctness across thekpaths, trig-
gering corresponding reflective logic.
•Scenario I: Consensus Correctness:If all judges achieve a
correct consensus, the sample is classified as simple enough
for the model’s inherent capabilities, bypassing reflection to
preserve the purity of the Codex.
•Scenario II: Systemic Failure:If all judges produce erro-
neous judgments, a calibration protocol triggers the Reflector
to audit shared biases across failed paths and generate defen-
sive negative traps, which then undergo an empirical valida-
tion loop.
•Scenario III: Divergent Judgments:When both correct and
incorrect paths coexist, the Reflector performs contrastive re-
flection. By identifying the divergence point in evidentiary
weighting between the “Winner” and the “Loser”, the Re-
flector distills positive experiences and negative traps.
2.4. Dynamic Codex Evolution
In this phase, reflection results are transformed into structured
experience nodesϵ, and their validity is maintained through a
statistical feedback algorithm. The Codex consists of a positive
rule setGand a negative trap setN. Each node is defined as
ϵ={c,α,f,u}, wherecis the protocol content,αis the au-
thority level, andfandurecord the frequency of use and the
number of successes, respectively. The system drives retrieval
and evolution via a reward functionS:
S=α×β×W+δ new
whereW=u/findicates the experience’s diagnostic success
rate across all historical inferences,β= 0.4serves as a scal-
ing factor balancing the historical success weight, andδ new is
the newbie bonus for samples withf <5, ensuring that new
knowledge is fully sampled.
Specifically, given the existing Codex, the LLM acts as a
clinical protocol auditor to perform the following evolutionary
operations:
•MODIFY: When a candidate experience provides more pre-
cise trigger boundaries, update the existing node and trigger
an evolutionary reward:α new = min(αold + 0.2,3.0).
•MERGE: When the semantic similarity exceeds the limit,
nodes are merged, inheriting the statistical weights PfandPu, and given an authority bonus of∆α= 0.3.
•ADD: Assign an ID (GidxorNidx) to a new clinical expe-
rience, initializingα= 1and the newbie bonusδ new = 0.5.
Following each task, the system refines the Codex through
semantic deduplication andS-driven elimination for high-
fidelity knowledge retention.
3. Experimental Setups And Results
3.1. Dataset and Preprocessing
Experiments are conducted on the DAIC-WOZ dataset [19],
a multimodal corpus comprising semi-clinical interviews con-
ducted by a virtual agent to assess psychological distress, fol-
lowing the official split (107 train, 35 val, 47 test) protocol to
ensure reproducibility. Given the presence of significant back-
ground noise and environmental interference in the raw audio
recordings, we employ ZipEnhancer [20] for denoising.
3.2. Implementation Details
The Moot-Court is implemented in a strictly training-free mode
with frozen LLM backbones, specifically DeepSeek V3.2 [21]
and Qwen3 series [22]. All agents within a single trial utilize
the same model version to ensure logical consistency. For a
raw audio, we extract acoustic biomarkersMusing Librosa for
interaction latency, Words Per Second (WPS), fundamental fre-
quency (F0), and Root Mean Square (RMS) energy, and parsel-
mouth for jitter and shimmer. To accommodate the input length
constraints of the audio language model, descriptive portraits
are generated by partitioning audio into 3-minute blocks. We
apply Qwen-Audio [23] for segment-level attribute extraction
and Qwen-max [22] for clinical narrative extraction. During
inference, the Judge ensemble consists ofk= 5independent
agents, adopting a logical gradient with sampling temperatures
ranging from 0.5 to 0.8. For performance evaluation, we mea-
sure Precision (Pre.), Recall (Rec.), and F1-score (F1).

4. Conclusion
We propose Moot-Court, a training-free reasoning engine that
frames multimodal depression detection as a structured judicial
proceeding. By bridging pathological symptom networks with
adversarial protocols, it resolves clinical contradictions without
weight updates. Evaluated on DAIC-WOZ, Moot-Court out-
performs fine-tuned models with higher interpretability. Moot-
Court thus establishes a scalable, mechanism-oriented paradigm
for multimodal depression detection.
