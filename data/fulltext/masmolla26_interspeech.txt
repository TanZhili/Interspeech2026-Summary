Improving streaming ASR with foundation models using emission policies

Abstract
Speech Foundation Models (SFMs) have achieved state-of-
the-art results in offline ASR, yet their application in streaming
environments remains limited due to the performance degra-
dation caused by restricted context. In this work, we propose
a model-agnostic, training-free streaming pipeline designed to
wrap around ASR models in a “black box” manner. Our ap-
proach integrates three key components: a sliding window au-
dio buffer for incremental data ingestion, token-level timestamp
management to eliminate redundant output, and text-only emis-
sion policies to govern output timing. We systematically eval-
uate several emission policies, assessing their impact on the
latency-accuracy trade-off across three English datasets. Re-
sults show that our pipeline enables timestamp-producing SFMs
like Parakeet and Canary to achieve near-offline transcription
quality in real-time environments without requiring access to
internal model tensors.
Index Terms: streaming decoding, speech foundation models
1. Introduction
In recent years, Foundation Models (FM) are being increasingly
applied in a great variety of machine learing applications [1] due
to their remarkable abilities for generalization and their ability
to perform well on tasks they where not trained to do so [2]. In
the field of Automatic Speech Recognition (ASR), Speech FMs
(SFM) earned the spotlight due to their multilingual capabilities
and good performance across different tasks and contexts [3].
However, most of these SFMs are designed to work in offline
conditions, and their performance degrades when they are ap-
plied to streaming ASR [4].
A straightforward approach to apply SFMs in streaming
ASR consists in performing chunked decoding by sequentially
splitting the input stream into fixed-size chunks to be pro-
cessed [5, 6, 7]. Although this approach enables SFMs to be
used for streaming ASR, transcription quality degrades with re-
spect to offline performance [8, 9]. This gap in performance
prevents full exploitation of high-throughput SFM architectures
for streaming ASR applications.
In this work, we first investigate the degradation in per-
formance of NVIDIA’s Parakeet-tdt-0.6b-v3 and Canary-1b-v2
SFMs when they are applied under streaming conditions. Then,
we propose to apply different emission policies such as Wait-
K [10], Hold-N [11] and LocalAgreement [11] investigating its
impact on the latency-accuracy trade-off. In contrast to simi-
lar work which has been focused on leveraging internal features
of SFMs to guide decoding [12, 13], our proposal is focused
on leveraging timestamps and applying text-only emission poli-
cies, which allows its application to other timestamp-producing
SFMs without requiring model-aware implementations. Exper-

2. Streaming ASR with emission policies
Streaming ASR using End-to-End models has been directly ad-
dressed by applying chunk decoding in a way that the speech
signal is sequentially processed based on fixed-length chunks.
These chunks are enlarged by adding some past and future audio
information in order to better represent the global context. Al-
though a straightforward approach, chunk decoding has proven
to be effective [14] and different techniques such as Continuous
Integrate-and-Fire (CIF) [15], Monotonic Chunkwise Attention
(MoChA) [16] or AlignAtt [17] have been proposed in order to
improve performance. All these techniques make use of specific
information in the model to guide the decoding process, thus re-
quiring internal access and model dependent implementations.
As an alternative, we propose a model-agnostic chunk-
decoding approach based on emission policies which can be
generally applied with independence of the ASR model as long
as it is able to produce timestamps. As shown in Figure 1,
it consists of three main modules: a sliding audio buffer that
handles incremental data ingestion; a repetition control mod-
ule that, based on token-level timestamps, filters redundant to-
kens; and an emission policy module which decides the token
emission timing. This approach allows to integrate ASR mod-
els in a “black box” fashion, resulting in a pipeline that can
be integrated with any architecture capable of producing time-
aligned transcriptions. The inference algorithm of the proposed
approach is described in Algorithm 1.

2.1. Sliding audio buffer
Conventionally, incremental data ingestion in streaming ASR
has been mostly managed frame-by-frame or chunk-by-chunk.
The frame-by-frame approach, mostly adopted by HMM-based
systems [18], processes the incoming data frame-wise. This
approach, while achieving the lowest theoretical latency possi-
ble, entails performing one forward step per frame, generally re-
sulting in computationally expensive decoding processes when
working with large models. On the other side, chunk-by-chunk
processing has become the standard approach for End-to-End
models. It generally results in a less expensive decoding pro-
cess, as it reduces the number of forward steps, although it intro-
duces an inherent latency proportional to the size of the chunks.
However, since one of the goals of our pipeline is to provide the
model with more acoustic context than traditional chunk-based
decoding, we adopted a sliding window approach.
The pipeline starts with an audio buffer that receives fixed-
length chunks of sizeL c. During the initial part of the audio
stream, the buffer grows cumulatively until it reaches a maxi-
mum window lengthL max. From this point on, when a new
chunk is added, it pushes the oldest chunk out of the buffer to
maintain a constant window size, making the audio buffer tran-
sition into a fixed-length sliding window mechanism. Formally,
the acoustic inputX t defined by the sliding window at a decod-
ing steptcan be expressed asX t =X[t·L c−L max,t·L c].
This approach ensures that the model has access to the most re-
cent audio information while maintaining a sufficient amount
of past information. Note that, as with other chunk-based ap-
proaches, the decoder is usually lighter than the encoder. There-
fore, transribing the entire window at each step does not add any
significant computational overhead.
2.2. Timestamp-based repetition control
Following the pipeline representation in Figure 1, the acoustic
information in the sliding window mechanism is then fed to the
ASR model to be processed. As the maximum size of the slid-
ing windowL max is set to be larger than the chunk sizeL c,
the model processes overlapping acoustic information in every
decoding step, leading to redundant tokens in the output across
decoding steps. To resolve this redundancy, we leverage token-
level timestamps generated by the model. At every decoding
step, the system stores a state variableT last representing the
timestamp of the last committed token. With this information,
for each new inference result, the system discards all the tokens
with timestamps referring to instants previous toT last. This
mechanism ensures a monotonic progression of the transcrip-
tion while also preventing the emission of duplicate text under
overlapping window settings.
2.3. Token emission control with emission policies
Once the transcription has been filtered, it is then added to an
output buffer that contains all candidate tokens for emission.
At this point, the generated partial transcription is ready to be
consolidated as output text. However, since the pipeline pro-
cesses the acoustic stream in an overlapping fashion, it allows
the model to re-generate predictions for a given acoustic region
as new acoustic data is added to the window and more context
can be provided. This feature allows the system to make correc-
tions to the output text once it has been already generated by the
model, although it produces a “flickering” effect at the output of
the system [19]. Due to this effect, the user perceives changes
over the last tokens of the transcriptions as the system performs
these corrections. In order to prevent flickering, we apply an
emission policy to the output buffer.
In the context of streaming ASR (and Speech Translation),
emission policies implement the decision-making logic used to
control emission timing of tokens as new audio arrives. Es-
sentially, during the decoding process, an emission policy de-
cides when a hypothesized token should be emitted, and thus
manages the trade-off between latency and accuracy. Without
a robust policy, a model might prematurely emit a word based
on incomplete acoustic context (leading to the aforementioned
flickering) or wait too long for future context, thereby breaking
the purpose of streaming decoding [20].
In this work, we specifically propose to use emission poli-
cies that are able to work solely by having access to the output
text of the ASR model. Since we are only treating the models as
black boxes, we do not have access to specific internal tensors
required to apply other policies. In this way, we consider two
types of emission policies: static and dynamic. Static emission
policies work as a fixed “look-ahead” buffer within the output
stream. For this type of policy, we propose to use Wait-K [10]
and Hold-N [11]. On the other side, dynamic policies act as a
validation gate between consecutive decoding steps. As a dy-
namic policy, we propose to use LocalAgreement [11].
2.3.1. Wait-K
Wait-Kis a simple yet effective fixed-delay policy originally
popularized in simultaneous machine translation. In the context
of streaming ASR, the model is required to “wait” forKaddi-
tional input chunks, frames or seconds. Subsequent emissions
follow a deterministic schedule where each token is conditioned
on a fixed amount of look-ahead. While simple to implement,
the primary drawback of Wait-Kis its lack of flexibility, as it
applies a uniform delay regardless of the phonetic complexity
or the model’s confidence in a specific segment of audio.
2.3.2. Hold-N
The Hold-Npolicy (often referred to as a “stability” buffer)
works by delaying the emission of the lastNtokens in a hypoth-
esis. As the model processes new audio, it may update the most
recent predictions. By “holding” the lastNtokens, the system
ensures that it only commits to text that has been “pushed out”
of the buffer by subsequent tokens. Similar to Wait-K, this pol-
icy introduces a constant latency overhead proportional toN.

2.3.3. LocalAgreement
LocalAgreement (LA) is a dynamic emission policy designed to
maximize stability without the rigid latency penalties of fixed-
delay methods. It operates by comparing hypotheses generated
at consecutive time steps or across overlapping acoustic win-
dows. A token is only considered “stable” if it appears in the
same position across a number of consecutive decoding steps.
Because it adapts to the model’s consensus, LA can quickly
emit high-confidence tokens while naturally waiting longer for
ambiguous segments. Following the findings of [21], it is con-
sidered that the policy achieves its best performance when only
comparing the last two hypotheses at any given time. Follow-
ing this result, we apply the LA policy by computing the longest
common prefix (LCP) to the last two hypothesesH t andH t−1.
2.3.4. LocalAgreement-Levenshtein
A notable limitation of LA is its strict requirement for exact
token-to-token identity, which can lead to unnecessary delays in
the presence of minor character variations or punctuation shifts.
To address this, a popular solution is to introduce a more relaxed
consensus mechanism based on the Levenshtein distance.
LocalAgreement-Levenshtein (LA-Lev) is a modified ver-
sion of LA that, instead of demanding a perfect match across
consecutive decodes, it computes the edit distance between two
consecutive hypothesesH t andH t−1. Then, a segment is
cleared for emission only if the edit distance falls below a pre-
defined thresholdτ. By adjusting the value ofτ, LA-Lev al-
lows for more relaxed decoding strategies, potentially sacrific-
ing some WER score on behalf of emission latency.

TED talks with professional yet spontaneous speech; and Earn-

companies and is designed to evaluate ASR robustness “in the

For the streaming baseline figures, we used existing setups that
are available in the NeMo toolkit without any additional train-
ing. These setups make use of a chunk-based decoding strategy

Rare the sizes in seconds of the left context, the chunk and the

to offline results with LA, LA-Lev and Wait-K, while Canary-
v2 is able to improve the Earnings22 and TedLium-v3 offline

4. Conclusions
We have proposed a model-agnostic streaming ASR pipeline
capable of achieving close to offline results using black box
models with timestamped output. We showed that the slid-
ing window approach combined with timestamp-guided repe-
tition control allows to minimize WER on small chunk setups.
Furthermore, when combined with text-only emission policies,
the system is capable of sequentially processing long acoustic
samples while minimizing “flickering”. The result is a model-
agnostic pipeline able to perform streaming and long-form in-
ferences with competitive results. Promising directions for pos-
sible future work are on the one hand evaluating more SFMs,
and on the other hand studying the capabilities of multi-task
models like Canary-v2 to perform simultaneous ST.
