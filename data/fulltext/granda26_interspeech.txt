Technology for Low-Resource Languages

Abstract
Over 7,000 languages are spoken worldwide, yet speech tech-
nology focuses on high-resource ones, leaving many underrep-
resented. Current strategies prioritize scaling data and compute,
often overlooking linguistic structure. Motivated by evidence
that related languages share phonetic features, we examine
whether Self-Supervised Learning (SSL) models can leverage
genealogical relationships to improve transfer to low-resource
languages. Using WavLM-Large, we compare random multi-
lingual training with language-family-aware subsets. Family-
aware training outperforms random selection on downstream
tasks despite similar pretraining convergence. However, both
remain below the original WavLM-Large baseline, indicating
that linguistic structure enhances robustness under data con-
straints but complements rather than replaces large-scale pre-
training. Incorporating linguistic organization into SSL offers a
principled path toward more inclusive speech technology.
Index Terms: language family, low-resource languages, SSL
1. Introduction
Languages are not static systems: they evolve, diverge, and
influence one another across generations and geography. His-
torical linguistics has long established that languages change
through regular sound and branching descent from common an-
cestors [1]. Related languages within each family often retain
systematic correspondences in phonology, morphology, and
broader typological features due to shared inheritance [2]. Us-
ing data from the World Atlas of Language Structures (W ALS)
[3], a study demonstrated that certain features of languages,
such as lexicon and word order, remain highly stable within
families, while others fluctuate due to geographic contact rather
than heritage [4].
Although the world exhibits rich linguistic diversity, the
vast majority of the approximately 7,000 languages spoken to-
day remain severely underrepresented in speech technology [5].
Most are low-resource languages, which by definition [6] are
“less studied, resource scarce, less computerized, less privi-
leged, less commonly taught or low density”, placing them at
risk of digital marginalization. In speech technology, the study
of cross-linguistic structure is fundamentally constrained by the
scarcity and cost of annotated speech data. High quality tran-
scriptions require expert knowledge, especially in low-resource
languages, and are time-consuming. This imbalance has histori-
cally limited supervised approaches, as demonstrated by Zhong
et. al [7], Ranathunga et. al [8], and David et. al [9] in their
different studies related to Low-Resource languages represen-
tation in speech technology. The emergence of Self-Supervised

Learning (SSL) has significantly altered this landscape.
The introduction of Wav2Vec 2.0 [10] marked a turn-
ing point in speech representation learning, demonstrating that
contrastive predictive objectives on raw speech could achieve
state-of-the-art performance across downstream tasks. This
paradigm spurred subsequent work scaling wav2vec archi-
tectures to larger models and massively multilingual settings
[11, 12, 13, 14], alongside the emergence of alternative SSL
architectures such as HuBERT [15] and Data2Vec [16]. These
models, however, share a common characteristic in how training
data is handled: they rely on continuous audio streams without
an explicit strategy for sampling or organizing data based on lin-
guistic structure. Consequently, multilingual diversity is treated
primarily as a scaling variable rather than as a structured source
of inductive bias, with the data pipeline focusing on format and
scale over linguistic considerations.
Rather than exposing a SSL model to an arbitrary mul-
tilingual mixture, we constructed training sets composed of
multiple families, selecting two to three languages per family
when available. Our central hypothesis is that this structured
exposure encourages the model to learn family-level phonetic
regularities that generalize beyond the languages seen during
training. To evaluate this, we pretrained WavLM-Large model
[17] on 35 languages spanning diverse families and compare
family-aware sampling against random multilingual selection.
The resulting models, submitted as embedding extractors un-
der the Interspeech Unsupervised People’s Speech in the Wild
challenge, are evaluated on unseen languages across down-
stream tasks including Automatic Speech Recognition (ASR),
language identification (LID), and speaker clustering. Our find-
ings show that linguistic structure provides a meaningful induc-
tive bias, with family-aware pretraining outperforming embed-
dings learned through unstructured multilingual scaling.
2. Methodology
2.1. Data and Preprocessing
The Unsupervised People’s Speech in the Wild challenge, fea-
tured at Interspeech 2026, provides the dataset for our exper-
iments. The Unsupervised People’s Speech dataset [18] con-
tains over 1M hours of audio. To ensure data quality, the au-
thors performed two preprocessing analyses: V oice Activity
Detection (V AD) using Silero’s V AD pipeline, which identified
820k+ hours of actual speech, and Language Identification us-
ing Nvidia’s TensorRT-LLM implementation of Whisper Large
v3, which detected 89 languages across the corpus. More de-
tails about the statistics of the data can be found in the dataset
card from Hugging Face.

The core of our approach relies on accurately mapping the 89
languages found in the dataset to their respective families. This
mapping shown in Appendix A, Table 3, was constructed using
established linguistic resources such as Glottolog [19] and Eth-
nologue [20]. The distribution of audio segments in the full
dataset is highly imbalanced (Figure 2), with Indo-European
accounting for approximately88%of all available speech seg-

formed at the language level, not at the audio level. Our ob-

audio imbalance of the corpus. Therefore, larger families were
represented by a greater number of languages, while smaller
families were included in their entirety when only one language
was available. When possible, we ensured a minimum of two
languages per family to allow for within-family variability dur-
ing training. The final training dataset consisted of 32 languages
across multiple families, comprising≈56hours of audio. This
dataset will be referred to as “language-aware” from now on.
For the ”no-language-aware” evaluation, we constructed a
training set by randomly sampling audio files from the UPS
dataset without any language or family-based filtering. To en-
sure comparability, this subset was matched in total duration to
the language-aware configuration.
2.3. Models
In this work, we use WavLM-Large, a 316M-parameter
transformer-based self-supervised model with relative posi-
tional attention, trained through masked speech prediction and
denoising objectives [17]. Rather than using it off-the-shelf, we
perform continued pre-training on UPS data with a contrastive
objective and Gumbel-Softmax vector quantization. The convo-
lutional feature encoder is frozen to preserve well-learned low-
level acoustic features, while the transformer encoder and pro-
jection heads are updated. WavLM’s strong performance across
diverse downstream tasks on the Speech processing Universal
PERformance Benchmark (SUPERB) [21] indicates that its rep-
resentations capture transferable acoustic information, making
it an appropriate backbone for the UPS challenge.
2.4. Experiments
Two experiments were conducted under identical training pa-
rameters, using the no-language-aware and language-aware
subsets. For both setups, fixed-duration chunks were randomly
extracted from each audio file, using approximately 60 hours of
audio in total. Chunks of a fixed duration were extracted inde-
pendently from each audio file. The number of chunks extracted
per file scaled proportionally with its duration, such that longer
recordings contributed more chunks in absolute terms. How-
ever, a minimum of 8 chunks per file was enforced regardless of
3395
duration, meaning that shorter files were sampled more densely
relative to their actual length. Consequently, while sampling
was uniform within each file, it was not strictly uniform across
the corpus in terms of audio-duration contribution, as shorter
recordings were relatively overrepresented on a per-second ba-
sis.
Audio was resampled to 16kHz, converted to mono, nor-
malized using the feature extractor of the WavLM-Large model,
and trimmed or zero-padded to a fixed length. During training,
on-the-fly waveform-level augmentations were applied exclu-
sively to the training subset, consisting of stochastic speed per-
turbation and additive noise. A detailed description of these
augmentations is provided in Section 2.5. These techniques en-
courage robustness to natural variations in speaking rate and
background acoustic conditions [22], a design principle that
mirrors the pretraining objective of WavLM itself.
For the validation set, we used a dedicated held-out dataset
constructed under the same preprocessing pipeline, with the ex-
ception that no data augmentation was applied during training.
This split contained 27 files of 10 different languages not seen
during pretraining, selecting, when possible, one unseen lan-
guage per family. Distribution of this dataset is presented in
Appendix A, Table 4.
2.5. Experimental Setup
The WavLM Large model was fine-tuned using a self-
supervised contrastive pretraining objective on a multilingual
speech corpus. The training set yielded≈22,000chunks,
while the validation set consisted of≈7,000chunks. Each
chunk was fixed at 10 seconds, randomly extracted from its
source file by sampling a start position uniformly at random
within the valid range, with a minimum of 8 chunks extracted
per file. Training was conducted for 8 epochs with a per-device
batch size of 2 and gradient accumulation over 8 steps, result-
ing in an effective batch size of 16. The Adam W optimizer was
used withβ 1 = 0.9,β 2 = 0.98,ϵ= 1e−6, and weight de-
cay of0.01. A cosine learning rate schedule with 500 warmup
steps was applied, with an initial learning rate of 5e-5. Gra-
dient norms were clipped at 10.0. Mixed precision training in
bfloat16 was used throughout. The CNN feature encoder of
WavLM was kept frozen during training to preserve low-level
acoustic representations, while the transformer layers and pre-
training heads were updated. A time masking probability of
0.65was applied following the default WavLM pretraining con-
figuration. The Gumbel softmax temperature was decayed ex-
ponentially from2.0to0.1with a decay factor of0.99per step.
Two data augmentation strategies were applied exclusively to
training chunks: speed perturbation with a factor drawn uni-
formly from[0.9,1.1](applied with50%probability), and ad-
ditive white Gaussian noise at a signal-to-noise ratio drawn uni-
formly from[15,30]dB (applied with30%probability). No
augmentation was applied to the validation set. All experiments
were run on a CUDA-enabled GPU using Google Colab.

4. Discussion
Our results suggest that, under constrained data conditions, in-
telligently curated data can outperform indiscriminately scaled
data. Similar findings have been reported in underwater
acoustics [23], speech enhancement [24], and other domains
[25], where curated subsets surpassed larger randomly sam-
pled datasets of identical or greater size. This pattern aligns
with how SSL speech models learn, as representation quality
depends critically on the composition of negative pairs encoun-
tered during contrastive training [26, 27]. Random multilingual
sampling likely produces either trivially easy or false-negative
pairs [28], limiting discriminative pressure. This mechanism
offers a plausible explanation for the large performance gap ob-
served in the LID task.
However, the improvements were not limited to LID.
The language-aware configuration achieved substantially lower
3396
CER in ASR and higher ARI in speaker clustering. The CER
gains suggest that cleaner input distributions facilitate more sta-
ble acoustic representations, consistent with prior findings that
curated or noise-controlled data improves SSL robustness [24].
The ARI improvement may reflect two complementary effects:
(1) reduced acoustic noise and (2) increased structured diver-
sity across language families, which encourages the model to
learn more separable speaker representations. Since SSL objec-
tives depend on meaningful negative sampling [26, 27], struc-
tured multilingual diversity likely enhances clustering-friendly
embeddings compared to random heterogeneous sampling.
The superior performance of the language-aware configu-
ration can therefore be attributed to its principled structure. By
selecting languages across different families, the training cor-
pus imposes a hierarchical organization over the acoustic space.
This structured coverage resembles curriculum learning (CL),
where organizing training data according to meaningful rela-
tionships yields more stable and transferable representations
[29, 30]. In SSL speech models, curriculum-based strategies
have improved ASR [31], speaker verification [32], and end-
to-end speech systems [33, 34, 35]. Our results suggest that
language-family organization could function as a form of im-
plicit curriculum over linguistic structure.
While these findings are promising, they must be inter-
preted within the constraints of our experimental design. We
acknowledge that our experiments were conducted with a sin-
gle and small subset of audio data. Therefore, a more compre-
hensive study involving varying model sizes and data scales is
necessary to fully validate the generalization of this data-centric
approach.
Finally, while genealogical structure provides a powerful
organizing principle [2], language evolution is not purely tree-
like. Thomason (2001) [36] established that geographic prox-
imity and language contact drive structural convergence across
unrelated languages. T ¨or¨o et al. (2025) [37] demonstrate
that embedding distances correlate with both genetic and ge-
ographical distance, with minority languages often clustering
with geographically dominant ones rather than genetic rela-
tives. Together, these findings suggest that SSL models encode
both deep genealogical relationships and more recent contact-
induced convergence. Our work asks a complementary ques-
tion: given a fixed, imbalanced dataset, can genealogical knowl-
edge guide training strategies to better utilize existing data? Our
results suggest yes. Organizing training by language family
does not replace computation with human intuition; rather, it
reorients the model’s attention toward shared phonetic patterns,
allowing low-resource languages to benefit from related higher-
resource ones. The consistent improvements across LID, CER,
and ARI indicate that input structure and not merely data vol-
ume, has measurable consequences for what a model learns.
5. Conclusions
This work demonstrates that thoughtful data curation can be a
powerful tool for SSL model pretraining, even at modest scales.
Although 60 hours of audio is far below the data regimes typi-
cally associated with SSL training, our results reveal that struc-
turing the training corpus along linguistic family boundaries
yields remarkably capable representations on languages never
seen during pretraining. This suggests that the quality and or-
ganization of input data can partially compensate for its quan-
tity, a finding with meaningful implications for the preservation
and democratization of speech technology across the world’s
languages. Language remain as the primary medium through
which humans share knowledge and ideas, and making robust
speech systems accessible beyond high-resource languages re-
mains a critical open challenge.
It is also worth noting that the WavLM Large baseline,
without any continued pretraining, already achieved strong per-
formance across downstream tasks, underscoring the general-
ization capacity of large pretrained models even in zero-shot
cross-lingual settings. The fact that family-aware continued
pretraining improved over this already strong baseline suggests
that the ceiling for this approach has not yet been reached and
that larger, more carefully curated corpora could yield substan-
tially stronger representations.
We do not claim these results as universal law. Indeed,
recent studies have shown that languages often cluster more
cleanly by geographic proximity and areal features than by strict
genealogical descent [37]. This suggests that linguistic related-
ness is multidimensional, and our family-based groupings may
capture only one axis of similarity. Future work will expand
this investigation by training using family-based batches com-
posed of different languages and by combining language family
structure with geographic proximity to further understand how
structural and spatial factors jointly shape learned representa-
