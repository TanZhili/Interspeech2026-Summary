Abstract
Self-supervised learning (SSL) has achieved remarkable
progress in speech representation learning, yet most models are
pretrained on comparatively curated corpora with limited acous-
tic variability. We introduce theUnsupervised Speech in the
Wild (UPS) 2026 Challenge, a benchmark designed to eval-
uate representation learning under large-scale, heterogeneous
web audio conditions. Participants are restricted to pre-train
from scratch or continue pretraining from a checkpoint using
Unsupervised People’s Speech (UPS) dataset, comprising over
800,000 hours of publicly licensed web audio, with approxi-
mately 522,000 hours of detected speech, and are evaluated on
three complementary tasks: language identification, few-shot
automatic speech recognition, and speaker clustering.
Index Terms: multilingual, unsupervised, representation learn-
ing, speech recognition, language identification, speaker clus-
tering
1. Introduction
SSL has reshaped speech representation learning. Models such
as Wav2Vec 2.0 [1], Hubert [2], WavLM [3] or XLS-R [4]
have shown that large-scale model pretraining on audio, with no
transcripts, can produce expressive representations that transfer
effectively across downstream tasks such as automatic speech
recognition (ASR) or language identification (LID).
In order to understand the capabilities of SSL, a substan-
tial body of work has focused on cross-task evaluation. Bench-
marks such as LeBenchmark [5] established multi-task settings
that include common tasks such as ASR, while also incorporat-
ing distinct evaluation tasks such as emotion recognition, spo-
ken language understanding, and speech translation. Similarly,
SUPERB-SG [6] extended the original SUPERB [7] framework
by broadening task coverage by including voice conversion, out
of domain ASR, speech separation, and speech enhancement,
among others. These efforts emphasize evaluating the gener-
alization capabilities of learned representations across diverse
speech objectives.
In addition to task expansion, another line of work has em-
phasized scaling SSL evaluation across languages. Datasets
and challenges such as Xtreme-S [8], Fleurs [9], ML-SUPERB
[10], and ML-SUPERB 2.0 [11] have significantly broadened
multilingual coverage, enabling evaluation across hundreds of
languages. These projects prioritize cross-lingual generaliza-
tion, assessing how well learned representations transfer be-
tween languages with varying levels of resource availability.
While prior work has expanded evaluation across tasks and
languages, comparatively less attention has been paid to the

heterogeneity of the training signals. Many SSL models are
pretrained on relatively curated corpora, such as LibriSpeech
[12] and CommonV oice [13], consisting primarily of structured
speech recordings. In contrast, naturally occuring web speech
exhibits spontaneous dialogue, background music, environmen-
tal noise, and long-tailed linguistic variation. Understanding
how SSL methods perform on these data categories remains an
open question.
In order to address these limitations, we present the Un-
supervised Speech in the Wild Challenge, where the goal is to
improve self-supervised speech representation learning in mul-
tilingual settings while being restricted on the training dataset
to using only UPS. The challenge evaluates few-shot ASR and
LID for 73 languages, as well as speaker clustering for 70 lan-
guages with 398 different speakers, with at least 2 speakers
each.
This paper is organized as follows:
• Section 2 describes the UPS dataset as well as the evaluation
datasets used for LID, ASR, and speaker clustering.
• Section 3 outlines the challenge design rules, evaluation pro-
tocol and infrastructure, including task definitions, probe se-
tups, and reproducibility constraints.
• Section 4 presents baseline systems based on well-known
self-supervised encoders and reports results across the three
tasks, followed by an analysis of submitted systems and
leaderboard outcomes.
Our contributions can be summarized as follows:
1. We introduce the Unsupervised Speech in the Wild Chal-
lenge, a benchmark that evaluates speech representations
trained on large-scale, heterogeneous unlabeled web audio,
emphasizing robustness to real-world acoustic variability.
2. We propose a unified evaluation protocol spanning zero-
shot language identification, few-shot ASR via CTC probes,
and speaker clustering, assessing phonetic, linguistic, and
speaker-level information captured by learned representa-
tions.
3. We provide a reproducible benchmarking framework de-
ployed using Dynabench, with controlled execution enabling
fair comparison across systems.

2.2. Evaluation Datasets
The challenge evaluation relies on two derived datasets con-
structed from publicly available corpora. These datasets are
distinct from UPS and are used exclusively for benchmarking
generalization under labeled conditions.
2.2.1. FLEURS Subset for Language Identification and ASR.
The first evaluation set is derived from the FLEURS dataset
[9], a multilingual speech corpus covering a wide range of lan-
guages with transcriptions and speaker metadata, chosen as it
supported both language identification and few-shot ASR tasks.
For the challenge, we constructed a balanced subset spanning
73 languages, all of which are present in UPS. For each selected
language, a fixed number of utterances were sampled to create
train and test splits, resulting in 14,600 training utterances (200
per language) and 7,246 test utterances (100 per language). Au-
dio files were stored alongside a metadata.jsonl file containing
language identifiers, transcription fields, speaker gender (when
available), and additional metadata required for grouping and
evaluation. This controlled sampling strategy ensures consistent
per-language coverage while preserving the original linguistic
diversity of FLEURS.
2.2.2. VoxTube-Derived Subset for Speaker Clustering.
The second evaluation set targets speaker discrimination and is
derived from V oxTube [16]. From this corpus, we constructed
a subset covering 70 languages, 398 speakers, and 3,980 speech
segments (approximately 10 segments per speaker). For each
segment, a metadata entry specifies the language label and a
unique speaker identifier. Languages were filtered to ensure at
least two speakers per language, enabling meaningful cluster-
ing evaluation. The resulting dataset provides balanced mul-
tilingual coverage while maintaining sufficient intra-language
speaker diversity.
Together, these two evaluation datasets allow us to mea-
sure linguistic and speaker-level generalization of representa-
tions trained on UPS, while maintaining transparent and repro-
ducible data construction procedures.

3.1. Principles
Three core principles guided the challenge design:
Multi-Task Evaluation.Representations are evaluated
across three complementary tasks: multilingual language iden-
tification, few-shot automatic speech recognition, and speaker
clustering. This multi-task setup discourages overfitting to a
single objective and promotes robustness across linguistic and
speaker-level dimensions.
Reproducible Benchmarking.All probes, training proce-
dures, and evaluation scripts are fixed and executed within the
official benchmark infrastructure. Encoders are frozen during
downstream evaluation, and standardized seeds and hyperpa-
rameters are used to ensure reproducibility and fair comparison
across systems.
Unified Representation Interface.Each submission pro-
vides a single inference function that maps waveform audio to
frame-level embeddings. These embeddings are reused across
all downstream tasks, encouraging the development of general-
purpose speech representations.
3.2. Evaluation Protocol Overview
3.2.1. Task 1: Multilingual Language Identification.
Language identification evaluates the extent to which utterance-
level embeddings encode language-discriminative information.
For each utterance in the 73-language FLEURS subset, frame-
level embeddings produced by the encoder are temporally ag-
gregated into a single utterance representation. A linear clas-
sifier is trained on the training split and evaluated on the held-
out test split. Performance is measured using macro-averaged
F1 across all languages, ensuring equal contribution from each
language regardless of dataset size.
3.2.2. Task 2: Few-Shot Automatic Speech Recognition.
ASR evaluation measures how well representations encode pho-
netic and linguistic structure transferable to character-level tran-
scription. For each of the 73 languages in the FLEURS subset,
a lightweight linear projection head is trained with CTC loss on
limited labeled data while the encoder remains frozen. Char-
acter vocabularies are constructed independently per language
from training transcriptions. Predictions are greedily decoded,
and performance is reported as macro-averaged Character Er-
ror Rate (CER) across languages. This per-language setup pre-
vents cross-language leakage and ensures that improvements
stem from representation quality rather than multilingual super-
vision.

3.2.3. Task 3: Speaker Clustering.
Speaker clustering evaluates whether representations capture
speaker-discriminative characteristics independent of linguistic
content. Using the V oxTube subset, each speech segment is
mapped to a single embedding via temporal aggregation. Clus-
tering is performed independently per language using the ora-
cle number of speakers, and quality is measured using Adjusted
Rand Index (ARI). Final ARI is macro-averaged across eligible
languages to avoid bias toward languages with more speakers.
3.2.4. Aggregation and Leaderboard Reporting.
Each task produces a macro-averaged score across languages.
In addition to reporting per-task metrics individually, we com-
pute an overall ranking used for the main leaderboard and for
selecting top-Nsystems in analysis figures.
For each scored submission (out ofNtotal), we assign a
rank from1toNon each task, after which we compute the
mean rank using and equally weighted average. Therefore, each
task contributes equally to the overall score.
All evaluations are executed through Dynabench, which
standardizes model ingestion, enforces interface compliance,
and guarantees controlled, reproducible execution across sub-
missions. It also facilitates keeping the test set fully held out
from participants, which prevents overfitting.

4.2. Per-Language Difficulty Patterns
We next analyze per-language performance by aggregating met-
rics across submissions.
4.2.1. ASR difficulty is strongly script-dependent.
Languages with non-Latin or complex writing systems are
systematically harder under character-level CTC evaluation.
Japanese is a clear outlier with substantially higher mean CER
and high variance across submissions; this reflects a mismatch
between character-level decoding and logographic scripts. Ko-
rean, Amharic, Khmer, and Thai also rank among the hardest
languages for CER.

4.3. Fairness and Dataset Diagnostics
Finally, we present diagnostic analyzes intended to contextual-
ize the results.
4.3.1. Gender imbalance in clustering data.
The speaker clustering subset exhibits substantial gender im-
balance for several languages (e.g., Vietnamese and Ukrainian
skew heavily male, while Swedish skews female). Such imbal-
ances can bias clustering outcomes and should be considered
when interpreting per-language ARI.
4.3.2. Implications.
Overall, the results indicate clear progress on representation
learning from heterogeneous web audio, but also highlight per-
sistent challenges: (i) evaluation protocols can interact strongly
with orthography (ASR), and (ii) clustering-based diagnostics
are sensitive to per-language subset composition. These find-
ings motivate future iterations of UPS challenge to further refine
evaluation robustness while preserving the in-the-wild focus.
5. Conclusion
We introduced the Unsupervised Speech in the Wild (UPS)
2026 Challenge, a benchmark designed to evaluate multilin-
gual speech representations trained exclusively on large-scale,
heterogeneous web audio. By constraining training to the
UPS dataset and standardizing downstream evaluation through
frozen encoders and controlled probes, the challenge isolates
representation quality from task-specific modeling choices.
Across 80 scored submissions from 14 teams, results
demonstrate substantial progress in multilingual self-supervised
learning under realistic data conditions. However, no single sys-
tem dominates across language identification, few-shot ASR,
and speaker clustering, confirming that these tasks probe par-
tially complementary properties of speech representations.
Overall, the UPS Challenge highlights both the promise
and the limitations of current SSL approaches when exposed
to acoustically diverse, in-the-wild speech. We hope this
benchmark encourages continued research toward more robust,
language-agnostic, and speaker-aware representations trained
under realistic web-scale conditions.
