From Words to Sentences: Contextual Predictability Overrides
Phonetic Ambiguity in Lexical Competition

Abstract
Prior work shows that for isolated word recognition, sub-
phonemic variation modulates lexical competition in a graded
manner. Whether this modulation persists when sentence con-
text is available, however, remains unknown. We conducted
two cross-modal priming experiments using acoustically ma-
nipulated English minimal pairs. Experiment 1 (isolated words)
replicated the gradient effect of phonetic ambiguity, driven by
differences in sub-phonemic variation, on lexical competition.
In Experiment 2 (sentences), however, while lexical competi-
tion was better resolved in high- than low-predictability con-
texts, this competition was no longer modulated by phonetic
ambiguity. Our findings support theoretical accounts such as
predictive coding and Bayesian inference models, where top-
down expectations reduce the contribution of bottom-up signals
to lexical competition. These results suggest that models of spo-
ken word recognition built on isolated words may not fully ex-
tend to sentence comprehension.
Index Terms: speech perception, phonetic detail, sentence pre-
dictability, bottom-up processing, top-down prediction
1. Introduction
Spoken word recognition requires listeners to resolve competi-
tion among lexical candidates as the acoustic signals unfold. A
large body of work has established that this competition is sensi-
tive to fine-grained phonetic detail: for instance, a voiced word
likegold, produced with an ambiguous voice onset time (VOT)
near the voiced–voiceless boundary, activates bothgoldand its
voiceless competitorcoldmore equally than one produced with
a more canonical VOT. This graded activation modulates lexi-
cal competition during word recognition in real time [1, 2]. This
suggests that listeners do not simply categorize speech sounds
to identify certain words; rather, sub-phonemic variation con-
tinuously shapes competition among lexical candidates.
At the same time, spoken words rarely occur in isolation.
Sentence context generates expectations that strongly constrain
which words are likely to appear, and research has demonstrated
that these expectations influence spoken word recognition [3, 4,
5]. However, a critical question remains open: when sentence
context is available, does sub-phonemic variation still modulate
the competition dynamics among lexical candidates?
The existing evidence, despite its richness, does not answer
this question directly. Studies demonstrating sub-phonemic
sensitivity have predominantly used isolated words or minimal
carrier phrases [1, 2], revealing what the systemcando with
such phonetic detail when no other information is available, but
not what itdoeswhen contextual predictions are present. Con-
versely, studies of sentence context effects on word recogni-
tion have either focused on phoneme categorization rather than
lexical competition [3, 4, 6, 7], or they investigated how con-
text modulates competitor activation by phonetic manipulation
across phoneme categories rather than parametrically varying
phonetic detail at the sub-phonemic level [5]. As a result, it
remains unknown whethersub-phonemic variationknown to
shape isolated word recognition continues to modulate lexical
competition when sentence context is available.
This question is theoretically important because most theo-
ries of spoken word recognition are established using isolated-
word paradigms, yet they make different predictions about how
top-down expectations interact with bottom-up signals during
sentence comprehension. Under interactive activation accounts,
such as the TRACE model [8], high-level expectationssharpen
lower-level representations through top-down feedback. This
enhanced encoding of expected speech sounds should preserve
or amplify sensitivity to sub-phonemic variation during lexi-
cal competition. In support of this account, some work [9]
found more faithful neural tracking of acoustic-phonetic in-
formation when words were semantically more predictable in
naturalistic speech. In contrast, predictive coding frameworks
[10, 11] posit that strong top-down predictions explain away
expected input and suppress prediction errors. This diminishes
the amount of bottom-up evidence propagated to higher-level
processing (see also [12, 13]), thereby reducing the influence of
sub-phonemic variation on lexical competition. Lastly, a third
class of Bayesian inference accounts, exemplified by Shortlist B
[14], holds that top-down expectations adjust prior probabilities
over lexical candidates without altering bottom-up processing.
Under this view, when contextual predictability is high, pho-
netic evidence carries less weight compared to the strong prior,
similarly leading to weaker effects of sub-phonemic variation
on lexical competition.
To address this question, we orthogonally manipulated con-
textual predictability and phonetic ambiguity by varying sub-
phonemic acoustic cues. In a cross-modal priming paradigm,
listeners hear an auditory prime and perform a lexical decision
on a visual probe; theidentity advantage, defined as faster re-
sponses to a visual probe matching the target word than to its
minimal-pair competitor, provides a direct index of how compe-
tition between the two lexical candidates has been resolved. We
used VOT-manipulated English minimal pairs (e.g.,gold–cold)
in two experiments. In Experiment 1, auditory primes were pre-
sented in isolation to establish that sub-phonemic VOT variation
modulates the identity advantage. In Experiment 2, the same
phonetic tokens were embedded in spoken sentences with high
or low contextual predictability. If context sharpens pre-lexical
representations, VOT effects on the identity advantage should
persist or increase. If context overrides phonetic evidence at the
level of lexical competition—whether through prediction error
suppression or Bayesian prior dominance—VOT effects should

2. Method
Three groups of participants completed the norming experi-
ment, Experiment 1 and Experiment 2, respectively. All were
native speakers of English, recruited via Prolific, and completed
the experiments online using Gorilla [15].
All speech stimuli were generated using a text-to-
speech model to ensure consistent speaking rate across high-
predictability (HP) and low-predictability (LP) sentences, fa-
cilitating natural-sounding cross-splicing of the same phonetic
tokens into both contexts (see details below).
2.1. Norming experiment
A phonetic categorization task (N=50) was conducted to deter-
mine the voiced–voiceless category boundary for each item and
to guide stimulus selection for Experiments 1 and 2.
2.1.1. Stimuli
26 English minimal word pairs differing in word-initial stop
voicing (e.g.,bat–pat,dip–tip,gold–cold) were selected, with
log frequency differences less than 1.2 (SUBTLEXus; [16]).
For each pair, a 15-step VOT continuum was generated in 5 ms
increments using the Praat script of [17], which preserves nat-
ural covariation between VOT and other acoustic parameters
(e.g., f0, aspiration intensity). Continuum ranges were set to
−10–60 ms for /b/–/p/, 0–70 ms for /d/–/t/, and 5–75 ms for /g/–
/k/. Tokens with negative VOT values were created by attaching
a prevoicing segment of specified duration to a 0 ms VOT token.
2.1.2. Procedure
Participants were randomly assigned to hear continua from one
place of articulation (/b/–/p/, /d/–/t/, or /g/–/k/) and performed a
two-alternative forced choice task, categorizing the word-initial
sound as voiced or voiceless.
2.1.3. Token selection
Following [18], three tokens were selected from each contin-
uum to represent graded within-category phonetic ambiguity
relative to the voiced target words (e.g., “gold”). TheNo am-
biguityandSome ambiguitytokens were both clearly catego-
rized as voiced (i.e., categorically equivalent), but were selected
to be maximally distant on the VOT continuum to maximize
sub-phonemic variation. TheMax ambiguitytoken was the
step closest to the voiced-voiceless boundary, receiving approx-
imately equal proportions of both responses. Ultimately, 24 of
the 26 continua yielded suitable stimuli.
2.2. Experiment 1: Isolated word experiment
Experiment 1 (N=66) examined whether sub-phonemic VOT
variation modulates lexical competition when words are heard
in isolation.
2.2.1. Stimuli
As shown in Fig. 1, for each of the 24 minimal pairs, the three
VOT tokens (No,Some,Max) selected through the norming ex-
periment served as auditory primes. Each was paired with one
of three visual probes: the voiced target (Identical, e.g., “gold”),
the voiceless competitor (Competitor, e.g., “cold”), or an unre-
lated word (Unrelated, e.g., “rule”). The pairing of VOT am-
biguity level and visual probe type was counterbalanced across
participants, yielding 72 critical trials per participant (24 items
×3 conditions). An additional 144 fillers used auditory words
beginning with non-stop consonants, paired with 36 real-word
and 108 nonword visual probes to balance lexicality.
2.2.2. Procedure
On each trial, participants heard an auditory token. At its off-
set, a visual string appeared on screen, and participants made a
speeded lexical decision (word/nonword) via button press. Re-
action times and accuracy were recorded.

2.3. Experiment 2: Sentence experiment
Experiment 2 (N= 66) tested whether sentence-level pre-
dictability modulates the effect of sub-phonemic VOT variation
on lexical competition (see Fig. 2).
2.3.1. Stimuli
The same 24 target words from Experiment 1 were embedded
in HP and LP sentence contexts. Predictability was operational-
ized as word surprisal from the language model GPT-2 small
[19], where lower surprisal indicates higher predictability of a
word in a given context. Surprisal values of the voiced target
words ranged from 0.19 to 15.03 across all auditory sentences,
where the values were designed to be<5in HP sentences
(M= 3.06), and>5in LP sentences (M= 9.41).
The voiced target words within these sentences were then
replaced with the same VOT-manipulated tokens (No,Some,
Max) from Experiment 1 via manual splicing, ensuring that the
acoustic properties of target words were identical across iso-
lated and sentence contexts.
For each target word, three unique HP and three unique LP
sentence frames were constructed, each paired with one of the
three VOT levels. This yielded 144 unique auditory sentences
(24 targets×2 predictability levels×3 sentence frames). VOT
level, visual probe type, and predictability were counterbal-
anced across participants, resulting in 72 critical trials per par-
ticipant along with additional 144 fillers.
2.3.2. Procedure
Participants listened to auditory sentences while performing the
same lexical decision task as in Experiment 1. A visual probe
appeared at the offset of the target word embedded in the mid-
dle of sentences. To ensure attention to the auditory sentences,
yes/no comprehension questions followed 48 of the fillers.
3. Results
Participants with lexical decision accuracy>=.75or mean
RTs within three standard deviations from the group mean were
included (N=58 for Experiment 1;N=60 for Experiment 2).

3.1. Experiment 1: Isolated word experiment
The model revealed significant baseline priming (Unrelated
slower than Identical/Competitor;β=−0.024,p<.001) and
a significant identity advantage (Competitor slower than Identi-
cal;β= 0.017,p<.05). There was also a main effect of VOT,
with faster RTs as ambiguity increased (β=−0.018,p<.01).
Critically, lexical competition, as indexed by the identity
advantage, was modulated by VOT: the linear interaction (β=
−0.021,p < .01) indicated that the Competitor–Identical dif-
ference shrank as ambiguity increased, and the quadratic inter-
action (β=−0.016,p < .05) indicated this convergence ac-
celerated at maximal ambiguity. Planned comparisons (Tukey-
corrected) confirmed that the identity advantage was significant
at No ambiguity (β= 0.051,p < .05) and Some ambiguity
(β= 0.061,p < .01), but was eliminated at Max ambigu-
ity (β=−0.007,p=.93). These results establish that sub-
phonemic VOT variation directly modulates lexical competition
when words are heard in isolation. Figure 3 displays the model-
adjusted log RTs pattern for Experiment 1.

4. Discussion
The current study used two cross-modal priming experiments to
investigate how sub-phonemic variation modulates lexical com-
petition for words in isolation and in sentences varying in con-
textual predictability.
In isolated words (Experiment 1), VOT ambiguity directly
modulated the identity advantage, replicating the gradient effect

In sentences (Experiment 2), contextual predictability pre-
dominantly modulated lexical competition: the identity advan-
tage was larger in HP than LP contexts, indicating that lexi-
cal competition was effectively reduced under strong top-down
expectations. A continuous measure of differential surprisal
strengthened this finding, demonstrating that this context effect
was graded and item-specific. Crucially, the gradient sensitiv-
ity to sub-phonemic variation observed in isolated words was
no longer detectable in sentence-level processing, as evidenced
by the lack of VOT modulation on the identity advantage over
competitors. Our results suggest that regardless of contextual
predictability (HP or LP), lexical competition in listeners was
no longer sensitive to VOT ambiguity during sentence compre-
hension.
Taken together, the two experiments reveal striking differ-
ences between word-level and sentence-level speech process-
ing, providing critical constraints for theories of spoken word
recognition. In the Introduction, we contrasted two theoretical
predictions: (1) if top-down expectationssharpensbottom-up
signals, VOT effects on lexical competition should persist or
even amplify in sentences, particularly under HP contexts; (2)
if top-down expectations attenuate or override bottom-up pro-
cessing, VOT effects on lexical competition should diminish or
disappear. Our data are more consistent with the latter predic-
tion.
Under sharpening accounts, including interactive activation
models such as TRACE [8] and related proposals [9], top-down
feedback enhances expected speech signals, thereby preserving
or amplifying sensitivity to sub-phonemic variation for lexical
competition. Contrary to this prediction, the gradient VOT ef-
fects observed in isolated words were neither preserved nor en-
hanced in HP sentences, where prior context was expected to
strongly activate pre-lexical representations.
Instead, our results are broadly compatible with accounts in
which contextual predictions reduce the relative contribution of
phonetic evidence to lexical competition. These include both
predictive coding frameworks [10, 11] and Bayesian inference
models [14], which posit a downweighting of bottom-up signals
as top-down expectations strengthen. These proposals are con-
sistent with the present findings, where VOT effects observed
in isolated words were attenuated in HP sentences. While our
results cannot adjudicate between these two accounts, they con-
strain the theoretical landscape of speech perception models by
challenging the “signal sharpening” hypothesis.
One notable aspect of our findings is that VOT effects on
lexical competition were no longer detectable once sentential
context was present, even when sentence predictability was low.
One potential concern is that prosodic and local acoustic fac-
tors in the sentence context may have altered the perception of
VOT tokens that were normed in isolation, thereby reducing the
effective range of phonetic ambiguity across our three levels
(cf.[21, 22]). However, previous work has demonstrated that
listeners remain sensitive to VOT variation when words are em-
bedded in sentence contexts, as evidenced by reliable effects on
phoneme categorization [3, 4, 6]. This suggests that the null ef-
fect observed here is unlikely to be explained solely by a loss of
perceptual sensitivity to within-category VOT variation.
At the same time, the null VOT effect in sentences in the
current study should not be taken as evidence that phonetic de-
tail is discarded during sentence comprehension. Our cross-
modal priming paradigm assesses the functional consequence
of sub-phonemic variation for lexical competition, rather than
the fidelity of phonetic encodingper se. While prior work sug-
gests that fine-grained phonetic detail can be encoded and main-
tained even in sentence contexts [6, 7], these findings speak to
phonetic encoding rather than lexical competition. Moreover, in
[7], disambiguating context follows the target, requiring main-
tenance of phonetic detail until the contextual cues arrive. In
contrast, the predictive context in the present study precedes the
target, allowing prior expectations to modulate the processing of
upcoming phonetic detail. Notably, existing evidence remains
mixed regarding whether such top-down modulation driven by
contextual predictability enhances or attenuates phonetic encod-
ing [9, 13, 23, 24]. The present findings complement this liter-
ature by showing that, regardless of the exact mechanism at the
phonetic encoding stage, sub-phonemic variation does not de-
tectably influence how lexical candidates compete when a sen-
tential context is available. Identifying the precise threshold
at which contextual information begins to override the contri-
bution of sub-phonemic variation to lexical competition—and
whether this transition is gradual or abrupt—remains an impor-
tant question for future research.
In conclusion, our findings indicate that the processing of
isolated words is not a mere proxy for continuous speech; rather,
the presence of a sentential context and continuously unfolding
new information may change how different information sources
are utilized to resolve lexical competition. Theories of spoken
word recognition built primarily on isolated word paradigms
may overestimate the role of fine-grained phonetic detail in the
ecologically typical case of sentence comprehension.
