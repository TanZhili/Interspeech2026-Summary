Abstract
Gender expansive voices remain underrepresented in speech
datasets, which could cause challenges with generalizability in
zero-shot Text-To-Speech (TTS) systems. We evaluate six zero-
shot TTS models on two datasets: one containing non gender
expansive voices (N-GE) and one with gender expansive (GE)
voices from self-identified speakers. Human ratings found that
2 of the 6 models synthesize less similar GE voices compared to
N-GE voices, and 3 have improved performance for GE voices.
Automated evaluations yield conflicting results, with speaker
similarity and automated mean opinion score predictors rating
performance of GE voices inconsistently between models and
human ratings. This highlights a potential blind spot in evalua-
tion pipelines, emphasizing a need for more representative data
and metrics that better capture human perceptual judgments.
Our findings stress the need to include transgender, queer, and
minority communities in creation of better datasets and model
training.

1. Introduction
Artificial intelligence technologies in text-to-speech (TTS) sys-
tems and natural language processing (NLP) have enhanced
human-computer interaction, often at the expense of minority
groups [1, 2]. While these offer benefits, they inherit and am-
plify societal biases, leading to ethical and practical concerns
that remain unaddressed [3]. Recent studies have found that
end-to-end TTS models can render ‘gay voices’ to sound less
‘gay’, as determined by queer people [4], erasing key aspects of
a speaker’s identity. Research indicates that models trained on
gender-specific datasets under-perform when compared to non
gender-specific datasets when used for model training [5], sug-
gesting that current data collection practices may inadvertently
limit voice diversity. We evaluate zero-shot TTS performance
in replicating voices from underrepresented groups, and exam-
ine whether similar biases are present in evaluation algorithms.
’Zero-shot TTS’ refers to TTS systems that can generate speech
for unseen speakers using a few second voice sample with-
out additional fine-tuning. In this study we focus on “Gender-
Expansive” (GE) voices, which refers to voices from speakers
who self-identify as not being cisgender We use the term “Non
Gender-Expansive” (N-GE) to refer to cisgender speakers.
1.1. Data Bias and the Necessity for Diverse Training Sets
The ability of current state-of-the-art (SOTA) zero-shot TTS
to clone voices with high fidelity is dependent upon the di-
versity and quality of their training data. Studies such as the
Globe corpus work [6] demonstrate that models trained on di-
verse datasets are better able to generalize to unseen voices.
Similarly, a multilingual model has shown promising results in
adapting to a variety of linguistic features across different lan-
guages [7]. However, pervasive issues in data collection, such
as sampling skew and aggregation distortions compromise the
representativeness of these datasets [8]. For example, the Com-
mon V oice dataset [9] reports near zero-percent participation
from transgender individuals. Moreover, as an example within
NLP, a large text dataset disproportionately removed queer and
trans-related text, leading to data on queer people being de-
fined by those without lived experience [10]. A barrier in de-
veloping equitable TTS and speech analysis systems is ensur-
ing training datasets accurately represent real world voice di-
versity, as inadequate representation can lead to significant per-
formance disparities [6]. Racial disparities have been found in
automated speech recognition, as well as gender and dialect bi-
ases in YouTube’s automatic captions [11, 12]. Although trans-
gender, queer, and other underrepresented groups constitute a
smaller proportion of the population [13, 14], oversampling or
other corrective measures can be essential to ensure these voices
are accurately represented, but these techniques are rarely used
[15, 16].
1.2. Failure of Speech-based Machine Learning Systems
The challenges associated with biased voice datasets extend be-
yond TTS systems. Deepfake audio detection algorithms often
fail when confronted with out-of-distribution data [17], which
can result in cloned voices from marginalized groups being un-
detected, increasing risks of impersonation. In a similar vein,
speaker verification systems have exhibited significant perfor-
mance degradation, failing to categorize transgender individuals
as the same person when they employ gender-affirming voice
modification techniques [18]. This undermines the technical re-
liability of these systems and contribute to a broader sense of
exclusion among affected users, reinforcing the perception that
such technologies are not designed for all demographic groups
[19].
1.3. Inherited Societal Norms and the Impact on Speech
Generating Device Users
Issues of gender-induced bias in voice synthesis is especially
impactful on frequent users of devices that utilize this technol-
ogy, especially as voice models tend to inherit prevailing soci-
etal gender norms [20]. When not tackled, these biases may
be amplified, prompting participants in a recent study to advo-
cate for the use of gender-neutral voices as a mitigation strategy
[20]. This issue also has a particular effect on users of speech
generating devices, who already face stigmatization within soci-

1.4. Research Objectives and Contributions
Motivated by these challenges, this study addresses two key re-
search questions:
1.V oice Cloning Bias:Do zero-shot TTS systems have per-
formance differences, as judged by human listeners, on GE
voices?
2.Comparative Bias Analysis:Does a bias exist in ma-
chine learning evaluation algorithms used for speech analy-
sis, when compared against other evaluation metrics and sub-
jective perceptual metrics?
2. Methods
2.1. V oice Synthesis and Preprocessing
Six zero-shot TTS models were used to assess potential bi-
ases in synthesizing GE voices: CosyV oice2, E2TTS, F5TTS,
XTTS, Zonos, and LinaSpeech [7, 24, 25, 26, 27, 28].
CosyV oice2, E2TTS, F5TTS, and XTTS were all chosen as ex-
amples of models trained on large multilingual, and potentially
more diverse, datasets. ZONOS was chosen as a current indus-
try model, and LinaSpeech is used as an example of a smaller
zero-shot TTS model.
Two datasets were utilized for assessing performance. N-
GE voices, the Globe Dataset [6] was used, containing assumed
majority N-GE speaker samples. To select speakers from this
dataset, an even split was created between “male” and “female”
speakers, and only selected participants with accents labeled as
“United States English”. Gender and accent data were provided
by the metadata of this dataset. We note that the creators of
the GLOBE dataset have assumed the gender of speakers who
provided no gender data and assigned a gender label using a
classifier model [6]. This could cause incorrect classification as
these often fail for speakers whose voices fall within ambiguous
ranges [29]. For GE speakers, the ‘Mid-Atlantic Gender Expan-
sive Speech’ (MAGES) dataset [30] was used which contains
GE voices based on an individual’s gender self-identification,
and these labels are maintained in our analyses.
Audio used as reference for generation is created by con-
catenating audio files from each speaker, to reach an approx-
imate duration of 10 seconds. A reference sample generated
in this way was created for all 14 MAGES speakers and 14
GLOBE speakers (chosen using the above criteria), which were
then used for synthesis. This sample size is used due to the lim-
ited availability of datasets explicitly containing GE speakers.
For each speaker, 15 phrases from the Harvard sentences
were synthesized using each model. All audio samples were
standardized to 16 kHz and -20 dB to ensure consistency in
evaluations.
2.2. Human Evaluation
16 participants were recruited to rate the synthesized voices.
Each participant listened to a 3-4 second sample from each
speaker used in the concatenated reference sample, and then
asked to rate the similarity of synthesized samples to this.
Subjective evaluations were conducted using a MUSHRA-like
paradigm to compare across models [31]. This MUSHRA-like
implementation is a perceptual study design which presents par-
ticipants simultaneously with a reference audio and a synthe-
sized sample from each model (6 samples total) per trial, with
3 trials total per reference speaker. The order of trials was ran-
domized. Participants rate each sample’s similarity to the refer-
ence audio on a scale between 0-100. A total of 8 speakers, 4
from each dataset, were used for the MUSHRA-Like assesment,
giving a total of 24 trials for a total of 144 ratings.
2.3. Machine Learning/Automated Metrics
All synthesized samples were also assessed using three mea-
sures, automated mean opinion score (AMOS), speaker similar-
ity, and word error rate (WER) [32, 33, 34]. speaker similar-
ity was calculated by computing cosine similarity between the
reference audio’s speaker embedding and each of the synthe-
sized sample’s embedding from this reference audio. Speaker
embeddings were extracted from the reference and synthe-
sized voices using ECAPA-TDNN, TitaNet-L and ReDimNet-
M (vb2+vox2+cnc-ft-mix), three models which provide speaker
embeddings [33, 35, 36, 37, 38]. AMOS was found using mod-
els trained to predict human mean opinion scores (MOS). For
this we used UTMOS [39] and a finetuned wav2vec2 model for
mean opinion score (MOS) prediction as described in Cooper,
et al. 2022 [32]. Between the speaker similarity ratings and
AMOS models respectively we calculated the Intraclass Cor-
relation Coefficient (ICC) to assess the consistency of the pre-
dicted ratings. WER was calculated using error between the ac-
tual generated audio’s transcription and the sentences used for
synthesis. Transcripts were generated using the ASR system
Whisper [34].

4. Discussion
Human evaluations using a MUSHRA-like evaluation found
performance differences in speaker similarity of outputs be-
tween GE and N-GE voices which varied across models. For
the models LinaSpeech and XTTS, participants rated synthe-
sized GE voices as significantly worse recreations of the ref-
erence audio than N-GE voices, whereas for F5TTS, E2TTS,
and CosyV oice2, the opposite results were observed, with N-GE
voices being rated as significantly less similar to the reference
audio.
For automated metrics, these models disagree on the per-
formance of the datasets depending on which evaluation model
is used. Speaker similarity metrics had disparities in how they
rated each dataset per model, with an overall significant dif-
ference between datasets for some speaker verification mod-
els which was not present for others. Most notably, all three
speaker similarity metrics for the ZONOS model disagree on
dataset performance, with ECAPA-TDNN predicting improved

These disparities both between evaluation models and their
differences with human ratings of speaker similarity draw into
question the applicability of these as automated testing mea-
sures. These discrepancies likely stem from the training data
used to develop these automated metrics, which may underrep-
resent queer and transgender voices, leading to difficulties in
predicting actual performance. Consequently, although auto-
mated tools can detect differences, they do not appear to ac-
curately reflect the qualitative judgments of human listeners.
Without addressing these issues with automated metrics, it be-
comes difficult to assess a models actual performance on minor-
ity voice groups.
We suggest a potential cause of the decreased performance
could be that models which performed worse on subjective eval-
uations were trained on datasets containing read speech (XTTS,
LinaSpeech), whereas two of the models (F5TTS, E2TTS) that
showed better performance were trained on spontaneous speech
datasets (no information is provided for other models). Spon-
taneous speech captures nuances of real speech in compari-
son to read speech, leading to a greater data diversity, even if
the dataset doesn’t explicitly contain gender-expansive speak-
ers. Although the datasets used by these models do not state
the queer-identities of their speakers, F5TTS and E2TTS were
trained on the EMILIA dataset [40] which utilizes “in-the-wild”
data, and so these datasets likely contain more diverse individ-
uals. Poor replication of queer voices also happens with other
TTS models [4], in that ‘queer’ aspects of these voices are re-
moved from synthesized voices when suitable datasets are not
used, which may come as a result of the model not being able
to learn these aspects. This could also provide an explanation
as to why agreement between automated metrics appears to be
higher for GE voices for most TTS models, if analysis of speech
samples follows a similar pattern in removing queer aspects of
voices, and as such these speakers may end up clustering in
similar score ranges between models, though this would require
future research to investigate.
4.1. Data Scarcity and Ethical Dilemmas in Curating Inclu-
sive Datasets
A potential cause of the issues presented here is due to the
limited availability of queer speech datasets. We were only
able to find two speech datasets explicitly containing queer data
[18, 30], and only a single dataset that was suitable for assessing
a zero-shot TTS model’s performance on multiple queer voices,
and even then, only 14 voices were available within the dataset
utilized [30]. Without large high quality datasets it is hard to ex-
pect that the models would be able to perform better, especially
given that TTS models may fail to synthesize voices accurately
which are from sparser sections of a models latent space [41].
A naive solution is to create a large dataset of these voices.
However, this leads to concerns for queer communities. If
larger, high quality, and well labeled datasets are made pub-
licly available, the potential for abuse rises [4]. It is imperative
to be aware and ensure that transgender and queer communi-
ties are actively engaged within the dataset creation process, as
disclosure of identity of individuals within could cause harm to
communities and individuals. Moreover, strict guidelines sur-
rounding data usage, privacy, and consent will be critical to en-
sure that risks associated with misuse are avoided. This could
also be enhanced through anonymization techniques, controlled
access protocols, or secure data repositories. Further, it is im-
perative that the principle of ’Nothing about us without us’ is
followed, meaning that those the dataset is supposed to repre-
sent must also be key contributors throughout the entire process.
While the pursuit of more inclusive datasets may lead to sig-
nificant technical gains, it’s important to ensure that the needs
and privacy of marginalized groups is placed at the forefront to
avoid unintended harm.
5. Conclusion
We investigated the ability of multiple zero-shot TTS models
to synthesize speech of GE speakers in comparison to N-GE
speakers, assessed via both human ratings and automated met-
rics. Further, we assessed whether automated metrics of speaker
similarity and AMOS are effective for evaluating performance
on GE speakers in comparison to subjective metrics, as well
as how different evaluation model’s consistency varies between
GE and N-GE datasets. Subjective metrics varied across mod-
els, with two models showing worse performance for GE speak-
ers, and three models showing better performance. The effect
size was greater when GE voices performed worse. Various
speaker similarity and AMOS models had performance predic-
tions inconsistent with human scores, and themselves, where
there is a disparity between the model’s predictions on whether
GE or N-GE voices perform better. These disparities could lead
to assumptions that assessed models have better or worse per-
formance on diverse voices than in reality. These results high-
light a need to enhance both model and evaluation performance,
as it is likely that the training data for these models underrep-
resents diverse voices. We argue for the creation of more in-
clusive datasets, however creation of these should be done with
caution, keeping queer and transgender communities’ needs at
the forefront.
