Abstract
Utilizing visual cues for speaker extraction has demonstrated
strong performance in noisy-reverberant environments. This
paper proposes a token-guided paradigm for audio-visual target
speaker extraction (A VTSE). Given a target speaker’s lip move-
ments in a facial video, the first goal is to extract coarse-grained
semantic tokens associated with the lip movements. Second, we
treat the predicted tokens as temporally aligned semantic cues to
guide the speaker extraction system. We evaluate our approach
on two benchmark datasets: V oxCeleb2-2Mix and LRS2-2Mix.
Evaluation results demonstrate that our token-guided approach
consistently outperforms conventional A VTSE systems.

1. Introduction
Target speaker extraction (TSE) plays a crucial role in various
real-world applications, including online meetings, hearing aid
design, and human–robot interaction [1, 2]. Conventional TSE
frameworks typically leverage deep neural networks (DNNs) to
extract each speaker’s voice from an audio mixture by condi-
tioning on an enrollment utterance [3, 4, 5, 6, 7]. While this ap-
proach has proven effective in audio-only scenarios, it encoun-
ters significant challenges when extended to complex acous-
tic environments [8]. To name a few, the enrollment utter-
ance is usually not temporally aligned with the mixture, pro-
viding only weak global conditioning without fine-grained tem-
poral guidance [5]. A short enrollment segment may lack suffi-
cient contextual information to capture dynamic speaking char-
acteristics such as prosody, articulation timing, and emotional
cues [9]. Differently, recent studies have explored leveraging
synchronous visual cues (such as lip movements) [10], which
are robust against the acoustic environment. Visual signals pro-
vide continuous and temporally synchronized contextual infor-
mation related to the target speaker’s speech, which is strongly
correlated with the phonetic content and temporal boundaries of
speech production [11, 12].
However, directly incorporating raw visual signals into ex-
traction systems is challenging due to modality heterogene-
ity [13, 14]. Notably, visual signals vary significantly in tem-
poral resolution and frame rate, making direct feature-level
fusion or alignment difficult. Therefore, directly condition-
ing extraction networks on raw visual signals often leads to
weak or unstable guidance. To address this, previous stud-
ies [15, 11, 16] typically employ a pre-trained face encoder

(e.g., 3D-ResNet18 [13]) to transform each visual frame into a

be more easily fused with audio features, providing additional
guidance for TSE [17]. In addition, lip embeddings are related
to speaker identity, speech activity, articulation style, and con-
tent information. They represent only correlated features, rather
than necessary variables for generating the target speech. More-
over, there exists a significant cross-modal gap: the audio mix-
ture resides in the acoustic space, while the lip embeddings re-
side in the visual space, forcing the network to learn a complex
cross-modal alignment mapping.
Compared to continuous lip embeddings, speech tokens de-
rived from self-supervised speech models [18, 19] or neural
codecs [20] provide a discrete, causally grounded representa-
tion that is more tightly coupled with the speech generation
process. Conditioning A VTSE systems on such tokens rather
than lip embeddings reduce audio-visual ambiguity and enables
the model to focus on speech-content-aware target speech re-
construction rather than relying on audio-visual correlations. To
this end, as shown in Fig. 1, we aim to map audio-visual streams
into a discrete semantic space, where semantic tokens serve as
temporally aligned cues to guide the TSE process.
In this paper, we propose a novel token-guided target
speaker extraction (TGTSE) framework that introduces discrete
tokens as semantic clues for audio-visual TSE (A VTSE). The
key idea is that discrete semantic tokens provide a modality-
agnostic, semantically-meaningful, and temporally-consistent
representation that bridges the visual modality with the acous-
tic domain. This design is particularly advantageous for the
following reasons: 1 discrete tokens exhibit strong temporal

2.2. Token Predictor
Figure 2 illustrates the token predictor. A frozen face encoder
pre-trained on lip-reading task (e.g., 3D-ResNet18 [13]) is em-
ployed to extract frame-level visual features. To match the tem-
poral resolution of tokens, an upsampling layer interpolates the
features along the time axis, i.e.,
V = FaceEncoder(v)∈ RNv×H , (7)
S = Upsample(V )∈ RM×H , (8)
where H denotes the feature dimension of each visual frame.
To enhance the accuracy of token prediction, we also use
mixture as the input to the token predictor. Following previous
work [13], we transform the mixture y into 321-dimensional
spectral magnitudes |STFT(y)| ∈ RTmix×321 via short-time
Fourier transform (STFT) with a40 ms window and 10 ms hop-
length at 16 kHz sample rate. Since each token corresponds
to approximately 20 ms of speech (as extracted by WavLM-
Large), each token spans two STFT frames. As a result, the
total number of STFT frames satisfies Tmix = 2 M. To align

R = Conv1D(|STFT(y)|)∈ RM×H , (9)

eS = TransformerEncoder(PE(S))∈ RM×H ,

Finally, a linear classification head produces the logits:
ℓ = W X + b, (11)
where W∈ RH×K and b∈ RK are the learnable weights and

feature to probability.
2.3. Token-Guided Speaker Extractor
To investigate the effectiveness of the token-guided approach,
we use time-frequency (T-F) and time-domain speech extraction
networks.
2.3.1. Time-Frequency Domain Speaker Extractor
We first transform the mixture waveform y into its complex
spectrogram representation Y = STFT(y)∈ RT×F , where F
denotes the number of frequency bins. A convolutional encoder
is then applied to map the real and imaginary (RI) components
into a latent representation:
g = Conv2D([R(Y ),I(Y )])∈ RQ×T×F . (12)
whereR(·) andI(·) respectively extract RI components. Q
denotes the embedding dimension of each T-F unit. To incor-
porate token embeddings e∈ R1×M×D, two linear layers re-
spectively transform the feature dimension D to F and 1 to Q.
Then, we upsample the token sequence from M frames to T
frames. These steps result in ee∈ RQ×T×F . We fuse the token
embeddings with the mixture representation by concatenation
and a linear projection:
eg = Linear([ee, g])∈ RQ×T×F , (13)
which is processed by a stack of T-F modeling blocks (e.g., TF-
GridNet [24]) to refine the mixture representation:
G = TF-Blocks(eg)∈ RQ×T×F . (14)
Finally, a deconvolutional decoder maps G back to complex
spectrogram and iSTFT reconstructs target speaker’s speech:
ˆs = iSTFT(DeConv2D(G))∈ RN . (15)
2.3.2. Time-Domain Speaker Extractor
Time-domain network consists of three main components: a
convolutional encoder, a masking-based estimator, and a lin-
ear decoder with overlap-and-add reconstruction. Formally, the
processing pipeline is:
Z = ReLU(Conv1D(y))∈ RM×L, (16)
M =E(Z, e)∈ RM×L, (17)
ˆZ = Z⊗M∈ RM×L, (18)
ˆs = Overlap-Add(Linear( ˆZ))∈ RN , (19)
where Z is an M-frame embedding sequence of dimension L,
and⊗ is element-wise multiplication. The mask-based estima-
torE(·,·) incorporates the token embeddings into the mixture
embeddings and produces a multiplicative maskM that selec-
tively enhances the components of target speaker while attenu-
ating interfering sources. Finally, the decoder reconstructs the
time-domain waveform from the masked features ˆZ via a lin-
ear layer followed by the standard overlap-add synthesis [25],
producing the estimate ˆs.
3. Experimental Setup
To validate TGTSE, we use two benchmarks, V oxCeleb2-2Mix
and LRS2-2Mix, which are respectively derived from V ox-
Celeb2 [32] and LRS2 [13]. Both datasets are constructed using
the same configuration following the pre-processing pipeline in
ClearerV oice [31], in which 20, 000, 5, 000, and 3, 000 two-
speaker mixtures are respectively simulated for training, vali-
dation and testing, and there is no speaker overlap across all
partitions. The target speech is mixed with interfering speech
at a signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) randomly sampled from−10 to
+10 dB. All audio signals are sampled at 16 kHz, while video
signals are processed at 25 frames per second, with each frame
converted to a 1× 112× 112 grayscale image. Each of the
training and validation samples consists of3-second speech seg-
ments, whereas test samples use full-length utterances. Further-
more, we evaluate the generalization capability on three cross-
domain datasets: LRS3 [33], Grid [34] and TCD-TIMIT [35].
For the token predictor, we report its accuracy at token pre-
diction. In addition, we evaluate the A VTSE performance us-
ing four commonly adopted metrics: signal-to-distortion ra-
tio (SDR) [36], scale-invariant SDR (SI-SDR) [22], perceptual
evaluation of speech quality (PESQ) [37], and short-time objec-
tive intelligibility (STOI) [38].
For the token predictor, each transformer encoder consists
of 6 layers with a hidden size of 256, a feed-forward dimen-
sion of 1024, and 8 self-attention heads (totaling 25.4M param-
eters). We optimize the network using AdamW [39] starting
with a learning rate of 1× 10−4, and employ a cosine learning
rate schedule with linear warmup during the first 10% train-
ing epochs, and then decays following a cosine function for
the remaining epochs. For speaker extraction, we employ two
extraction networks: TFGridNet [30] and MossFormer 2 [31],
which are state-of-art networks respectively in the T-F and
time domain. Following the symbols defined in their origi-
nal papers, we set the hyper-parameters of TFGridNet to D =
128, B = 6 , I = 1 , J = 1 , H = 256 , L = 4 , E = 8 (re-
sulting in 11.3M parameters), and those of MossFormer 2 to
R = 24 , N = 512 , K = 16 , N′ = 256 , L = 2 (leading to
55.7M parameters). Please do not confuse these symbols with
the ones defined in this paper. The models are trained for up to
50 epochs. For TFGridNet, the learning rate is set to 5× 10−4
with Adam [40], which is decayed by a factor of 0.5 if the val-
idation loss does not improve for two consecutive epochs. For
MossFormer2, we use the same training strategy as the token
predictor.

5. Conclusions
We have proposed TGTSE, a token-guided TSE approach lever-
aging visual cues for speaker conditiioning. It converts raw vi-
sual signals to semantic tokens to improve target-speaker ex-
traction. Evaluation results on two representative benchmark
datasets show that TGTSE consistently outperforms existing
A VTSE networks.
