Abstract
Large language models (LLMs) have expanded from text to
speech, giving rise to Speech Large Models (SLMs). A
key challenge is aligning speech and text representations,
which becomes harder in multilingual settings. Existing
methods often freeze LLM parameters and train the encoders
on multilingual multi-task data, often collapsing language
representations into an shared space and hurting performance.
We introduce Progressive Alignment Representation Training
(PART), a multi-stage and multi-task framework that performs
fine-grained alignment across languages and diverse speech
tasks. During training, we progressively unfreeze the SLMs
parameters, while introducing different types of multilingual
tasks stage by stage to enhance multilingual speech–text
alignment. Experiments on multiple mainstream benchmarks
show that PART surpasses conventional approaches, with
analysis confirming its ability to balance language-specific
distinctions for robust multilingual speech-text alignment1.
Index Terms: Multilingual Speech Processing, Speech-Text
Alignment, Large Language Models
1. Introduction
In the research of large language models (LLMs), the
scope has expanded from text to other modalities. Among
them, Speech Large Models (SLMs) that use speech input
and output have been widely studied and applied, showing
impressive performance in tasks such as speech recognition [1],
translation [2, 3], and speech synthesis [4].
The mainstream architecture of current SLMs usually
consists of a pre-trained speech encoder connected to an LLM
through an adapter [5, 6]. Within this framework, a central
challenge is how to effectively align speech representations
with the textual representations of the LLM [7, 8]. This need
becomes more critical and difficult in fine-grained multilingual
scenarios. In practice, the conventional approach often mix
multiple multilingual data, such as ASR (Automatic Speech
Recognition) task and S2TT (Speech-to-Text Translation) task
during training. A common approach is to keep the LLM
parameters frozen and train the speech encoder on multilingual
speech data so that it aligns with the LLM input layer [9,
10]. However, this common approach can collapse audio
representations across languages in the feature space, reducing
the encoder’s ability to capture fine-grained multilingual speech
differences [11, 12]. Moreover, it often yields only coarse

We propose aProgressiveAlignmentRepresentation
Training approach (PART) for Multilingual SLMs. In this
approach, the training is divided into stages to separate within-
language alignment from multilingual speech data and multiple
tasks alignment, preventing excessive convergence of audio
representations across languages. For different speech tasks,
LLM parameters are dynamically activated, allowing the speech
encoder to focus on semantic mapping within each language
while the LLM leverages its multilingual modeling strength.
In the final stage, multi-tasks are introduced to fine-tune the
LLM, further improving its ability in multilingual instruction
understanding and generation. Overall, our method enables
a collaborative division of labor between the speech encoder
and the LLM, preserving language-specific distinctions while
enhancing complex multilingual speech tasks.
Compared with traditional training methods, our multi-
stage and multi-task alignment approach achieves better
performance on CommonV oice 15, and Fleurs(ASR tasks),
as well as CoV oST2 (S2TT task). In addition, analytical
experiments show that the effectiveness and generality of our
method for multilingual speech modality alignment. Our
contributions are summarized as follows:
• We propose a multi-stage, multi-task alignment frame-
work for multilingual SLMs, which separates in-language
alignment from multilingual alignment to better preserve
language-specific features.
• We design a task-dependent activation strategy that freezes
the LLM in in-language ASR tasks while activating it in
multilingual tasks, allowing the audio encoder and LLM to
play to their respective strengths.
• We further introduce a final text-based fine-tuning stage
that enhances multilingual ability, leading to improved
performance across ASR and S2TT tasks.

2.2. Model Architecture
As in [15, 16], we first extract log-mel spectrogram features
Mfrom the input speech signalXusing a feature extractor.
These features are then fed into a pre-trained multilingual
speech encoder (parameterized byθ se) to obtain linguistic
representationsX ling. Subsequently, a lightweight adaptor
module, randomly initialized with parametersθ adaptor and not
pretrained, projectsX ling into the embedding space of a
large language model (LLM, parameterized byθ llm), resulting
in the aligned feature representationX align. This adapted
representation is then concatenated with the embedding of
the instruction tokenX I, and the combined input is passed
to the multilingual LLM. Finally, the LLM generates the
corresponding text. Model details are shown in Figure 1 (a).

3. Experimental Setup
3.1. Data and Settings
The training data includes two tasks, ASR and S2TT. For ASR,
there is a total of 810k hours of commercial purchases data
covering 10 languages: Chinese (zh), English (en), Japanese
(ja), Korean (ko), Cantonese (yue), German (de), French (fr),
Russian (ru), Spanish (es), and Italian (it). The S2TT task data
encompasses 434k hours, covering language pairs such as zh-
en, ja-en, de-en, fr-en, es-en, it-en, ru-en, as well as en-zh, en-ja,
en-de, en-sv, en-id, and en-ar. The main sources of S2TT data
are: 1) open-source datasets: CoV oST2 [17], TED-LIUM [18],
and MuST-C [19]; 2) constructing S2TT data by translating
ASR transcripts into target languages and The rest comes from
commercial purchases of 48k hours.

3.2. Model Specifications
The speech audio encoder is initialized with the SenseV oice-
large encoder [26], approximately 700M in size. The
LLM is based on Qwen2.5 [27] enhanced with multilingual
continuing pre-training in advance. The Adapter consists of
two transformer layers and one CNN layer. We experiment
with two sizes of the Qwen2.5 model, 1.5B and 7B
parameters, corresponding to PART-2B and PART-8B. Training
is performed using 256 NVIDIA A800 GPUs for three epochs,
and inference is performed using greedy decoding.
3.3. Baseline
To demonstrate the effectiveness of our model, we compare
against a broad set of baselines, including Whisper-large-
v3 [24], widely used for multilingual speech tasks, as well
as representative multilingual speech LLMs such as Qwen2-
Audio [10], Qwen2.5-Omni [11], MinMo [28], Speech-
LLaMA [29], and LLaST [16]. Furthermore, to verify the
benefits of progressive training, we implement a two-stage
training variant, denoted as Baseline-2stage, and compare it
with PART under the same model size and training data budget.

For Multilingual ASR:The experimental results are shown

of similar scale, indicating that our training recipe is highly
effective and yields more balanced performance across different
language directions.

4.2. Ablation Study of Staged ASR→S2TT Training, LLM
LoRA, and Progressive Encoder Unfreezing
We use PART (our method) as the base training recipe: the
first three stages train ASR only to build more stable speech
representations, and the final stage switches to joint ASR and
S2TT training for task alignment and transfer. We then perform
three stepwise ablations:
(1)w/o ASR→S2TT (on top of PART):Starting from PART,
we remove the staged ASR→S2TT schedule and train
ASR+S2TT jointly throughout all three stages.
(2)w/o LLM LoRA (on top of (1)):Based on setting (1), we
further disable LoRA fine-tuning of the LLM.
(3)w/o progressive encoder unfreezing (on top of (2)):
We remove staged encoder unfreezing (last 8 layers→full
encoder) and unfreeze the full encoder after training the audio
projector.

4.3. Analyzing the Multi-Stage Alignment of PART
To further validate the effectiveness of the PART framework
in cross-lingual and cross-task representation alignment, we
conduct a quantitative analysis of gradient consistency across
multiple training stages, inspired by [30]. Following the
experimental setup in [30], we extract task-level gradients
from a fixed sample set at each stage and calculate the cosine
similarity between ASR and S2TT tasks.
Figure 3:Gradient cosine similarity matrices across the three
training stages of PART. The2×2panel visualizes the evolution
of task-level alignment between ASR and S2TT tasks.
As illustrated in Figure 3, the results demonstrate that
gradient similarity among different languages within ASR
tasks strengthens steadily as training progresses, confirming
the effective fusion of underlying speech representations.
Meanwhile, the gradient interactions between S2TT and ASR
exhibit a distinct stage-dependent pattern, validating that
multi-stage strategy of PART successfully guides the orderly
organization of transfer gradients within the parameter space.
Notably, while the overall alignment increases, the similarity
matrix still shows much higher similarity within each language
than across languages, making the language boundaries clearly
distinguishable.
5. Conclusion
This work proposes PART, a stage-by-stage, multilingual
multi-task training paradigm that decouples intra-language
alignment from cross-lingual alignment. The staged approach
preserves language specificity while fully exploiting the LLM’s
multilingual modeling capacity, avoiding over-convergence of
speech representations in multilingual settings. Experiments
on Common V oice 15, FLEURS, and CoV oST2 demonstrate
consistent gains on both ASR and S2TT. Mechanistic analyses
and ablation studies further validate the effectiveness of the
progressive training strategy. Overall, PART provides a
general and transferable paradigm for multilingual speech–text
alignment, and offers valuable insights for future optimization
of multilingual speech-language models.
