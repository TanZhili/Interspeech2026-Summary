Abstract
Standard TTS metrics such as MOS and mel-cepstral dis-
tortion provide global scores but do not locatewheresynthe-
sis diverges from natural speech. To examine this, four sys-
tems (Tacotron2-DDC, FastSpeech2, Glow-TTS, MixerTTS)
are analysed across 13,100 matched LJ-TTS utterances. At the
prosodic level, globalF 0 variability is compressed (d=−0.55)
while local pitch reversals increase (d= +0.82), suggest-
ing a cross-timescale dissociation, rather than monotone into-
nation. Timing metrics show no reliable deviation, localizing
the deficit toF 0 coordination. At the segmental level, vowel
spaces shrink to 9–30% of the human baseline, directional for-
mant biases indicate articulatory undershoot, and locus analysis
confirms reduced place-conditioned coarticulation most consis-
tently for alveolars. Deviations at these two levels are largely
uncorrelated, suggesting prosodic organisation and segmental
precision are distinct dimensions of synthesis quality conflated
by standard metrics.

1. Introduction
Neural TTS systems have achieved mean opinion scores ap-
proaching human parity on standard benchmarks [1, 2, 3].
Perceptual indistinguishability and acoustic-phonetic fidelity
are separate properties [4, 5]. Speech is organised hierarchi-
cally across multiple timescales. At the suprasegmental level,
utterance-level declination contours govern local prominence
peaks. At the segmental level, articulatory gestures are con-
ditioned on adjacent phonetic context through coarticulation.
These structures are not redundant surface detail; they support
intelligibility under adverse conditions, enable expressive mod-
ulation, and stabilise linguistic contrast.
TTS evaluation does not formally separate these repre-
sentational levels and has leaned on perceptual ratings and
spectrogram-level distances that collapse phonetic dimen-
sions [4, 6]. These subjective evaluations often lack method-
ological transparency, limiting reproducibility and interpretabil-
ity [7, 8]. Recent phonetically informed work has begun to
close this gap, revealing system-specific weaknesses invisible
to MOS: some models excel in pitch while failing in voice qual-
ity, others the reverse [9, 10, 11]. Coarticulation in neural TTS
has not been studied through locus equations [12, 13]; supraseg-
mental and segmental organisation have not been jointly evalu-
ated at corpus scale across multiple architectures.
These gaps point to a unified question: do phonetic devia-
tions in neural TTS concentrate at a single level of organisation,

2. Related Work
MOS-based evaluation has well-known limitations. Wag-
ner et al. [4] argue that perceptual scores collapse across lin-
guistically distinct dimensions, obscuring structural deficien-
cies. Cooper et al. [5] show that standard objective metrics
poorly capture phonetic organisation, and Galdino et al. [6]
find that F0-RMSE dominates prosody evaluation despite con-
flating global contour shape with local perturbations. Chan
and Kuang [9] propose a two-tier framework separating dis-
crete prosodic categories from continuous acoustic realisa-
tions, showing that comparable MOS can mask distinct acous-
tic strategies. We extend this by scaling the analysis to a large
corpus without manual annotation and linking suprasegmental
structure to segmental organisation.
In prosody-specific evaluation, Latorre et al. [14] study
evaluation methodology for TTS intonation and show that the
choice of F0 measurement design materially affects how into-
nation quality is judged. Kenter et al. [15] demonstrate that
introducing prosodic variation into TTS output improves per-
ceived naturalness. Per-phoneme F0 modeling has been applied
to improve pitch accuracy, while prosodic naturalness can be
enhanced through masked autoencoder pre-training [16, 17, 18].
These studies target local pitch accuracy but do not examine
whether such improvements preserve global contour organisa-
tion across the utterance, the cross-timescale relationship we
specifically address.
At the segmental level, vowel space area is an established
marker of articulatory precision [19, 20, 21], applied to TTS
fine-tuning and cross-lingual accent assessment [10, 11]. Lind-
blom’s H&H theory [22] attributes reduced vowel peripherality
to articulatory undershoot under low communicative demand.
Locus equations [12] quantify consonant–vowel coarticulation
by regressing F2 at vowel onset on F2 at vowel midpoint by
consonant place; human alveolar slopes fall below 1, reflecting
strong place-conditioned F2 adjustment [13]. This framework
has not previously been applied to neural TTS.

3.2. Prosodic features and LASSO analysis
F0 is extracted with Parselmouth/Praat [29] (75–500 Hz;
10 ms hop). We compute 21 utterance-level features in four
groups:F0 dynamics(range and SD in semitones, linear
slope, quadratic curvature);F0 micro-variability(mean and
SD of frame-to-frame|∆F0|, smoothness ratio, jitter, shim-
mer, voiced-segment duration CV);contour shape(rising ratio,
pitch inflection, direction reversals per voiced frame, and
longest monotonic F0-run ratio); andtemporal/intensity(voiced
ratio, speaking rate, voiced-segment mean, final F0 drop and
velocity, intensity range, SD, slope). Pooled human-vs-TTS
comparisons use Mann–WhitneyUwith rank-biserialr= 1−
2U/(n 1n2)and Cohen’sd(estimating the average human–TTS
difference across architectures); per-system tests use paired
Wilcoxon on matched clips to isolate architecture-specific de-
viations. All 21 comparisons are Benjamini–Hochberg cor-
rected [30]. Because the 21 prosodic features are mutually
correlated, per-feature effect sizes cannot identify which carry
independentdiscriminative signal. To address this, we fit a
LASSO logistic regression [31] with L1 regularisation and
nested 5-fold cross-validation: the inner loop selects C, and the
outer loop estimates AUC.
3.3. V owel-space and coarticulation measures
Segmental analyses target two complementary properties:
vowel-space dispersion, indexing acoustic target realization,
and place-conditioned F2 movement, indexing consonant–
vowel coarticulation. MFA [32] boundaries from the human
recordings are transferred to the matching TTS clips by clip ID.
We manually audited 150 matched human–TTS utterance pairs
and found that boundary-transfer discrepancies rarely affected
the vowel-internal regions from which formants were sampled.
Residual alignment error is further constrained by three checks:
formants are sampled at vowel-internal time points rather than
boundaries, pooled timing measures show no reliable human–
TTS displacement, and token retention rates of 85–92% show
no systematic variation by vowel category. Together, these
checks make it unlikely that the vowel-space and F2 trajectory
patterns are driven by alignment artefacts.

5. Discussion
5.1. RQ1: Why does the prosodic dissociation arise?
In natural speech, prosodic structure operates hierarchically.
Prominence peaks rise sharply, declination arcs fall gradually,
and boundary tones delimit phrases. This organisation both
produces large global F0 excursions and suppresses random
local voicing perturbations [33]. The elevatedpitch in-
flectionsin TTS reflect perturbations that superordinate
structure would normally suppress; the compressed range and
SD reflect the absence of the purposeful movements that such
structure produces. The two effects may reflect a common lim-
itation: pitch events are generated at the frame level without
sufficient long-range coordination to integrate them into coher-
ent contours.
This pattern persists across all four architectures at the
prosodic level, suggesting it reflects a structural consequence of
per-utterance training objectives rather than any particular gen-
eration mechanism. Listeners are sensitive to F0 variance devia-

5.2. RQ2: Why does vowel space collapse and coarticula-
tion weaken?
The observed vowel space reduction (70–91%) substantially
exceeds the second-language accent effect reported for cross-
lingual TTS [11]. This magnitude suggests that the deficit is un-
likely to arise from cross-lingual generalisation difficulty alone.
Rather, it points to a structural limitation of text-to-spectrogram
training, which does not explicitly enforce vowel peripheral-
ity. The per-vowel biases are consistent with articulatory un-
dershoot: peripheral vowels converge toward the centre of the
acoustic space, suggesting that the model does not fully realise
the extreme articulatory configurations required for high-front
/i/ or high-back /u/. Locus equation analysis provides an addi-
tional kinematic perspective. In TTS speech, vowel F2 values
show weak conditioning on the F2 characteristics of preced-
ing consonants, indicating reduced place-conditioned coartic-
ulation at the token level.
Across architectures, the phonetic patterns differ in sys-
tematic ways. FastSpeech2 shows the smallest prosodic devi-
ations but the most severe vowel collapse (91% reduction). Its
duration predictor may stabilise timing, whereas parallel feed-
forward generation may provide a weaker pathway for conso-
nantal context to shape adjacent vowel formants. Glow-TTS
exhibits the largest pitch inflection excess (d= +1.50) while
preserving relatively sharper spectral structure; its flow-based
sampling may introduce local F0 variability without relying on
the MSE mel-spectrogram objective often associated with for-
mant blurring in attention-based models. MixerTTS retains the
largest portion of the vowel space (30% of the human baseline)
and shows only moderate prosodic deviation, suggesting that
an MLP-Mixer backbone may better maintain spectral enve-
lope structure. Tacotron2-DDC displays the strongest global F0
compression (d=−0.85for F0 range), consistent with over-
smoothing under autoregressive attention with the same MSE
objective. These patterns suggest that prosodic robustness can
coexist with segmental degradation, and vice versa, although
the architectural interpretation remains provisional and requires
validation under controlled vocoder and training conditions.
5.3. Implications for evaluation and future work
To assess the relationship between prosodic and segmental de-
viations, we computed Spearman rank correlations between F0
range (the primary global prosodic measure) and vowel reduc-
tion magnitude (the primary segmental measure) for each sys-
tem and for human speech. Correlations were near zero across
all conditions, including human speech (ρ=−0.012; TTS:
ρ∈[−0.031,+0.028]; allp>0.05except a small Tacotron2-
DDC correlationp= 0.023). The absence of linear association
suggests that prosodic and segmental deviations vary indepen-
dently across utterances.
This finding has practical implications. Suprasegmental
and segmental measures capture different aspects of speech and
should be evaluated together, rather than treated as interchange-
able indicators of naturalness [4]. The relationship between
prosodic variation and vowel space expansion, suggested by
adaptive models of speech production [22], may only emerge
in more expressive or conversational speech. Examining this in
future synthesis work could provide valuable insights into how
prosody and articulation interact in naturalistic contexts.
The scope of this study is limited to read speech from a
single speaker in one language, synthesised by four two-stage
neural TTS systems. Because read speech constrains prosodic
and coarticulatory variability, the observed human–TTS gap
is likely conservative and would widen in more expressive or
conversational speech. Extending the analysis framework to
newer end-to-end architectures (e.g., VITS, StyleTTS2) is fu-
ture work. Since the prosodic dissociation appears to stem from
per-utterance training objectives, we expect it to persist.
6. Conclusion
We evaluated four neural TTS architectures over 13,100 paired
utterances. At the prosodic level, the systems exhibit a cross-
timescale dissociation. Global F0 variability is compressed
(d=−0.55), while local pitch reversals are elevated (d=
+0.82), the largest effect observed. In contrast, timing met-
rics do not differ significantly from the human reference, lo-
calising the deviation to F0 organisation rather than rhythmic
structure. At the segmental level, vowel spaces contract to 9–
30% of the human baseline, vowel targets shift toward the cen-
tre of the acoustic space, and locus analysis shows weakened
coarticulation, most consistently for alveolars. Together, these
findings indicate systematic centralisation and weakened inter-
segmental coordination. The prosodic and segmental deviations
are largely uncorrelated, suggesting that they reflect distinct di-
mensions of synthesis quality that MOS conflates. Across ar-
chitectures and utterances, pitch inflection rate, F0 standard de-
viation, and vowel triangle area consistently separate synthetic
from human speech, providing a scalable acoustic-phonetic
complement to MOS for future TTS evaluation.
