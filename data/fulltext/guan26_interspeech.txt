Abstract
Speech is produced in everyday contexts that involve different
body positions, yet body position is rarely modelled in acoustic
analyses. This study tests whether static body position (supine,
sitting, standing) affects vowel formants F1 and F2 in New
Zealand English using a within-speaker /hVd/ list (11 vowels)
from 9 speakers. Linear mixed-effects models with vowel, age,
and sex show small position-related differences, with the clear-
est contrasts involving supine relative to sitting and standing.
Position effects vary by vowel, age, and sex. V owel-specific
contrasts are significant for a small subset of vowels, most
clearly in high-front categories for F2. In this dataset, F1 po-
sition differences are clearer for female speakers, and F2 po-
sition differences are significant in the younger female condi-
tion. These findings suggest that body position should be doc-
umented when interpreting or combining vowel-formant mea-
surements across recording configurations.
Index Terms: speech acoustics; body position; vowel formants;
New Zealand English
1. Introduction
Speech production reflects coordinated articulatory and respi-
ratory control. Acoustic studies commonly characterise vowel
production using formant frequencies, particularly the first and
second formants (F1 and F2) [1, 2, 3]. Because the vocal tract
is shaped by multiple interacting subsystems (e.g. tongue, lips,
jaw, and larynx), small physiological adjustments can introduce
measurable acoustic variation [2, 3]. V owel-formant variation
is often studied in relation to between-speaker factors such as
age and sex [4, 5, 6], but body position during recording is
a less commonly modelled source of within-speaker variabil-
ity. This issue is particularly relevant in constrained contexts
such as MRI-based speech research, where speech is typically
recorded in the supine position, as well as in laboratory and field
settings where body position can vary across sessions [7, 8].
Previous studies suggest that position can influence vowel
acoustics, but reported effects are generally small and vary
across studies [7, 9, 10]. Across comparisons between upright
and supine positions, position-related differences are often re-
stricted to particular vowels rather than reflecting a uniform
shift of the entire vowel system [8, 9, 10]. Such variability has
been interpreted as incomplete and speaker-specific compensa-
tion for changes in gravitational orientation [7]. Results remain
difficult to compare across studies because they differ in lan-
guage variety, speech materials, and modelling choices.
Position-related effects are also plausibly conditioned by
vowel type and speaker factors. Articulatory adjustments
needed to maintain vowel targets under changes in gravity or
body configuration may differ across vowels. Consistent with
this, prior articulatory studies report posture-related differences
in vocal-tract shape and tongue posture across vowel produc-
tions [11, 12, 13, 14].
In addition, age and sex are associated with systematic dif-
ferences in vocal-tract anatomy and acoustic characteristics of
speech, which may influence how speakers accommodate the
supine position [15, 16, 17, 18]. These considerations motivate
evaluating position effects as varying across vowels and speaker
demographic factors, rather than assuming a general change that
applies to all these conditions. Accordingly, a key contribution
of the present study is to test whether position effects in New
Zealand English (NZE) are vowel-dependent, and whether they
can be influenced by age and sex conditions.
The present study addresses this gap with a controlled
within-speaker investigation of position-related variation in
NZE. Using an isolated-word /hVd/ set produced in three static
positions (supine, sitting, standing), we test whether body po-
sition affects F1/F2 and whether any effects vary across vowel
types and speaker demographic features. This design provides
a focused test of whether recording position introduces system-
atic offsets in vowel formant estimates in NZE, and it clarifies
where in the vowel system such offsets are most likely to appear.
It also has practical implications for cross-study comparability
when vowel-formant data are combined across setups that dif-
fer in body position (e.g. supine MRI vs. upright laboratory
recordings).
Therefore, the following research questions are addressed
here:
• Do F1 and F2 estimates show a general difference across
body positions in isolated /hVd/ words?
• Are position-related effects distributed uniformly across
vowels, or are they restricted to specific vowel types?
• Do position-related patterns differ by speaker factors (age
and sex conditions) in this dataset?
To address these questions, we analysed the hVd data using
a matched modelling approach and evaluated position effects in
terms of their direction and magnitude, their distribution across
vowel types, and their variation by age and sex.
2. Methods
2.1. Participants and speech materials
We analysed recordings from 9 native NZE speakers (5 male, 4
female) spanning two age bands (20–25 years,n= 5; 45–50
years,n= 4). Speakers were grouped by age band and sex
for analysis. Recordings were collected previously as part of
a body-position recording project and re-analysed here. Each

speaker completed the same materials in three static position
conditions (sitting, standing, supine).
Recordings were made inside a closed WhisperRoom SE
2000 Series sound-isolation enclosure. A Shure SM58 micro-
phone was positioned at approximately 20 cm from the mouth;
placement was adjusted across position conditions to maintain
this distance. In the supine condition, the microphone was repo-
sitioned relative to the speaker’s face and oriented toward the
mouth to preserve the same mouth-to-microphone distance. Sit-
ting and standing were produced in a naturally upright posi-
tion with both feet on the ground, and the supine condition was
recorded while speakers lay on the floor.
The speech material consisted of an isolated-word hVd cor-
pus designed to provide broad NZE vowel coverage while con-
trolling segmental context. Materials were presented in five list
orders. Each order comprised the same 11 hVd words arranged
in a different sequence. All 11 target words were produced in
each position; two words were repeated within each list, yield-
ing 13 tokens per list. Presenting multiple list orders reduced
order effects while keeping the phonetic frame constant across
positions.
The word set covered 11 vowels (/i:/, /I/, /e/, /æ/, /3:/, /u:/,
/U/, /o:/, /O/, /5/, /5:/), corresponding to the vowel categories
examined in previous acoustic work on New Zealand English
[19]. This provided broader vowel coverage than studies using
a small set of representative vowels, while allowing position to
be compared within a consistent hVd frame. All speakers pro-
duced the same materials in each position condition, enabling
within-speaker position comparisons. We focus on the hVd ma-
terials because they provide a consistent phonetic context and
balanced sampling across vowel types.
2.2. Annotation and acoustic feature extraction
Automatic word alignment was performed using WebMAUS
Basic, selecting the New Zealand English (NZE) language
model, to generate TextGrid files [20, 21]. Alignments were in-
spected and, where needed, segment boundaries were manually
corrected using waveform and spectrogram cues in Praat. For
each vowel token, F1 and F2 were measured at a manually anno-
tated target point within the vowel interval. Target points were
placed in the most stable portion of the vowel to minimise on-
set/offset transitions and strong coarticulatory influence; when
within-token movement was evident, the target was placed in
the region of greatest stability rather than at a fixed proportional
time point.
Formant tracks were generated using the ASSP tracker
(forest) [22], and then manually reviewed and corrected in
EMU-R by inspecting candidate tracks and refitting or rese-
lecting tracks when required [23]. Track selection was guided
by wideband spectrogram cues, plausible formant spacing, and
smooth trajectories. Tokens with unresolved tracking errors af-
ter manual review were excluded; no additional filtering criteria
were applied. All subsequent analyses used token-level mea-
surements.
2.3. Vowel-space visualisation
We provide a descriptive Bark-scaled vowel-space plot for the
hVd task (Figure 1) by plotting mean F1 against mean F2 for the
11 vowels in each position. V owel means were computed per
speaker and then averaged across speakers to obtain position-
level vowel means, which are connected by polygons. Points
show each position’s centre of gravity (CoG), defined as the
mean F1 and F2 across the 11 position-level vowel means.
2.4. Statistical analysis
Formant values were converted from Hz to Bark prior to mod-
elling. Linear mixed-effects models were fitted separately for
F1 (Bark) and F2 (Bark), with position (supine, sitting, stand-
ing) as the predictor of interest. Fixed effects included V owel,
Age, and Sex, and Speaker was included as a random intercept
to account for repeated measurements within individuals.
Statistical analyses were conducted in R [24]. We started
from a full model including position interactions with vowel,
age, and sex up to the four-way term position×vowel×age×
sex. To keep the fixed-effect structure manageable and inter-
pretable, we simplified the position-related interaction terms via
backward elimination withstep()(lmerTest; [25]). Given the
limited speaker sample, this helped avoid an unnecessarily com-
plex interaction structure.
We assessed interaction terms using likelihood-ratio tests
comparing nested models. The reduced model was then refitted
for estimation and inference. Both models included a random
intercept for speaker. As the reduced models include interaction
terms involving position, pairwise position contrasts were eval-
uated within the relevant factors rather than reporting one set of
contrasts averaged across all vowels and speakers.
To characterise the direction and magnitude of position
differences, we computed estimated marginal means (EMMs)
and tested the three pairwise position contrasts (supine–sitting,
supine–standing, sitting–standing). We also computed vowel-
specific position contrasts (the same three position comparisons
within each vowel) to identify which vowel types show dif-
ferences. For all EMM-based contrasts,p-values were Tukey-
adjusted across the three position comparisons within each con-
dition, and results are reported separately for each formant.
Throughout, we usep<0.05as the significance threshold.

3.4. Discussion
In this dataset, body position is associated with small differ-
ences in vowel formants. The clearest contrasts involve the
supine condition compared with sitting and standing, while
differences between the two upright positions are generally
smaller. This is consistent with Figure 1, where the position
polygons largely overlap and the CoG points show only a mod-
est displacement.
For F1, position differences vary by vowel type and by sex
(Table 1). In the vowel-specific comparisons, significant con-
trasts are confined to the supine–sitting comparison and occur
for /I/, /i:/, and /u:/ (Table 4). The sex-conditioned contrasts
show clear F1 differences for female speakers, while the male
contrasts are not statistically significant in this dataset (Table 2).
However, given the small number of speakers and the observed
between-speaker variation, this pattern should be treated as a
description of this dataset rather than as a general conclusion
that F1 position effects differ by sex.
For F2, position differences vary across vowel categories
and by age and sex (Table 1). In the vowel-specific compar-
isons, significant contrasts occur in the supine–standing com-
parison and are concentrated in the high-front region, with /I/
and /i:/ showing the clearest differences; an additional signifi-
cant contrast occurs for /5:/ (Table 4). In the age×sex contrasts,
statistically significant F2 position differences are observed in
the younger female condition (Table 3).
Although each age–sex condition includes few speakers,
statistically significant position contrasts are clearest for female
speakers in this dataset (F1: Table 2; F2: Table 3). This high-
lights that age and sex are important to consider when evaluat-
ing position effects, which is why the age×sex contrasts (Ta-
ble 3) are informative in addition to the vowel-specific results
(Table 4). Given the small and uneven speaker sample and the
within-vowel Tukey adjustment, these patterns should be inter-
preted cautiously [7].
The vowel-specific results are consistent with prior stud-
ies suggesting posture effects are vowel-dependent rather than
uniform across vowel types [7, 9, 10]. This pattern matches
previous work suggesting that upright–supine differences are
typically small in magnitude and may vary across speakers,
with only limited vowel-level effects emerging reliably [7, 9].
The clearest vowel-level F2 differences occur in high-front cat-
egories, where higher F2 in supine is consistent with a more
front tongue configuration. One possibility is that some speak-
ers maintain front-vowel targets differently when lying down,
yielding measurable F2 differences for these vowels. More gen-
erally, posture-related differences in vocal-tract configuration
can shift vowel formants [8]. However, the direction of F2 shifts
is not consistent across studies; for example, a previous study
reports F2-lowering in supine for some vowels [26]. Such dis-
crepancies may relate to differences in speaker samples, vowel
sets, and measurement choices, as well as between-speaker vari-
ation [7].
These results suggest that body position should be recorded
and reported along with other recording conditions when com-
paring or combining vowel-formant datasets. Since position
differences vary by vowel, age, and sex, it can be useful to
examine key vowels and the age×sex contrasts. This is es-
pecially relevant when supine recordings are unavoidable (e.g.
MRI-based studies) and formant values are compared with up-
right laboratory recordings [7, 8].
4. Conclusion
This study examined whether static body position (supine, sit-
ting, standing) affects vowel formants in New Zealand English
using isolated /hVd/ words. Position-related differences are
small in this dataset, with the most evident contrasts involving
supine relative to sitting and standing. Position effects are not
uniform across the vowel inventory and are statistically signifi-
cant for a small subset of vowels, particularly in high-front cate-
gories for F2. In this dataset, sex-specific contrasts show clearer
