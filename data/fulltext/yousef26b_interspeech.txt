Abstract 
Brief clinic voice disorder assessments miss daily voice use. As 
an Interspeech 2026 data challenge (NeckVibe), we released a 
large-scale dataset for vocal hyperfunction (VH) detection, with 
customized ambulatory hand-crafted voice features. The dataset 
was recorded during one week of daily activities using a 
smartphone system that captured neck skin-surface vibration 
via an accelerometer—including 582 individuals with VH and 
healthy controls contributing 46,400 monitored hours. For 
phonotraumatic VH detection, all teams exceeded baseline 
(AUC = 0.82), with the best test AUC = 0.93. 
Nonphonotraumatic VH detection was harder: four teams 
exceeded baseline (AUC = 0.78), with the best AUC = 0.86. 
Top methods used within-day variability, nonlinear models 
(XGBoost/CatBoost), speech–singing cues, and feature-to-
feature ratios. The challenge yielded new insight s into 
ambulatory VH monitoring for real-world assessment and 
personalized voice management.

1. Introduction 
Vocal hyperfunction (VH)—characterized by excessive or 
unbalanced laryngeal muscle activity during phonation—is a 
leading cause of voice disorders [1, 2]. VH occurs in two main 
forms: phonotraumatic VH (PVH) and nonphonotraumatic VH 
(NPVH). PVH involves vocal fold tissue trauma, often resulting 
in benign lesions such as nodules or polyps [3-5]. NPVH is 
marked by chronic vocal fatigue and degraded voice quality, 
but without visible signs of structural vocal fold injury [6, 7]. 
Despite its prevalence, the etiology and persistence of VH 
remain poorly understood [1]. While daily voice use is believed 
to play a key role  [8-10], the specific behavioral and  
communicative factors contributing to VH are not yet well 
defined [1]. This is largely because clinic-based recordings and 
laboratory conditions capture only brief snapshots of voice use, 
which often fail to represent how speakers behave in real 
communicative and environmental contexts [11-14]. 
This clinical gap has driven growing interest in ambulatory 
monitoring systems that individuals can wear during daily 
activities to capture and quantify real-world voice use [15-21]. 
Microphone-only ambulatory recordings are often corrupted by 
environmental noise and other nearby speakers, which makes 
reliable voice tracking difficult outside the lab [20]. To reduce 
this sensitivity, early ambulatory voice monitors used neck-
mounted contact microphones or accelerometers (ACCs) [13, 
15-21]. Rather than recording airborne sound, these sensors 
measure the vibrations of the neck skin driven mainly by vocal 
fold oscillations [11]. As a result, the signal primarily reflects 
the wearer’s own voicing and is less affected by background 
acoustic interference [15-21]. These systems have successfully 
enabled continuous tracking of voice measures in real-world 
settings [13, 22-26]. However, many ambulatory systems 
cannot store the full ACC waveform and instead log only 
frame-based averages of basic measures due to limited on -
device memory and power constraints [11, 16, 27, 28]. By 
discarding the raw signal, this summary-only approach can 
limit the derivation of richer ACC-based measures that more 
directly reflect vocal function relevant to vocal hyperfunction 
[22, 23, 29]. 
To address this limitation, prior work introduced an 
ambulatory voice monitoring system that enabled continuous, 
real-world tracking of vocal behavior [22-24, 26, 30]. This 
ecologically valid approach is especially valuable for VH 
because it can reveal maladaptive daily voice-use patterns—
including triggers, contexts, and temporal dynamics that brief 
self-reports or clinic examinations often miss—and help clarify 
when and how VH develops. Although these early results were 
promising for distinguishing VH from healthy controls, most 
prior models relied on relatively simple linear classifiers, rather 
than leveraging the temporal structure of the voice measures. 
This leaves an opportunity to develop more sophisticated, yet 
clinically interpretable, machine learning models that may 
capture nonlinear patient–control differences and better reflect 
the complex, real-world variability of voice use. 
Building on this foundation, we launched the NeckVibe 
Challenge at Interspeech 2026 to catalyze the development and 
benchmarking of advanced models that move beyond 
summary-only features by leveraging the temporal structure of 
week-long ambulatory recordings across thousands of hours. 
By releasing a unique large-scale dataset (46,400 hours) from 
individuals with VH and vocally healthy controls, we aimed to 
evaluate approaches that improve diagnostic accuracy while 
remaining generalizable and clinically interpretable, and 
ultimately advance understanding of how real-world voice use 
relates to VH pathophysiology. 
2. Challenge setup   
2.1. Data collection with a voice monitoring system 
The dataset comprised ambulatory, daily-life voice monitoring 
data from patients with PVH and NPVH, along with vocally 
healthy controls matched on sex, occupation, and age (within 
±5 years). Matched control group s were denoted PVH-C 
(matched controls for PVH) and NPVH-C (matched controls 
for NPVH). The corpus included 582 individuals (F = female, 
M = male): 213 PVH (F = 194, M = 19; age = 27.4 ± 10.4 years), 
169 PVH-C (F = 156, M = 13; age = 25.9 ± 8.6), 116 NPVH (F 
= 84, M = 32; age = 42.2 ± 14.4), and 84 NPVH-C (F = 61, M 
= 23; age = 39.6 ± 14.3), where ages are reported as mean ± SD.

The study was approved by the institutional review board 
at Mass General Brigham. Participants completed an initial visit 
at Massachusetts General Hospital or Boston Medical Center 
before receiving an ambulatory monitoring kit for daily-life 
recording. Written informed consent was obtained during this 
lab visit, and eligibility was confirmed, including vocal health 
and diagnostic status. The healthy group completed a laryngeal 
endoscopic examination to verify healthy vocal folds and was 
interviewed to confirm no history of voice disorders. Patients 
underwent a multi-step clinical evaluation to confirm diagnosis, 
including medical history, perceptual evaluation of voice 
quality, laryngeal stroboscopic imaging, and acoustic and 
aerodynamic assessments of vocal function [31]. 
During the in-lab visit, participants received the voice 
monitor kit, with instructions on proper wear, use, and device 
placement (Figure 1). The voice monitor device  is a 
smartphone-based system that enables continuous, real-world 
monitoring of voice [11]. It captures vocal characteristics 
through a lightweight neck-mounted uniaxial ACC (BU-27135, 
Knowles Electronics, Itasca, IL); the ACC is placed just below 
the thyroid prominence and taped with skin-safe adhesive to 
capture anterior neck-surface skin vibrations associated with 
phonation [1, 14]. The ACC connects directly to a smartphone 
platform via a wired cable that records and stores the raw 
vibration signal (11,025 Hz; 16 -bit resolution; high-pass 
filtered at 0.9 or 60 Hz, depending on hardware, to remove 
motion artifacts) [16]. After in-field data acquisition, the stored 
raw ACC signal was calibrated to units of cm/s² [32].  An 
additional in-field calibration was performed using an auxiliary 
microphone included in the kit (H1 Handy Recorder, Zoom 
Corporation, Tokyo, Japan). Before each monitored day (once 
per day), participants—while wearing the ACC—produced a 
prolonged /a/ that started loud and gradually decreased to soft 
phonation, holding the microphone at 15 cm from the lips. The 
synchronized ACC and microphone amplitudes were then used 
in a linear regression to estimate the equivalent sound pressure 
level (SPL) at 15 cm from the raw ACC signal magnitude.

2.2. Data processing and feature extraction 
Prior work has shown that skin-surface vibrations, captured by 
ACCs, carry physiologically meaningful information related to 
vocal fold vibration and can be used to extract features relevant 
for vocal health assessment. We computed a range of clinically 
interpretable vocal measures reflecting different aspects of 
phonation [24, 30]. We processed each monit oring day 
individually by first splitting the recording into 50 ms, non-
overlapping time frames. Using a voice detector algorithm, we 
automatically detected whether these frames contained voicing 
information. From each voiced frame, 14 voice measures were 
derived as time series features. Three feature categories were 
obtained: (1) SPL estimated from the ACC signal magnitude 
using a linear regression between the auxiliary microphone and 
ACC signal [32]; (2) five features derived directly from the 
ACC waveform including ACC magnitude, cepstral peak 
prominence (CPP), difference between the magnitudes of the 
first and second harmonics (H1–H2), spectral tilt, low-to-high 
spectral power ratio (L/H ratio); and (3) eight model-based 
features obtained via impedance-based inverse filtering to 
derive multiple high-bandwidth glottal airflow measures from 
the ACC signal [30, 33]:  IBIF H1–H2 (first–second harmonic 
magnitude difference), HRF (harmonic richness factor), MFDR 
(maximum flow declination rate), Flow (estimated glottal 
flow), SQ (speed quotient), OQ (open quotient), NAQ 
(normalized amplitude quotient), and CQ (closing quotient).  
We also provided frame-level timestamp metadata, which 
stored the time of day for each frame in MATLAB serial 
datenum format (date was de-identified). We included binary 
frame-level masks (0/1) that label each frame as voiced vs. 
unvoiced, voiced singing vs. voiced speech (non-singing) [25], 
and recording on vs. paused/stopped. All features were stored 
as time-series data in MATLAB (.mat) files. Files were named 
using the subject ID and de-identified monitoring date (e.g., 
NV128_YYYYMMDD.mat), where each .mat file corresponds 
to one full monitored day and contains the 14 frame -based 
features, frame timestamps, and the corresponding masks. 
2.3. Challenge setup and shared dataset 
The Interspeech 2026 NeckVibe Challenge provided 
participating teams with a  multi-day ambulatory voice 
monitoring dataset. After registration opened, teams accessed 
the dataset by completing a Data Transfer and Use Agreement 
reviewed and signed by an authorized institutional official. Data 
sharing was conducted under an approved Mass General 
Brigham Institutional Review Board protocol amendment 
permitting release of the de-identified dataset to participating 
institutions. The challenge comprised two binary classification 
tasks. Task 1 was to classify PVH as the positive class (label = 
1) and all other groups as the negative class (label = 0: NPVH, 
PVH-C, NPVH-C). Task 2 was to classify NPVH as the 
positive class (label = 1) and all other groups as the negative 
class (label = 0: PVH, PVH-C, NPVH-C).  
The released dataset contained 46,400 hours of ambulatory 
recording across all participants. On average, participants 
recorded 11.25 ± 1.63 hours per waking day over one week. The 
dataset was split into training (80%; 468 subjects) and held-out 
test (20%; 114 subjects) subsets. The split was stratified by 
group label to preserve class proportions, with all recordings 
from a given subject assigned to a single subset to prevent 
subject-level leakage. The provided training set included the 
time-series .mat feature files for each monitoring day. The IBIF 
features were not available for certain recordings (e.g., in-
person aerodynamic calibration was not possible during the 
pandemic). Participating teams in the challenge were instructed 
to verify the presence of IBIF features when loading each .mat 
file and to treat unavailable values as missing data. 
In addition, a CSV metadata file was provided as part of the 
training dataset with the following information: subject ID, date 
(de-identified recording day index preserving monitoring order 
without revealing calendar dates), biological sex, and group 
label (PVH, NPVH, PVH-C, and NPVH-C). Other participant 
metadata (e.g., age and occupation) were not included in the 
released dataset to avoid potential confounds and to keep 
analyses focused on the vocal measures, which were the 
primary targets of the challenge. Teams received the training 
dataset for model training, along with a comprehensive guide 
and feature dictionary describing the file contents.  
The held-out test set was released later for final model 
evaluation and contained the same feature file types, but 
without group labels; only subject IDs were provided. Teams 
submitted predictions for each task using CSV templates with 
three columns: subject ID, predicted positive-class probability 
(0–1), and predicted label (0/1). Although each subject had 
multiple monitoring days in the test set, teams were required to 
provide one final prediction per subject for each task. Teams 
were ranked by area under the receiver operating characteristic

curve (AUC); we additionally computed accuracy (Acc), 
sensitivity (Sens), specificity (Spec), and F1-score (F1). 
3. Challenge results  
Following the challenge announcement, 19 teams registered, 9 
completed the data-access requirements, and 6 delivered final 
submissions for each classification task. The results summarize 
performance across teams. 
3.1. Task 1: PVH detection 
All teams achieved strong test set classification performance 
(Table 1), with AUC = 0.87–0.93, exceeding the baseline (AUC 
= 0.82) [22, 30]. Team SR achieved the highest AUC (0.93), 
followed closely by Team DD (0.92); both teams had identical 
operating-point performance (Acc. 84%, Sens. 79%, Spec. 
88%, F1 79%). Team SM achieved the highest sensitivity 
(81%) with lower specificity (81%), indicating a lower decision 
threshold. In contrast, Team VA prioritized specificity (94%) at 
the expense of sensitivity (62%), reflecting a more conservative 
(higher) threshold. Even the lowest-performing submission 
exceeded the baseline by approximately 0.05 AUC points.

We next highlight the main methodological choices of the 
top two performing teams. Team SR achieved the best PVH 
performance by preserving within-day structure. Rather than 
collapsing each day into a single subject-level summary, they 
split each monitoring day into 10 partially overlapping 
windows and computed five summary statistics (e.g., median 
and SD) for each voice feature per window. They trained a 
regularized XGBoost classifier on window-level samples with 
subject-level validation, and further improved robustness by 
averaging predicted probabilities from XGBoost and logistic 
regression before applying a fixed decision threshold. 
In contrast, Team DD did not use windowed features to 
capture within-day variation. Instead, they aggregated days into 
a single subject-level feature set, but engineered additional 
features that reflected temporal dynamics within subjects. For 
each voice feature, they first computed frame-to-frame change 
(delta, Δ) and change of change (ΔΔ), then summarized these 
sequences with statistics (e.g., mean/SD and 95th-percentile 
magnitude) to quantify within-day variability (e.g., SD of ΔΔ 
H1–H2). They also added ratio features, similar to a coefficient 
of variation (e.g., ΔSD/mean CPP), the reverse ratio linking 
feature value and variability (e.g., mean CPP/ΔSD), and frame-
level feature-to-feature ratios such as CPP/H1–H2. Day-level 
model probabilities were averaged to produce subject -level 
predictions. Using recursive feature elimination with cross-
validation, their validation AUC results showed that ratio 
features (0.89) outperformed basic dynamic (0.87) and static 
(0.85) features in comparative analyses. After testing multiple 
classifiers, logistic regression performed best, outperforming 
more complex models such as XGBoost. 
3.2. Task 2: NPVH detection 
For Task 2 (NPVH detection), Table 2 shows that performance 
was generally lower and more variable than for PVH detection, 
with a wider range of AUC values (0.58–0.86) and a shift in 
team ranking. Four teams exceeded the baseline (AUC = 0.78). 
Teams SM and VA achieved the highest AUC (0.86), but with 
different operating points: Team SM had the most balanced 
sensitivity/specificity trade-off, with the highest sensitivity 
(78%) and specificity of 83.5%, whereas Team VA showed 
higher specificity (89%) with substantially lower sensitivity 
(48%). Team NK achieved the highest accuracy (87%) and 
specificity (97%) but at the cost of sensitivity (48%), suggesting 
a bias toward the negative class. The last two teams (BB, DD) 
showed reduced discriminative power for detecting NPVH, 
with AUC values below the baseline by >0.14. Team DD, in 
particular, exhibited very low sensitivity (4%) despite high 
specificity (98%), capturing very few NPVH cases correctly.

The top-ranked Team SM used a hybrid classical machine 
learning and deep learning approach, focusing on voiced 
portions of the data (excluding singing and silence frames). 
They computed day-level summary statistics (e.g., means and 
percentiles) and complexity metrics (entropy, Gini), then 
aggregated these into subject-level features (e.g., mean, min, 
max across days) to train a CatBoost classifier; similar to Team 
DD, they also added feature-to-feature ratios. In parallel, they 
trained a Multiple Instance Learning (MIL) model representing 
each subject with up to 24 windows, where each window is a 
multi-channel, frame-level feature matrix; window embeddings 
were combined via attention pooling to yield a subject-level 
prediction. The two branches were fused using logistic  
regression stacking. Training used Stratified Group K -Fold 
with subject ID grouping to prevent leakage, and final labels 
used a 0.5 probability threshold. In validation, CatBoost 
outperformed MIL (AUC 0.751 vs. 0.717), and stacking 
provided only a modest gain (AUC 0.760). 
The second-ranked Team VA used an interpretable, 
context-aware pipeline by computing feature summaries 
separately within five contexts (all frames, voiced only, 
unvoiced only, speech only, singing only). Within each context, 
they extracted daily summary statistics—both standard (e.g., 
mean, mode, SD) and robust (e.g., Bowley’s/non-parametric 
skewness, excess/Crow’s kurtosis, bimodality coefficient; for 
example, CPP kurtosis within singing) distributional metrics. A 
predictive feature subset was then selected via a greedy additive 
search. They trained a gated, L1-regularized logistic-regression 
model to jointly predict PVH and NPVH (multi-task) using 
subject-level, stratified cross-validation, with two parallel 
streams (magnitude vs distributional features ) whose 
predictions were combined. Final subject-level probabilities 
were obtained by averaging across monitoring days and taking 
the ensemble mean across 10 fold-specific models. 
The third-ranked Team NK observed better performance 
with CNN–MIL than XGBoost/LightGBM, likely because they 
trained directly on 10-s window time series across the full 
recording duration to capture temporal patterns. Their tree -
based pipeline used three-level aggregation (frames to 10-s 
windows; windows to day-level distributional summaries plus 
duration and voicing percent; days to subject-level summaries 
plus number of days), yielding 1237 features; however, AUC 
performance (0.67) still did not match that of CNN–MIL (0.77). 
4. Discussion and conclusions 
Prior baseline work showed that simple classifiers using ACC-
derived voice features—summarized with standard statistics 
(e.g., mean/median, SD/interquartile range, 5th/9 5th 
percentiles, and skewness/kurtosis)—achieved moderate 
discrimination of PVH versus controls (AUC = 0.81–0.82) and 
NPVH versus controls (0.78), with similar performance 
reported in larger cohorts [22, 23, 30, 34]. Building on prior 
work, this challenge highlighted methods that further improved 
baseline AUC values. However, because the challenge tasks 
(PVH vs. non-PVH; NPVH vs. non-NPVH) differed from prior 
baseline studies (PVH vs. PVH-C; NPVH vs. NPVH-C), direct 
comparisons should be interpreted cautiously. 
PVH detection can be improved by capturing within-day 
variability. The top approaches highlighted two strategies: 
training on repeated window-level samples within each day to 
capture time-of-day patterns (e.g., a feature that is low in some 
periods but not others); or integrating features that explicitly 
quantified short-term frame-to-frame dynamics (e.g., 
Δ/ΔΔ/slope) beyond static summaries.  Coupling features 
through ratios (e.g., CPP/spectral tilt or CPP/H1–H2) can boost 
PVH detection by highlighting trade-offs between periodicity 
and spectral shape that better reflect changes in phonatory 
control than either feature alone. This idea aligns with prior 
work such as the Daily Phonotrauma Index (ACC -level 
skewness relative to H1–H2 variability) and has shown promise 
for PVH detection [35], supporting the value of relationship-
based features over single metrics.
