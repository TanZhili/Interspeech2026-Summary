How Language-Independent Are Emotional Attributes?
A Study on Training Data Scaling and Cross-Lingual Generalization
D´aniel Halmai ID 1, G´abor Gosztolya ID 1,2
1 Institute of Informatics, University of Szeged, Szeged, Hungary
2 HUN-REN–SZTE Research Group on Artificial Intelligence, Szeged, Hungary
halmaid@inf.u-szeged.hu
Abstract
Current state-of-the-art Speech Emotion Recognition (SER)
systems rely on lots of training data, which is available only
for a few languages such as English. For low-resource lan-
guages, using a cross-lingual model or adapting it to the tar-
get language might be an alternative to training a mono-lingual
model from scratch. However, it is not clear what constitutes a
‘low-resource’, i.e. where model adaptation loses its edge over
the mono-lingual approach. We investigate this topic by train-
ing and adapting several SER models on English and Mandarin
data, focusing on the emotional attributes of arousal, valence
and dominance. Our results show that, for arousal, model adap-
tation is beneficial even with two hours of data, while for va-
lence 10-20 hours of adaptation material is required to match the
performance of a mono-lingual model trained with 100 hours of
data. This indicates that task-specific model adaptation may be
an effective approach in low-resource speech emotion recogni-
tion problems.
Index Terms: Speech emotion recognition, WavLM, MSP-
Podcast, BIIC Podcast
1. Introduction
Speech is not simply a means of communication: our emotions
are constantly present in it, whether consciously or implicitly.
The use of words, the pace of speech, the tone of voice, and
other paralinguistic cues reveal the inner state of the speaker,
allowing to automatically deduce the emotions from the speech
signal (i.e. Speech Emotion Recognition, SER [1, 2]). An au-
tomatic emotion recognition system has many potential appli-
cations, such as in education [3], in the pathological field [4]
and in general human-machine interaction [5, 6]. This field has
been intensively studied for well over a decade [7], while in the
recent years its development was accelerated by the use of deep
neural networks and self-supervised learning [8].
An obvious limit in automatic speech emotion recognition
is the amount of training data available. Although in the past
few years large corpora have became available (most notably,
MSP-Podcast [9]), this is not the case with most scenarios, e.g.
for most languages [10, 11]. To build a SER system with lim-
ited resources, there are three straightforward options. First,
one can train a model on the data available in the target lan-
guage. Second, one can rely on a model trained on some large
corpus, even if it is of a different language, and hope that this
cross-lingual (or even cross-cultural) setup will not hinder the
performance too much on the target language. The third option
is to use both kinds of data combined in some way – for exam-
ple by taking the model trained on the ‘resourceful’ language
and further fine-tuning it on the target language (i.e. domain
adaptation [12]). Generally, the third option can be expected to
lead to the best performance, but it is unclear how much training
data will be required in the target language to effectively adapt
the original model. Furthermore, the different models might de-
liver uneven performance depending on the emotional content
of the actual speech utterance; for instance, the cross-lingual
performance might differ markedly for different emotional cat-
egories or attributes (e.g. for arousal and valence) since emotion
expressions may differ from one language to another [13].
To answer these questions, in this study we trained emo-
tion recognition models on two quite different languages: En-
glish and Taiwanese Mandarin. We measured the performance
of the trained models on the Mandarin corpus in each case, test-
ing several training setups. As straightforward solutions, we
trained models on only one language, using roughly 100 hours
of data in both cases (mirroring cross-lingual and intra-lingual,
but ‘resourceful’ scenarios). To simulate low-resource settings,
we selected several smaller subsets of the Mandarin dataset, and
trained SER models both from scratch and by fine-tuning the
networks originally trained on all 100 hours of the English data.
We focused on the emotion dimensions; instead of summarizing
model performance by one single metric value, we examined
the arousal, valence and dominance attributes one by one.
2. Related Work
Most SER studies, even if investigate multiple languages, do not
present cross-corpus experiments, just report the performance
of an approach on several languages [14, 15, 16, 17]. One of
the reasons for this might be that it is hard to convert the emo-
tional categories into a shared set of labels. A few studies per-
formed that, though; for example, Ma et al. kept only four cat-
egories (angry, happy, neutral and sad) and measured the intra-
corpus and cross-corpus performance of several architectures
on 32 datasets in 14 languages [18]. Heracleous and Yoneyama
went even further by training a joint multi-lingual model for
Japanese, English and German [19]. Pastor et al. trained Hu-
BERT and WavLM models for the same four categorical emo-
tions on IEMOCAP extended with a few minutes from EmoDB
and RA VDESS. They found that including more in-corpus train-
ing data might drastically increase the performance [20].
If one investigates emotional attributes (such as arousal and
valence) instead of categorical class labels, performing cross-
corpus (and thus cross-lingual) experiments becomes easier.
(Although, to avoid scaling issues, it is standard to convert the
attributes to a binary set of low and high (or to the triplet of low,
neutral and high).) Eyben et al., back in 2010, evaluated several
subsets of a large hand-crafted attribute set on three German
and one English datasets both in an intra-corpus and in a cross-
corpus manner [21]. Later, Schuller et al. repeated a similar set
of experiments on six datasets, using both categorical labels and

The study which is the closest to our research question is
that of Neumann et al. [24]. They used the IEMOCAP (En-
glish) and Recola (French) corpora, focusing on the attributes of
arousal and valence (again, converted to binary). Besides train-
ing mono-lingual and multi-lingual models, they tested model
adaptation, i.e. fine-tuning a model on the target language.
Their results are mixed, though: regarding arousal, the perfor-
mance of the adapted model roughly matched that of the multi-
lingual DNN, but for valence most results were around the level
of random guessing.
In general, all these works used small datasets (even IEMO-
CAP is only around 12 hours), and very few experimented with
cross-lingual model adaptation. When investigating attributes
like arousal and valence, they reduced the task to a binary classi-
fication one, discarding important details in model performance.
Predictions of valence were of low quality, perhaps since most
of the works preceded the transformer era. Finally, the few ex-
periments involving model adaptation were either not interested
in the amount of adaptation data [24], or they used only a few
minutes [20]. In contrast, here we (i) rely on a current back-
bone with a transformer architecture (i.e. WavLM), (ii) utilize
relatively large amounts of training data and several variations
of adaptation data, and (iii) adapt the deep learning models in a
cross-lingual manner (while also training them from scratch).
3. Data
In our work, we used the BIIC-Podcast corpus [25] and the
MSP-Podcast corpus [9]. Although these datasets differ in their
language (Taiwanese Mandarin and English, respectively) and
were created by different research teams, these datasets are in-
tentionally very similar. Both datasets include naturalistic spon-
taneous recordings, use continuously expanding, non-static data
collection, and are annotated following the same protocol.
The audio clips of both datasets are annotated with emo-
tional attributes and with a categorical emotion. The emotional
attributes are arousal, valence and dominance, each annotated
on a 1-7 Likert-type scale. The categorical emotion can be
anger, contempt, disgust, fear, happiness, neutral, sadness, sur-
prise and other. (Clips where the annotators could not reach a
clear majority were labeled as a tenth category: mixed.) In this
paper, we focus on the emotional attributes.
3.1. MSP-Podcast
To the best of our knowledge, MSP-Podcast is the largest speech
emotion dataset today. It consists of freely available podcast
recordings in English. Each audio clip was annotated by at least
five workers and the final score was determined by the average
value. The authors also published individual responses. We
used v1.11 of the corpus, and only those clips which had a cate-
gorical emotion label among the eight emotions (i.e. discarded
utterances with the “Other” and “Mixed” labels), leaving cca.
65k recordings with a total duration of 104 hours.
3.2. BIIC-PodCast
The BIIC-Podcast corpus is a Taiwanese Mandarin dataset.
Similar to the MSP-Podcast, the recordings were collected from
publicly available online sources that provide creative commons
(CC) licenses under CC-BY or CC-BY-SA; however, the au-
thors collected not only podcasts, but also monologues, conver-
sations and other audio materials. Each clip was annotated by
3 to 5 annotators (the final score was determined by the aver-
age value); however, the individual votes are not available in
the corpus. In this corpus, the number of audio records with
“Other” and “Mixed” label is negligible. It contains 70k audio
recordings, of which 81% is the training set, with 102 hours.

4. Methods
4.1. WavLM
To create our models, we chose WavLM as the backbone model.
Three types of WavLM model configurations are available:
WavLM Base, WavLM Base+ and WavLM Large [26]. The
main differences between the models are the number of param-
eters, the number of transformer layers and the amount of data
used during pre-training. We used the WavLM large 1 due to
its performance, and because it is widespreadly used for emo-
tion recognition [27, 28, 29]. This model has 316M parameters,
24 transformer layers, and it was pre-trained by using over 94k
hours of training data.
4.2. Models
To examine the effect of limited training data, we selected sub-
sets of the BIIC-Podcast corpus of roughly 1, 2, 5, 10, and 20
hours, and trained separate models on each subset from scratch.
In the following, these models are referred to as the ‘Direct’
models. We also trained a model on the whole training data
(102h) of the BIIC-Podcast corpus (‘Direct-100h’).
To explore language independence, we first trained a
WavLM Large model on the MSP-Podcast corpus. This English
SER model was then fine-tuned on the BIIC-Podcast subsets to
assess the effect of cross-lingual adaptation; these models are
referred to as ’Transfer’ models. To follow the naming conven-
tion, the basic English model will be denoted as ‘Transfer-0h’.
5. Experimental Setup
To create our models, we used the SpeechBrain framework,
which is an open-source, all-in-one toolkit that facilitates re-
search and development in speech processing [30].

Concordance Correlation Coefficient (CCC), both evaluated on
each emotional attribute. To reduce the effect of randomness,
we trained each model using 5 different random seeds, and we
report the mean metric scores obtained. This setup also allows
us to compare the significance of the differences between model
performances; we used the Mann-WhitneyUtest [31] (also
known as the Wilcoxon rank-sum test).
6. Results
Fig. 1 shows the Pearson’s correlation and the CCC results on
the development set by emotional attributes. In the 0h column,
we can observe the pure performance of the English model on
the Taiwanese dataset, while the 100h column corresponds to
the model trained only on the BIIC-Podcast corpus. Compar-
ing these two monolingual models, the performance difference
is roughly the same for the three attributes and the two met-
rics (around 0.05. . .0.1 absolute). The exception is CCC for
the dominance attribute, where the two models produce quite
similar scores (0.293 vs. 0.307), probably due to the relatively
low CCC value of the Mandarin-trained model compared to the
Pearson’s Correlation value (i.e. 0.307 vs. 0.412). It is also
apparent that the values measured for valence and dominance
are a lot worse than for arousal, even for the within-language
model (i.e. Direct-100h), which indicates that these attributes
are harder to predict for Mandarin. (This is not the case, for
example, for English [32].)
Examining the cases with limited training data, for the
arousalattribute the Transfer models led to better results than
the Direct models in all cases, and the difference is statisti-
cally significant (i.e.p <0.05) for any amount of in-language
training data and for both metrics. With 10 hours of Mandarin
data used for model adaptation (i.e. ‘Transfer-10h’), we can
even surpass the performance of the Direct-100h model. How-
ever, neither this nor any other Transfer model led to a statisti-
cally significant improvement (p >0.05 in all cases). Yet this
means that, at least on the development set, even one hour of in-
language (i.e. Taiwanese Mandarin) data is enough to adapt a
DNN model previously trained for English to attain the perfor-
mance of a model trained with 100 hours of Mandarin speech.
In contrast, forvalenceanddominanceno ‘Transfer’
model was able to surpass the mono-lingual case with 100 hours
of data (i.e. ‘Direct-100h’); on the contrary, all scores were sig-
nificantly worse (with the exception of CCC with 2, 10 and 20
hours used for adaptation). Model adaptation kept its edge over
training the models from scratch when the training data was at
most 2 hours for the valence attribute, though. This underlines
that arousal seems to be relatively language-independent, but
the other two attributes are really specific to the given language.
Figure 2 presents the Pearson correlation and CCC results
on the test set for the three emotional attributes. Overall, a sim-
ilar trend can be observed as on the development set; however,
the absolute scores are consistently lower. Model adaptation
leads to significantly better values than direct training in all six
cases with only 1 and 2 hours of adaptation data (p <0.01),
and this holds for some other cases as well (in all cases with the
arousal attribute when using Pearson’s correlation, for 5 hours
with dominance and Pearson’s, and for 5 and 10 hours for va-
lence with both evaluation metrics).
The superiority of model adaptation is clearly shown by
the fact that the performance of the within-language model (i.e.
‘Direct-100h’) could be achieved with significantly less train-
ing data, while sometimes it could even be surpassed. That is,
the ‘Transfer-10h’ model produced a significantly better Pear-
son’s score for arousal, while there was no difference in the
performance measured by CCC. For the valence scores, model

by using similar training material on a different language.
7. Conclusions and Discussion
In this study we investigated dimensional emotion recognition
in low-resource settings. To this end, we trained a WavLM
Large model on 100 hours of English data, and trained it fur-
ther with significantly smaller amounts of Mandarin recordings.
These adapted models were compared to DNNs trained from
scratch only on Mandarin speech.
We found model adaptation to be quite successful: for tiny
amounts of data (i.e. 1 and 2 hours) in the target language, it led
to statistically significant improvements over the mono-lingual
approach (using the same Mandarin data) for all three emotion
dimensions (i.e. arousal, valence and dominance), and kept its
edge for specific attributes (most notably, arousal and valence)
even when we used more Mandarin data. With only 2 hours of
Mandarin speech, the adapted model was on par with the mono-
lingual model trained on 100 hours in arousal and dominance
prediction, while with 10 hours it could even outperform it. For
valence, though, 20 hours of adaptation material was required
to catch up with the monolingual 100-hours model.
Of course, one might expect arousal prediction to be quite
easy to carry over to another language, while doing this for va-
lence is expected to be a lot harder. However, this is not what
we experienced: the prediction of valence (and dominance) was
harder than that of arousaleven for the within-language model.
In our opinion this might be due to the fact that mostly English
data was used to pre-train the WavLM Large model, although
this hypothesis is really hard to verify.
Regarding the limitations of our study, we performed the
experiments only for two, although very different languages.
Another limitation can be that we employed only one DNN ar-
chitecture, and investigated only the emotional attributes. In the
near future we plan to extend our tests with categorical emo-
tion recognition experiments, and with incorporating other lan-
guages as well, provided that we can obtain large enough cor-
pora which have annotations consistent to the MSP-Podcast and
BIIC-Podcast corpora.
