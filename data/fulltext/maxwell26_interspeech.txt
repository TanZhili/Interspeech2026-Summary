Abstract 
While automated speech technologies for pronunciation 
learning are increasingly used, most tools offer limited 
feedback and lack scientific grounding. Phonetic variability in 
ESL speech and world Englishes is often ignored. We 
developed a curated dataset t o support personalised feedback 
for Australian English pronunciation. Word -level forced 
alignment and manual annotation were applied to a 
demographically stratified subset of the CommonVoice corpus. 
Linguistically informed coding classes, such as vowels and tune 
types, were used to label “errors” (pronunciation differences 
from AusEng). This paper outlines the scientifically grounded 
annotation framework, presenting preliminary findings on error 
patterns across three accent groups: Hong Kong, South Asia, 
Indonesia. We also address challenges in using open data, 
including demographic imbalance. This work highlights 
collaboration betwee n phonetics and computer science and 
provides an open-access dataset with a transparent framework. 
Index Terms : Australian English, data curation, annotation 
framework, pronunciation, phonetic features, speech 
engineering, open data 
1. Introduction 
1.1. Background 
Over the past two decades, rapid developments have been made 
to integrate computer-assisted learning into English as a second 
language (ESL) classrooms, leading to increased popularity of 
AI-based learning tools . Recent research has shown that the 
multimodality of new digital technologies can  assist with 
creating tailored and individualised learning experiences,  and 
automated feedback on performance is especially useful for 
teaching spoken English [1]. AI-mediated pronunciation 
training systems have the capacity to  provide leaners with 
specific detail about their pronunciation, thus offering targeted 
support based on the learner’s first language (L1) background 
and specific difficulties  [2]. Further, ASR-based systems are 
considered particularly useful in providing real -time 
evaluation, vital in understanding one’s own pronunciation and 
its progress over time [3]. 
Despite these developments, automated technologies 
support only specific aspects of pronunciation  [1], with most 
tools offering limited feedback and not necessarily aligned with 
academic or professional communication demands of adult 
learners [4]. Existing tools also rarely address features beyond 
individual sounds, overlooking prosodic features, and failing to 
consider substantial phonetic variability in L1 English and ESL 
speech across world Englishes, including mainstream (e.g., 
Australian English  (AusEng, [5]) and contact varieties 
developed in multilingual settings (e.g., Indian English, [6]). It 
is well -accepted in the literature that prosody, encompassing 
intonation (melodic patterns across an utterance) , prominence 
and rhythm, is essential for structuring discourse and engaging 
listeners, yet it remains under-taught due to time constraints and 
limited teacher training  [7]. Prosodic features are crucial for 
effective communication, influencing turn-taking, information 
structure, persuasive argumentation,  emotional and attitudinal 
expressions and more . [8, p.1] reported “positive effects of 
prosody-oriented training on the production of segments, 
especially when training focuses on syllable structure and 
prosodic prominence (stress and accent) .” Recent research 
suggests that there is a strong interplay between segmental and 
prosodic features when native listeners rate speech for 
intelligibility, comprehensibility, and degree of perceived 
foreign accentedness, and some prosodic features are known to 
affect ratings more than others (e.g., [9]).  
Increased mobility and large numbers of international 
students enrolled in tertiary institutions world-wide also calls 
for more context- and domain -specific approaches to 
embedding pronunciation feedback into the curriculum . 
Countries such as the UK, Canada, Australia and New Zealand 
have become popular destinations for international students 
who would like to improve their English language skills [10]. 
The two top countries for student enrolments in Australia in 
2025 were China (23%) and India (17%) [11]. Developing 
spoken English language skills among international students is 
an integral part of any degree, impacting their engagement and 
learning experience at the university  as well as  their 
employability upon graduating  [12]. However, apart from 
general speaking and oral presentation skills, there is a gap in 
ESL pronunciation pedagogy and, as a result, a lack of 
accessible tools tailored to advanced learners in the Australian 
context. This highlights the need for closer collaboration across 
practitioners and the speech community, including phoneticians 
and engineers. 
Critically, the kinds of modelling required for effective AI-
based pronunciation feedback is often sweeping and not based 
on scientific or pedagogical principles [13]. A recent study by 
[14] showed that one of the most highly rated commercially 
available AI pronunciation feedback tools penalised advanced 
learners for phonetic features that are common in AusEng (e.g., 
schwa onglides and creaky voice). As noted by [15], “[c]losing 
sociophonetic gaps earlier in the pipeline improves everything 
downstream—accuracy, equity, and user trust.”  
1.2. Dataset for model training and evaluation 
The quality of any pronunciation assessment system is 
fundamentally constrained by the data on which it is trained and 
evaluated. Yet recent work has relied on datasets that are often 
limited in linguistic diversity [16]. SpeechOcean762, for 
instance, contains only Mandarin L1 speakers, while EpaDB 
includes only Argentine Spanish speakers [17]. Without 
datasets representative of diverse learner populations, 
downstream models risk encoding narrow notions of  “correct” 
pronunciation. Beyond linguistic coverage, a persistent barrier 
is the limited availability of open, well -documented corpora. 
Many datasets are proprietary or restricted, and those that are 
publicly available often lack the detailed metadata, especially 
phonetic annotations needed for meaningful analysis of learner 
speech [18]. Open -access initiatives , such as Mozilla ’s 
CommonVoice [19], have accumulated large volumes of 
speech data, but these resources require substantial curation 
before they can serve as research-ready datasets. 
This paper addresses both gaps by deriving an openly 
accessible, phonetically annotated subset of CommonVoice,

1.3. Interdisciplinary alignment 
Effective pronunciation assessment requires the integration of 
expertise from both phonetics and computer science, yet the 
two fields often diverge in how they view pronunciation 
differences and what constitutes an “error” for the purpose of 
data annotation and training. Computer science frameworks 
tend to treat pronunciation as a binary condition (correct versus 
incorrect) while (socio)phonetic and sociolinguistic approaches 
recognise a continuum of acceptable variation [20, 21]. Our 
annotation framework was developed through ongoing 
collaboration between these perspectives, aiming to produce 
categories that are both linguistically meaningful and 
computationally tractable.  The term “error” is applied as a 
working terminology to allow consistent categorisation of 
pronunciation differences, specifically in relation to AusEng. 
1.4. Framework and reproducibility 
Dataset development papers increasingly serve not only as data 
releases but as methodological guides, enabling reproducibility 
and community building  [22, 23]. In this paper, we detail the 
full pipeline from corpus selection and demographic 
stratification to the design and application of linguistically 
informed annotation categories. The goal is to provide a 
transparent framework that others can adapt to different target 
varieties or learner populations. 
2. Project aims 
The dataset and the annotation framework  presented in this 
paper form part of a larger project  which focuses on the  
development and implement ation of  an AI driven, practical, 
web-based app that delivers high -quality automated speech 
feedback aiming at enhancing pedagogical approaches to 
teaching English pronunciation to international students in the 
Australian tertiary setting. The app will be embedded into the 
curriculum, beginning with a graduate level subject ESLA90001 
Professional Speaking Communication , characterised by  
consistently high enrolment s over the past six years ( > 110 
students per semester ) and strong student satisfaction. 
Developed by Maxwell and Loakes, the subject targets spoken 
communication skills and pronunciation, underpinned by 
phonetics and speech science . Designed for multilingual 
learners navigating academic and workplace communication,  
namely speakers of English as L2 and those who speak other 
varieties of English (L1 or L2), the subject curriculum 
integrates teaching prosodic features alongside segmental 
features, the former introduced early in the course structure (in 
week 4 of a 12-week course). Despite high student satisfaction, 
qualitative comments from students in the end -of-semester 
subject evaluation surveys highlighted the demand for self -
guided feedback tools, and this was one of the motivating 
factors for the broader project from which this research stems. 
The main aim of this paper is to present a curated dataset of 
word-level pronunciation errors, stratified by demographic 
markers. An additional aim is to present challenges that arise 
when developing high qual ity models, both from a linguistic -
phonetic and a computational viewpoint. 
3. Methodology 
3.1. Dataset from CommonVoice 
Our dataset is derived from the English partition of the 
CommonVoice corpus (version 21.0, 14 March 2025)  [19]. 
CommonVoice was selected for three key properties: open 
licensing, which permits redistribution and supports 
reproducibility; self-reported demographic metadata, including 
age range, gender, and accent label; and global contributor base, 
which provides a ccess to speakers from diverse L1 
backgrounds. Together, these properties enable stratified 
sampling across demographic variables.  However, the corpus 
also presents limitations , not all immediately evident . 
Recordings vary in acoustic environment, and transcriptions 
use standard English orthography rather than phonetic 
transcription. Metadata fields, being self -reported, are subject 
to incompleteness and potential inaccuracy. We acknowledge 
these as trade -offs inherent in prioritising openness and scale 
over controlled laboratory conditions. 
3.2. Demographics of subset of CommonVoice 
In our study, three broadly defined “accent” groups were 
selected, based on country/region: Hong Kong, Indonesi a and 
South Asia  (consisting of speakers who self-identified India , 
Pakistan and Sri Lanka as their countries of residence). These 
groups represent some of the major L1/regional backgrounds 
among international students in Australian tertiary settings (see 
Section 1.1) and are based on demographic profiles of previous 
cohorts enrolled in Professional Speaking Communication. The 
majority of speakers are in their 20s, with 40% female. Accent

3.3 Word-level forced alignment 
Word-level time boundaries were generated using the Montreal 
Forced Aligner (MFA) version 3.3.9 [24], which maps 
orthographic transcriptions to audio via a pronunciation lexicon 
and an acoustic model built on the Kaldi toolkit.  We used the 
pretrained English acoustic model english_mfa v3.1.0 with the 
corresponding pronunciation dictionary. Out-of-vocabulary 
(OOV) words , which are present in the C ommonVoice 
transcriptions but not in the dictionary, were handled using the 
english_us_mfa grapheme-to-phoneme (G2P) model . 
Alignment produced TextGrid files for each recording.  These 
were uploaded to a transcription annotation platform (described 
in Section 3.5) where annotators could visually inspect and 
correct any inaccuracies in word boundaries. 
3.4 Annotation coding scheme 
Scientifically and pedagogically grounded annotation was 
carried out by three trained phoneticians (see Section 3.5 for 
details) using principles of AusEng transcription  [5] and the 
Autosegmental-Metrical theory, widely used to describe 
prosody and intonation across typologically diverse languages 
and language varieties [25, 26]. The annotation coding scheme 
was developed through iterative refin ement of categories 
grouped by their phonological status/feature and the 
corresponding phonetic characteristics (e.g., consonant, 
fricative). Each iteration of the coding scheme was applied to a 
sample of data and modified , removed or collapsed with 
another category, as determined by the research team. The final 
coding scheme includes 13 classes (major groups, e.g., vowels, 
consonants, prominence) and 53 categories  (e.g., vowel type, 
consonant deletion, prominence/density). The remainder of this 
section provides an overview of the main classes and categories 
relevant to the presentation of the results. 
Following the pedagogical approach to teaching 
pronunciation in Professional Speaking Communication and to 
cater for a  discipline-diverse student cohort (many unfamiliar 
with the technical linguistic jargon ), prosodic features were 
adapted based on their main communicative and pra gmatic 
functions in spoken discourse. The class pitch was used to 
annotate patterns in the use of individual speakers’ pitch

windows in relation to each utterance  (e.g., pitch narrow  for 
narrow pitch range). The class nuclear tune, corresponding to a 
stretch of speech from the last accented word to the right edge 
of the phrase boundary, encompassed global tune categories as 
opposed to anything more fine -grained, e.g., fall, level, rise  
(noting rises in statements vs questions) . Annotations for 
nuclear tunes were limited to syntactically complete sentences 
or phrases. Pause insertion  under the class pauses was 
annotated in the instances of atypical phrasing patterns, e.g., a 
pause between a noun and a verb; however further refinement 
is in p rogress to investigate the types of pauses and whether 
they are fluent or disfluent. Prominence focused on phrase-level 
prominence, including accentuation patterns (e.g., content vs. 
function words) and overall distribution of accentually 
prominent syllables across an utterance  (prominence/density). 
When coding for lexical prominence ( stress), we only 
considered stress placement in a wo rd, without closely 
examining the phonetic cues to stress. 
Errors in  vowels were coded with reference to Wells’ s 
standard lexical sets [27], where a group of words with the same 
vowel is represented by a lexical item (e.g., DRESS - /e/ in 
AusEng). This approach was applied for eas e of providing 
feedback and to mitigate confusion based on cross-varietal 
differences in vowel inventories in world Englishes. Precisely 
for the same reason, we did no t examine the exact spectral 
qualities of each vowel but focuse d on whether a contrast was 
maintained (e.g., KIT vs. FLEECE). Vowel a nnotations also 
included categories for rounding, insertion and deletion.  
Errors in the production of consonants were categorised by 
manner of articulation  (e.g., stops, fricatives) and place, the 
latter having a single category accompanied by annotation 
dependent on the sound in question. The class liquid involved 
noting pronunciation of a rhotic vs. a lateral sound (and vice 
versa), such as the “wrong” rhotic/lateral characteristics in a tap 
or alveolar approximant. Absence/presence of rhoticity was not 
treated as  “erroneous”, considering variation across world 
Englishes. Categories were also created to identify 
mispronunciations in syllable onsets and codas , voicing 
(voiced_plus vs. voiced_minus), aspiration (limited to 
canonically aspirated /p t k/ in onsets in AusEng (following [5]). 
For both vowels and consonants , we coded for insertion, 
annotating instances where a sound was inserted within or 
across a syllable boundary.  Categories were also created for 
word-level syllable deletion, syllable insertion or merger. 
Mispronunciations due to spelling were captured under the 
class and the corresponding category spelling, e.g., errors based 
on the confusion with the “ea” spellings or the pronunciation of 
the past tense suffix “ed” where the final consonant voicing is 
phonetically conditioned. Categories corresponding to  
connected speech processes under the class linking included all 
types of linking across words boundaries, e.g., consonant-to-
consonant (C-to-C), V-to-V, etc. 
Coding for rhythm, we focused on the reduction of 
unstressed syllables, marked by changes in duration and vowel 
quality. As a result of the complex interplay between prosodic 
and segmental features [6, 8], this class was dependant on other 
classes and categories , such as t he use schwa in reduced 
syllables or vowel insertion in consonant clusters to mitigate 
L1-based phonotactic constraints. Instances of other 
overlapping categories (e.g., prominence and pitch range) were 
annotated using the full coding scheme, while differentiating 
phrase-level (mostly prosodic, annonated on a sentence tier ) 
and word-level (segmental, annotated on a word tier) features.  
3.5 Annotation process 
Annotations were conducted using a purpose -built web-based 
platform developed within our research group. The platform 
ingests MFA-generated TextGrid files alongside corresponding 
audio recordings, presenting annotators with word -level 
segmentations for each speaker. Quang and Turnbull guided the 
setup of the platform interface, determining tiers and columns . 
The platform is currently under institutional IP review and will 
be documented separately in future work. Annotation tasks 
were assignable, allowing distribut ion of distinct sets of 
recordings across annotators. Maxwell, Loakes and Gregory  
performed annotations, with the majority annotated by Gregory. 
For each recording, annotators code d pronunciation errors 
following the coding scheme described in Section 3.4 and 
provided holistic comments on the speaker's overall 
performance. A dedicated evaluation view enables the lead 
annotator to review and compare annotations, supporting 
quality control and consistency. The next phase of the project, 
currently in progress, is annotation validation, measuring inter-
rater reliability. This paper reports on a total of 859 annotations. 
4. Preliminary results   
Work on this project is ongoing, involving an iterative process 
to inform the final model. Here we present preliminary results 
based on the analysis of the dataset and annotations to date.  
4.1. Distribution of classes 
Fig. 1 shows the number and distribution of observations per 
annotation class, presented by accent (columns 1-3) and across 
accent groups (column 4 , “ overall”). As illustrated in the 
overall column, almost half of the total annotations relate to 
segmental errors (vowels and consonants, 49.9%). Aside from 
spelling which only relates to around 35 errors (4.1%), the 
remaining 46.1% (again, almost half) are prosodic errors. The 
most common error after vowels and consonants is pauses 
(10.2% of errors) followed by (lack of) linking (6.8%) and 
rhythm (6.5%). This could be due to the interplay across 
classes, i.e., pausing between word boundaries often (but not 
always) relates to deviation in linking, in turn affecting rhythm.

Similarly, schwa relates to prosodic features.
