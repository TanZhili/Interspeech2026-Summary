Abstract
This paper proposes a causal, low-latency reference-enhanced
keep-speech active noise control (KSANC) method, in which
a deep neural network is used to enhance the reference signal
by suppressing speech components while retaining noise. As
a result, speech components in the error signal can be effec-
tively preserved after control. The proposed method is vali-
dated through experiments using measured headphone impulse
responses. The results demonstrate that the proposed approach
improves speech intelligibility and speech quality in noisy con-
versational scenarios for headphone applications, compared
with all baselines. Moreover, the method exhibits robustness to
different speech signals and directions, noise types and direc-
tions, as well as varying signal-to-noise ratios. Listening exam-
ples are available athttps://github.com/RaoLi666/
RSE_KSANC.git.
Index Terms: keep-speech active noise control, reference en-
hancement, speech intelligibility, speech quality improvement
1. Introduction
Noise pollution detrimentally affects human comfort and health.
While passive control is often ineffective in certain scenarios
due to constraints on material volume and weight, active noise
control (ANC) has been widely adopted in applications such as
headphones, automobiles, and aircraft, due to its ease of imple-
mentation, lightweight structure, and effectiveness in attenuat-
ing low-frequency noise [1, 2, 3, 4]. Noise also degrades the
quality of speech communication; however, neither passive nor
active noise control techniques inherently distinguish between
noise and speech. Consequently, noise attenuation inevitably
compromises speech quality. For instance, the reference mi-
crophone in ANC headphones captures both ambient noise and
speech. Speech components is suppressed at the ear by the ANC
system, which combined with the passive attenuation of the
headphone shell, significantly degrades communication quality.
In conversational scenarios, in addition to turning ANC
off, hear-through schemes based on high-pass filtering and gain
compensation of external microphone signals have been em-
ployed to compensate for passive attenuation, aiming to achieve
acoustically transparent listening [5, 6, 7, 8]. Nevertheless, it is
evident that noise is also admitted “transparently”. Patel et al.
integrated superdirective microphone array beamforming with
multi-reference feedforward ANC to develop a directional hear-
through headphone system [9]. In this system, ambient sound
is first attenuated by ANC, after which the beamformed signal
is injected into the ear to facilitate spatially selective listening.
However, this approach fails when speech and noise arrive from

In 2020, Niu et al. conducted listening experiments with
human-subjects in an anechoic chamber and showed that ANC
enabled on earplugs and earmuffs improves speech recognition
accuracy under simultaneous noise and speech conditions [10].
A natural implication of this observation is that, if ANC sup-
presses noise exclusively without affecting speech, the speech
intelligibility and quality of the error signal could be further
improved. This concept is referred to as keep-speech ANC
(KSANC) in this paper.
KSANC was first explored in the DeepANC [11], where
a convolutional recurrent network (CRN) was used to replace
the control filter in a conventional ANC system. This approach
enables noise attenuation while preserving speech, resulting in
improved speech intelligibility and quality in the error signal.
However, CRN-based approaches inevitably introduce frame-
level latency, making it difficult to satisfy the strict causality
constraints in practical applications such as headphones.
To improve the causality margin of KSANC, this paper pro-
poses a reference signal enhancement (RSE) method, which
uses a neural network to enhance the reference signal, while the
control filter remains a conventional FIR filter. The method in-
troduces no additional delay and preserves low system latency,
leading to an increased causality margin.

2.2. RSE Network
WaveNet [12] is a causal fully convolutional model that oper-
ates directly in the time domain, making it well suited for ANC
tasks. When implemented with a convolution stride of 1 and
causal padding, the network introduces no additional algorith-
mic delay. Motivated by these properties, we build upon and tai-
lor the WaveNet framework to the RSE task under strict causal-
ity constraints.
Figure 2:The neural network architecture.
The employed network architecture is illustrated in Fig-
ure 2. The overall structure follows the original WaveNet frame-
work, with several modifications introduced after the skip con-
nections to better accommodate the RSE task. Specifically, the
1×1 convolution after the skip connections is replaced by the
causal convolution, and the activation function is changed from
ReLU to tanh. The architecture employs an initial causal con-
volution with a kernel size of 32 and 128 channels, followed
by four stacks, each consisting of 12 layers with 128 channels
and a kernel size of 5. The output head comprises three causal
convolution layers with channel dimensions of 128→64→32
→1 and kernel sizes of 5, 5, and 256. Using the loss function
Lenh (detailed below) as an example, the modified architecture
reduced the validation loss from6.9×10 −5 to5.9×10 −5
compared with the original model, demonstrating the effective-
ness of the modifications. The modified model contains approx-
imately 9.53 M parameters and requires approximately 38.12 G
MACs to process one second of reference signal. The network
was trained for 100 epochs using the Adam optimizer with an
initial learning rate of5×10 −5. The learning rate was decayed
by a factor of 0.9 every 10 epochs.
2.3. Loss functions
To suppress the noise componentd(n)in the error signal while
preserving the speech componentv e(n), the two loss functions
for enhancing the reference signal are:
Lenh =E[d(n) +s∗w∗g φ (x+v r,d+v e)]2 (9)
Lrefsep =E[x(n)−g φ (x+v r,d+v e)]2,(10)
whereL enh evaluates the KSANC performance at the error mi-
crophone by jointly considering noise suppression and speech
preservation, whileL refsep evaluates the noise extraction perfor-
mance at the reference microphone. For training withL enh, the
optimal Wiener solution of the control filterwis computed fol-
lowing [13, pp. 130–131] and substituted into Eq. 9 to evaluate
Lenh, which is then minimized to optimize the network param-
etersφ. In contrast, training withL refsep directly minimizes the
mean squared error between the network output and the noise
componentx(n)at the reference microphone to optimize the
network parametersφ.

3.2. Noise and speech datasets
Following Shi et al. [14] and Luo et al. [15], band-limited noise
signals were generated by partitioning the full frequency band
(0–4000 Hz) into multiple subbands with different bandwidths.
The training set consisted of 31 frequency bands obtained us-
ing bandwidths of 4000, 2000, 1000, 500, and 250 Hz. The
validation and test sets were each constructed using 7 disjoint
frequency bands, with bandwidths and spectral locations dis-
tinct from those in the training set and from each other. For
each frequency band, 100 random noise signals of 10 s duration
were generated at a sampling rate of 8 kHz. To further evaluate
performance under real-world noise conditions, additional test
signals were generated using noise recordings from the DCASE
datasets [16, 17], including fan, engine, bearing, and gearbox
noises. For each noise type, 700 segments of 10 s duration were
randomly selected and used for evaluation.
The speech data were drawn from the LibriTTS corpus [18].
We randomly selected 200, 50, and 50 speakers for the training,
validation, and test sets, ensuring no speaker overlap and a bal-
anced gender distribution. The recordings were segmented into
10 s utterances, from which 3100, 700, and 700 samples were
randomly selected to match the corresponding noise datasets.
During experiments, the energy of the speech driving sig-
nal was adjusted such that a specified signal-to-noise ratio
(SNR) was achieved at the reference microphone, defined as

4. Experimental results
In the experiments, the ANC control filter was adaptively up-
dated using the Recursive Least Squares (RLS) algorithm with
a forgetting factor of 0.99999 and an initial covariance matrix of
0.01I. Speech intelligibility and quality were evaluated using
STOI and the non-intrusive metric DNSMOS (OVRL) [19], re-
spectively, computed from the error signal over the last 3 s after
the system reached steady state. Since conventional ANC may
suppress speech components, DNSMOS was adopted to assess
perceptual speech quality.

As shown in Table 1, the RSE-based KSANC trained with
Lenh achieves the best overall STOI and DNSMOS performance
across a wide SNR range, consistently outperforming the Un-
processed case, Conventional ANC, DeepANC and the RSE-
based KSANC trained withL refsep. The inferior performance
of DeepANC is mainly due to to its larger processing delay,
which violates the causality constraint. Although theL refsep-
trained model yields higher coherence between the enhanced
reference and target noise, this does not translate into improved
STOI or DNSMOS, as its optimization is less aligned with the
downstream ANC objective of jointly attenuating noise and pre-
serving speech. In contrast,L enh is more directly tailored to
KSANC, resulting in superior perceptual performance. The
slightly lower coherence ofL enh compared with Conventional
ANC at SNR = −5 dB arises primarily above 2 kHz and has
negligible perceptual impact, as ANC is predominantly effec-
tive at low frequencies.
Compared with the Unprocessed case, Conventional ANC
provides only marginal DNSMOS improvement at SNR =
−5 dB, with no consistent STOI gains or benefits at higher
SNRs. This limited improvement is attributed to the stationary
band-limited white noise used, which minimally affects intelli-
gibility, while Conventional ANC may suppress speech compo-
nents.

5. Discussion
This paper presented a reference-enhanced active noise control
method based on a time-domain WaveNet architecture with a
large causality margin. The proposed method with two dis-
tinct training strategies was validated through experiments us-
ing measured headphone impulse responses. Results show that
the training strategy relying solely on the reference signal is
insufficient, whereas the error-domain training strategy effec-
tively suppresses noise while preserving speech, leading to con-
sistent improvements in both speech intelligibility and percep-
tual quality in noisy conversational scenarios. In addition, the
proposed method demonstrates robust generalization to unseen
conditions, including novel SNRs, speech signals, noise types,
and source directions. Given the relatively high parameter count
of the current WaveNet-based model, future work will focus
on model optimization and parameter reduction to facilitate de-
ployment on resource-constrained devices such as headphones.
