Abstract
In the conversational artificial intelligence era, a learner’s voice
is vital, yet automated scoring often fails to capture them due
to data scarcity and privacy constraints. We propose a meta-
learning-based framework for adaptive multimodal expert spe-
cialization, transforming the system into an adaptive listener.
Instead of a one-size-fits-all model, our approach uses a mix-
ture of experts architecture in which subnetworks learn to spe-
cialize in diverse vocal profiles. This synergy enables the model
to rapidly “learn to learn” from limited samples, thus providing
a personalized and accurate reflection of a speaker’s true capa-
bilities. Our framework achieves an F1-score greater than 84%
in classification and a mean squared error of 0.225 in regres-
sion. By optimizing deep model performance under extreme
data constraints, this framework offers a robust, scalable solu-
tion for automated English coaching that balances adaptation
with predictive accuracy. Our code is publicly available.1

1. Introduction
English language tests have long been a standard measure
for assessing English proficiency for specific contexts such as
studying, emigrating, or working. Since it is used worldwide,
the demand for English proficiency testing is growing in to-
day’s globalized society. However, most recent English tests
are scored manually by highly qualified examiners, which re-
sults in a bottleneck in which the examiners bear a significant
amount of grading pressure. As a result, English proficiency
examinations cost more and take longer to complete.
Cheng et al. [1] established a framework for automated En-
glish proficiency assessment by defining objective criteria such
as pronunciation, fluency, and prosody. These metrics have
been shown to effectively represent higher-level linguistic fea-
tures, including vocabulary usage [2] and sentence structure [3],
thereby ensuring more consistent evaluations. Beyond basic
proficiency, research has increasingly focused on multitask [4]
communication skill assessment in diverse settings. For public
speaking, studies have analyzed monologs through nonverbal
cues such as eye contact, facial expressions, and gesture con-
trol [5, 6]. In dialog-based contexts, such as job interviews,
researchers have used features such as speaking turns [7], body
language, and prosodic variations to predict personality traits
and employability [8, 9, 10].

Capturing intermodal synergies is essential for effective cue
integration. Consequently, research has evolved from simple
feature concatenation [11, 12] toward attention-based fusion for
modeling intricate cross-modal interactions [13].
Recently, meta-learning has emerged as a strategy for rapid
adaptation under limited data. It has been applied to low-
resource machine translation [14] and natural language gener-
ation for task-oriented dialogue systems [15]. Its key contri-
bution lies in learning a task-agnostic initialization that enables
fast convergence and effective transfer to unseen domains.
The privacy-sensitive nature of audiovisual dialogues
strictly limits large-scale data availability, creating a significant
bottleneck for traditional deep learning models. Thus, we lever-
age meta-learning to overcome data scarcity through fast adap-
tation. By training on augmented meta-tasks, the model estab-
lishes an optimal initialization for rapid learning. Furthermore,
we integrate a mixture of experts (MoE) architecture to spe-
cialize the model for multimodal cues, thereby ensuring high-
precision evaluation even with minimal training data.
This study aims to answer the following research questions:
•RQ1: To what extent can a mixture of experts architecture
capture diverse features to deliver accurate predictions?
•RQ2: How can meta-learning enable rapid adaptation despite
data scarcity for this specific domain?
2. Proposed Method
A structured evaluation basis is essential for implementing ad-
vanced techniques for automated spoken language assessment,
for instance, the Spoken English Evaluation (SEE)2 framework.
Aligned with CEFR standards [16], the SEE framework breaks
down proficiency into five core dimensions, which comprise a
total of 20 specific criteria. Range evaluates the breadth of lin-
guistic resources on the basis of the range of topics (R1) and vo-
cabulary (R2), alongside circumlocution (R3) and the precision
of the language (R4). Accuracy focuses on grammatical con-
trol, specifically sentence structure (A1), subject-verb agree-
ment (A2), pronouns (A3), tenses (A4), and the correct use of
conjugation and prepositions (A5), thereby ensuring structural
and semantic precision.
With respect to delivery, fluency measures speech produc-
tion ease through accent (F1), tempo and pausing (F2), intona-
tion and fluidity (F3), and the ability to speak freely (F4). The
interaction dimension assesses sociopragmatic skills, including
participation (I1), conversational ease (I2), clarifications (I3),
and the use of conversational cues (I4). Finally, coherence

2.1. Generating Data for Meta-learning
Meta-learning requires training on various tasks so that the
model can learn how to learn and find a suitable set of initial pa-
rameters for learning from unseen data. However, the datasets
we used were too small to be partitioned into the distinct sub-
tasks necessary for this training process. Therefore, the data
was enhanced by cutting the videos into smaller overlapping
segments, each was required to include at least one interviewee
statement and maintain within a duration range. The process of
cutting the video into segments is based on the assumption that
the cut segments will still reflect the quality of the entire video.
2.2. Feature Extraction
To capture the complementary nature of verbal and non-verbal
cues, we leveraged multimodal signals. We extracted high-level
features using state-of-the-art pretrained models, which pro-
vide robust representations learned from massive datasets. They
were then concatenated and normalized by a standard scaler.
•Acoustic features:We used DisV oice 3 to extract 103 key
prosody features (which are based on [19, 20]), including
pitch, duration, and energy.
•Linguistic features:We used the pretrained model Qwen3
0.6B [21]4 to extract text embeddings from transcripts.
•Turn-taking and rubric-guided features:We integrated
statistical descriptors, including the utterance count and dura-
tion statistics such as the mean, standard deviation, skewness,
and kurtosis, to model conversational dynamics. We also ex-
tracted lexical and fluency measures, such as the word count

2.3. Meta-learning Approach
Figure 1 depicts the meta-learning workflow, highlighting the
transition from a meta-learned initialization to fast adaptation
across diverse assessment tasks. We assessed English profi-
ciency by predicting multiple outputs across various scoring cri-
teria. These distinct language aspects may conflict when jointly
optimized under a single parameter set. To mitigate such inter-
ference, we treated each scoring criterion as a collection of sub-
tasks and adopt a meta-learning framework that enabled flexible
task-specific adaptation.
To learn a shared parameter initializationθfor rapid adap-
tation to specific scoring criteria, we employ model-agnostic
meta-learning (MAML) [24]. This approach enables task-
specific parametersθ ′
k to specialize while leveraging com-
mon representations. To improve computational efficiency, we
adopted first-order MAML (FOMAML), which ignores second-
order derivatives during meta-optimization while maintaining
performance comparable to that of the original framework.
2.4. Model Architecture
The modality-specific representations were first projected
through a linear layer followed by layer normalization to map
features from different modalities into a unified dimensional-
ity. The normalized feature vectors were then stacked along the
modality dimension and fed into a Transformer encoder, which
enabled explicit modeling of inter-modal interactions via self-
attention. The encoder outputs were then fused using attentive
pooling to highlight the most informative features.
We used a MoE [25] architecture with 16 experts, which al-
lows the model to capture common features across scoring crite-
ria while also preserving unique characteristics by specializing
the knowledge acquisition for different experts. Additionally, a
top-2 gating approach acted as a router, which allowed for more
flexible and balanced expert allocations, and also helped reduce
computational costs as the model did not need to optimize all
weights for each learned sample. The two most relevant experts
were selected and integrated via a weighted sum.
Expert-derived features were fed into a multihead scorer,
where each head is specialized for a scoring criterion. This de-
sign enabled the model to leverage shared representations cap-
tured by preceding layers while applying this condensed knowl-
edge to make flexible, criterion-specific predictions.

2.5.2. Regression Task
Instead of conventional MSE-based continuous regression, we
employed soft-discretized regression. Since English grades use
discrete steps (integers or one-decimal values), we transformed
the task to output logits representing grade probabilities, sim-
plifying model output.
The loss function we used for this problem is as follows:
LR =L KL +αL LB +βL MSE +γL Margin
whereL KL represents the KL-divergence loss,L LB represents
the load balancing loss for the MoE architecture,L MSE repre-
sents the MSE loss, andL Margin represents the margin-penalized
MSE loss (which penalizes samples for which the loss exceeds
0.5). We used the following hyperparameters:α= 0.01,
β= 0.1, andγ= 0.3.
3. Experiments
Experiments were conducted to assess MoE feature capture
(RQ1) and rapid meta-learning adaptation (RQ2). For RQ1,
the MoE architecture successfully captured diverse features
through specialized experts, ensuring high accuracy. For RQ2,
our framework enabled rapid adaptation, consistently outper-
forming non-meta-learning baselines.
3.1. Datasets
Our model is evaluated primarily on the ETS Vericant dataset
[23], which contains synchronized audio, video, and tran-
scripts. The dataset includes samples from 427 nonnative
English speakers (aged 9–16) in university admission inter-
views. The data covers both remote and in-person sessions us-
ing SEE criteria to support our comprehensive English assess-
ment; these were processed via AWS Amazon diarization for
precise speaker-turn timestamps and personally identifiable in-
formation redaction. To prepare the dataset for meta-task con-
struction, videos were segmented into 200–250 second clips us-
ing a one-utterance stride. This duration was selected since 3-
minute segments have been shown to reach a Spearman-Brown
coefficient of 0.95 relative to the full video [26].
To assess the generalizability of the model, we also incor-
porated the MIT Interview dataset [27], which consists of 138
samples of recorded mock interviews used for hiring recom-
mendation. Segments were similarly prepared at 120–200 sec-
onds per clip, reflecting the shorter average response duration
compared to the ETS Vericant dataset.
3.2. Experimental Setup
The experiments were conducted using an NVIDIA A40 GPU.
We used Bayesian Optimization (BO) with the BoTorch [28] li-
brary to tune the hyperparameters of the model, specifically: the
inner loop learning rate (LR) ([1e−6,1e−1]), the outer loop LR
([1e−7,1e−2]), the LR range for adaptation during the testing
phase ([[1e−6,0.5],[1e−7,0.05]]), the number of training steps
per meta-task ([2,3]), and the number of training steps for the
original data ([50,60]). The optimization started with 1 trial at
the center of the search space and 8 Sobol quasi-random trials
for broad coverage. Using these as a baseline for the surrogate
model, 11 additional trials were conducted using the BoTorch
generator to iteratively select promising hyperparameter con-
figurations based on an acquisition function.
To optimize the hyperparameters, we addressed classifica-
tion and regression tasks by maximizing the F1 score and mini-
mizing the MSE, respectively. These objectives were monitored
using a 10% hold-out validation set from the training data.
3.3. Evaluation
For classification, we evaluated the model using Macro F1 to
ensure a balanced assessment across potentially imbalanced
classes. The samples were categorized based on dataset-specific
thresholds: for the ETS Vericant dataset, scores were grouped
into lower (class 0, below 4) and higher (class 1, 4 and above);
for the MIT Interview dataset, binary classification (high vs.
low hirability) was performed on the basis of the median hiring
recommendation score. For the regression task, performance
was measured in terms of mean squared error (MSE) against
ground-truth scores.

The results of the ablation study, which are presented in Ta-

the framework. Replacing the MoE architecture with a dense

3.5. Discussion
The framework achieves an 84.88% F1-score and a 32.4% error
reduction over the baseline. By combining MoE with meta-
learning, the system captures diverse features and enables rapid
adaptation to unseen tasks with minimal gradient updates, en-
suring robust performance under extreme data constraints.
Fast adaptation enables seamless deployment in low-
resource domains. By providing a granular map of skill gaps,
the engine catalyzes learner-centered education, offering a scal-
able solution for environments requiring precise, automated
feedback with minimal annotated data.
Despite outperforming the baseline significantly, the re-
liance of our method on pretrained features remains a limita-
tion. This bottleneck may restrict domain sensitivity, hindering
the capture of fine-grained features.
4. Conclusion
This research confirms that a MoE architecture effectively cap-
tures diverse multimodal features, providing the specialized
representation necessary for high-accuracy automated scoring
(RQ1). Furthermore, the integration of meta-learning enables
fast adaptation to unseen tasks with minimal gradient updates,
successfully overcoming extreme data scarcity in this specific
domain (RQ2). This synergy between expert specialization
and rapid learning establishes a scalable foundation for high-
precision, low-resource educational tools. Moving forward, we
aim to integrate SSL to reduce reliance on pretrained models
and leverage LLMs to bridge the gap between numerical as-
sessment and personalized pedagogical support, ultimately re-
defining the landscape of learner-centered education.
