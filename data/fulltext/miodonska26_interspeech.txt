Age-dependent acoustic changes of the frication noise in dental sibilants
produced by typically developing Polish children between 5 and 8 years of age

Abstract
The paper aims to investigate which acoustic properties of Pol-
ish dental fricatives /s, z/ are related to the age and sex of typ-
ically developing child speakers between 61 and 96 months of
age. We analyzed speech samples collected from 88 children
assessed as producing /s, z/ with a target (dental) place of artic-
ulation. The analysis comprises a set of typical spectral features
and spectral features of the frication noise, which are treated as
markers of sibilant articulation patterns. Linear mixed-effect
models showed a significant age-related increase of fricative
formant distance and spectral energy above 6 kHz for /s/ pro-
ductions and a decrease in spectral energy below 2,5 kHz for
/z/. The results present an age-dependent spectrum shift that
may indicate improvement in articulatory precision. The results
are a preliminary step in quantifying the acoustic patterns in the
sibilant articulation development in typically developing chil-
dren.
Index Terms: Polish sibilants, child speech, acoustic analysis,
linear mixed-effect models
1. Introduction
The age range at which children should achieve sibilant acquisi-
tion is described variously in different languages [1]. According
to Polish research [2], after the age of three, children have the
physiological opportunity to form an alveolar and palatal frica-
tion. In the fourth year of life, dental sibilants /s,z,ţ,dz/ occur
with increasing frequency to become established when the child
reaches the age of four. Similarly, the acquisition of retroflex
consonants /ù,ü, tù, dü/, should become firmly established by
the age of five [2]. However, this group of consonants is at-
tributed to the most distortions, and the prevalence of sigmatism
is considered the most common articulation abnormality among
preschool children [3, 4]. According to research conducted as
part of the standardization of tests for articulation assessment,
the so-called phonetic substitutions concern approximately 60%
of children who produce the sounds /s, z/, and the manner of
pronunciation depends on the age of the speakers [5].
Maddieson et al. [6] emphasized that even a one-millimeter
difference in vocal tract positioning can significantly affect the
production of fricative noise specific for sibilants. The precise
shaping of turbulent airflow is essential to producing clear sibi-
lant sounds. Given this complexity, deviations in sibilant articu-
lation may indicate an ongoing developmental process. The cor-
rect articulation of sounds is the result of, among other things,
phonetic-phonological competence [7], which develops with

normative patterns is crucial for accurate diagnosis and effec-
tive speech therapy intervention, but so is defining the baseline

The acoustic properties of sibilants, including Polish dental
fricatives, have been widely studied. Most research has focused
on adult speakers [8, 9, 10, 11, 12], though studies on children’s
speech have also been conducted [13, 14, 15, 16]. Researchers
typically analyze spectral moments [11, 17, 18, 19] and frica-
tion formants [20, 16, 12], while some also examine noise band
energies and related measures [20, 21].
Previous studies on Polish children’s fricative production
provide valuable insights. Miodo´nska et al. [20] further investi-
gated retroflex fricatives in Polish preschool children, includ-
ing those with sigmatism, demonstrating that frication noise
features differentiate pronunciation patterns. Łobacz & Do-
brza´nska [16] analyzed Polish fricatives in 19 typically devel-
oping children aged 4–7, showing that dental sibilants exhibit
higher fricative formant frequencies than retroflex fricatives.
They also suggested that as children grow older, their fricative
productions gradually approximate adult norms. However, the
knowledge of acoustic cues related to changes in articulation in
typically developing children is still very limited.
1.1. Current work
This work investigates which acoustic properties of Polish den-
tal fricatives /s, z/ are related to the age and sex of typically
developing child speakers between 61 and 96 months of age.
We analyze speech samples collected from 88 children whom
a speech-language pathologist assessed as producing /s, z/ with
a target (dental) place of articulation. Our main question is:
are there any consistent acoustic differences showing an im-
provement in articulatory precision between younger and older
children? The analysis comprises a set of typical spectral fea-
tures, which are treated as features that may change due to the
growth of the vocal tract, and spectral features of the frication
noise, which serve as markers connected to sibilant articula-
tion patterns. The statistical analysis employs linear mixed-
effect (LME) models. Similarly to [16], we include dental
fricatives in different acoustic contexts, as we aim to find some
general, context-independent characteristics; the differences are
then considered as random effects in the model structure. In the
further run, the results are a preliminary step in investigating
the patterns of subtle continuous changes occurring in the pro-

2. Materials
The data analyzed in this study were obtained within a larger
project on the articulation of sibilants by Polish preschool chil-
dren, where we gathered a corpus of multimodal (video and
multichannel audio) signals of child speech [22]. As this re-
search focuses on acoustic analysis, we use only a single signal
recorded with the microphone positioned in front of the mouth.
The recordings took place in several kindergartens and one pri-
mary school. The recording sessions included speech signal
acquisition and a speech assessment carried out by a speech-
language pathologist (SLP). Diagnoses were documented using
a specialized questionnaire that assessed participants’ anatom-
ical and articulatory characteristics. During the speech record-
ing process, children named images displayed on a computer
screen. Then, to collect productions of sibilants that more rarely
occur in the language and are difficult to present in understand-
able images, some additional words were repeated by a partici-
pant after the SLP who conducted the recordings. The collected
speech corpus comprised over 50 words and phrases, encom-
passing all twelve Polish sibilants in various word positions and
phonetic contexts. For this study, we took 14 words contain-
ing dental fricatives /s, z/, as detailed in Table 1. Audio data
was recorded at a sampling rate of 44.1 kHz. The project re-
ceived approval from the local Bioethical Committee, and in-
formed consent was obtained from the participants and their le-
gal guardians.
For this study, we selected participants based on the follow-
ing characteristics of their articulation of dental fricatives /s, z/,
as determined during speech evaluation: (1) dental (adult-like)
place of articulation, (2) adequate voicing: voiced for [z] and
unvoiced for [s] productions, (3) no speech disorders or patholo-
gies (e.g., nasality, palatalization) concerning the articulation of
the analyzed sounds. Eighty-eight children met the criteria and
were included in the analysis. Table 2 provides an overview of
the age and sex distribution within these groups.
3. Methods
3.1. Signal processing
We segmented and annotated sibilants using spectrograms and
waveforms. The signals were divided into 20 ms frames with
a 10 ms overlap. These frames were then used to compute the
short-time Fourier transform (STFT), resulting in a spectral res-
olution of 50 Hz. Then, we extracted two groups of spectral
acoustic features based on the STFT representation: 12 whole-
spectrum features and 29 frication noise features. Calculations
were performed in MATLAB R2023b.
Whole-spectrum acoustic features were extracted according
to the procedures detailed in [23]. We employed spectral mo-
ments (spectral centroid, spread, skewness, and kurtosis) and
other measures: spectral crest, decrease, entropy, flatness, flux,
rolloff point, slope, and pitch (only for the analyzed voiced
fricative).
Frication noise band features were extracted following the
procedures reported in [20, 24]. In this study, we included frica-
tive formants (FF 1–FF4) and their levels (FFL 1–FFL4), frica-
tive formant relations (FFR 12–FFR34) and distances (FFD 12–
FFD34), which reflect bandwidths between consecutive pairs
of FFs. We also employed measures concerning the highest-
amplitude peak in the middle-frequency band (2 −7 kHz) peak
amplitude (PA) and its corresponding frequency (peak fre-
quency, PF). Following literature, we also applied energies in
subsequent frication bands NE 0–NE9 determined for consecu-
tive 500 Hz subbands from 2 to 7 kHz [20].
3.2. Statistical analysis
We conducted a statistical analysis using LME models to inves-
tigate differences in spectrum-based and frication-based acous-
tic features between speakers of different ages and sexes. To en-
sure analysis focused on the most stable portion of the frication
segment, we excluded the first and last 25% of frames, retain-
ing the middle 50% of frames for modeling. The final number
of frames included in the model is shown in Table 2. LME
models were applied separately to each of the 41 acoustic fea-
tures outlined in Section 3. To identify the most suitable model
structure, we explored different configurations, based on key

4. Results and Discussion
From 12 general spectrum features (e.g., spectral moments)
none showed significant changes in terms of speaker’s sex or
age. The pitch, which is a typical characteristic related to the
structure of the vocal folds and, interchangeably, to age, was
similar across the whole group of children included in the study.
This may result from the fact that the age span in the consid-
ered group was 25 months (2y;1m), which could have been too
short to notice a change not masked by the inter-child vari-
ation. In preschool children, the larynx and vocal folds are
still relatively small and do not undergo significant growth until
puberty-related changes [25].
Sex-related effects were not detected for any of the frication
noise characteristics either. However, significant age-related ef-
fects were found for several noise-based features, as presented
in Table 3. For unvoiced fricative /s/, we found a statistically
significant age-related increase of values of FFD 12, NE 8, and
NE9. FFD 12 is a difference between frequencies of the second
and the first fricative formants (FF 2-FF1) and covers a band of
approx. 2 to 4 kHz. The increase of this distance (10 Hz for
each month of age) are related to slight, insignificant shifts of

5. Conclusion
The results of this study reveal an age-dependent spectral shift,
which may indicate increasing articulatory precision in develop-
ing speech. This finding represents an initial step toward quan-
tifying acoustic patterns associated with changes in sibilant ar-
ticulation in typically developing children. Importantly, these
results highlight the value of acoustic analyses as a supplement
to traditional auditory assessments performed by speech thera-
pists. Acoustic studies provide objective measurements that are
particularly useful for analyzing noise components in speech
sounds such as sibilants. By identifying measurable acoustic
characteristics of sibilant production, this research contributes
to a better understanding of the variability and stability of these
sounds in early speech development.
