Bayesian Generalized Additive Multilevel Models for Accurate ERP Latency 
Estimation under Moderate Downsampling

Abstract 
Estimating when event-related potential (ERP) effects begin 
and end helps characterize the timing of neural processing, but 
fitting single-trial Bayesian Generalized Additive Multilevel 
Models (GAMMs) at high sampling rates is computationally 
demanding. Downsampling can reduce this burden, but 
balancing accurate latency estimation with manageable 
computational cost is a key challenge. This study compared 
ERP latency estimates derived from Bayesian GAMMs across 
sampling rates, from full resolution down to an extreme 
downsampled rate, in a passive oddball paradigm . The 
mismatch negativity latency remained stable from full to 
moderate resolutions, with little shift in onset. In contrast, the 
late discriminative negativity showed a later onset and shorter 
duration as the sampling rate decreased. At the extreme 
resolution, both components remained detectable, but with 
reduced temporal precision. Overall, downsampling can reduce 
computational cost, but extreme downsampling should be 
avoided. 
Index Terms: Event-related potentials, Bayesian GAMM, 
temporal downsampling, latency estimation, mismatch 
negativity, late discriminative negativity 
1. Introduction 
Accurate latency estimation is essential in event-related 
potential (ERP) studies because latency is a key index of the 
timing of neural processing [1]. Traditional ERP analyses rely 
on grand averaging to enhance signal visibility [1, 2], but this 
approach may blur trial - and individual-level temporal 
variability, potentially biasing latency estimates [3].  Bayesian 
Generalized Additive Multilevel Models (GAMMs) provide 
precise estimates of ERP latency [4, 5]; however, fitting such 
models to high-resolution data is computationally demanding, 
and temporal downsampling is therefore often used as a 
practical solution. The cost-accuracy trade-off of this practice 
remains poorly understood, particularly because ERP 
components differ in their temporal characteristics. This study 
examines how temporal resolution affects Bayesian GAMM-
based estimates of ERP latency, specifically, onset, offset, and 
persistence duration, with particular attention to component-
specific latency variability. 
1.1. Challenges in estimating ERP latency 
Electroencephalography (EEG) is a non-invasive neuroscience 
technique for recording the brain’s electrical activity from the 
scalp [6]. ERPs are time-locked voltage fluctuations extracted 
from EEG that reflect neural responses to specific events or 
stimuli [1]. They are widely used to investigate sensory, 
cognitive or motor processes. A primary goal in ERP research 
is to determine when neural responses differ across 
experimental conditions or groups and how long these 
differences persist. Accordingly, ERP latency plays a critical 
role in interpreting the timing of underlying neural processes 
[1]. 
Despite its importance, precise latency estimation remains 
methodologically challenging. ERP data are high-dimensional 
and exhibit substantial variability across trials and individuals. 
In addition, ERPs are characterized by a low signal-to-noise 
ratio: task-related signals are typically small in amplitude and 
embedded in ongoing EEG activity, as well as contaminated by 
physiological artifacts such as eye movements and muscle 
activity [1, 7]. Grand averaging across trials and individuals is 
commonly used to enhance component visibility [1], but 
variability in response timing can lead to temporal smearing, 
reducing sensitivity to subtle latency differences [1, 8]. As a 
result, latency estimates derived from averaged waveforms may 
not fully reflect underlying temporal variability. 
1.2. Bayesian modeling of ERP time courses and the 
trade-offs of temporal downsampling 
Bayesian GAMMs provide a flexible alternative for modeling 
ERP time courses. By treating time as a smooth, continuous 
predictor and incorporating hierarchical structure (e.g., trials 
nested within participants), these models account for non-linear 
dynamics and inter-individual variability [4, 5, 9]. This 
hierarchical structure is useful for unbalanced designs and ERP 
datasets, where the number of observations or accepted trials 
may vary across participants, conditions, or groups. Rather than 
requiring equal numbers of trials in each design cell, Bayesian 
GAMMs can use all available observations while accounting 
for participant-level differences. In a Bayesian framework, 
posterior distributions quantify uncertainty in estimated 
trajectories [4, 10, pp. 63-79], enabling onset and offset to be 
estimated from the fitted curves rather than from peak-based 
measures alone. 
At the same time, f itting Bayesian GAMMs to high-
resolution trial-level ERP data can be computationally 
demanding. This is especially the case when analyses involve 
multiple conditions, groups, or multilevel structures, all of 
which increase model complexity and computational burden. In 
addition, Bayesian GAMM analysis often requires repeated 
model fitting for checking, comparison, and refinement as part 
of a routine workflow [10]. In practice, temporal downsampling 
is often used to reduce computational load [4]. While this 
strategy may appear to simply decrease the number of time 
points, it may also reduce temporal precision and make latency 
estimates less stable. The extent of these consequences remains 
unclear. In particular, the impact of reduced temporal resolution 
may depend not only on the sampling interval but also on the 
stability and dispersion of latency across trials and individuals.

latency variability 
ERP components differ not only in amplitude, but also in the 
dispersion of their latency across trials and individuals. Earlier

components, such as Mismatch Negativity (MMN), are often 
associated with sensory or perceptual encoding processes and 
may show relatively constrained latency variability [1, 11]. 
Later components, such as Late Discriminative Negativity 
(LDN), are commonly linked to higher-level cognitive 
processes and may be more sensitive to contextual, attentional, 
and individual-level influences [12, 13]. As a result, later 
components may show broader inter-trial and inter-individual 
variability in their timing. 
These component-specific differences are important when 
temporal resolution is reduced. When latency variability is 
relatively limited, key temporal features of the component, such 
as onset timing and offset timing, tend to occur within a narrow 
temporal range across trials and participants [3]. In such cases, 
downsampling may introduce discretization error and reduce 
the temporal precision of latency estimates, but its impact may 
remain relatively modest because the underlying latency 
distribution is narrow. However, when latency variability is 
broader, component-related effects are distributed across a 
wider temporal range, making the timing less well-defined. 
Under these conditions, downsampling may increase 
uncertainty in model-based onset and offset estimates. Thus, the 
same degree of downsampling may have unequal effects across 
ERP components, depending on the stability and dispersion of 
their underlying latency distributions. 
1.4. Testing temporal-resolution effects on MMN and 
LDN latency 
We focus on two components elicited in the passive auditory 
oddball paradigm: MMN and LDN. In this paradigm,  
infrequently presented deviant stimuli are interspersed among 
frequently presented standard stimuli, and ERP effects are 
quantified as the deviant-minus-standard contrast over time. 
The MMN component, typically observed around 100-250 ms 
after stimulus onset, is associated with early sensory change 
detection and automatic deviance processing [11]. The LDN 
component follows MMN and extends into later time windows, 
reflecting subsequent stages of auditory discrimination and 
potentially higher-level processing [12, 13]. In speech 
perception, however, the time windows of both MMN and LDN 
may shift later because speech stimuli often involve more 
complex acoustic and phonological processing. These two 
components therefore provide a useful test case for examining 
whether the effects of temporal downsampling differ across 
ERP components with relatively constrained versus broader 
latency distributions [15, 16].  
This study therefore evaluates ERP latency estimates, 
specifically onset, offset, and persistence duration, across ERP 
components that differ in latency variability. It compares 
latency estimates across multiple temporal resolutions and tests 
whether sampling-rate effects differ between components with 
constrained latency distributions and those with broader 
temporal dispersion. We predict that latency estimates for 
components with lower latency variability, such as MMN, will 
remain relatively stable across sampling rates, whereas 
estimates for components with greater latency variability, such 
as LDN, will diverge more as the sampling rate decreases. By 
linking sampling-rate effects to component-specific latency 
variability, this study clarifies how temporal downsampling 
affects ERP timing estimates and informs the practical use of 
Bayesian GAMM-based latency estimation for balancing 
computational cost against temporal precision. 
2. Methods  
2.1. Participants and experimental design 
EEG data were collected in a speech perception study 
investigating the integration of creaky voice in Mandarin Tone 
3 (T3). The study followed the same general experimental 
protocol as reported in [17], where the task and stimulus design 
were described in detail. A passive auditory oddball paradigm 
was used, consisting of 600 standard trials and 100 deviant 
trials, with at least two standards presented between successive 
deviants. All stimuli were 400 ms in duration and presented at 
70 dB. The carrier syllable was /ia/ produced with T3. The 
standard stimulus was characterized by pitch only, while the 
deviant additionally included creaky voice during the middle 
third of the syllable.  
Twenty right-handed native Mandarin speakers 
participated. All had normal hearing and no history of language 
or neurological disorders. During EEG recording, participants 
watched a self-selected silent movie with subtitles. This 
procedure helped maintain engagement while directing 
attention away from the auditory stimuli, so that MMN and 
LDN responses could be interpreted as reflecting pre-attentive 
auditory processing rather than task-driven attention [11, 12, 
13]. 
2.2. EEG recording and preprocessing 
EEG signals were recorded using a SynAmps 2 amplifier 
(NeuroScan Inc.) with a 64-channel cap at 1000 Hz. Data were 
preprocessed using FieldTrip [18]. The raw signals were re-
referenced to the mastoids and filtered using a 0.5-30 Hz band-
pass filter. Artifacts from eye movements and blinks were 
corrected using independent component analysis (ICA) [19]. 
Continuous data were segmented into epochs from -200 ms to 
600 ms relative to the stimulus onset (0 ms), with the pre-
stimulus interval serving as the baseline. Trials with amplitudes 
exceeding ±75 μV were rejected. After preprocessing, more 
than 420 standard and more than 70 deviant trials were retained 
for each participant, with an overall epoch acceptance rate 
exceeding 70%. 
2.3. Temporal resampling 
To examine the influence of temporal resolution, analyses were 
conducted on the original resolution (1000 Hz) and on 
downsampled versions representing mild (500 Hz), moderate 
(250 Hz), and extreme (100 Hz) reductions in temporal 
resolution. Downsampling was performed in R [20] on the 
cleaned single-trial data to ensure that all datasets were derived 
from the same preprocessed signals. 
For each target sampling frequency (fs), temporal resolution 
was defined as Dt = 1000/fs ms. Time points were mapped to the 
corresponding sampling grid, and amplitudes within each bin 
were averaged. This procedure reduced temporal density while 
preserving trial structure. Because the data were band -pass 
filtered at 0.5-30 Hz prior to resampling, the Nyquist criterion 
was satisfied for all sampling rates, including 100 Hz, thereby 
avoiding aliasing. 
2.4. Bayesian GAMM analysis 
All models were fitted using ERP time course data from the Fz 
electrode. This electrode was selected based on previous studies 
showing that MMN and LDN were typically optimal at fronto-
central sites [11, 21]. 
2.4.1. Model specification 
ERP time courses were analyzed using Bayesian GAMMs 
implemented in R via the neurogam package, developed by [4] 
for the temporal localisation of M/EEG effects, with a brms 
backend [9]. The analysis covered the full -200 to 600 ms epoch 
to estimate time-varying differences between deviant (DEV) 
and standard (STD) stimuli. To retain participant-level 
variation while reducing computational cost, we used the 
summary multilevel option (multilevel = “summary”), rather 
than a full trial-by-trial model. Under this option, neurogam fits 
the model to time-resolved participant-level summaries (mean 
and SD) to account for variability in the summary estimates. 
Each model was fitted with four chains and 2,000 iterations per 
chain, including 1,000 warm-up and 1,000 posterior iterations. 
The same model structure was applied at all sampling rates. The

The model included a fixed effect of condition (DEV vs. 
STD), participant-level random intercepts and slopes, and 
condition-specific smooths of time fitted with thin -plate 
regression splines [5]. Participant-level random intercepts and 
slopes were included to model between-participant variability 
at the group level, whereas participant-level random smooths 
were omitted to reduce computational burden. 
MMN and LDN are defined as negative deflections in the 
DEV-minus-STD waveform, that is, DEV – STD < 0. Because 
the cluster detection procedure identifies positive effects, the 
condition difference was coded as STD – DEV before testing. 
Under this coding scheme, positive clusters correspond to 
negative deflections in the original DEV -minus-STD 
waveform. 
Temporal smoothness was controlled using  the basis 
dimension k [5]. Candidate values of k were assessed using the 
recommend_k function in the neurogam package [4], which 
compares models using the Widely Applicable Information 
Criterion (WAIC). Lower WAIC values indicate better 
expected out-of-sample fit. In selecting k, we considered both 
WAIC and model diagnostics, favouring values that improved 
fit without causing substantial sampling problems.  The k-
recommendation results suggested k = 20 at 1000 Hz, 500 Hz 
and 250 Hz, and k = 30 at 100 Hz. However, because substantial 
divergent transitions were observed at 100 Hz across all tested 
k values, the final models were fitted with k = 20 to balance fit, 
sampling stability, and consistency across temporal resolutions. 
The estimated STD-minus-DEV difference was tested at 
each time point using 95% posterior credible intervals. To 
identify temporally sustained effects, credible time points were 
required to form a contiguous cluster lasting at least 40 ms. This 
was implemented in testing_through_time() by setting 
threshold = 40 for the 1000 Hz dataset, threshold = 20 for the 
500 Hz dataset, threshold = 10 for the 250 Hz dataset, and 
threshold = 4 for the 100 Hz dataset. As a sensitivity check, we 
compared minimum cluster-duration criteria of 20 ms, 30 ms, 
and 40 ms. Because the 20 ms and 30 ms criteria were more 
prone to isolated transient detections, we retained 40 ms as a 
conservative continuity requirement.  
2.4.2. Model convergence and sampling diagnostics 
Reliable interpretation of posterior estimates requires adequate 
Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) convergence and 
sampling quality. These were assessed using R -hat, the 
divergence rate, and the minimum Bulk_ESS and Tail_ESS 
values [22, 23]. R-hat reflects agreement across chains, with 
values close to 1 indicating good convergence. The divergence 
rate indicates potential sampling problems and should ideally 
be zero. Bulk_ESS and Tail_ESS summarize the effective 
sample size in the center and tails of the posterior distribution, 
with larger values indicating more reliable estimates. Models 
were considered to show acceptable sampling stability when R-
hat was below 1.01, the divergence rate was 0%, and the 
minimum Bulk_ESS and Tail_ESS values were at least 100 per 
chain (approximately 400 in total for four chains).  
3. Results 
3.1. Convergence diagnostics and sampling quality 
Table 1 summarizes convergence and sampling diagnostics for 
each model. The 1000 Hz, 500 Hz, and 250 Hz models showed 
acceptable sampling stability, with R-hat values of 1.00, 0% 
divergences, and minimum Bulk_ESS and Tail_ESS greater 
than 100. This indicates that the posterior distributions were 
sampled adequately, supporting reliable interpretation of the 
corresponding parameter estimates and credible intervals. In 
contrast, the 100 Hz model showed poorer sampling stability: 
R-hat values of up to 1.04, 5% divergences, and substantially 
lower minimum Bulk_ESS and Tail_ESS values. These 
diagnostics suggest that posterior estimation was less stable for 
the 100 Hz model, and its results should therefore be interpreted 
with greater caution.

Figure 1 shows the observed and Bayesian GAMM fitted 
STD-DEV waveforms with 95% credible intervals and detected 
clusters across the four temporal resolutions. Across all 
sampling rates, the model detected both an early deflection in 
the MMN window and a later, smaller deflection in the LDN 
window. However, the fitted waveform followed the observed 
waveform most closely at 1000 Hz, 500 Hz, and 250 Hz, while 
the 100 Hz model showed the least precise fit . The MMN 
pattern was similar from 1000 Hz to 250 Hz, whereas the LDN 
pattern varied more in timing and duration. At 100 Hz, both 
effects remained visible, but the detected MMN time window 
was broader, and the 95% credible intervals were wider. 
Overall, model fit and temporal precision decreased as the 
sampling rate decreased, with the most noticeable decrease at 
100 Hz. 
3.2. MMN-related cluster  
A primary positive cluster corresponding to the MMN-related 
effect was detected at all four sampling rates. In the 1000, 500, 
and 250 Hz models, its timing was highly consistent . The 
cluster spanned 227-345 ms at 1000 Hz (118 ms), 230-348 ms 
at 500 Hz (118 ms), and 232-364 ms at 250 Hz (132 ms), 
respectively. Across 1000-250 Hz, the onset varied by only 5 
ms, showing strong stability of the MMN -related latency 
estimate. The offset shifted slightly later at lower sampling 
rates, leading to a modest increase in duration. By contrast, at 
100 Hz, the primary cluster was broader, spanning 240-460 ms 
(220 ms). 
3.3. LDN-related cluster 
A later positive cluster corresponding to the LDN time window 
was also detected at all four sampling rates. The detected 
windows were 512-577 ms (65 ms) at 1000 Hz, 530-574 ms (44 
ms) at 500 Hz, 540-572 ms (32 ms) at 250 Hz, and 520-570 ms 
(50 ms) at 100 Hz. Across the 1000, 500, and 250 Hz models, 
the offset changed little, whereas the onset shifted more, leading 
to a shorter detected duration at 500 and 250 Hz. In the 100 Hz 
model, the estimated time window was similar to that at full 
resolution. 
Overall, the results show a graded, component-dependent 
effect of temporal downsampling. From 1000-250 Hz, the 
MMN-related onset was largely preserved, although the offset 
shifted later and the detected duration increased slightly. By 
contrast, the later LDN-related cluster showed larger shifts in 
timing and a shorter duration. At 100 Hz, both clusters 
remained detectable, but with less consistent onset estimates. 
Importantly, poorer model convergence suggests reduced 
reliability of posterior estimates at this low sampling density.

4. Discussion 
This study examined how temporal downsampling affects 
Bayesian GAMM-based ERP latency estimates, focusing on 
onset, offset, and persistence duration for two components with 
different levels of latency variability: MMN and LDN. The 
results showed a graded, component-specific pattern. The 
MMN-related effect remained relatively stable in its latency 
estimates from full to moderate temporal resolutions, whereas 
the later LDN-related component showed greater changes in 
onset and duration as the sampling rate decreased. At the most 
extreme downsampling level, the MMN-related effect showed 
a broader estimated time window, while the LDN-related effect 
showed a time window similar to that at full resolution. 
However, poor model convergence and sampling quality at this 
sampling rate indicated reduced temporal precision and less 
reliable estimates. 
4.1. Moderate downsampling largely preserves 
primary MMN timing 
Under moderate downsampling, the MMN -related latency 
estimates identified at full temporal resolution were largely 
preserved. The onset changed by only a few milliseconds, and 
the offset showed modest shifts. This suggests that, for an early 
component with relatively constrained latency variability, 
moderate downsampling introduces only limited distortion in 
model-based onset estimates. This finding is consistent with 
prior work describing MMN as an early, relatively tightly time-
locked response in oddball paradigms [1, 11, 15]. 
4.2. Later LDN timing is more vulnerable to reduced 
temporal resolution 
By contrast, the later LDN-related effect was more sensitive to 
reduced temporal resolution. Under moderate downsampling, 
its onset shifted later, and its duration became shorter. This 
suggests that downsampling did not affect all ERP components 
in the same way. Instead, the effect depended partly on the 
temporal characteristics of the component. This pattern is 
consistent with the finding that later ERP activity is often more 
temporally diffuse and may therefore be more vulnerable to 
reduced sampling density [12, 13]. In practice, moderately 
reducing the sampling rate may be acceptable when estimating 
the onset of a stable early ERP component, but such reductions 
may have a larger impact on later activity and on estimates of 
later component timing [14].

for Bayesian GAMM-based latency estimation
