Abstract
This study examines speech rhythm across English vari-
eties spoken in Northeast (NE) India using traditional rhythm
metrics and computational speech approaches. The dataset in-
cludes five NE Indian English varieties from different L1 back-
grounds, viz. Angami, Assamese, Khasi, Meiteilon, and Mizo,
together with two Hindi varieties spoken by native Hindi speak-
ers and Assamese speakers. Durational rhythm measures were
used to investigate language grouping patterns and served as
features for the linear mixed-effects models, Hierarchical Ag-
glomerative Clustering, and Support Vector Machine analyses.
The results revealed considerable overlap among the NE Indian
English varieties, whereas the Hindi varieties were more clearly
separated. Latent embedding vectors also showed a clear sep-
aration between Hindi and English varieties, while the NE In-
dian Englishes overlapped. A strong correlation between %V
and PC1 suggests that temporal rhythmic patterns are partially
captured in the embeddings.

1. Introduction
Northeast (NE) India presents an interesting case for prosodic
study as speakers from numerous distinct first-language (L1)
backgrounds use English and Hindi extensively in everyday
communication. English, one of the official languages of India,
plays a central role in the linguistic ecology of this region, func-
tioning as a language of education, administration, and inter-
group communication, alongside local languages, while Hindi
is also used as a lingua franca, although its degrees of accep-
tance vary across states and communities [1]. Several studies
have looked into the acoustic properties of NE Indian English
varieties, in which L1 influenced second-language (L2) English
[2, 3, 4]. However, relatively little attention has been paid to the
rhythm characteristics of the English varieties spoken by the
NE Indians. All these factors raised a question of whether the
speech rhythm differentiates varieties shaped by different L1s
or reflects convergence across groups.
Early studies conceptualised speech rhythm in terms of dis-
crete rhythmic classes, claiming that languages could be cat-
egorised based on their characteristic timing patterns: stress-
timed, syllable-timed [5, 6], and mora-timed languages [7, 8, 9].
Although typologically influential, subsequent empirical study
has shown that neither strict syllable nor foot isochrony is sys-
tematically realised in natural speech and is phonetically unat-
tested [10]. A shift toward metric-based rhythm analysis fol-
lowed the introduction of the acoustic segmentation model [11].
This model uses vocalic and consonantal intervals to quan-
tify the rhythmic organisation based on the proportion of vo-
calic intervals in the utterance(%V), the standard deviation of
vocalic interval durations(∆V)and the standard deviation of
consonantal interval durations(∆C). It demonstrated that lan-
guages are not categorical, but rather fall along a continuum of
temporal organisation. Building on this insight, more metrics,
namely, pairwise variability indices (nPVI,rPVI), were pro-
posed to capture durational variability between successive in-
tervals [12]. Further refinement came with rate-normalised in-
terval measures,VarCo-CandVarCo-V, which allow rhythmic
variation to be assessed independently of speech rate [13, 14].
Moreover, VarCo-V revealed that L2 speakers occupied an in-
termediate rhythmic position between their L1 and L2, pro-
viding evidence of L1 influence [14, 15]. Despite extensive
research on speech rhythm, key questions remain regarding
the discriminability of closely related varieties produced by
speakers with different L1 backgrounds. Previous studies on
Swedish [16] and Finnish, [17] have used embeddings from the
Tallinn University of Technology-trained language identifica-
tion (LID) model voxlingua107-xls-r-300m-wav2vec [18]. To
the best of our knowledge, except for one work that used latent
embeddings to classify L1 and L2 Hindi [19], no prior work has
utilised language- or dialect-based latent embeddings for cat-
egorising accented speech in Indian languages. The analysis
highlights prosodic similarities and differences between native
and non-native speech, offering insights into L1 influence on L2
prosody.
The current study aims to assess whether rhythm metrics
and latent embeddings can distinguish five NE Indian English
varieties and two Hindi varieties. First, we examined tempo-
ral rhythm patterns using rhythm metrics, and we evaluated
differences using linear mixed-effects models. Secondly, us-
ing Hierarchical Agglomerative Clustering [20], we examined
groupings via an unsupervised clustering approach. For super-
vised classification, we trained a Support Vector Machine [21]
to determine how accurately rhythm features predict language
varieties. Finally, to situate rhythm within a broader acoustic-
phonetic context and to check whether latent embeddings corre-
late with temporal rhythm measures, we employ latent embed-
ding vectors extracted from the speech recordings to classify
languages. These approaches altogether provide a comprehen-
sive view of rhythmic and acoustic variation across five North-
east Indian English groups and two Hindi groups.

3. Methodology
3.1. Participants and materials
Speech data were collected from 70 adult speakers, divided
into seven groups, each with 10 speakers. Five groups rep-
resented NE Indian English spoken by individuals whose L1s
were Angami, Assamese, Khasi, Meiteilon, and Mizo. Two ad-
ditional groups consisted of Hindi passage readings produced
by native Hindi speakers and by Assamese speakers. All speech
consisted of read productions of “The North Wind and the Sun”.
The five English groups read the English version of the passage,
whereas the two Hindi groups read a Hindi translation of the
same text. All participants reading the English passage were
educated speakers who had completed their studies in English-
medium schools, ensuring high reading proficiency. Speakers
who read the Hindi translation of the passage were literate in
Hindi and fluent readers. Speakers were asked to read the pas-
sage at their normal and comfortable rate without hesitation or
errors.
3.2. Speech data annotation and feature extraction
Speech segmentation and annotation were performed manually
using Praat [23]. Each recording was first divided into Breath g
Groups (BG). Then, within each BG, phones were segmented,
and all the phoneme-level intervals were labelled as vocalic (V)
or consonantal (C). A Praat script extracted interval durations.
A Perl script then computed the eight standard rhythm metrics
(%V ,∆V ,∆C, VarCo-V , VarCo-C, nPVI-V , nPVI-C, rPVI-C).
These measures served as input to all subsequent statistical and
machine-learning procedures.
3.3. Rhythm Metrics and Statistical Analysis
Pearson correlation analysis was conducted to assess relation-
ships among the rhythm metrics. This analysis provides insight
into how different metrics capture related aspects of rhythmic
variability and temporal organisation in speech. Based on the
previous studies [11, 12], a rhythm-plane scatter plot was gen-
erated using the metric pair (%V vs nPVI-V) to check whether
varieties cluster or overlap in a temporal two-dimensional vi-
sualisation. Mean values for each rhythm metric were calcu-
lated for each language variety. Statistical comparisons across
varieties were performed using Linear Mixed Effects (LME)
models implemented in R using the lme4 package [24, 25, 26].
In these models, language variety was included as a fixed ef-
fect, and speaker identity was treated as a random intercept to
account for individual speaker variability. The general model
structure was: Metric∼Language+ (1|Speaker). Signifi-
cance testing for fixed effects was conducted using Type II Wald
chi-square tests.
3.4. Clustering and Classification
Unsupervised clustering was performed using Hierarchical Ag-
glomerative Clustering (HAC) with Ward’s linkage and Eu-
clidean distance on z-standardised rhythm vectors. HAC was
used to test whether the rhythm metric features alone produce
clusters based on their L1s. Dendrograms were examined to
determine whether clusters correspond to the varieties. A lin-
ear Support Vector Machine (SVM; LinearSVC, C=1.0, class-
balanced) was employed to evaluate how effectively the rhythm
metrics distinguish the seven varieties. The eight rhythm met-
rics were z-score normalised prior to modelling. A balanced
test set was constructed by sampling an equal number of to-
kens from each variety. Performance was assessed using a row-
normalised confusion matrix, and feature importance was esti-
mated from the mean absolute coefficients of the linear SVM.
3.5. Latent Embedding Vectors
We conducted the categorisation of L1-L2 Hindi and L2 En-
glish. To maintain uniformity, we added L1 English data
(ENG) [27, 28, 29] for latent embedding-based classification.
The categorisation of the eight speech classes is based on
BG-level representations computed using embeddings from the
language identification (LID) model V oxlingua107-xls-r-300m-
wav2vec [18]. Using the V oxlingua107 dataset, the XLS-R
300M model was fine-tuned for LID, and it produces 2,048-
dimensional latent embedding vectors of input utterances [30,
31]. To analyse the differences between L1 and L2 speech,
we transformed the embedding space using Linear Discriminant
Analysis (LDA). Given the sample size per class in our dataset
being lower than the dimensionality of the embedding space,
we first reduced the dimensionality of the space to 42 using
principal components analysis (PCA) to avoid overfitting the
LDA model. 42 dimensions correspond to the lowest number
of utterances in one of the classes. These 42 latent embedding
vectors were then projected on a 2-dimensional LDA space. To
examine the relationship between latent speech representations
and rhythmic properties, correlations were computed between
the principal components of the embeddings and the rhythm
metrics. Pearson correlations were computed for each pair of
principal components and rhythm metrics. Multiple testing cor-
rections were applied using both the Bonferroni correction and
the false discovery rate (FDR) procedure.

4.3. Latent Embedding Vectors
The 42-dimensional latent embedding vectors of the eight
classes were projected onto a 2D LDA space. The scatterplot
as shown in Figure 4 reveal clear clustering patterns across
L2-accented Englishes. The AHI overlaps with HIN, indicat-
ing a possible adaptation to L2 prosody, while its closeness to
ASM indicates Assamese L1 influence on both L2 Hindi and
English speech. The five northeast Indian L1 groups speaking
L2 English form a cluster, indicating a common Indian English
prosodic pattern with subtle L1-specific shifts. Strong over-
lap among the LUS, NJM and MNI clusters likely reflects their
Tibeto-Burman L1 background as against the L1s of ASM and
KHA belonging to the Indo-Aryan and Austro-Asiatic groups.
LDA projections primarily separate embeddings by language in
the first dimension and by prosodic style in the second. Com-
pared to HIN and ENG, the L2s show greater dispersion, indi-
cating possible variability in L2 proficiency. The dendrogram
in Figure 5 further shows strong similarity among the North-
east Englishes, followed by L1 English. The L1 and L2 Hindi
groups appear at the highest linkage distance, indicating lan-
guage differences. L1 English doesn’t cluster with L2 English,
indicating great prosodic differences between Northeast Indian
English and British English.
To further investigate the relationship between embed-
ding representations and rhythm measures, Pearson correlations
were computed between the embedding principal components
and the rhythm metrics. Among the metrics examined, the
strongest association was observed between the first principal
component (PC1) and the vocalic proportion (%V), showing a
strong positive correlation (r= 0.77,p <0.001). This re-
lationship remained significant after both Bonferroni and false
discovery rate (FDR) correction. PC1 also showed moderate
negative correlations with several variability-based rhythm met-
rics, including∆C (r=−0.41), rPVI (r=−0.35), and nPVI-
V (r=−0.30). However, these correlations were substantially
weaker. Overall, these results suggest that variation in %V is
strongly reflected in the primary dimension of the embedding

5. Conclusions
This paper examined rhythmic variation across five NE In-
dian English varieties and two Hindi varieties using traditional
rhythm metrics, then classified the varieties based on these
rhythm-metric features. Although the rhythm plane plot and
statistical tests showed significant differences, the overall range
of rhythm metric values across the five English varieties was
narrow, indicating that the varieties remain broadly similar in
their rhythmic patterns. HAC exhibited significant overlap
within the English varieties, although it clustered the Hindi va-
rieties separately. The SVM classification revealed that the En-
glish varieties show only subtle rhythmic differences, whereas
the Hindi varieties remain rhythmically distinct. Finally, the
embeddings revealed that self-supervised speech representa-
tions capture numerous acoustic and prosodic cues that extend
beyond the durational rhythm measures. Overall, this work
highlights the significance of combining traditional phonetic
methods with latent speech representations.
