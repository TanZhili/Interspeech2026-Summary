Codec-induced Mismatch, Speech Duration, and Speaker-dependent Effect in
a DNN-based Forensic Speaker Recognition System
Guangmou Deng ID 1, Bruce Xiao Wang ID 1, Vincent Hughes ID 2
1 Department of English and Communication, Hong Kong Polytechnic University, China

Abstract
The performance of forensic automatic speaker recognition
(FASR) systems under the combined effects of codec-induced
mismatch and different questioned-speech (QS) durations re-
mains insufficiently understood, particularly at the individual
speaker level. This study investigates the combined impact
of representative lossy speech codecs (G.711 A-law, AMR-
NB, and Opus) and QS duration on a DNN (ResNet34-MHA)-
based FASR system. System-level performance was generally
good across all codec conditions, especially with>30s speech.
Codec effects vary, however, with low-bitrate AMR-NB causing
the greatest degradation in performance and Opus having mini-
mal impact. Crucially, individual-level analyses reveal substan-
tial speaker-dependent variability that is obscured by system-
level metrics: some speakers exhibit persistently poor or un-
stable performance, and speakers with poorer overall perfor-
mance are more sensitive to codec-induced mismatch. These
findings demonstrate that promising system-level performance
does not guarantee good performance for all speakers and high-
lights the need for individual-level analysis when testing DNN-
based FASR systems.
Index Terms: forensic automatic speaker recognition, speaker
embedding, codec-induced mismatch, net-speech duration,

1. Introduction
Forensic voice comparison (FVC) typically involves comparing
two speech samples: one questioned sample (QS) from an un-
known offender, and one known sample (KS) from a suspect.
The task of the expert is then to estimate the likelihood ratio
(LR) that quantifies the strength of evidence in support of the
same-speaker versus different-speaker propositions.
Mismatch between samples introduced by technical degra-
dations is a critical consideration in FVC, with channel mis-
match being one of the most frequent sources of variability in
casework. Channels can degrade speech signals in two pri-
mary ways. First, the combined effects of microphone proper-
ties and channel-specific bandwidth limitations alter the spec-
tral envelope of the speech signal, e.g., F1 tends to be esti-
mated as higher, whereas F4 is lower, in telephone-transmitted
recordings compared with high-quality recording [1, 2]. Sec-
ond, lossy compression codecs, designed to improve transmis-
sion efficiency, introduce irreversible distortions and artifacts by
prioritizing perceptually salient components while sacrificing
fine-grained spectral and temporal details that may be informa-
tive for FVC. In forensic casework, such technical mismatch is
often compounded by limited amounts of available speech: the
QS is typically channel-transmitted and is often more limited in
duration, whereas the KS is recorded using a high-quality mi-
crophone during police interview. The combined effect of these
factors introduces substantial uncertainty, posing challenges for
FVC.
Driven by technological advances and the modern require-
ments of forensic science for empirical validation [3], foren-
sic automatic speaker recognition (FASR) is increasingly being
adopted in casework practice internationally [4, 5]. Currently,
deep neural network (DNN) embedding-based systems have
demonstrated state-of-the-art performance under forensically
realistic conditions involving complex channel mismatch [6, 7].
DNN embeddings are highly speaker-discriminative representa-
tions abstracted from variable-length speech signals [8]. Back-
end statistical models of the FASR system use these embed-
dings to calculate numerical likelihood ratios (LRs). A small
numbers of studies [9, 10, 11] have investigated the perfor-
mance of DNN-embedding-based systems under codec-induced
mismatch and variable speech durations. However, the implica-
tions of these studies for forensic purposes are limited in three
key ways:
1. Forensic scenarios involve a wide range of channel condi-
tions and codecs based on different technologies. Previ-
ous studies have largely focused on single codec or matched
condition, with a lack of systematic, parallel evaluations for
forensic purposes.
2. Most codec-related studies and investigations of net speech
duration [9, 11] consider these factors in isolation. It remains
unclear how codec-induced mismatch interacts with speech
duration, or whether the amount of speech required for re-
liable system performance differs across mismatched condi-
tions.
3. As demonstrated in recent FASR research [12, 13], systems
considered “state-of-the-art” based on overall performance
may still perform poorly for some speakers. Existing bench-
marking studies typically evaluate overall system-level per-
formance without individual-level analysis, masking variabil-
ity in how individual speakers are treated under specific con-
ditions, and offer limited insight into how codec conditions
might impact performance for specific speakers, which is
critical for the forensic interpretation in individual cases.
The current study aims to investigate the effect of differ-
ent representative codecs on a DNN-based FASR system across
various QS durations. By incorporating individual-level analy-
sis, this study examines how codec-induced mismatch and QS
duration jointly impact individual-level performance, assessing
whether the system performs consistently across all speakers,
and identifying key speaker-dependent effects.

2. Methods
2.1. Materials
Non-contemporaneous speech data from 102 young male Hong
Kong Cantonese (HKC) speakers (mean age 23, range 18-33)
were used. Each speaker contributed 4 recordings across two
sessions (2 per session) separated by at least three weeks: a
mock police interview and a topic-card-elicited free talk task
in Session 1, and an accomplice-style conversation and a fur-
ther free talk task in Session 2. All recordings were collected
in a sound-treated laboratory using a close-talk microphone
(about 30 cm) to avoid clipping. Recordings were stored as
44.1 kHz/16-bit PCM.wav, and manually segmented to retain
speech-active portions of the target speaker only, such that the
recording durations equal the net speech durations used in sub-
sequent analyses. These high-quality (HQ) samples served as
the basis for following codec degradation:
2.1.1. G.711 A-law
G.711 A-law [14] is a typical lossy compression algorithm used
in the landline telephony. It achieves a bit-rate of 64 kb/s by
applying logarithmic compression to waveform, followed by 8-
bit PCM quantization at an 8 kHz sampling rate.
2.1.2. Adaptive Multi-Rate Narrowband (AMR-NB)
AMR-NB is a parametric speech codec, widely used in mobile
telecom. Unlike G.711 A-law, it represents the speech signal by
a set of time-varying model parameters (e.g., LPC coefficients)
rather than the waveform. AMR-NB supports eight discrete bit-
rate modes of 8 kHz sampling rate, and two bit-rate settings
were considered in the present study: a high bit-rate condition
at 12.2 kb/s and a low bit-rate condition at 6.7 kb/s.
2.1.3. Opus
Opus [15] is a modern codec widely employed for V oIP telecom
and voice messaging in social applications (e.g., WhatsApp).
Opus is designed to make more efficient use of available bit-
rate by psychoacoustic models under low-latency scenarios. In
this study, Opus was configured to approximate the high-quality
voice messaging settings commonly used in social application,
with a sampling rate of 16 kHz and a target bit-rate of 128 kb/s.
G.711 A-law and AMR-NB conditions were simulated us-
ingFFmpeg[16], and the Opus condition using thelibopus[17].
2.2. Front-end acoustic feature extraction
Recordings were resampled and converted to 8 kHz/16-bit
PCM.wav and then processed with a pre-emphasis filter with
a coefficient of 0.97. Log-Mel filterbank (Fbank) features are
computed using a 25ms Hamming window and a 10ms frame
shift, with a 512-point FFT applied to obtain the spectrum. The
spectrum (20-3900 Hz frequency range) was subsequently sam-
pled by 64 Mel-filterbanks and log-scaled, yielding a [64,T]
Fbank matrix. Cepstral mean and variance normalization [18]
is applied as a front-end mismatch compensation strategy.
2.3. Deep speaker representation modeling
We adopted an architecture based on a ResNet34 backbone [19]
enhanced with multi-head attentive statistics pooling (MHA,
see [20]), referred to as ResNet34-MHA.
Given a Fbank feature of lengthT, the network first mod-
els the frame-level speaker representation using the ResNet34
backbone and aggregates it into a fixed-length utterance-level
representation via the MHA pooling layer. The ResNet34 con-
sists of four residual layers with{3, 4, 6, 3}basic blocks with
{32, 64, 128, 256}output channels. Each basic block follows
the standard ResNet34 formulation described in [19].
After the final residual layer, the feature tensor is reshaped
by flattening the frequency and channel dimensions, yielding a
sequence of frame-level feature vectors. An 8-head MHA pool-
ing layer then estimates frame-wise attention weights over time
and computes weighted mean and standard deviation statistics
per channel. Statistics from all heads are concatenated to form
a holistic utterance-level representation, then projected by a lin-
ear layer into a compact 512-dimensional speaker embedding.
The ResNet34-MHA is trained on the V oxCeleb1+2
datasets [21, 22], comprising 7,205 speakers. To enhance gen-
eralization under complex acoustic conditions, we applied ex-
tensive data augmentation during training: additive noise from
MUSAN [23] and room reverberation simulated from RIR
dataset [24] are randomly introduced with varying intensities;
temporal-spectral masking [25] is used by randomly masking
contiguous regions of the input Fbank. During training, each
mini-batch consists of 256 augmented Fbank sequences (200
frames × 64 bins). The network was optimised with the ad-
ditive angular margin (AAM-) softmax loss [26] (margin 0.2,
scale 30) using Adam. The learning rate is gradually warmed
up to 0.001 over the first 5 epochs and then decayed following
a cosine annealing schedule for 105 epochs. The final model
was obtained by averaging parameters from the last 10 epochs
to improve evaluation stability.
2.4. Back-end statistical interpretation and testing protocol
68 (out of 102) HKC speakers were used to train an Linear Dis-
criminant Analysis-Probabilistic Linear Discriminant Analysis
(LDA-PLDA) model for feature-to-score computation, with the
remaining 34 used to generate trials between questioned and
known conditions for calibration and testing by leave-one/two-
out cross validation. LDA-PLDA was adopted because it pro-
duced LR scores that incorporate both similarity and typicality,
which is essential for forensic interpretation [27], while cosine
scoring provides only similarity measures. Although relatively
small compared with datasets commonly used in non-forensic
speaker verification, the training set was sampled to reflect a
narrowly defined and relatively homogeneous relevant popula-
tion, i.e., young male HK Cantonese speakers, and was there-
fore considered representative. In addition, this study focuses
on condition-wise effects, rather than absolute performance es-
timates. Session 1 recordings were treated as the KS, and Ses-
sion 2 recordings as the QS, ensuring the cross-session varia-
tion [28]. Codec degradation was applied only to the QS before
pairing with the KS for testing. QS durations from 5s to 90s
were tested in 5s increments by retaining the first 5Nseconds of
each QS recording, while recordings of the LDA-PLDA train-
ing set and KS in the test set were untruncated and all exceeded
90s, reflecting typical forensic casework in which such samples
are generally unconstrained. After excluding 3 QS recordings
shorter than 90s, the test set contained 130 same-speaker (SS)
and 4,292 different-speaker (DS) trials.
A total of 272 speaker embeddings from the training set
were used to train an LDA model, with the dimensionality
reduced to 50. This transformation aims to retain speaker-
discriminative information while mitigating mismatch effects,

2.5. Evaluation metrics
2.5.1. System-level performance
The Log-likelihood-ratio Cost (C llr) was used to assess system
performance.C llr penalizes a set of SS and DS LR outputs for
producing contrary-to-fact LRs for SS and DS propositions [32,
33]. AC llr less than 1 means the system provides informative
LRs, and values closer to 0 indicate better performance.
2.5.2. Individual-level performance
To evaluate individual-level performance, the by-speakerC llr
(Cspk
llr ) was computed using SS LRs for a given individual and
DS LRs between that individual and all other speakers in the
testing set [12]. This metric quantifies how well the system
avoids producing contrary-to-fact LRs for trials involving that
speaker; higherC spk
llr indicate poorer performance. It’s worth
noting that theC spk
llr is estimated from limited LRs per speaker,
and is therefore expected to exhibit higher sampling variability
than system-levelC llr. Accordingly, we focus on identifying
systematic patterns, rather than diagnosing the cause of poor
performance for individual speakers.

4. Discussion
The present study examined the performance of a DNN-based
FASR system involving codec-induced mismatch conditions.
The system-level performance is promising, even when mis-
match associated with low-bitrate speech compression. Re-
sults are consistent with previous findings [9], showing that
mismatch involving codecs of the lower-bitrate setting can lead
to greater degradation in DNN-based FASR performance than
mismatch involving higher-bitrate compression algorithms. Our
results also suggest that modern perceptual coding schemes
(Opus) are substantially more effective at preserving speaker-
specific information than earlier generations of speech codecs.
The present study focuses on the impact of typical lossy codecs
encountered in communication channels. In real forensic sce-
narios, the spectral characteristics of speech signals may be fur-
ther affected by a wide range of factors (see Section 1), and may
involve multiple coding stages, e.g., speech transmitted over a
landline network is subsequently recorded via a mobile device.
Such multi-stage codec effects warrant further investigation.
Although a codec introduces consistent distortions, its im-
pact on individual-level performance can vary markedly across
speakers. In particular, speakers who are already challenging
for the system are more sensitive to lossy-compression distor-
tions. An ideal and generalizable model should produce speaker
representations that are robust across different acoustic condi-
tions, and such robustness should be realized consistently rather
than varying across speakers. When robustness is realized in a
speaker-dependent manner, factors that the model is intended
to disentangle from speaker-specific information may dispro-
portionately affect certain speakers, leading to greater within-
speaker variability in the embedding space and poorer perfor-
mance for those speakers. Our findings provide evidence con-
sistent with this mechanism. However, current speaker embed-
ding models are typically trained on data that are limited in di-
versity [34], and optimized using loss functions that prioritize
overall discrimination over the limited training data. Conse-
quently, robustness may unavoidably be poorer for some speak-
ers. In future work, understanding the properties of speakers
who are more sensitive to channel mismatch may support a
more targeted application of FASR (e.g., defining an appropri-
ate reference population under specific case).
5. Conclusion
This study examined the impact of lossy compression and
QS duration on a DNN-based FASR system. While system-
level performance remained promising under a range of codec-
induced mismatch conditions, individual-level validation re-
vealed speaker-dependent variability from multiple dimensions
that was not apparent from overall metrics alone. Our study
highlights the speaker-dependent effect, pointing to a broader
limitation of current DNN-embedding models that is difficult to
fully address under current training paradigms.
