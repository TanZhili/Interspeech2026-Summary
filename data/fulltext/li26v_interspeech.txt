Abstract 
This study explores the acoustic properties of contrastive tones 
at different heights in different vowel contexts  and in different 
tonal systems. Contrastive tones were recorded from two 
Chinese Xiang dialects that distinguish tones at three and four 
heights respectively. The tones contrast in three vowel contexts, 
including apical [ ɹ̩], high-front [i], and low -back [a]. The 
measurements included F0 trajectories, mean F0, duration, 
intensity, as well as voice qualities such as H1* -H2*, HNR, 
CPP. The main findings are: (i) high tones generally showed 
shorter duration, higher intensity, and more periodicity than low 
tones; (ii) for vowel contexts, different tones  had a wider F0 
range in [i] than in [ɹ̩] and [a]; (iii) for different tonal systems, 
the results of XGBoost+SHAP analyses confirmed the high 
importance of F0 cues in distinguishing the tones and a 
tendency for a tonal system with more height distinctions to rely 
on more cues in differentiating its tones. 
Index Terms : tonal contrast, voice quality, vowel context, 
tonal system, Chinese dialect 
1. Introduction 
1.1. Tone with multiple cues 
Tone is the use of pitch to distinguish meaning, such as [ma] 
with a high-level tone (or 55 using Chao’s numerals) referring  
to 妈 ‘mom’ while [ma] with a falling tone (51) corresponding 
to 骂 ‘to scold’ in Mandarin. Fundamental frequency (F0) has 
been long recognized as a major acoustic correlate of tone. That 
being the case, extensive recent studies have shown that t ones 
can be realized through the integration of multiple acoustic cues, 
including fundamental frequency (F0), duration, intensity, and 
voice qualities [1, 2].  
1.2. Tones and vowel contexts 
Different vowels such as [i] vs. [a] are produced with different 
articulatory gestures, thus different shapes of the vocal tract, 
which have different spectral properties. For vowel and pitch, it 
has long been established that vowels at different heights may 
have different intrinsic pitch properties [3]. For English male 
speakers, for example, [i] has an average F0 of 129 Hz while [a] 
has an average F0 of 120 Hz  [4, 5]. For different vowels, the 
different intrinsic F0 properties may lead to vowel -specific 
acoustic properties in the realization of a tonal category. For the 
same tonal category, such as Tone 1 (high- level or 55) in 
Mandarin, previous studies reported diffe rent mean F0 values, 
e.g., 175 Hz in the [i] context vs. 154 Hz in the [a] context for 
male speakers [6].  
Apical vowels are syllabic approximants [7, 8], such as the 
rime of [s ɹ̩] 思  ‘to think’  in Mandarin,  which are usually 
homorganic to their preceding sibilant consonants. Different 
from most vowel segments, apical vowels in Mandarin Chinese 
and Chinese dialects are typically featured by the presence of

Tones in apical vowels are shown to have special properties, 
e.g., the same tone with a lower HNR in an apical vowel as 
compared with in a non-apical vowel [14]. 
1.3. Tonal inventory and tonal space  
Tonal languages differ in the number of tonal categories and 
tonal heights. Chinese dialects have diverse tonal inventories, 
for example, two tones in Honggu [15], four tones in Mandarin, 
and six tones in Cantonese [16, 17] . For the F0 cues of tones, 
studies in the literature observed that, somewhat contrary to 
intuition, a larger tonal inventory might not necessarily 
correlate with a larger F0 range [18] . On the other hand, a 
complex tonal system, such as five level tones in Black Miao, 
may make use of phonation cues to distinguish tones at different 
heights [1].  
1.4. Objectives 
This study explores the acoustic properties of tones in multiple 
aspects along with established studies [1, 2, 19] , with three 
objectives. First, focusing on tonal contrast, we explore the 
contribution of different acoustic properties in the realization of 
tones at different heights. Second, referring to the intrinsic pitch 
difference in different vowels, we explore the realization of the 
same tonal contrasts in different vowel contexts, e.g., apical 
vowel vs. non-apical vowels. Third, focusing on tonal systems, 
we examine the potential difference in the use of  acoustic cues 
across different tonal systems that distingu ish tonal heights at 
fewer vs. more levels.  
2. Methodology 
For the objectives of this study, we recorded tones from two 
Chinese dialects with different tonal systems. We measured the 
acoustic properties of the tones in different vowel contexts and 
examined the relative weightings of the acoustic properties  in 
distinguishing the tones in each vowel context.  
2.1. Materials 
In this study, we focused on the tones of two Chinese Xiang 
dialects, Shouyan (SY) [20] , which is reported to have an 
inventory of six contrastive tones /43, 11, 33, 31, 51, 24 /, and 
Meihua (MH) [21] , which also has an inventory of six 
contrastive tones /51, 21, 55, 33, 44, 35/. Of interest to this study 
is the fact that, focusing on level tones or those close to level 
tones, the two dialects differ in the number of tonal heights: SY 
distinguishes tones at three h eights, namely /43, 33, 11/, while 
MH distinguishes tones at four heights, namely /55, 44, 33, 21/.

2.2. Speakers 
Nine SY male speakers and eight MH male speakers 
participated in the recording, who lived regularly in the two 
areas. No speakers reported speaking or hearing impairment.  
2.3. Procedure  
The recordings were done at quiet rooms close to where the 
speakers lived . The stimulus words were presented using an 
iPad, one word on each screen. The target words were 
embedded in the carrier sentence 我读 __ 九回 ‘I read __ nine 
times’, which was shown on the upper part of a screen. For each 
target word, a visual image correspo nding to its meaning 
appeared in the lower part of the same screen, e.g., an image of 
rooster for [ts ɹ̩43] 鸡 ‘rooster’. For each target word, a speaker 
produced nine tokens.  
The recording devices included a head -worn Microphone 
(Shure SM10A-CN) connected to a portable recorder (Tascam 
DR-100MKIII). The recordings were done at a sampling rate of 
44.1 kHz with a resolution at 16 bit. The recorded sound files 
were processed and segmented in Praat [22] . The tones were 
measured across the vocalic portion of each syllable, including 
F0 trajectories, mean F0, duration, intensity, etc., using the 
Praat script ProsodyPro 6.1.3 [23] . The voice qualities of the 
vocalic portions were measured using V oiceSauce [24] in 
MATLAB R2025a [25], including properties such as H1*-H2*, 
Harmonic-to-Noise Ratio (HNR), Cepstral Peak Prominence 
(CPP), and Strength of Excitation (SoE), etc.  
2.4. Statistics  
Statistical analyses were performed in the R environment using 
the lme4 [26]  and lmerTest [27]  packages to fit linear mixed -
effects models (LMMs). Prior to modeling, the data were Z -
score normalized. Specifically, a growth curve analysis was 
employed for the F0 data.  Data visualization was primarily 
conducted using ggplot2. Separate models were constructed for 
the two dialects, MH and SY, respectively. 
Following the “ keep it maximal ” principle advocated by  
[28], we incorporated both random intercepts and random 
slopes for all confirmatory hypothesis testing. For the F0 and 
voice quality metrics, Tone Category  and Vowel Context were 
entered as fixed effects, inclu ding their interaction. The 
random-effects structure included Speaker and Item. In cases of 
non-convergence, the models were simplified or the optimizers 
were adjusted to ensure stability. Post -modeling diagnostics 
were performed, and the models were refit after the trimming 
of outliers based on residual analysis. Main effects and 
interactions were evaluated using the mixed() function from the 
afex package [29], while post -hoc pairwise comparisons were 
conducted using the emmeans package [30]. 
Additionally, we employed a series of combined analyses 
of XGBoost [31] and SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations ) 
to explore the relative contributions of the multiple acoustic 
cues to tonal contrast. Hyperparameters were optimized via 
cross-validation and early stopping was implemented to prevent 
overfitting. Model performance was evaluated using confusion 
matrices and the contribution of each feature was quantified 
through XGBoost feature importance and SHAP values. 
3. Results 
3.1. F0, duration, intensity  
Figure 1 below illustrates the F0 trajectories of the tones in the 
three vowel contexts across SY and MH, in which a s ymbol 
such as t55 refers to the tonal category reported as 55 in the 
literature, without indicating its tonal value as observed in the 
current study.  
 
Figure 1: F0 trajectories (Hz). 
Focusing on the three vowel contexts, the [i ] context 
introduced a relatively larger F0 range of the contrastive tones, 
as compared with the apical [ɹ̩] and the low-back [a]. The same 
tendency held true across SY and MH, with a slight difference: 
in SY the vowel [a] had the smallest F0 range of its tones while 
in MH the apical [ɹ̩] has the smallest F0 range.  
In SY, the relative F0 trajectories across the three vowel 
contexts generally matched the relative pitch heights of the 
three tones as reported in the literature [20] , with variation in 
the relative distance between the F0 trajectories of the three 
tones. Growth curve analysis (GCA) results indicated that mean 
F0 differed significantly between all tone pairs across the three 
vowel contexts. Furthermore, the slope of t43 was significantly 
steeper than those of t11 and t33 (all ps < .05). Additionally, in 
high-vowel environments, signific ant differences in curvature 
were observed between t43 and t11 and t33 (all ps < .05).  
In MH, the same held for the three tones t55, t44 and t33, 
whose F0 trajectories generally matched their relative heights 
as reported in [21]  across the three vowel contexts. The F0 
trajectories of t21 in MH, however, showed different patterns 
in relation to the other tones across the three vowel contexts: 
lowest and slightly below t33 in the [ɹ̩] context, between t33 and

in different vowel contexts. In SY, a higher tone was constantly

both [i] and [a] contexts, consistent with the trend observed in

in contrast to its longest duration in [i] and [a] . Statistical 
analyses confirmed significant differences in t he durations of 
t33 across the three vowels, with a shorter  duration in [ɹ̩] than 
in [i] ( p < .05) and [a] ( p < .001) and a shorter duration in [i] 
than in [a] (p < .01).

Focusing on vowel contexts, the [a] context generally 
introduced a higher intensity than the apical [ɹ̩ ] and the high-
front [i]. This pattern is consistent with the established 
observation in the li terature that low vowels tend to have a

Across SY and MY, a higher tone generally had a relatively 
higher intensity across most of the vocalic portions of the tones. 
In SY, for example, t43 and t33 constantly had a higher

3.2. Voice qualities  
Figure 4 presents the H1* -H2* profiles of the tones across 
different vowel contexts, by which a higher/positive value 
correlates to more breathy voice and a smaller value close to 
modal voice. Focusing on the different vowel contexts, a 
general pattern turned out to be that the contrastive tones had a 
larger H1*-H2* difference in the [i] context than in the other 
two vowel contexts. In [ɹ̩ ] and [a]  alike, the contrastive tones 
had a relatively smaller difference in term of H1* -H2*. 
Specifically, the H1*-H2* values for t 11 were significantly 
lower than those for t33 and t43 in the [i] and [a] contexts ( all 
ps < .05). Interestingly, however, this trend was reversed in the 
[ɹ̩] environment (p < .001). 
For MH, similarly, in the [i] context, the high tones t44, t55 
generally had a high H1*-H2* value (all ps < .05), indicting 
more breathy voice associated with these two tones. One pattern 
that stood out was that, in the [i] context of MH, the phonation 
of the t33 differed greatly from the other tones by being low.   
 
Figure 4: H1*-H2* profiles (dB). 
Figure 5 presents the HNR35 values of the tones, by which 
a higher value indicates more periodicity. As a general pattern 
across SY and MH, a high tone such as t43 and t55 turned out 
to have a higher HNR profile, indicting more periodicity, as 
compared with a low tone such as t11 or t21 (all ps < .05).  
Across the three vowel contexts in SY and MH, the 
dynamic change of HNR (e.g., the rising of HNR values) tended 
to be more salient in the two high vowels ( [ɹ̩] and [i]) than in 
the low vowel [a]. Across the three vowels, the HNR profiles 
generally reached their peaks during the latter halves of a tone, 
except for t43 in SY and t21 in MH, which reached their peaks 
during the first halves. The latter coincided with the fact that

3.3. Comparing two tonal systems  
For the two tonal systems in SY and MH, a series of 
XGBoost+SHAP analyses were performed for their tones in the 
three vowels respectively, to determine the relative importance 
of the acoustic and voice properties in distinguishing the tones. 
As shown in Figures 8 and 9, for both dialects, the results 
confirmed the high importance of F0 cues such as mean F0 in 
distinguishing the tones, with varied relative importance of 
voice quality cues across vowel contexts.  
4. Conclusions 
For vowel contexts, the results in this study showed: (i) the F0 
range of the different tones tends to be larger in the [i] context 
than in [ɹ̩ ] and [a] across the two tonal systems; (ii)  the voice 
properties of low tones tend to have more diverse patterns than 
those of high tones across different vowels.  For tones at 
different heights, the measurements showed: (i) higher tones are 
shorter than lower tones across vowel contexts, except for the 
apical vowel [ɹ̩] in MH; (ii) high tones are generally louder than 
low tones across different vowels, except for the high intensity 
profile of Tone 21 in [a] in MH; (iii) higher tones are in general 
more periodic than lower tones, as indicated by the 
measurements of HNR and CPP across the three vowel contexts.

The results supported the contention that, in a tonal system, 
tones at different heights may have multi -dimensional 
differences. The different patterns across vowel contexts 
indicated that the same tonal contrast might be realized with 
different weightings of cues in different vowel contexts . The 
comparison between SY and MH further suggested that a tonal 
system with more height distinctions might rely on more cues 
to differentiate its tones.
