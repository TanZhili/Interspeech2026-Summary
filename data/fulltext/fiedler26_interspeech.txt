Abstract
Heart failure (HF) causes recurrent acute decompensation
(ADHF) requiring hospitalization. Fluid overload affects the
vocal tract, making voice a potential non-invasive biomarker for
early decompensation detection. Prior work used features from
sustained vowels, relying on active patient engagement, limiting
real-world applicability. We propose contrastive time-proximity
(CTP), a self-supervised method learning HF-predictive repre-
sentations from natural speech. CTP trains a segment-attentive
LSTM to minimize embedding distances for recordings<3
days apart (presumed similar health) while maximizing them
for>100days apart (presumed different), exploiting HF’s pro-
gressive nature. On a two-center dataset (68 ADHF patients),
classical acoustic features achieve similar within-center accu-
racy but fail cross-center. CTP features suggest more robust
cross-center generalization, outperforming random and human
baselines. Attention analysis reveals focus on breathing-related
segments.
Index Terms: heart failure, vocal biomarkers, self-supervised
learning, contrastive learning, speech representation learning
1. Introduction
Heart failure (HF) affects approximately one in four people dur-
ing their lifetime [1], with a five-year mortality rate of around
50% [2]. The syndrome is characterized by progressive fluid
overload and cardiac remodeling, leading to recurrent episodes
of acute decompensated HF (ADHF) – a life-threatening condi-
tion requiring hospitalization – associated with worsening ven-
tricular function [3, 4].
Early detection of impending ADHF is crucial for timely
intervention [5]. Several remote monitoring approaches exist,
including vital sign tracking, lung congestion assessment, and
haemodynamic monitoring via cardiac implants [6]. Among
these, non-invasive methods such as body weight monitoring
lack sensitivity due to confounding factors like appetite loss,
with only half of patients gaining weight prior to hospital-
ization [7, 8, 9]. Haemodynamic monitoring, while more ef-
fective, requires costly and invasive device implantation [10].

This trade-off between sensitivity and invasiveness motivates
the search for novel non-invasive, cost-effective biomarkers.
Recent studies have demonstrated that fluid accumulation
in ADHF affects not only peripheral and pulmonary compart-
ments, but also the vocal tract [11, 12, 13], subtly changing
patients’ voices. V ocal fold fluid balance influences phonation
threshold pressure and voice quality [14, 15], with even minor
laryngeal edema potentially causing detectable vocal changes.
Consequently, several studies have proposed vocal biomarkers
for HF monitoring, finding voice features predictive of HF sta-
tus [16, 17, 18].
Most prior work has relied on manually engineered fea-
tures from sustained vowel phonations [16]. These require
patient engagement, consistent recording conditions, and con-
trolled technique, making them poorly suited for continuous
real-world monitoring. Natural speech, in contrast, can be cap-
tured effortlessly during everyday activities. However, standard
acoustic features may not be robust to the inherent variabil-
ity in content, timing, and recording conditions of real-world
speech. Furthermore, there is no obvious hand-crafted feature
that directly captures HF status from natural speech. This mo-
tivates a representation learning approach: rather than engi-
neering features manually, the model may learn complex, ro-
bust representations from data. Self-supervised learning has
proven highly effective for learning such representations in
healthcare applications [19], including medical imaging [20],
multi-modal clinical data [21], gait analysis [22], and vocal
biomarker extraction [23]. In this paper, we proposecontrastive
time-proximity(CTP), a self-supervised pre-training strategy to
learn HF-predictive speech representations from natural speech
recordings. The key insight is that HF is a progressive dis-
ease [24]: a patient’s health state evolves gradually, so record-
ings close together in time likely reflect similar states while dis-
tant ones differ. Unlike patient-invariant contrastive learning
on cardiac signals [25], CTP makes representations sensitive to
longitudinal change. We formalize this as a contrastive objec-
tive (Section 2.3) and evaluate the learned features on a two-
center clinical dataset with ADHF patients, comparing against
random, human, and classical acoustic baselines. Our contribu-
tions are:
1. A novel pre-training objective for learning HF-relevant
speech representations from weakly labeled longitudinal

2. Methods
2.1. Datasets
Neither dataset is publicly available: the pre-training data is
proprietary telemonitoring data collected under GDPR, and the
clinical evaluation data contains protected health information;
to our knowledge, no suitable public dataset exists for this task.
2.1.1. Pre-Training Data
The pre-training dataset consists of longitudinal speech record-
ings from HF patients participating in a telemonitoring program
conducted by Noah Labs GmbH . Patients record their voices
daily using a smartphone application, performing tasks includ-
ing reading a fixed poem (mean duration 11.3 s, SD 4.0 s) and
fun facts (mean 7.0 s, SD 3.5 s). At the time of analysis, the
dataset contained 51,736 natural speech recordings from 392
German-speaking patients. This dataset lacks validated clin-
ical HF endpoints and is thus consideredweakly labeled. It is
used to pre-train our model via contrastive time-proximity (Sec-
tion 2.3).
2.1.2. Clinical Evaluation Data
For evaluation, we use data from the V AMP-HF trial
(NCT06566911) [26], which enrolled patients hospitalized with
ADHF at two academic centers: the Charit´e Berlin (32 German-
speaking patients) and Mayo Clinic Rochester (36 English-
speaking patients). Patients recorded speech tasks daily from
admission to discharge. We use the admission (“wet”) and dis-
charge (“dry”) recordings as extreme-state labels, yielding a to-
tal of 68 patients for evaluation.
2.2. Feature Extraction Architecture
We adapt the d-vector model [27], originally designed for
speaker verification (Figure 1). First, audio waveforms are re-
sampled and converted to mel-spectrograms. These spectro-
grams are partitioned into segments of 160 frames (≈800 ms
each, the d-vector default). Each segment is processed inde-
pendently by a 3-layer bidirectional LSTM, producing frame-
level representations. Aframe attentionmechanism computes
attention weights for each frame via a learned linear projection
followed by softmax normalization, aggregating frame embed-
dings into a single segment embedding.
We extend this withsegment attention: a second atten-
tion layer aggregates segment embeddings into a single 256-
dimensional recording-level representation, enabling the model
to focus on informative temporal regions within recordings of
arbitrary length (Figure 1). The feature extractor contains ap-
proximately 4.5 million trainable parameters.
To investigate how input resolution affects learned repre-
sentations, we evaluate three configurations:A(16 kHz, 40 mel
bins),B(44 kHz, 40 mel bins), andC(44 kHz, 120 mel bins).
Configuration A matches the original d-vector settings and is
initialized from a publicly available speaker-verification check-

2.3. Pre-Training: Contrastive Time-Proximity
Since our pre-training dataset lacks validated clinical labels,
we employ a self-supervised objective that derives training sig-
nals from the temporal structure of the data itself. CTP is
based on the assumption that HF is a progressive syndrome [24]
with health status changing gradually over time. Consequently,
recordings from the same patient taken close together in time
(“proximal” pairs) should reflect similar health states, while
recordings far apart (“distal” pairs) are more likely to differ. We
define proximal pairs as recordings with at most 3 days tempo-
ral difference, and distal pairs as those with at least 100 days
difference (Figure 2). These thresholds were chosen based on
clinical guidance on HF progression rates and to ensure suffi-
cient, balanced training pairs (approximately 134k distal and
156k proximal). To mitigate the risk that acute decompensation
violates the proximity assumption, proximal pairs were filtered
to exclude recordings coinciding with known HF events or clin-
ically significant weight changes. We do not claim this configu-
ration is optimal. A systematic threshold sensitivity analysis is
left for future work.
The pre-training objective minimizes the cosine embedding
distance for proximal pairs while maximizing it for distal pairs,

2.4. Classical Acoustic Feature Baseline
As a baseline, we compare against a set of 29 classical acoustic
features commonly used in speech analysis [28]. These include
temporal features (speaking rate, articulation rate, phonation ra-
tio, pause rate, mean pause duration), fundamental frequency
measures (mean F0, F0 standard deviation in semitones), inten-
sity features (mean dB, range ratio dB), voice quality measures
(harmonics-to-noise ratio, cepstral peak prominence), spectral
characteristics (spectral slope, spectral tilt, spectral center of
gravity, spectral standard deviation, skewness, kurtosis), and
formant features (F1–F3 means, standard deviations, and band-
widths B1–B3).
2.5. Evaluation Framework
We frame ADHF detection as a ranking task: the model must
correctly order a patient’s admission and discharge recordings
by decompensation level. As a patient may contribute multiple
recordings per state, accuracy is computed over all admission–
discharge recording pairs. This formulation accounts for inter-
patient variability in absolute decompensation levels and aligns
with the clinical goal of detecting trends in patient status.
Two ranking heads are evaluated: (1)XGBoost Ranker[29],
a gradient-boosted tree approach that treats embeddings as
frozen features; (2)Neural Ranker[30], a learned pairwise
ranking function that fine-tunes the feature extractor (LR10 −4)
jointly with the ranking head (LR10 −2) for one epoch.
The evaluation follows a two-level design:
•Level 1 (Charit ´e clinic):Leave-one-patient-out cross-
validation (LOPO) on German patients, measuring general-
ization to unseen patients within the same clinic.
•Level 2 (Mayo clinic):Predictions averaged across all Level
1 LOPO checkpoints are tested on English patients from
an unseen clinic, measuring cross-language and cross-center
generalization.
All experiments are repeated across 50 random seeds to
ensure robust performance estimates. Results are compared
against a random baseline (50% accuracy) and a human rater
baseline described below. All experiments were conducted on
a single NVIDIA L4 GPU with 32 GB RAM. Pre-training re-
quired approximately 5 days per configuration; downstream
evaluation took approximately 1 minute per seed.
2.6. Human Comparison
To assess the inherent difficulty of the ranking task and establish
a reference baseline, we obtained human evaluation of record-
ings via a custom web application. Annotators were presented
with admission and discharge recordings for a given patient in
randomized order and asked to select the “more wet”, i.e., more
congested, recording. They could also provide free-text ex-
planations of their decisions, which were categorized manually
into perceptual cues such as breathing patterns and voice timbre.
Ten complete annotation sets were obtained from nine raters, in-
cluding machine learning experts, acoustic specialists, trained
HF nurses and a medical doctor. Disaggregating by expertise,
the single medical doctor was the strongest rater (0.66), whereas
the two trained HF nurses reached only 0.53 and 0.56, indicat-
ing the aggregate is held down by non-clinical raters while clin-
ical training alone did not guarantee strong performance. For
inter-rater agreement, two patients were ranked correctly by all
nine raters and five more by all but one, while most patients
were ranked inconsistently.

Notably, the CTP configurations with the best pre-training
performance (B, C) do not yield the best downstream perfor-
mance, particularly on Level 2, suggesting that success on the
pre-training objective does not automatically translate to clini-

3.3. Temporal Attention Analysis
To provide insight into the model’s decision-making process, a
visualization of the temporal attention of the CTP-A model for

4. Discussion
Our results suggest that CTP pre-training can learn speech rep-
resentations predictive of ADHF status that generalize across
clinical centers and languages. Whereas classical acoustic fea-
tures appear to overfit to center-specific characteristics (e.g.,
recording conditions, demographics, or language) and fail on
Level 2, CTP-A maintains robust performance across both lev-
els, suggesting the learned representations capture more fun-
damental HF-related voice changes. Speaker verification opti-
mizes for speaker-discriminative, content-invariant representa-

The mismatch between pre-training and downstream perfor-

The model’s focus on breathing-related segments aligns with
physiological expectations: fluid overload in ADHF affects res-
piratory function and may manifest in breathing patterns during
speech [14, 15], suggesting potential physiological relevance of
the learned representations.
Limitations:The evaluation dataset is small (N= 68),
limiting statistical power for formal hypothesis testing; all com-
parisons should be interpreted as exploratory. The evaluation
relies on admission/discharge labels without continuous mea-
sures of decompensation. Human rankings were available only
for Level 1. Code is not publicly available as it stems from pro-
prietary research.
5. Conclusion
We introduced contrastive time-proximity (CTP), a self-
supervised method for learning HF-predictive speech represen-
tations that generalize across centers and languages, with atten-
tion concentrating on breathing-related segments. These results
motivate further validation of speech-based biomarkers for non-
invasive HF monitoring in larger remote cohorts.
