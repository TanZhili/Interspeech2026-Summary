Abstract
Although studies have shown subglottal resonances (SGRs) to
be relevant for various speech processing tasks, their integra-
tion into speech-based applications has been limited by the dif-
ficulty of acquiring them and the absence of automated estima-
tion methods. In this study, we address this challenge by de-
veloping a framework that generates accelerometer signals that
contains the subglottal signals directly from speech via a super-
vised multi-scale spectral learning approach. Additionally, we
introduce a novel algorithm for automatically estimating and
tracking SGRs from continuous speech. Our method achieved
low RMSE values (approximately 30 Hz, 50 Hz, and 80 Hz for
Sgr1, Sgr2, and Sgr3, respectively) thus, demonstrating its ac-
curacy and robustness. Furthermore, experiments conducted on
the TIMIT dataset show that the estimation error remains be-
low 7 cm, highlighting the method’s applicability to real world
applications.
Index Terms: subglottal resonances, supervised spectral learn-

1. Introduction
The subglottal system (SGS), comprising the tracheobronchial
airways and surrounding tissues, plays a vital role in speech
production by driving airflow through the larynx and vocal tract
[1, 2, 3]. When the glottis is open, the SGS acoustically cou-
ples with the vocal tract, introducing additional pole–zero pairs
in its transfer function. These correspond to the natural fre-
quencies of the SGS, known as subglottal resonances (SGRs)
[1, 4]. The lowest three resonances (Sgr1, Sgr2, and Sgr3) have
served diverse roles in applications related to speech produc-
tion and perception including automatic speaker normalization
[5, 6] speaker height estimation [7], speech recognition [8, 9],
and lung health monitoring [10]. They have been shown to be
particularly effective, especially in limited-data scenarios [7].
SGRs are typically estimated from neck-skin vibration
recordings obtained via invasive [11] or noninvasive methods
[12]. Invasive methods include placing miniature pressure
transducers below the glottis via an endotracheal tube or ob-
taining measurements from patients who have undergone laryn-
gectomy [13]. Noninvasive approaches use an accelerometer
placed at the cricoid cartilage to capture vibrations related to
subglottal pressure [14]. The resulting accelerometer signal is
then used to estimate the SGRs. However, both methods require
specialized and costly setups [15], limiting broader use. There-
fore, one of the goals of this study is to develop a method for di-
rectly obtaining accelerometer signals from speech recordings,
which can then be used to estimate SGRs for any given speaker.
This would in turn open up and significantly broaden research
opportunities in this area. Furthermore, existing methods for

estimating SGRs from accelerometer measurements typically
target each of the three lowest resonances individually, either
manually or in asemi-automaticfashion [16]. In contrast, this
study aims to automate the estimation process and also track the
three lowest resonances concurrently from a continuous stream
of speech. Compared to speech signals, accelerometer signals
are typically weaker and may be contaminated by noise due to
excessive air pressure or measurement artifacts [17]. Also, due
to the multi-scale distribution nature of the spectrum, to obtain
accelerometer signal from speech signal, we adopt a multi-scale
spectral learning approach based on efficient convolutional neu-
ral networks (CNNs). This approach effectively captures both
short-term and long-term speech variations across multiple time
scales. An overview of the proposed framework is shown in Fig.

using the generated accelerometer waveform, an SGR estima-

periodic regions of continuous speech.
The main contributions of this paper are as follows: 1) A su-
pervised multi-scale spectral learning model to produce concise
accelerometer waveforms from speech signals. 2) A novel al-
gorithm to automatically estimate and track the first three SGRs
from continuous voiced speech sounds. To the best of our
knowledge, this study is the first attempt to automatically es-
timate and track the first three SGRs simultaneously from con-
tinuous speech.
2. Dataset
The dataset used in this study was the WashU-UCLA corpus
[18]. It consists of simultaneous microphone and accelerometer
(subglottal) recordings of 50 (25 male, 25 female) adult Amer-
ican English speakers. The corpus is made up of 35 monosylla-
bles (14 ‘hVd’ and 21 ‘CVb’ words, where C is [b, d, g], and V
includes all American English monophthongs and diphthongs)
embedded in the carrier phrase,“I said a again”, with 10
repetitions of each word by each speaker, resulting in 17,500 in-
dividual microphone and accelerometer waveforms. All record-
ings were sampled at 48 kHz and the bit resolution was 16
bits/sample. The corpus had speaker height annotations. We

3. Supervised speech to accelerometer
waveform model
Inspired by PrimeK-Net [19], we utilize a supervised multi-
scale spectral learning framework via group prime-kernel CNN
to obtain concise accelerometer waveforms from speech. The
model uses a novel Group Prime-Kernel (GPK) feed-forward
network to extract temporal and frequency features at multiple
scales. Input channels are divided into groups, each processed
with a different prime-sized kernel to enhance spectral represen-
tation. The overall architecture of the model follows a U-Net-
like encoder-decoder structure [20], shown in Fig. 2, which cap-
tures both local and contextual information via downsampling
and upsampling paths. The encoder uses multiple GPKConv
blocks, followed by pooling layers to progressively reduce the
spatial resolution and increase feature abstraction, while the de-
coder mirrors this structure, using upsampling and GPKConv
blocks to reconstruct the spectrogram. The skip connections be-
tween the encoder and decoder layers help retain fine-grained
spectral details lost during downsampling by linking each en-
coder layer to its mirrored decoder layer, giving the decoder ac-
cess to both low-level detail and high-level context. The model
takes spectrogram as input and outputs same, which is then con-
verted back to waveform. The multi-scale convolutional ap-
proach is designed to handle the diverse types of patterns that
are present in the frequency domain of speech, which occur
at different scales by using filters that can learn from multiple
scales simultaneously. The prime-kernel design, which refers to
kernel sizes with prime numbers, avoids periodic overlap effects
thereby improving accuracy and reducing feature redundancy.
We trained the model from scratch making use of the seen
data group, employing the same model setup as in [19] with the
microphone (speech) and accelerometer recordings as input and
target, respectively. The seen data group were split into training
and test sets. The test set was composed of four microphone
recordings with their corresponding accelerometer recordings
for the 45 speakers in the dataset, while the rest of the record-
ings made up the training set. All recordings were downsam-
pled to 16 kHz.
4. Automatic SGR estimation and tracking
In this section, we present the algorithm used for automatic es-
timation of SGRs (Sgr1, Sgr2 and Sgr3) from the accelerometer
signals and for automatic tracking of the three SGRs simultane-
ously from continuous speech.
4.1. Automatic SGR estimation

This process results in a high energy, low frequency, longer du-

appear in vowel spectra as pole-zero pairs, which can disrupt the

5. Results and Discussion
5.1. Speech to accelerometer signal generation
First, we trained and test our supervised speech to accelerome-
ter waveform model on the seen data group using a batch size
of2with the AdamW [25] optimizer for up to 1000k steps.
These hyperparameter values were chosen based on grid search
performed on the training set to obtain the best results. To
further test its performance and robustness, the trained check-
point is used to test the unseen data group, where the model is
given speech waveforms as input to generate the correspond-
ing accelerometer waveforms, which are then compared with
the ground truth. During training, we experimented with vari-
ous prime multi-scale kernel sizes to obtain the best results. To
evaluate the quality of the generated accelerometer signals, we
use the Perceptual Evaluation of Speech Quality (PESQ) [26]
and Log Spectral Distance (LSD) measures. It is important to
note that PESQ, which gives score from−0.5to4.5, is used
in this study as a reference-based objective metric to measure
the preservation of speech-related information in generated ac-
celerometer signals; thus, we do not interpret PESQ as a mea-
sure of perceptual quality of accelerometer signals, but rather
as a relative metric to evaluate improvements in speech-driven
signal generation. For PESQ, the higher the score, the better the
performance while the opposite remains true for LSD.

To evaluate the proposed automatic SGR tracking algorithm, we
provided it with four sentences of data (about 8 seconds of con-

SGRs using continuous speech for the WashU-UCLA corpus

dard deviation measurements. This implies that the adaptive
LPC and dynamic pre-emphasis features of the proposed algo-
rithm contributed not only to improved accuracy but also to the
effective handling of discontinuities.

correlation with height, underscoring their value for improving
downstream speech tasks and real-world applications.
6. Conclusion
This paper presented a supervised multi-scale spectral learn-
ing model that generates accelerometer signals from speech
for accurate SGR estimation, along with a novel algorithm for
automatic SGR estimation and tracking in continuous speech.
The results showed the generated signals closely match ground
truth, demonstrating accuracy and robustness. Reported low
within-speaker variation (COV<5%) confirms that SGR re-
mains effectively constant for a given speaker. Further cross-
dataset evaluation experiment on TIMIT achieved height esti-
mation error below 7 cm, indicating strong generalization and
practical applicability of the proposed method. Future work will
further integrate these SGRs into various downstream speech
tasks.
