Abstract
Large Language Model-based Text-to-Speech (LLM-TTS) sys-
tems have achieved remarkable progress in speech synthesis.
However, evaluating their ability to handle Chinese polyphonic
characters remains unexplored. This paper introduces Poly-
Bench, a comprehensive benchmark for evaluating Chinese
polyphone disambiguation in LLM-TTS systems. We con-
struct three test sets covering 494 polyphonic characters and
88 polyphonic words across diverse categories. Additionally,
we explore automatic pronunciation labeling using Large Au-
dio Language Models (LALMs) and benchmark 17 open source
systems. Evaluation results reveal that even the best system
achieves only 82.02% pronunciation accuracy on polyphonic
characters, with significant performance gaps in dialectal, col-
loquial, and literary categories, revealing the limitations of cur-
rent TTS systems in polyphone disambiguation and the need for
improvement.
Index Terms: LLM-based TTS, polyphone disambiguation,
automatic pronunciation labeling, benchmark dataset
1. Introduction
Large Language Model-based Text-to-Speech (LLM-TTS) sys-
tems have rapidly emerged as the dominant paradigm in speech
synthesis. These systems achieve unprecedented naturalness
and expressiveness, demonstrating impressive zero-shot voice
cloning and style control capability [1–7]. However, their per-
formance on polyphone disambiguation remains unexplored.
Languages such as English and Chinese contain polyphonic
words whose pronunciation depends on contextual meaning.
This is particularly pronounced in Chinese, where a single char-
acter can have multiple pronunciations with distinct meanings.
Mispronouncing these characters severely degrades speech in-
telligibility and naturalness. In traditional TTS systems [8–10],
text is first converted to phonemes by a Grapheme-to-Phoneme
(G2P) module before being fed into the acoustic model. This
explicit pronunciation conversion allows for direct evaluation
of polyphone disambiguation accuracy based on the module’s
predictions. In contrast, LLM-TTS systems operate directly
on raw text tokens, incorporating the disambiguation process
implicitly within the LLM structure. This inherent integration
makes direct evaluation impossible. A commonly used met-
ric for assessing the intelligibility of such systems is Word Er-
ror Rate (WER) or Character Error Rate (CER), computed via
Automatic Speech Recognition (ASR). However, these metrics
often fail to reliably detect pronunciation errors, as a system
may mispronounce a character while the ASR still recognizes it
correctly. Furthermore, publicly available benchmark datasets
for Chinese polyphone disambiguation [11–13] suffer from an-
notation errors, incomplete coverage of polyphonic character
pronunciations, a lack of common polyphones, and insufficient
coverage of domain-specific contexts.
To address these limitations, we introduce PolyBench, a
dedicated benchmark for evaluating Chinese polyphone disam-
biguation in LLM-TTS systems. Our primary contributions are
threefold:
1.Comprehensive Test Sets: We construct three complemen-
tary test sets covering 494 high-frequency polyphonic char-
acters and 88 polyphonic words across diverse categories.
2.Automatic Pronunciation Labeling: We investigate the fea-
sibility of using Large Audio Language Models (LALMs)
for automatic polyphone pronunciation annotation in synthe-
sized speech, identifying Qwen3-Omni-Instruct [14] as a re-
liable alternative to costly manual annotation.
3.Extensive Benchmarking and Analysis: We conduct com-
prehensive evaluations of three G2P models and 17 state-of-
the-art (SOTA) LLM-TTS systems on PolyBench, providing
in-depth analysis.
2. Related Work
2.1. Polyphone Disambiguation Datasets
Several datasets have been proposed for Chinese polyphone
disambiguation. Park et al. [11] introduced the CPP (Chinese
Polyphone disambiguation with Pinyin) dataset, containing 623
polyphonic characters with 10,254 sentences in its test split.
Zhao et al. [12] proposed MCP (Modern Common Polyphone)
dataset, which comprises 57,000 sentences from various do-
mains, providing approximately 500 labeled sentences per poly-
phone. The CVTE-poly dataset [13] further increased the scale
to approximately ten times that of CPP and achieved more bal-
anced label distributions.
However, these datasets exhibit notable limitations. The
CPP test set contains annotation errors and lacks coverage of
frequently-used polyphones; moreover, 425 polyphonic char-
acters in CPP have only one pronunciation. The MCP dataset
is limited to only 114 polyphonic characters. The CVTE-poly
dataset primarily introduces a new training set and reuses the
CPP test set. To address these limitations, our work constructs
a more comprehensive test set for polyphone disambiguation.

Score (MOS) tests and objective metrics. Subjective evaluation
commonly includes MOS for naturalness, Comparative MOS
(CMOS) for relative preference, and Similarity MOS (SMOS)
for speaker similarity. While reliable, these methods are costly
and time-consuming. Objective metrics commonly used for au-
tomatic evaluation include WER, computed via ASR to assess

3. PolyBench
Figure 1 illustrates the complete pipeline of PolyBench, encom-
passing both dataset construction and the evaluation process.
All constructed test sets and the prompts used for sentence gen-
eration are publicly available in the project repository1.
3.1. Main Dataset Construction
Our main dataset construction followed a rigorous four-stage
process to ensure quality, coverage, and diversity.
Extraction and Filtering.We first extracted high fre-
quency characters and words with multiple pronunciations from
the 7th Edition ofContemporary Chinese Dictionary[18], fo-
cusing on those falling within the 3500 Level-I characters of
The Table of General Standard Chinese Characters[19]. Then,
we applied rigorous rule-based filtering to these initial entries,
obtaining 494 polyphonic characters and 88 polyphonic words
(90.2% retention rate). Our filtering criteria are:
• Interjection exclusion: Removed 7 interjection characters
(e.g., “啊”(ah), “哦”(oh)) whose pronunciations vary with
emotional expression, speech rate, and contextual tone
sandhi, making them unsuitable for consistent evaluation.
• Meaning-based filtering: Excluded senses marked as variant
characters (同“*”), non-standard forms, or archaic usages not
part of modern standard Chinese. Removed cases where mul-

tiple pronunciations shared the same meaning, such as the
character ’盖’ (G ˇe/G`ai) in Figure 1. Since both pronuncia-
tions can serve as surnames, they created ambiguity in the
evaluation targets.
• Single-pronunciation deletion: Excluded 56 characters with
only one valid pronunciation after meaning-based filtering.
LLM-Based Sentence Generation.For each retained
polyphonic character and word, we used DeepSeek-V3.1 [20]
to generate contextual sentences based on the extracted dictio-
nary entries. A detailed prompt was designed to instruct the
model to act as a Chinese linguistics expert, generating sen-
tences for all pronunciations and meanings. For entries pro-
vided with example phrases, the model was instructed to ex-
pand them into complete sentences; for entries containing only
meanings, it generated contextually appropriate sentences. The
prompt required structured output including: polyphonic char-
acter/word, pronunciation, meaning, example phrase (if appli-
cable), the generated sentence with Pinyin annotation, and a cat-
egory label. Category labels were assigned according to the dic-
tionary markings:Common(no special marking, everyday us-
age),Surname(marked as〈名〉姓),Dialectal(marked as〈方〉),
Colloquial(marked as〈口〉), andLiterary(marked as〈书〉).
Manual Verification.All generated sentences underwent
manual review by native Mandarin speakers. Annotators veri-
fied: (1) contextual appropriateness ensuring unambiguous pro-
nunciation determination, (2) naturalness and grammatical cor-
rectness, (3) accurate Pinyin annotation following dictionary
entries, and (4) correct category assignment based on dictionary
markings. This process yielded the final main test set compris-
ing 6016 sentences.
3.2. Supplementary Datasets
Along with the main test set, we constructed two specialized test
sets to evaluate specific aspects of polyphone disambiguation:
DictWords.We extracted word entries containing the 494
target polyphonic characters from the dictionary. We found that
111 of these polyphonic characters had only one common pro-
nunciation across the extracted words. Considering that these
pronunciations are almost never mispronounced by both hu-

mans and TTS models, we excluded all words containing such
characters. Then, for each remaining (character, pronunciation)
pair, we selected the top five highest-frequency words based on
BCC corpus statistics [21], excluding those already covered in
the main test set. This resulted in 2,137 words covering 375
characters. This set evaluates lexical-context disambiguation—
whether models correctly pronounce polyphonic characters in
common compound words.
ALLinONE.For each of the 494 polyphonic characters ex-
tracted in Sec. 3.1, we used DeepSeek R1 [22] to construct
a single meaningful sentence containing all pronunciations of
that character. We prompted the model with the extracted lexi-
con entries and manually checked the generation results to en-
sure logical correctness and semantic naturalness. This resulted
in 494 sentences with 1,138 polyphone instances. This chal-
lenging set requires models to correctly disambiguate multiple
pronunciations within the same sentence, testing robust contex-
tual processing and long-range dependency modeling.
3.3. Automatic Pronunciation Labeling
During evaluation, all assessed LLM-TTS models synthesize
speech for every utterance and word across three test sets, gen-
erating approximately 6 hours of audio per system. Since ver-
ifying the correctness of polyphone disambiguation in LLM-
TTS requires analyzing the actual pronunciation in the synthe-
sized speech, and manual annotation at this scale is impractical,
a reliable automated approach is necessary.
To validate the reliability of automatic labeling approaches,
we first manually annotated the actual pronunciation of all poly-
phonic characters in the ALLinONE test set (generated by one
representative model from those under evaluation). This man-
ual annotation serves as the reference standard. We then ex-
plored the feasibility of utilizing two categories of off-the-shelf
models for automatic pronunciation labeling: (1) Traditional
ASR model with phoneme output: Kaldi [23] with a pre-trained
Mandarin acoustic model 2, and MMSpeech [24]; (2) LALMs:
Baichuan-Audio [25], StepAudio-R1.1 [26], MiMo-Audio [27],
and Qwen3-Omni-Instruct [14]. For the LALMs, we prompted
them to transcribe the entire speech into Pinyin based on the
audio’s actual pronunciation and then extracted the target poly-
phone pronunciations.

4. Evaluation
4.1. Evaluated Models
We benchmark 20 open-source models:
•3 G2P models: the G2P module in F5-TTS [10], G2PM [11],
and G2PW [28];
•17 LLM-TTS models: CosyV oice2 [1], CosyV oice3 [2],
V oxCPM1.5 [7], Spark-TTS [29], Index-TTS 2 [5], OuteTTS
[30], Qwen3-TTS [3], Chatterbox [31], Orpheus-TTS [32],
VibeV oice [33], Step-Audio TTS [34], LLaSA [35], GLM-
TTS [6], FireRedTTS-2 [4], HiggsAudio-v2 [36], Qwen2.5-
Omni [37] and Qwen3-Omni-Instruct [14].
4.2. Evaluation Metrics
We report three metrics:
•CER: Computed by transcribing synthesized speech using
the Qwen3-Omni-Instruct and comparing it against the ref-
erence text. This metric measures overall intelligibility and
robustness.
•Poly-CharAcc(Polyphone Character Accuracy): The per-
centage of correctly recognized polyphonic characters in the
transcription.
•Poly-PyAcc(Polyphone Pinyin Accuracy): The percentage
of correctly pronounced polyphonic characters. This is our
primary metric for evaluating disambiguation capability. A
pronunciation is considered correct only if the initial conso-
nant, final, and tone all match the reference.
For G2P models, we include only the Poly-PyAcc metric.

Notably, we observe minimal inter-system variance in Poly-
CharAcc (within 4%), while Poly-PyAcc exhibited substantial
variation (15%). Furthermore, intra-system performance con-
sistently showed a 10%–20% gap between Poly-PyAcc and
Poly-CharAcc. These observations collectively confirm that
character recognition alone is insufficient for evaluating poly-
phone disambiguation, as a system can correctly recognize a
character (high CharAcc) while the pronunciation is completely
wrong (low PyAcc).
Among all systems, FireRedTTS-2 achieves the best overall
performance. McNemar’s test confirms that its Poly-CharAcc
is comparable to Qwen3-TTS and Index-TTS2, while its Poly-
PyAcc is significantly better than all other systems (on all sen-
tences and common category). Despite this dominance, 18% of
polyphonic characters remain mispronounced. This highlights
both the comprehensive coverage and elevated difficulty of our
constructed dataset, as well as substantial room for improve-
ment in TTS polyphone disambiguation.
Although the LLM-TTS results are affected by automatic
labeling errors (meaning their actual performance may be
higher or lower than reported), and direct comparison with G2P
models may not be entirely fair, analysis in Section 3.3 con-
firmed that the reported results are reasonably close to actual
performance. Moreover, when the performance gap is suffi-
ciently large, comparisons remain meaningful. We observe that
the best-performing LLM-TTS system surpasses G2P models in
Pinyin accuracy, particularly in the common category, suggest-
ing an advantage of the LLM architecture and scaling with data.
However, G2P models remained competitive in the surname and
literary categories. Among them, G2PW outperformed all TTS
systems (except Index-TTS2) in these categories. This discrep-
ancy may be related to a lack of specific domain training data in
the LLM-TTS models.
Performance varies dramatically across different polyphone
categories. Accuracy is highest for common usage and low-
est for dialectal terms. All systems struggle with dialectal and
colloquial categories, indicating severe insufficiency in domain-
specific training data. Specifically, for the colloquial category,
low performance may also be attributed to the fact that pronun-
ciation in everyday speech often diverges from dictionary pro-
nunciations. Additionally, the difference in rankings across dif-
ferent categories further suggests an imbalanced distribution of
training data.
4.3.2. Results on Supplementary Test Sets
The two right columns of Table 2 show results obtained on Dict-
Words and ALLinONE test sets. FireRedTTS-2 again leads,
and demonstrates statistically significant superiority over all
other systems (except for Index-TTS 2 on the ALLinONE set).
On the DictWords set, most systems exhibit higher score than on
the other two test sets, suggesting that common lexical contexts
are easier to handle. The 23.3% performance gap between the
best (FireRedTTS2) and worst (Chatterbox) systems on Dict-
Words highlights significant robustness differences among the
models. On the challenging ALLinONE set, even the best sys-
tem achieves only 202 out of 494 characters fully correct (all
polyphone pronunciations in the sentence correct), indicating
that handling multiple polyphones in a single sentence remains
a substantial challenge.
5. Conclusions
We present PolyBench, a comprehensive benchmark designed
for evaluating Chinese polyphone disambiguation in LLM-TTS
systems. Our benchmark comprises three test sets covering 494
polyphonic characters across diverse linguistic contexts. Rigor-
ous filtering rules and manual verification ensure dataset qual-
ity. Through systematic evaluation of automatic labeling meth-
ods, we demonstrate that Qwen3-Omni-Instruct can reliably re-
place human annotators for large-scale pronunciation evaluation
Benchmarking 17 SOTA LLM-TTS systems shows significant
progress in the field. However, even the best systems achieve
only 82% accuracy in polyphonic characters disambiguation,
with particular poor performance on dialectal and colloquial
categories. These results highlight key areas for future improve-
ment in LLM-TTS.
For future work, we plan to extend polyphone coverage be-
yond the 3500 most common characters to include more poly-
phones, and explore more accurate pinyin labeling methods to
reduce tone errors.
