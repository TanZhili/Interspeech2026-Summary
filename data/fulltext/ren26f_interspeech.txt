Can Hands Articulate? Kinematic, Acoustic, and Perceptual Analyses of Vowel
Production via External Resonators in Kaxi

Abstract
The source-filter model is foundational in speech science, but
the potential for external filters to replace the vocal tract re-
mains underexplored. This research investigates Kaxi, a Chi-
nese folk art in which a reed serves as the source and hand
movements act as an external resonator. Kinematic, acoustic,
and perceptual analyses of five Kaxi vowels were performed
to determine whether hands can articulate intelligible speech.
Kinematic tracking reveals that hand postures create a distinct
spatial map analogous to tongue-based vowel spaces. Acous-
tically, Kaxi employs a staircase-like formant tuning strategy
to maintain vowel distinctiveness despite high fundamental fre-
quencies. Perceptual evaluations with 24 listeners demonstrate
relative robust intelligibility. These results confirm that hands
can function effectively as an external speech filter, offering
insights into speech plasticity and presenting a potential non-
invasive tool for clinical speech rehabilitation.
Index Terms: Kaxi; source-filter; external resonance; vowel
intelligibility; formant tuning
1. Introduction
Speech production and perception are intrinsically linked
through the acoustic characteristics of human speech. The
source-filter model [1] is a widely accepted framework for un-
derstanding this relationship. According to this model, speech
signals are generated by a sound source, such as vocal fold vi-
bration, and subsequently shaped by the vocal tract filter. Dy-
namic movements of the articulators, including the tongue and
lips, change the shape of the vocal tract, thereby altering its res-
onant frequencies, known as formants. These formants serve as
key acoustic cues that enable listeners to distinguish between
different vowel categories.
However, the effectiveness of this model depends on the
proper functioning of the speech organs. Physical impairments
can significantly disrupt the typical source-filter pathway, such
as a laryngectomy affecting the source or a glossectomy affect-
ing the filter. Nevertheless, many individuals exhibit remark-
able plasticity, developing compensatory strategies by adapting
the movement of their remaining articulators [2]. This reliance
on vocal tract shaping is so fundamental that even when the
natural sound source is entirely replaced by an external device
such as an electrolarynx, the speaker must still precisely manip-
ulate their internal filter to produce distinguishable vowels [3].
This observation leads to an important question: could an ex-
ternal tool, rather than the internal vocal tract, be used to shape
vowel sounds? Exploring this possibility may inform the devel-

opment of innovative communication methods for individuals
with speech disabilities.
In the search for external means to filter sound, researchers
often turn to musical instruments as analogies. Instruments such
as violins and clarinets produce complex, vibrating sounds,
similar to the human voice [4].In the field of music acoustics,
long-time discussion has centered on which instruments exhibit
vocal-like timbre qualities [5, 6]. However, such discourse re-
flects a top-down aesthetic judgment rather than an objectively
verifiable acoustic fact [6]. Most musical instruments have a
fixed structure and lack the flexibility of the human vocal tract,
which can rapidly adjust its shape to produce a wide range of
vowel sounds [7]. As a result, there are still no effective exter-
nal tools that can independently control both pitch and vowel
quality in a manner comparable to human speech.
Furthermore, even if an external modulation mechanism for
formants is identified, a discrepancy may still arise between the
external sound source’s fundamental frequency (f0) and the typ-
ical f0 range of the human voice. This is especially challenging
at very high pitches, where the harmonics become more widely
spaced. Under such conditions, it is rather difficult to estimate
formants accurately in perception, which often results in re-
duced vowel intelligibility [8]. While singers may employ for-
mant tuning to increase acoustic energy and maintain loudness,
this technique can compromise vowel clarity [9, 10]. This raises
the question of whether improved methods can be developed to
optimize both loudness and vowel clarity at high pitches.
Kaxi, a traditional folk art from Northern China, provides a
practical example that addresses these questions. In Kaxi per-
formances, the artist produces sound by blowing through a reed,
which serves as the source of pitch (Figure 2a). Simultaneously,
the performer uses hand movements outside the mouth to mod-
ify the sound’s timbre, effectively functioning as an external
flexible resonator. Remarkably, listeners can clearly understand
spoken content, particularly in continuous speech.
To investigate how these acoustic effects arise in Kaxi, it is
essential to employ precise, measurable methods. In this study,
we recorded a skilled Kaxi performer to analyze the relation-
ship between his hand movements, the resulting acoustic pat-
terns, and vowel perception. The study seeks to address two
central research questions: (1) Do Kaxi vowels exhibit specific
kinematic and acoustic characteristics that facilitate successful
perception by listeners? (2) Do Kaxi vowels possess distinctive
formant distributions, and are these distributions influenced by
pitch? By examining Kaxi, we aim to deepen our understanding
of the source-filter model and to explore potential applications
in speech rehabilitation.

2. Methods
2.1. Data Collection
A 22-year-old male Kaxi performer from Luohe, Henan
Province, participated in this study. He learned Kaxi from fam-
ily members and began practicing at the age of 12. He was
fluent in Mandarin and the regional dialect, with no reported ill-
nesses when recording. He provided informed consent for the
use of his data in this research and received monetary compen-
sation.
A total of 197 Mandarin monosyllabic words with a (C)V
structure were selected for the study. This set included 53 a[a],
11 o[o], 16 e[7], 48 i[i], and 69 u[u] tokens (Chinese Pinyin
characters are shown first, followed by their International Pho-
netic Alphabet transcriptions; Pinyin is used hereafter). The
words were presented in Chinese characters, and the participant
was asked to read each word three times in normal speech and
in Kaxi. For the Kaxi trials, the hand movements were captured
by a front-right camera.
Recordings were conducted in a sound-attenuated room us-
ing Adobe Audition 2025 (44,100 Hz, 16-bit). Equipment in-
cluded a Maono PD200X microphone, Behringer XENYX 302
USB mixer, and XONAR U5 sound card.
2.2. Kinematic Analysis
Stable vowel segments with level tones were extracted from
hand-motion videos. MediaPipe package (0.10.21) in Python
(3.12) was used to track 21 anatomical landmarks (wrist, joints,
fingertips, etc.) in three dimensions, resulting in 63 fea-
tures per frame. The analysis focused exclusively on the
right hand to minimize occlusion-related inaccuracies associ-
ated with the left hand. Manual selection produced a repre-
sentative dataset of stable hand-shape frames for each vowel
(a, 36; o, 28; e, 19; i, 35; u, 37).
Principal component analysis (PCA) was performed in R
(4.5.1) to investigate intrinsic kinematic patterns. All coordi-
nates were normalized relative to the wrist (00 WRIST) to re-
move global translational variance, thereby ensuring that the
analysis addressed only postural deformation.
2.3. Acoustic Analysis
Each vowel segment was manually labeled in PRAAT (6.4.23).
F0 and formants ( F1, F2, F3) were measured using V oice-
Sauce [11] at 11 equal intervals.
Kaxi uses a reed as its sound source, and therefore its f0
(about 300–700 Hz) is higher than that of typical human speech.
When the f0 goes above 350 Hz, it becomes difficult to ac-
curately identify resonances using standard spectral analysis
or linear prediction methods [12]. Following the previous ap-
proach, we manually inspected multiple spectral slices from
wideband Praat spectrograms to optimize formant-estimation
settings for different vowels [13].
Formant data were analyzed in R. Even after adjusting for-
mant settings, there were still many outliers in the formant
measurements. Outliers were removed using Mahalanobis Dis-
tance within each vowel group, considering F1, F2, and F3 to-
gether and setting a Chi-square threshold of p = 0 .70. Ran-
dom Forest models (randomForest package, 500 trees) were
trained separately for normal speech ( N = 6 , 042) and Kaxi
(N = 4 , 887) to classify vowels based on the three formants.
Performance was evaluated using confusion matrices on a 20%
test set.
2.4. Perceptual Evaluation
This study recruited 24 native Mandarin speakers (balanced by
gender) to participate in a vowel identification task to assess
how well Kaxi vowels could be perceived. All participants re-
ported no speech-related disorders. They consented to the use
of their experimental data for academic research and received
compensation upon completion.
The experimental stimuli were created from speech sam-
ples collected in Section 2.1. We selected five single vow-
els (a, o, e, i, u) of level tone in both normal speech and Kaxi,
yielding 10 items. For each stimulus, a stable 200 ms segment
of the vowel was taken and then lengthened to 500 ms using
the Pitch-Synchronous Overlap and Add (PSOLA) algorithm
(Python psola package, version 0.0.1). 100 ms of silence was
added at both the beginning and end. 5 vowel stimuli with a
constant f0 of 200 Hz were additionally created using Praat
vowelEditor for participants’ practice.
After the practice, the normal speech and Kaxi vowels were
presented in two separate blocks of 10 randomized repetitions
each. In each trial, a fixation (‘+’) appeared on the screen for
500 ms. After hearing the audio, participants used a mouse to
choose one of the five Pinyin symbols ( a, o, e, i, u) or ‘?’ if
they could not identify the vowel. The task was carried out on
a laptop using a JavaScript-based HTML program developed
by the second author. Each participant completed 100 trials in
total, taking about 5 minutes.
Data from the identification task were analyzed using R.
Accuracy was analyzed with a mixed-effects logistic regres-
sion model. For reaction times (RT), only correct responses
between 0 and 5000 ms were included ( N = 2 , 195), and
the data were log-transformed before fitting a linear mixed-
effects model (LMM). Both models usedblock and targetVowel
as fixed effects and included participants as random intercepts.
Post-hoc comparisons were corrected using the Tukey method.
3. Results
3.1. Mapping Hand Gestures to Vowel Space
Camera-captured hand movement patterns vary significantly
during the production of different Kaxi vowels (Figure 2a).
PCA showed that the first two principal components together
explained 60.83% of the total variance in the data (PC1:
37.14%, PC2: 23.69%; see Figure 2b). In the space defined
by PC1 and PC2, the right-hand shapes for the five vowels were
arranged approximately according to tongue height (PC1 axis)
and tongue advancement (PC2 axis), creating a pattern similar
to a traditional vowel space.
The loadings of different landmark parameters reflect that
the PCs capture movement patterns with clear physical mean-
ing. PC1 exhibits strong negative values on the x/z-axis of
the thumb and strong positive values on the x/y-axis of the in-
dex and middle fingers. This means PC1 reflects the opposing
movement between the thumb and other fingers. PC2 is influ-
enced mainly by the joints of the ring finger and the base of the
thumb, which reflect detailed finger movements, such as curling
the palm sideways and bending fingers independently. Overall,
the PCA process turned complex hand movement data into clear
and meaningful motion features, which align closely with the
phonetic representation of vowel space.

In Kaxi, where the f0 range is from about 300 to 700 Hz,
the formants of all vowels show staircase-like tuning patterns
(Figure 1). For example, instead of rising continuously, the F1
of a fluctuates in a zigzag pattern between approximately 1,400
and 2,000 Hz by aligning with different harmonics. Initially,
F1 tracks h5 (300–350 Hz), then drops to align with h4 (up to
450 Hz), and subsequently matches h3 (500–630 Hz). Above
700 Hz, F1 appears to align with h2. This process results in a
repetitive cycle in whichF1 increases steadily along a harmonic
and then drops rapidly in the transition zones to align with the
next lower harmonic. A comparable discontinuous tuning is
also observed in formants of other vowels, as indicated by black
arrows in Figure 1.

The cognitive processing effort, as measured by reaction
times, increased significantly when perceiving Kaxi vowels.
For normal speech, participants responded quickly, with reac-
tion times for each vowel ranging from 940 to 1,110 ms. When
switching to the Kaxi vowels, reaction times became longer,
especially for certain vowels. The vowel o had the slowest re-
action time in Kaxi (1,522 ms), which was about 40% slower
than in normal speech. The vowel i also showed a large in-
crease in response time (1,219 ms), about 29% slower than nor-
mal speech.
Analysis of errors using a confusion matrix showed clear
patterns of which vowels were mistaken for others (see Fig-
ure 6). In normal speech, mistakes were rare and usually in-
volved confusing neighboring vowels, such as o being misper-
ceived as u (about 1.25%). In Kaxi, o was often confused withe
(15%) or u (8.3%), e was sometimes mistaken for a (10%), and
i had a high rate of ‘no response’ answers (about 12.1%).

4. Discussion and Conclusion
This study examined Kaxi, a unique form of speech mimicry,
from three academic perspectives: how hand gestures act as an
external resonator, detailed acoustic analysis, and how listeners
perceive Kaxi vowels. The results show that Kaxi vowels can
be just as communicative as natural human speech. Clear pat-
terns were found in how different hand movements produced
each vowel. Although the acoustic formant patterns of Kaxi
vowels are not identical to those in typical speech, they can still
be clearly separated in a three-dimensional acoustic space. Fur-
thermore, listeners were generally able to accurately identify the
five Kaxi vowel categories. Overall, these findings demonstrate
that using a reed as a sound source and shaping the sound with
both hands allows for the production of distinct vowel qualities
that closely resemble those of natural speech.
However, it is important to note that Kaxi has difficulty
clearly distinguishing certain individual vowels, especially be-
tween o[o] and e[7]. These two vowels are very similar in
both the hand gestures used and their acoustic formant patterns,
which likely led to frequent errors in the perception tests. This
confusion might be related to the complementary distribution
of [o] and [7] in Mandarin Chinese [14]. In real-life situations,
Kaxi performers rarely need to produce vowels in isolation, so
there may be little pressure to develop distinct hand shapes for
allophonic differences. Additionally, identifying vowels in iso-
lation is generally more challenging than recognizing them in
continuous speech [15, 16]. In continuous speech, higher-level
language processing and contextual clues help listeners resolve
ambiguities in speech sounds [17, 18], allowing Kaxi to achieve
intelligibility similar to that of normal speech.
This study also revealed that Kaxi uses a formant tun-
ing pattern that differs significantly from that of typical high-
pitched singing. Prior research indicates that singers typically
adjust their formants to rise continuously with a single har-
monic to maintain vocal power at higher pitches; however, this
approach often reduces the intelligibility of high-pitched vow-
els [19, 20]. In contrast, analysis of Kaxi vowels revealed that
formant tuning occurs only within specific fundamental fre-
quency ranges. This targeted tuning keeps the formants of a
given vowel within a relatively stable frequency range. This
finding answers why, even at high pitches, Kaxi is still able to
produce distinct vowel qualities.
In conclusion, this study provides empirical evidence,
based on kinematic, acoustic, and perceptual analyses, that
Kaxi effectively mimics natural human speech. Although Kaxi
vowels exhibit formant patterns distinct from those of typical
speech, listeners are still able to perceive them accurately. This
result offers theoretical insights into the non-linear relationship
between acoustic signals and speech perception. Additionally,
in cases where severe structural damage, such as glossectomy
for tongue cancer or cleft palate, impairs the vocal tract’s abil-
ity to produce normal speech, systematic training in Kaxi’s hand
articulation techniques may provide patients with a low-cost,
non-invasive method to regain near-normal speaking abilities.
Future research could systematically investigate Kaxi’s pro-
duction of diphthongs, consonants, and continuous speech, and
should also develop a more comprehensive database of hand
postures for different sounds. Although the number of Kaxi in-
heritors is gradually decreasing, we will also do our utmost to
expand the number of performers. These efforts will enhance
both theoretical understanding of speech production and per-
ception and practical applications in clinical speech rehabilita-
tion.
