Abstract
Variational Autoencoders (V AEs) are essential for large-scale
audio tasks like diffusion-based generation. However, exist-
ing open-source models often neglect auditory perception, re-
sulting in poor phase accuracy and stereo representation. We
propose ear-V AE, an open-source music reconstruction model
that optimizes the V AE training paradigm. Our contributions
are threefold: (i) A K-weighting perceptual filter applied prior
to loss calculation to align the objective with auditory percep-
tion. (ii) A phase-aware correlation loss improving stereo co-
herence and spatial consistency. (iii) A spectral supervision
scheme where magnitude loss is applied to all MSLR com-
ponents, while phase is supervised only on LR components.
Experiments show ear-V AE substantially outperforms leading
open-source models across diverse metrics, showing particular
strength in reconstructing high-frequency harmonics and spatial
characteristics.

1. Introduction
Achieving perfect, perceptually lossless reconstruction of com-
plex audio signals like music remains a central challenge in au-
dio engineering and machine learning. High-fidelity audio Vari-
ational Autoencoders (V AEs) [1] are foundational reconstruc-
tive components for many downstream tasks. These compo-
nents fundamentally differ from those of traditional generative
V AEs like MusicV AE [2]. While the latter prioritizes the gener-
ation of semantically authentic novel content, the former aims
to compress and decompress the original signal losslessly. To
achieve this, the model prioritizes perceptually significant de-
tails and discards imperceptible information. This process re-
lies heavily on psychoacoustic principles, such as utilizing per-
ceptual weighting curves like A-weighting or K-weighting, to
model the frequency-dependent sensitivity of human hearing.
However, modern audio V AE models often fail to effectively
integrate fine-grained perceptual weighting strategies into their
training paradigms.
Furthermore, the reconstruction of high-quality music re-
quires the accurate modelling of both phase and spatial in-
formation. Spatial information, often parameterized by the
Mid/Side (M/S) decomposition, is critical for accurately ren-
dering the stereo image. Moreover, audio transients and clar-
ity are determined by phase-related representations. However,
existing open-source models lack effective mechanisms to su-
pervise these critical factors and fail to fully leverage the M/S
representation for spatial reconstruction, which leads to audible

To address these shortcomings, we introduceϵar-V AE, an
open-source V AE model optimized for high-fidelity music re-
construction. Our model incorporates a K-weighting percep-
tual filter, which we demonstrate is psychoacoustically better
suited for music signals than A-weighting. To ensure phase co-
herence, we propose a DSP-driven loss function that implic-
itly optimizes the phase performance by supervising its correla-
tion. Additionally, we apply the reconstruction loss with a new
Mid/Side/Left/Right (MSLR) weighting scheme to maximize
the preservation of both spatial and spectral details. Through
these targeted designs,ϵar-V AE achieves state-of-the-art re-
construction performance across multiple objective evaluations,
setting a new benchmark for open-source high-fidelity audio
V AEs.
We summarize our contributions as follows: Firstly, we
analyse and integrate the K-weighting filter into the V AE train-
ing pipeline, aligning the reconstruction objective with psy-
choacoustics of music perception, in contrast to the commonly
used A-weighting. Secondly, we propose a novel phase-aware
loss function that supervises the correlation between reconstruc-
tion and real audio to model critical phase differences, thereby
enhancing transient clarity and phase coherence. Thirdly, we in-
troduce a novel supervision strategy that separately constrains
magnitude and phase, employing all MSLR components for
magnitude reconstruction while using only LR to ensure phase
coherence.

Leibler (KL) divergence loss to enforce a continuous Gaus-
sian prior for generation, the reconstruction task prioritizes the

a powerful adversarial component, leveraging discriminators

In contrast, the continuous representation approach offers a
potentially higher-fidelity pathway for reconstruction. The V AE
model from Stable-Audio-Open (SAO) [1] stands as a promi-
nent example of this approach, employing a V AE-GAN frame-
work with adversarial loss and a down-weighted KL divergence
to learn a continuous representation at a high compression rate.
3.ϵar-V AE
Our model, inspired by the V AE architecture of SAO, is a par-
tially convolutional V AE complemented by transformer-based
bottleneck layers, trained with a composite adversarial objec-
tive. As shown in Figure 1, the overall architecture consists
of a traditional V AE generator and a powerful time-frequency
domain discriminator. The generator encodes the input wave-
form into a latent representation and then decodes it back into
a waveform, while the discriminator distinguishes audio signals
between real and reconstructed versions, thereby guiding the
generator to produce higher-fidelity output.
3.1. Generator
Our generator employs an encoder-decoder architecture fea-
turing several key designs optimized for music reconstruction.
The encoder utilizes a series of strided convolutional blocks
with the SnakeBeta activation function [9], which outperforms
alternatives such as ELU in our experiments. The decoder
is designed asymmetrically: it mirrors the encoder’s convolu-
tional structure using transposed convolutions for upsampling
but also incorporates a powerful transformer module with RoPE
position embeddings [10] on the decoding path. This asym-
metric design delegates local feature extraction to the efficient
encoder, while the decoder’s transformers model global de-
pendencies, yielding superior performance over symmetric ar-
chitectures like Mimi [11]. We selected transposed convolu-
tions rather than upsampling-plus-convolution because the for-
mer preserves greater signal energy and perceptual loudness.
We prioritize these attributes over the slightly improved high-
frequency clarity offered by the latter. Finally, all convolutional
layers are weight-normalized [1] for training stability.
3.2. Discriminator
For adversarial training, we employ a Multi-Scale STFT Dis-
criminator as the MSD, inspired by Encodec [5]. This approach
assesses the stereo music signal across various STFT resolu-
tions, enabling the detection of a wide range of artifacts from
coarse spectral errors to fine-grained phase inconsistencies.
While some works like BigVGAN [9] apply the comple-
mentary MSD on the Constant-Q Transform (CQT) representa-
tion, we find this approach degrades the V AE’s ability to repre-
sent music. As shown in Figure 2, CQT overemphasizes low-
to-mid frequency melodic features, leading to a loss of high-
frequency harmonics, whereas the STFT provides a more bal-

3.3. Loss functions
Multi-Scale Log-Magnitude LossTo guide the generator’s
spectral amplitude reconstruction, we adopt the multiscale
STFT loss formulation from EnCodec [5].L stft−mag computes
the L1 distance between the normalized logarithmic magnitudes
of the predicted and target spectrograms over a set of different
STFT resolutions, effectively capturing both coarse harmonic
structures and fine temporal details.
Feature-Map LossWe also employ the feature-matching
loss from EnCodec [5] to enhance perceptual quality. This loss,
Lfmap, minimizes the L1 distance between the intermediate
feature maps extracted from the discriminator’s layers for the
ground-truth and generated audio.
Adversarial LossWe employ the least-squares GAN ob-
jective from BigVGAN [9] for adversarial training, defining the
generator lossL Adv(G)and the discriminator lossL Adv(D).

Leibler (KL) divergence loss, which aligns the posterior distri-
butionq(z|x)with a standard normal priorN(0,I)to promote
a continuous and well-structured representation.

4. Experiments and Results
4.1. Datasets
Our model is trained on a combination of large-scale public
datasets and a high-quality, proprietary in-house dataset. The
training process is conducted in two stages: pre-training and
continue-training. First, we use a diverse mix of public data in-
cluding FSD50K [13], FMA [14], and DISCO-10M [15]. Sub-
sequently, for the continue-training stage, the model is trained
on our in-house dataset of approximately 10,000 hours of pro-
fessionally produced music for further high-fidelity align.
4.2. Data Pipeline
To ensure data quality, we designed a two-stage filtering
pipeline, applied progressively. All datasets undergo Stage 1
for format standardization, while only our in-house data is sub-
jected to the full pipeline.
Stage 1: Format and Loudness StandardizationTo
avoid artifacts associated with upsampling, files natively sam-
pled below 44.1 kHz are discarded. Only files with native sam-
pling rates of 44.1 kHz or higher are retained and downsampled
to 44.1 kHz stereo. Next, we filter based on perceived loudness.
Using the LUFS-I metric [16], we retain only tracks with an in-
tegrated loudness between -22 and -5 LUFS, removing acousti-
cally extreme examples.
Stage 2: True Peak FilteringFor our in-house data, we
discard tracks with true peak levels exceeding +1dB to handle
signal clipping. Unlike the any-clip-rejection strategy from En-
codec [6], this lenient criterion is deliberately chosen to accom-
modate the moderate, intentional clipping common in modern
music mastering.
4.3. Training Details
Theϵar-V AE employs a convolutional encoder/decoder struc-
ture with strides[2,4,4,4,8]and a 128-dimensional la-
tent space, including mean and scale for 64 dimensions
respectively. The decoder is augmented with 2 trans-
former layers using RoPE. The multiscale discriminator and
reconstruction loss functions use STFT window sizes of
[2048,1024,512,256,128]. The full model contains 141M pa-
rameters.
We train all models on 8 A100 GPUs using the AdamW
optimizer with a learning rate of3×10 −4,β 1 = 0.5, and
β2 = 0.9. The loss weights are set as follows:λ stft−mag = 50,
λcorr = 10,λ fmap = 20,λ adv = 1, andλ KL = 10−6 for
reconstruction. We determineλ i empirically to balance the
magnitude ranges of different loss components and observe the
model is generally robust to small variations in coefficients.
However, the reconstruction quality decreases notably when
λKL >10−5.
We train the model for 2M steps over three distinct stages:
Stage 1: Warm-up (10k steps)We train only the gener-
ator using the STFT and KL losses, accompanied by a linear
learning rate warm-up.
Stage 2: Pre-train (1M steps)On the public datasets, we
activate all loss components and train the generator and discrim-
inator alternately. The learning rate is halved at 200k, 400k, and
600k steps.
Stage 3: Continue-train (1M steps)We continue train-
ing on our in-house dataset using the same loss configuration
and alternate training scheme as in Stage 2, with a constant
learning rate.

4.5. Ablation Study
Our design choices are validated through a stepwise ablation
study using a smaller in-house dataset. As detailed in Table 2,
we quantify the contribution of each component using SISDR,
Mel Distance, and STFT Distance metrics.
Impact of Stereo and Spatial SupervisionOur investi-
gation into stereo supervision strategies, shown in theDifferent
MSLR Combinationblock, reinforces the importance of pure
L/R phase guidance. Supervision usingonly M/Sdegrades per-
formance compared to theonly L/Rbaseline, confirming that
forcing phase convergence on mid-side components may distort
phase cues. Furthermore, the addition of theCorrelation Loss
significantly boosts SISDR to 9.85. This metric improvement
demonstrates that explicitly modeling the correlation between
channels enhances spectral coherence more effectively than in-
dependent channel supervision alone.
Analysis of Auxiliary CQT DiscriminatorWe explic-
itly analyze the trade-off introduced by the auxiliary discrimi-
nator. As illustrated in theRemove Auxiliary CQT Discrimina-
torsection, attenuating the discriminator weight (lessCQT) and
subsequently removing it (noCQT) yields consistent improve-
ments in objective metrics, improving SISDR to 10.56 and low-
ering Mel Distance to 0.54. This trend suggests that for the
music reconstruction task, removing the CQT-based supervi-
sion allowsϵar-V AE to optimize full-band frequency response,
thereby achieving higher signal fidelity and cleaner spectro-
grams as discussed in section 3.2.
Impact of Perceptual Weighting and ArchitectureThe
final comparison highlights the efficacy of our frequency
weighting and architectural choices. The proposedK-Weighting
outperforms standardA-Weighting, proving its capability to pre-
serve critical mid-to-high frequency details that A-weighting
might attenuate. Finally, incorporating theTransformer Block
culminates in the best overall performance, quantitatively val-
idating that self-attention mechanisms are essential for mod-
eling long-range dependencies and refining fine-grained har-
monic structures.
5. Conclusion
In this paper, we presentϵar-V AE, a variational autoencoder that
sets a new state-of-the-art for high-fidelity music reconstruc-
tion. Our ablation studies confirm that the carefully designed
components, including novel perceptual and phase-based su-
pervisions, contribute significantly to the superior performance.
Furthermore, we believe that addressing the model’s tendency
to attenuate subtle spatial effects by exploring targeted loss
functions remains a promising research direction. Tackling
these challenges will unlock the next generation of controllable
and realistic generative music models.
