Abstract
We introduceGLAD-CSpeech, a linguistically-grounded
benchmark for genuine Chinese dialect speech supporting au-
tomatic speech recognition (ASR), text-to-speech (TTS), and
dialect identification (DID). The dataset spans 16 dialect di-
visions—8 Mandarin subgroups and 8 major non-Mandarin
groups—covering all dialect regions recognized in Chinese di-
alectology. It includes speech from 23 representative locations
and distinguishes Genuine Dialect from Accented Mandarin to
enable controlled study of the Mandarin–Dialect continuum.
The corpus contains over 163 hours of speech, with studio
recordings for TTS and diverse real-world recordings for ASR,
covering daily and application-oriented scenarios. We provide
dialect-aware transcriptions and Mandarin–dialect parallel texts
for all utterances. Baseline systems for ASR, TTS, and DID are
reported. GLAD-CSpeech is released free for non-commercial
research use.
Index Terms: automatic speech recognition, text-to-speech, di-
alect identification, Chinese dialects, normalization
1. Introduction
Despite rapid advances in speech technologies such as au-
tomatic speech recognition (ASR) and text-to-speech (TTS)
across multilingual settings [1], dialectal speech remains a sys-
temic and underaddressed challenge [2, 3]. Model performance
degrades substantially when transferred from standard language
varieties to non-standard dialects, owing to mismatches in
phonology, lexicon, and morphosyntax. A critical bottleneck
persists in the absence of high-purity, linguistically grounded
benchmarks capable of systematically diagnosing model failure
points along the Mandarin–dialect continuum [4, 5].
Chinese presents a particularly acute case. As a macro-
language, it is characterized by extensive internal variation, an
enormous speaker population, and strikingly low mutual intelli-
gibility across its constituent varieties [6]. Yet existing Chinese
dialect resources are marked by severe imbalance across vari-
eties, a lack of standardized evaluation protocols, and an overre-
liance on geo-tagged but linguistically unverified data or mixed-
speech corpora that fail to capture authentic dialectal structure.
While such geographic categorization may offer intuitive
convenience for general audiences, it proves fundamentally
problematic for DID and downstream tasks such as ASR and
TTS. Dialect boundaries rarely coincide with administrative

likely to perform well across speakers from Henan, Hebei, and

labels obscure rather than reveal.
To address these gaps, we propose GLAD-CSpeech
(Grounded Linguistically Aligned Dialectologically-complete
Corpus for Chinese Speech), a Genuinely Linguistically-
grounded Across-regional Dialect Corpus for Chinese Speech,
built with linguists and covering major dialect groups. Our con-
tributions include: (i) a full-coverage benchmark that spans 16
dialect regions and 23 representative dialect points, enabling a
unified evaluation of Chinese dialect variation; (ii) a principled
separation of Genuine Dialect vs. Accented Mandarin, allow-
ing controlled comparisons between accent robustness and true
dialect divergence; (iii) an expert-guided pipeline for sampling,
transcription and dialect lexicon construction, yielding authen-
tic data with a consistent 3-layer annotation scheme (§3.3); (iv)
standardized, deployment-oriented ASR and TTS baselines to
facilitate reproducible benchmarking.
2. Related Work
Several open-source corpora have advanced Chinese dialect
speech processing by improving data scale and accessibility.
KeSpeech [4] offers large-scale recordings spanning Standard
Mandarin and multiple subdialects, enabling robustness bench-
marking. Mandarin-centric corpora like AISHELL [7, 8] and
industrial releases like MagicData1 provide strong baselines and
practical training data, supporting dialect modeling via transfer
and adaptation. Web-scale collections such as the WenetSpeech
series [9, 10, 11] further expand high-resource varieties (e.g.,
Sichuan and Wu), accelerating single-dialect ASR progress.
A remaining challenge is that current resources are often
built with different labeling conventions, making cross-dialect
comparability and evaluation difficult. In-the-wild web au-
dio may introduce domain skew and Mandarin-mixed speech,

3. GLAD-CSpeech
GLAD-CSpeech is a linguistically grounded benchmark cor-
pus for real-world Chinese dialect speech. The corpus to-
tals an estimated over 163.3 hours of audio, with the final re-
lease projected to exceed this figure. It is organized into two
complementary subsets—Genuine Dialect and Accented Man-
darin—reflecting a principled distinction between speech that
exhibits non-Mandarin grammatical structures and dialectal lex-
icon, and speech that retains Mandarin grammar while display-
ing dialectal phonological influence.
A defining characteristic of GLAD-CSpeech is its adher-
ence to dialectological taxonomy rather than administrative ge-
ographic labels, ensuring systematic coverage of all major di-
alect groups recognized in Chinese dialectology, which renders
GLAD-CSpeech suitable as a multi-task benchmark, supporting
ASR, TTS, and DID evaluation, as well as Standard-to-Dialect
and Dialect-to-Dialect translation via Mandarin–dialect aligned
supervision [13]. Consistent transcription conventions and task-
ready metadata are maintained across all subsets.
3.1. Comprehensive Dialect Coverage via Dialectological
Taxonomy
Following theLanguage Atlas of China[6]—a widely recog-
nized and authoritative reference for the geographic classifica-
tion of Chinese dialects [14, 15, 16]—GLAD-CSpeech encom-
passes 16 dialect regions, comprising 8 major non-Mandarin di-
alect groups and 8 Mandarin subgroups. Under the principle of
representative sampling, 23 dialect points were strategically se-
lected by prioritizing population-dense and socioeconomically
influential cities to ensure robust downstream generalization.
The detailed geographic distribution of all regions and sampling
points is documented in Table 2.
3.2. Genuine Dialect and Real-world Scenarios
GLAD-CSpeech is recorded in real-life settings and explic-
itly targets practical deployment conditions through broad sce-
nario coverage and the deliberate inclusion of hard cases. For
ASR, prompts span 13 application scenarios (Fig. 1a), with
Daily Life as the dominant category, supplemented by smart
and service-oriented scenarios that expose models to interaction
patterns prevalent in modern speech systems. TTS data follows
a parallel structure (Fig. 1b), supporting both naturalistic and
application-driven synthesis.
Task types reflect pragmatic diversity [17]. Each dialect
point comprises 6 speakers: 4 dedicated to ASR tasks, and 2
contributing to both ASR and TTS tasks (Table 3). For ASR
(Fig. 1c), Narration, Question, Answer, and Dialogue are kept
in near-equal proportions, with Long Text additionally included
as a hard case [18, 19] for long-context modeling. For TTS
(Fig. 1d), Narration constitutes the dominant type, while Ques-
tion, Answer, and Dialogue are retained as interactive formats.

3.3. Multi-layer Aligned Annotation Schema
GLAD-CSpeech provides a three-layer annotation system: (1)
Mandarin text as a shared semantic anchor across dialect points;
(2) dialect transcription preserving the most natural dialectal re-
alization; and (3) a per-dialect-point lexicon glossary standard-
izing dialectal vocabulary and supporting evaluation. The Man-
darin–dialect parallel design yields aligned supervision for ASR
and TTS, and further enables machine translation between Man-
darin and dialect varieties under a unified semantic pivot.
Dialect transcription follows a character-first principle with
phonetic grounding [20, 21, 22], applied in order of decreas-
ing certainty: popular/colloquial characters are prioritized, fol-
lowed by etymological characters, then homophonic charac-
ters with IPA, and finally “□(IPA)” (Unicode U+25A1) where
no suitable character exists. Natural speech phenomena such
as fillers and hesitations are preserved to reflect authentic us-
age. Conversational recordings additionally include overlap an-
notation to facilitate research on realistic multi-speaker condi-
tions [23, 24]. All recordings span diverse consumer-grade de-

4. Experiments
This section presents comprehensive experimental evaluations
of GLAD-CSpeech across ASR, TTS, and DID tasks, validat-
ing the advantages of our linguistically-grounded classification
framework for authentic dialectal speech modeling.
4.1. ASR Task
We conduct a zero-shot ASR evaluation on GLAD-CSpeech
and the related dataset KeSpeech, using open-source baselines
(Whisper-large-v3 [25], Dolphin [26]) and a commercial base-
line (Qwen3-ASR [27]) with dialect recognition capability. We
report character error rate (CER, %) as the primary metric.

Task Setup. We focus on 6 representative dialect points:
Chengdu (Southwest Mandarin), Xi’an (Central Plains Man-
darin), Changsha (Xiang), Taiyuan (Jin), Wenzhou (Wu), and
Meixian (Hakka), covering Mandarin and non-Mandarin groups
as well as broad geographic regions (west, central plains, and
east of China), providing a diverse spectrum of intelligibility
and dialectal divergence. The test portion totals around 24 h
of speech, with an average of about 4 hours per point, and
each point contains 650 utterance-level W A V files. Overlapped
speech segments are included without special handling to reflect
real usage. We additionally run the same protocol on KeSpeech
open-source test data for Chengdu and Xi’an.
We calculate CER by comparing recognition results with
dialect-layer transcriptions, which preserve dialect-specific lex-
icon and grammatical constructions (described in §3). This
choice reflects the intended objective of dialect ASR and avoids
conflating recognition with Mandarin normalization.
Results and Discussion. Table 4 shows the average CER
of each model across dialect points. The gap between Mandarin
accents and non-Mandarin dialects is substantial, and perfor-
mance is highly dialect-dependent: Wenzhou (Wu) and Meix-
ian (Hakka) are the most challenging, while Mandarin points
are markedly easier. This pattern suggests that accent robust-
ness alone is insufficient for genuine dialect ASR, where lexical
and grammatical divergence becomes a dominant error source.
CER on KeSpeech is much lower than on the corresponding
GLAD-CSpeech Mandarin points, suggesting that even within
the same Mandarin subgroups, GLAD-CSpeech better reflects
the practical difficulty of dialect-facing ASR through richer di-
alectal realizations and more realistic speaking conditions.
4.2. TTS Task
We evaluate dialectal speech synthesis on Central Plains Man-
darin (Xi’an), motivated by two practical considerations: (i)
current TTS models still provide limited support for dialect
generation [22], and (ii) dialect difficulty varies widely across
China (§1). We therefore select Xi’an as a representative
yet moderate-difficulty point, and adopt GPT-SoVITS 2 as our
backbone, a robust TTS framework that supports Standard Man-
darin but lacks native support for Chinese dialect synthesis.
Task Setup. We fine-tune GPT-SoVITS on the Xi’an TTS
subset (3.93 hours; 2 speakers). Crucially, we utilize the Layer 2
Dialect Transcription (§3.3) as the input. This layer’s character-
first rule with phonetic grounding is essential: it allows a model
without prior dialect support to bypass standard Mandarin pro-
nunciation constraints and directly learn the mapping between
dialectal text realizations and their corresponding acoustic nu-
ances. Evaluation uses 50 expert-designed dialect texts cover-
ing dialectal grammar, dialect-specific vocabularies and modern
smart-scenario terms, with an average duration of about 6 sec-
onds. We employed 6 native Xi’an speakers to rate the samples
in a blind, single-stimulus MOS test.
Metrics and results. We report four MOS results (Ta-
ble 5): MOS (overall quality), Prosody MOS (PMOS), intelli-
gibility MOS (IMOS), and accent-authenticity MOS (AMOS).
The model achieved solid performance, with an overall MOS
of 3.78 and IMOS reaching 3.91. Most notably, the AMOS
score of 3.65 demonstrates that despite the backbone’s lack of
inherent dialect capabilities, GLAD-CSpeech’s high-purity an-
notations effectively transfer regional phonetic nuances. These
results confirm that GLAD-CSpeech serves as a high-fidelity

4.3. DID Task
To validate GLAD-CSpeech, we conduct a Dialect Identifica-
tion (DID) task on the same dialect points under a speaker-
independent protocol [28], using 4,086 utterances from 4 speak-
ers for training and 844 from 2 unseen speakers for testing.
Task Setup. We evaluate two models with claimed dialect
recognition capabilities. FireRedLID [29], a dialect identifica-
tion model based on a mixed taxonomy of provincial and lin-
guistic categories, is evaluated zero-shot. Dolphin [26], a mul-
tilingual speech foundation model with a similar mixed taxon-
omy, is extended with a 6-way linear classification head and
fine-tuned on our training split, where the 6 classes correspond
to the 6 dialect points, each representing a distinct dialect group.
Results and Discussion. As summarized in Table 6, Fir-
eRedLID achieved a Macro F1 of 72.29% in a zero-shot setting,
indicating that mixed provincial-linguistic taxonomies trans-
fer poorly to dialectologically defined boundaries. Dolphin-
FT reached a Macro F1 of 99.08%, demonstrating that even a
lightweight classification head, when fine-tuned on dialectolog-
ically grounded data, yields strong discrimination across dialect
groups. The high separability on unseen speakers confirms that
GLAD-CSpeech captures robust phonetic boundaries, validat-
ing the categorical clarity of our expert-guided corpus.
5. Conclusion and Future Work
We present GLAD-CSpeech, a linguistically grounded bench-
mark designed to address the evaluation gap in Chinese dialect
speech processing. By prioritizing linguistic purity and cate-
gorical clarity over data volume, GLAD-CSpeech provides a
high-fidelity diagnostic tool covering 16 dialect regions and 23
representative points. Baseline experiments across ASR, TTS,
and DID tasks demonstrate that current systems remain brittle
under genuine dialects, with particularly large degradations on
non-Mandarin varieties such as Wu and Hakka, underscoring
the practical value of dialectologically grounded evaluation re-
sources for robust and inclusive speech technology.
Future work will extend GLAD-CSpeech in scale and eval-
uation coverage. The three-layer annotation scheme (§3.3) will
support more types of tasks including Dialect-to-Standard and
Dialect-to-Dialect translation with controllable lexical choice
and dialectal style transfer. We plan to diversify speaking styles
and strengthen baselines via dialect-aware training and more ro-
bust modeling of long-context and overlapping speech.
