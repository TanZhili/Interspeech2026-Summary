Tongue-Shape Strategies for Standard Mandarin Retroflex Sibilants:

Abstract
Standard Mandarin retroflex sibilants are often described as
non-prototypical retroflexes and may show diverse articulatory
realisations. Using tongue ultrasound imaging, we examined
five female native speakers from diverse regional backgrounds
producing rhyme-matched alveolar-retroflex sibilant minimal
pairs in a carrier sentence. Tongue contours were extracted
from target frames, represented as 42 points, and aligned by
centroid centring. Unsupervised clustering of contour-based
shape descriptors yielded six clusters, which were consolidated
into three super-strategies (domed, humped, and concave), in a
scheme that differs from prior descriptive frameworks. Com-
positional clustering of super-strategy proportions revealed sys-
tematic between-speaker differences. Overall, consistent with
prior work, Standard Mandarin retroflex sibilants permit multi-
ple tongue-shape strategies, and speakers show strong, speaker-
specific preferences that group them into stable clusters.
Index Terms: Standard Mandarin, retroflex sibilants, ultra-
sound tongue imaging (UTI), articulatory strategy, unsuper-

1. Introduction
1.1. Retroflexes in Standard Mandarin
In Standard Mandarin (Putonghua), one character typically cor-
responds to one syllable(C 1)VT(C2). Here, C 1 is the initial
consonant (onset); C2 can be realised as /n,N/; V is the vocalic
nucleus (a monophthong or diphthong); and T is the tonal value.
The sequence VC 2 is often treated as a single unit, the rhyme.
In principle, C1 and the rhyme could combine freely; however,
due to phonotactic constraints, not all combinations are attested
in the lexicon. In Standard Mandarin, retroflex sibilants occur
only in the onset position C1.
Retroflexion is commonly described as being produced by
curling the tongue tip up and back so the underside of the tongue
touches or approaches the alveolar ridge [1]. Compared with
consonants at adjacent places of articulation (e.g., alveolar or
dental), retroflex is often considered to reflect greater articula-
tory complexity and difficulty [2].
The retroflex series is a major characteristic of Standard
Mandarin, which is typically described as having three sets of
so-called ‘retroflex’ sibilants that contrast phonemically with
their alveolar counterparts: /ts-tù/, /ts h-tùh/, and /s-ù/ [3]. The
phonological description of Standard Mandarin is largely based
on Beijing Mandarin. However, because Standard Mandarin
is widely spoken as a lingua franca by speakers of diverse na-
tive Chinese varieties, and because many such varieties lack
retroflex sibilants, Standard Mandarin retroflex sibilants may
show substantial inter-speaker and within-speaker variation in
their articulatory realisations.
However, these Standard Mandarin ‘retroflex’ sibilants lack
the strong posteriority characteristic of prototypical retroflex
segments (which typically involve a set of articulatory proper-
ties, including apicality, posteriority, sublingual cavity forma-
tion, and tongue retraction [2]). Accordingly, Standard Man-
darin retroflex sibilants are better characterised as marginal or
non-prototypical members of the broader retroflex category.
1.2. Previous research on retroflex in Standard Mandarin
Several studies have examined the acoustic properties of
retroflex sibilants in Standard Mandarin. Previous work has
also investigated pronunciation variation in the /s-ù/ contrast
(and its merger), focusing on acoustic-perceptual correlates and
geographical patterning [4, 5]. However, much of this work
considers retroflexes only within broader sibilant categories in
Standard Mandarin [6], rather than focusing specifically on
the alveolar-retroflex contrast itself. Moreover, many studies
concentrate on a limited set of well-studied regional varieties
(e.g. Beijing Mandarin and Taiwan Mandarin) and characterise
alveolar-retroflex distinctions primarily in acoustic terms, often
using spectral moments (especially centre of gravity, COG) and
formant-based cues [4, 7, 8].
In addition to acoustic analyses, previous research has em-
ployed articulatory methods to investigate tongue configuration
in the production of retroflex sibilants. Ultrasound tongue imag-
ing (UTI) is a non-invasive method for visualising tongue sur-
face in real time. Existing UTI work on Standard Mandarin
retroflex sibilants has largely focused on a limited range of well-
studied varieties. In Beijing Mandarin, ultrasound studies re-
port that many speakers do not show systematic tongue-tip curl-
ing; instead, they employ a range of tongue-shape patterns (e.g.
bunched or humped) to realise the contrast [9]. In Taiwan Man-
darin, ultrasound has been used primarily to assess whether the
alveolar–retroflex contrast has merged [4, 10].
Because Standard Mandarin is used as a lingua franca by
speakers from a wide range of native Chinese varieties, artic-
ulatory realisations of retroflex sibilants may vary systemati-
cally across speakers with different regional backgrounds. Ex-
isting ultrasound studies on the /s-ù/ contrast have already re-
ported substantial inter-speaker and context-dependent differ-
ences in tongue postures, including varying degrees and types
of articulatory merger, which are not always captured by acous-
tic measures alone [4]. Together with large-scale evidence that

3. conduct speaker-level compositional analyses to compare
how individual speakers distribute their productions across
the identified tongue-shape categories.
2. Method
2.1. Participants and stimuli
We employed tongue ultrasound imaging to examine the tongue
gestures used in the production of Standard Mandarin retroflex
sibilants. Throughout this paper, Standard Mandarin refers
to the standardised variety known across communities asPu-
tonghua,Guoyu, orHuayu, as distinct from the broader Man-
darin branch of Sinitic (which also includes regional varieties
such as Southwestern Mandarin). We use it to denote a common
articulatory target rather than a normative output: all speakers
self-identified as reading Standard Mandarin and aimed at their
own notion of its standard pronunciation, while their actual pho-
netic realisations may vary. It is this variation in realisation that
the present study aims to investigate.
Six female native speakers of Standard Mandarin (mean age
= 27 years), all reporting no history of speech or hearing dis-
orders, were recruited. One participant was excluded owing
to poor ultrasound imaging quality, leaving five speakers for
analysis. These five came from diverse regional backgrounds:
Speaker 1 was from New Zealand, with family origins in Fujian,
China; Speaker 2 was from Wuhu, Anhui, China; Speaker 3 was
from Malaysia, with family origins in Fujian, China; Speaker 4
was from Mianyang, Sichuan, China; and Speaker 5 was from
Yuci, Shanxi, China. Despite these varied backgrounds, all five
were judged to have native-level Standard Mandarin proficiency
on the basis of a background questionnaire and the first author’s
assessment.
Given the limited sample size, the study is exploratory.
Nevertheless, we aimed to maximise diversity in speaker back-
grounds by sampling speakers from different countries and with
varied regional and family dialect backgrounds, in order to
provide an initial characterisation of articulatory variation in
retroflex sibilant realisations.
Following Luo [9], this study examined the Standard Man-
darin alveolar-retroflex contrast and restricted the stimulus set
to attested Standard Mandarin characters. Candidate characters
were identified primarily fromHanyu fangyin zihui[11], which
is a compendium of attested characters organised by onsets and
rhymes. Rhyme-matched minimal pairs were constructed by
pairing retroflex onsets (/tù,tù h,ù/) with their alveolar counter-
parts (/ts, ts h, s/). Given the phonotactic constraints noted in
Section 1.1, we retained only rhymes that permitted all three
alveolar-retroflex pairings (/ts-tù/, /ts h-tùh/, and /s-ù/), and re-
stricted all target items to high-level Tone 1, a level tone, to
minimise potential tonal influences on tongue configuration.
This design yielded 21 minimal pairs (42 characters), providing
broad coverage of the alveolar-retroflex onset contrast across

2.2. Recording setup and data collection
Audio and ultrasound data were collected simultaneously in a
whisper room (sound-attenuated booth). Speech was recorded
using a studio-grade large-diaphragm condenser microphone
(RØDE NT1, 5th Generation), with the signal routed through
a Roland OCTA-CAPTURE USB audio interface to the record-
ing computer. Tongue motion was captured concurrently using
a wireless ultrasound probe (UProbe-C, Sonostar). The ultra-
sound acquisition settings were as follows: imaging depth =
220 mm and dynamic range = 80 dB, with a frame rate of 10
frames per second (fps). To minimise probe displacement, the
probe was stabilised with a 3D-printed holder. The setup used
one computer (PC1) and an external display (PC2) for stimulus
presentation and ultrasound display/recording, respectively (see
Fig. 1a).
Participants were seated in front of the setup, facing the mi-
crophone, and held the probe in place using the holder. Stimuli
were presented on PC1 via a MATLAB GUI written by the first
author. Each item was embedded in a carrier sentence,wo ba
du hao(‘I read well’), which allowed relatively natural pro-
duction while controlling the surrounding segments and keep-
ing the target item in a comparable prosodic position. Because
each character in Standard Mandarin typically corresponds to
one syllable, the carrier sentence comprised five characters (five
syllables), with the target stimulus always placed in the medial
third position. Participants were instructed to read the sentence
once for each stimulus in Standard Mandarin, and stimuli were
presented in randomised order. During the experiment, the ex-
perimenter operated PC1 and PC2 to record the audio and ultra-
sound data. Participants were remunerated for their time.

2.3. Data processing
Audio and ultrasound recordings were first synchronised in
Adobe Premiere Pro, after which target frames were extracted.
The target frame was selected from the consonant interval as
the frame showing maximal constriction. To standardise image
orientation, the ultrasound images were corrected for rotation
so that the tongue tip appeared in the lower-right region of the
image. Tongue contours were then traced semi-automatically
from the target frames in EdgeTrak [12] (see Fig. 1b) and saved
as coordinate (.con) files; each contour was represented by a
set of 42 discrete points. Given the limited imaging range of
the ultrasound probe, the anterior and posterior extremes of the
tongue surface could be obscured by acoustic shadowing from
the mandible and the hyoid bone, respectively. We therefore re-
tained only the visible tongue-surface segment spanning from
the posterior tongue body to the anterior tongue blade, exclud-
ing the extreme tongue tip and the most posterior tongue root
when these were outside the field of view.
We then collected all tongue-contour (.con) files. Each con-
tour was aligned in the probe coordinate system by centring the
contour (i.e., subtracting the contour centroid), which reduces
global offsets attributable to probe placement and inter-speaker
anatomical differences while preserving the original scale and
shape (i.e., no rotation or size normalisation). The resulting
dataset provides one 42-point tongue contour per token under a
consistent coordinate system.
2.4. Data analysis
2.4.1. Unsupervised clustering of tongue shape by token
To characterise potential tongue-shape strategies used for
retroflex production, we conducted an unsupervised clustering
analysis on the 42-point tongue contours in R extracted from
.con files corresponding to retroflex tokens. To avoid impos-
ing speaker-specific priors during strategy discovery, speaker
identity was not used in the clustering step; instead, tokens
were grouped solely based on contour-derived representations.
We extracted a set of contour-based shape descriptors captur-
ing global contour extent (xrange, yrange), a global curvature
summary (P|∆2y|), extrema locations (peak/trough indices),
front/back linear slopes (points 1-21 vs. 22-42), two-peak (bi-
modal) contour descriptors (bimodality, valley depth, peak sep-
aration, peak indices), and piecewise slope/curvature computed
over six equal point segments along the 42-point contour. The
feature matrix wasz-score standardised, and pairwise token dis-
tances were computed in the standardised feature space. Clus-
tering was performed using Ward.D2 hierarchical clustering fol-
lowed by dendrogram cutting with dynamicTreeCut. The final
solution was selected via an automated grid search over deep-

2.4.2. Super-strategy grouping
Among the six clusters obtained from the unsupervised analysis
(Fig. 2), Clusters 2, 4, and 6 showed a clear single peak in the
posterior portion of the contour, whereas Clusters 1 and 3 were
comparatively flatter overall. Cluster 5 exhibited a bimodal
profile with a pronounced mid-contour concavity between two
peaks. In Luo’s typology, retroflex tongue shapes were primar-
ily characterised as humped versus bunched. In line with Luo’s
approach, our data clearly contained humped configurations,
defined by a single prominent and well-localised peak. How-
ever, we did not observe a clear bunched pattern as described
by Luo (i.e., a higher and tighter bulge in the mid-to-posterior
region with a relatively lower or more retracted anterior por-
tion). Instead, we observed a domed configuration, in which
the contour is relatively flat with a broad, non-pointed eleva-
tion, as well as a distinct concave configuration characterised
by a midline dip. We therefore consolidated the six clusters into
three higher-level super-strategies: domed (Clusters 1 and 3),
humped (Clusters 2, 4, and 6), and concave (Cluster 5). After
outlier removal, speaker-level proportions were computed for

2.4.3. Unsupervised clustering of speakers by super-strategy
composition
Specifically, each speaker’s three-part proportion vector was
centre log-ratio (CLR) transformed, and Ward.D2 hierarchical
clustering was performed in the CLR space. The number of
speaker groups was selected by maximising the mean silhou-
ette coefficient; under this criterion, the optimal solution was
k= 2(mean silhouette = 0.626), separating speakers into{1,
4}versus{2, 3, 5}. Within the latter group, the dendrogram
suggested an additional split (visually corresponding to 2, 3 vs.
5; see Fig. 3). In distributional terms, the three super-strategies
differed significantly across speakers (Monte Carloχ 2 test,p
<0.001). To assess branch stability, we additionally used pv-
clust bootstrap resampling and reported AU and BP values. The
key branches showed AU = 1.00 (BP = 1.00) under the current
bootstrap settings.
3. Discussion
3.1. Strategy typology of Standard Mandarin retroflex sibi-
lants
Using unsupervised clustering of ultrasound tongue-contour
data, we identified three recurrent tongue-shape strategies for
Standard Mandarin retroflex sibilants: domed, humped, and
concave. Speaker-level analyses showed systematic individual
differences in how these strategies were deployed. Together,
these findings suggest that even within the retroflex sibilant cat-
egory, speakers may use different articulatory strategies to re-
alise the intended contrast. Notably, our goal is not to map
geographical distributions; rather, in a small but background-
diverse ultrasound sample, we characterise recurrent articula-
tory strategies and quantify speaker-level differences in their
strategy compositions. Whether these compositions align with
geographically structured merger patterns remains to be tested
in larger, targeted samples.
In terms of tongue-contour morphology, the domed strat-
egy is comparatively flat overall but shows a broad elevation;
the humped strategy displays a single, relatively well-localised
peak, typically in the posterior region of the contour; and the
concave strategy exhibits a bimodal profile with a pronounced
mid-contour dip. Importantly, because ultrasound contours pri-
marily capture the visible tongue-body region and do not di-
rectly measure the tongue tip, we avoid making direct observa-
tional claims about ‘tongue-tip curling.’ Nevertheless, the ob-
served tongue-body shape patterns suggest that characterising
Standard Mandarin retroflex sibilants does not necessarily re-
quire assuming a single prototypical ‘retroflex’ tongue-tip con-
figuration; instead, similar perceptual targets may be achieved
via multiple tongue-body shaping strategies. This interpretation
is broadly compatible with prior claims that Standard Mandarin
retroflex sibilants can be implemented via diverse articulatory
configurations [9].
Relative to Luo’s typology [9], which emphasised humped
and bunched retroflex tongue shapes, we observed clear humped
patterns but did not find clear contour-based evidence for a
distinct bunched configuration (i.e., a higher and tighter mid-
to-posterior bulge accompanied by a relatively lower or more
retracted anterior portion). One plausible explanation con-
cerns differences in participant composition and language back-
ground: Luo’s sample was relatively homogeneous and closer
to a narrowly defined, Beijing-centred profile of ‘Standard
Mandarin’ users, whereas we intentionally included speakers
with more diverse regional and heritage backgrounds (including
overseas Chinese communities; see Section 2.1). This broader
sampling better captures cross-speaker variation in retroflex re-
alisation, and may therefore yield strategy distributions and
tongue-contour patterns that differ from those reported in Luo.
3.2. Speaker-specific strategy preferences
The significant speaker effect supports strong individual prefer-
ences in retroflex sibilant implementation: some speakers pre-
dominantly use humped shapes, others favour domed shapes,
and some exhibit a higher proportion of concave shapes (see
Fig. 4). Moreover, compositional clustering based on the three
super-strategy proportions yielded a two-group solution, al-
though the dendrogram suggested an additional within-group
split (see Fig. 3). Together, these results indicate that strategy
composition provides a compact descriptor of articulatory style
in Standard Mandarin retroflex production. This is consistent
with the view that Standard Mandarin retroflex sibilants can
be realised via multiple feasible articulatory configurations, and
that strategy use varies systematically across speakers.
3.3. Implications and future work
Future studies should validate the proposed strategy typology
in a larger, more diverse sample of Standard Mandarin speak-
ers, and use complementary articulatory measures (e.g., higher
frame-rate ultrasound, electromagnetic articulography, intraoral
pressure and airflow) to more directly evaluate the robustness
and underlying mechanisms of these tongue-shape strategies.
Strategy labels and speaker-level strategy compositions can also
be used as higher-level predictors to examine their relationships
with acoustic measures and specific language backgrounds.
