Online Audio-Visual Target Speaker Extraction with Viseme-Guided
Lightweight Visual Pretraining

Abstract
Audio-visual target speaker extraction (A V-TSE) leverages vi-
sual information to recover a target speech signal from multi-
speaker mixtures. However, existing systems are often built
with non-causal architectures and incur high computational
cost, which limits real-time deployment. While some stud-
ies reduce the overall complexity by designing lighter separa-
tion backbones, comparatively little attention has been paid to
improving deployability by optimizing the visual branch. In
this work, we revisit the design of visual cues and employ
visemes as the visual guidance signal. Compared with conven-
tional VSR-pretrained visual representations, viseme pretrain-
ing yields a more lightweight, robust, and interpretable cue. In
addition, we propose an online viseme-guided TSE system that
achieves strong performance under the lowest computational
budget.
Index Terms: speech recognition, human-computer interac-
tion, computational paralinguistics
1. Introduction
Target speaker extraction (TSE) aims to recover a target speech
signal from a multi-talker acoustic scene by leveraging an aux-
iliary cue, offering a practical solution to the classic cocktail
party problem [1, 2]. Such cues can be categorized by modal-
ity, including (i)audio cuessuch as a pre-recorded enrollment
utterance from the target speaker [3, 4, 5], (ii)visual cuessuch
as a face track synchronized with the target’s lip movements
[6, 7, 8, 9], and (iii)spatial cuessuch as the direction of ar-
rival (DoA) or other location-related information about the tar-
get talker [10, 11, 12].
Recent studies show that audio-visual TSE can outperform
audio-only counterparts in separation quality and robustness.
Specifically, audio-visual systems are more reliable with same-
gender speakers or strongly overlapping interferers because lip
movements provide complementary articulatory cues that re-
duce speaker ambiguity [13]. They are also more robust at low
SNRs because visual observations (especially mouth dynamics)
are not corrupted by acoustic noise [14]. Moreover, cameras
are widely available on smartphones, PCs, and meeting devices,
making visual cues easy to acquire and potentially obviating the
need for users to record an enrollment utterance in advance [15].
However, most existing audio-visual TSE systems adopt
non-causal architectures and incur high computational cost,
which limits real-time deployment on edge devices. Recent
work has explored fully causal designs to support streaming in-
ference [16, 17]. Nevertheless, the high multiply-accumulate
(MAC) count can still lead to unacceptable latency and power

consumption for on-device processing. Prior studies reduce the
MACs in the audio branch to lower computational cost and im-
prove streaming efficiency [18, 19, 20]. In contrast, less atten-
tion has been paid to optimizing the visual front-end for deploy-
able audio-visual systems.
Most audio-visual systems adopt a visual encoder pre-
trained for lip reading, i.e., visual speech recognition (VSR)
[21, 22]. Because VSR is challenging, such encoders often have
many parameters and high computational cost. To reduce visual
computation, [23] trains the visual encoder jointly with the sep-
arator and replaces the visual backbone from ResNet-18 [24]
to BlazeNet [25], reducing visual computation from 12.9 G to
2.1 G MACs. In what follows, we refer to the task-specific vi-
sual representation learned via such joint training as amouth
cue. However, this line of work mainly focuses on network
architectural choices and does not explicitly re-examine the de-
sign of visual cues. In addition, [15] retains only visual voice
activity detection (VV AD) as the visual cue, which greatly sim-
plifies the visual front-end. This strategy is effective for lightly
overlapped mixtures, but it may fail under fully overlapped con-
ditions where fine-grained mouth dynamics are needed to dis-
ambiguate the target speaker.
Motivated by these observations, we revisit the design of
visual cues and employ visemes as the visual guidance signal.
In this paper, a viseme denotes a set of phonemes with indistin-
guishable lip shapes [26] (e.g., /p/, /b/, and /m/), which differs
from [27], where the embedding produced by a visual encoder
is referred to as a “viseme”.
We pretrain the visual front-end with a viseme recognition
objective, which provides explicit and task-aligned supervision
and yields a more interpretable representation than a jointly
learnedmouth cue. Compared with VV AD, viseme-level su-
pervision preserves articulatory dynamics beyond speech activ-
ity, thereby providing finer-grained guidance in fully overlapped
mixtures. Compared with VSR, viseme recognition uses a sub-
stantially smaller label inventory and is generally less demand-
ing, which facilitates a lightweight visual model and encourages
embeddings to encode articulatory–phonetic patterns useful for
conditioning speech separation.
Building on this viseme-guided visual design, we further
develop a fully causal online A V-TSE system and evaluate its
separation quality and MAC count. Our contributions are sum-
marized as follows:
• To the best of our knowledge, we are the first to systemat-
ically investigate viseme-recognition pretraining as a visual
cue for audio-visual target speaker extraction. Experimen-
tal results show that viseme-recognition pretraining is effec-
tive in our online A V-TSE system, achieving comparable or
slightly better performance than (i) jointly learnedmouth-cue
representations and (ii) VSR-pretrained visual encoders.

To build viseme-level supervision, we convert the transcript
of each video into a viseme sequence. Specifically, we first
obtain phoneme sequences using the open-source grapheme-
to-phoneme (G2P) toolkit 1 [29], which consults the Carnegie

phonemes to visemes following Lee and Yook [28]. Table 1
summarizes the phoneme-to-viseme mapping used in this work.
These generated viseme sequences provide the training
targets for viseme recognition. However, a straightforward
viseme-recognition pretraining model is typically non-causal
and may be too heavy for real-time deployment. To obtain
an online-capable and lightweight visual front-end while pre-
serving recognition accuracy, we adopt a cross-modal teacher–
student distillation framework, as described next.

2.2. Online Viseme Recognition via Cross-Modal Distilla-
tion
We train an online viseme recognition front-end using a
teacher–student distillation framework. As illustrated in Fig. 1,
we first train a non-causal audio-visualteachermodel using the
same overall architecture and joint CTC/attention criterion [31]
as Auto-A VSR [22]:
Lsup = (1−λ att)Lctc +λ attLatt.(1)
The teacher takes synchronized audio and video as input and
is optimized for viseme recognition, providing strong posterior
targets for distillation.
We then freeze the teacher and train a fully causal video-
onlystudentmodel for real-time inference. To satisfy stream-
ing constraints while keeping the model lightweight, we make
the following modifications to the Auto-A VSR-style visual
pipeline: (i) we apply causal padding in the initial 3D convo-
lution to prevent future-frame leakage; (ii) we replace ResNet-
18 with ShuffleNetV2 [32] to reduce computation; and (iii)
we replace the non-causal Conformer encoder with Emformer
[33]. Emformer is a streaming Transformer that processes input
in chunks and summarizes long-range context via an explicit
memory bank, enabling efficient low-latency inference com-
pared to full-sequence self-attention.
In addition to the supervised joint CTC/attention loss, we
introduce a distillation loss that encourages the student to match
the teacher’s posterior distributions. Specifically, given teacher
and student logitsz t andz s, we define

3. Viseme-Guided TSE
Fig. 2 illustrates the proposed viseme-guided target speaker ex-
traction (TSE) system. Given a mixture waveform, we com-
pute its STFT and project the time–frequency representation
into a latent feature space using a 1-D convolution. In parallel,
the input video stream is fed into an online viseme recognition
front-end (frozen during separator training) to produce frame-
level viseme embeddings. To match the temporal resolution of
the audio features, the viseme embeddings are processed by a
stack of temporal convolutional networks (TCNs) followed by
an upsampling module. The resulting visual features are then
concatenated with the audio features and passed through a sep-
arator backbone consisting of6TFSkiM blocks [35]. Finally,
a 1-D convolution maps the separator output back to the STFT
domain, and the enhanced waveform is obtained via iSTFT.
We adopt TF-SkiMNet [35] as the separator backbone.
However, TF-SkiMNet is originally designed for speech en-
hancement and relies on a convolution-based Local-T module
to model local temporal patterns. For TSE, longer-range tempo-
ral dependencies are often more critical for maintaining target-
speaker consistency under strong overlap. Therefore, we re-
place the convolutional Local-T module with a chinnel-wise
LSTM [36] to better capture long-context dynamics while pre-
serving streaming compatibility.
We train the separator with a sum of a magnitude-spectrum
reconstruction loss and a time-domain SI-SNR loss. Letˆsand

4. Experimental Setup
4.1. Dataset
We conduct experiments on three widely used audio-visual
speech datasets: LRS2[37], LRS3[38], and V oxCeleb2[39].
Audio is sampled at 16 kHz and video is recorded at 25 fps.
Viseme recognition.We train the viseme recognition
model using only LRS2. Specifically, we use thepretrain
andtrainsubsets for training and report results on thetest
subset.
Target speaker extraction.For A V-TSE, we adopt the
mixture datasets constructed in [40], including LRS2-Mix,
LRS3-Mix, and V ox2-Mix. LRS2-Mix contains an 11-hour
training set and a 3-hour validation set. LRS3-Mix contains a
28-hour training set and a 3-hour validation set. V ox2-Mix con-
tains a 56-hour training set and a 3-hour validation set. Each
mixture segment is 2 s long, and the signal-to-interference ra-
tio (SIR) is uniformly sampled from[−5,5]dB. The test sets
derived from the three datasets follow the same protocol as in
previous work [41, 42].
4.2. Hyperparameters and Training Settings
Viseme recognition front-end.Except for the architectural
changes required for streaming inference, we follow the Auto-
A VSR configuration for the hidden dimensions of all compo-
nents. For Emformer, the left context is set to 40 frames. In the
teacher–student distillation framework, we setλ att = 0.2for
the joint CTC/attention criterion, the distillation temperature to
T= 2, and the distillation weight toλ KD = 0.3.
For both the non-causal teacher and the causal student in
viseme recognition, we use Adam with a OneCycle learning-
rate schedule (cosine annealing) and mixed-precision training.
The learning rate increases from6×10 −6 to3×10 −4 during
the first 5 epochs, and then decays to3×10 −7 by the end of
training. We train for 80 epochs in total with a batch size of 12.
Viseme-guided TSE.For the separator, we keep the same
configuration as the original TF-SkiMNet, except that the hid-
den size of the modified channel-wise LSTM is set to 32. We
train the TSE model with mixed precision and the Adam opti-
mizer using an initial learning rate of10 −3. We adopt a Re-
duceLROnPlateau scheduler that monitors the validation loss,
with a reduction factor of 0.5, patience of 4 epochs, and a mini-
mum learning rate of10 −6. The batch size is 32.

5.2. Viseme-guided TSE
To comprehensively evaluate the performance and generaliza-
tion of the proposed system, Tables 3 and 5 report the in-domain
and cross-domain results, respectively. Here,in-domainmeans
that the model is trained and tested on mixtures constructed
from the same dataset (e.g., trained on LRS2-Mix and evaluated
on the LRS2-Mix test set). In contrast,cross-domainevaluates
robustness under domain mismatch, where the model is trained
on mixtures from one dataset but tested on mixtures from differ-
ent datasets. In this work, all cross-domain models are trained
on V ox2-Mix and then evaluated on the LRS2-Mix and LRS3-
Mix test sets.
Viseme cue vs. mouth cue.Compared with Baseline-1,
Baseline-2 replaces the jointly learnedmouth cuewith the pro-
posed pretrained viseme embedding. As shown in Table 3, this
replacement yields consistent gains on LRS2-Mix in-domain
evaluation, while achieving comparable performance on LRS3-
Mix and V ox2-Mix. We attribute the smaller improvements on
LRS3/V ox2 to the fact that the viseme front-end is pretrained
only on LRS2; therefore, applying it to LRS3 and V ox2 intro-
duces a visual domain shift for the viseme recognizer, which
makes the extracted embeddings less accurate and the down-
stream conditioning more challenging.
Viseme cue vs. VSR cue.Relative to Baseline-3, which
uses a VSR-pretrained encoder, the proposed system achieves
higher separation quality and better robustness in most evalu-
ated metrics (Tables 3 and 5), indicating that viseme pretraining
can provide an effective and task-aligned visual cue for the pro-
posed online A V-TSE system.
Overall in-domain and cross-domain performance.
Among the compared systems, the proposed online viseme-
guided TSE system provides a favorable trade-off between
separation quality and MAC count, with the lowest reported
computational cost and the best overall in-domain results on
LRS2-Mix and LRS3-Mix across SI-SNR/PESQ/ESTOI. On
V ox2-Mix, it remains competitive in SI-SNR (slightly below
Baseline-1) while achieving higher perceptual quality and in-
telligibility. In the cross-domain evaluation, the proposed sys-
tem attains the strongest results on LRS2-Mix across all three
metrics, and achieves the highest PESQ/ESTOI on LRS3-Mix
while remaining close to the top SI-SNR, indicating competitive
performance under the evaluated domain mismatch settings.
Ablation on separator modification.Finally, we conduct
an ablation study to validate the effectiveness of our separator
modifications. In Table 3,TF-SkiMNet ∗ denotes the original
TF-SkiMNet configuration (without our Local-T replacement).
Switching fromTF-SkiMNet ∗ to the modifiedTF-SkiMNet
yields consistent improvements on LRS2-Mix, confirming that
the proposed long-context modeling enhancement benefits TSE.
6. Conclusions
This paper revisited the design of visual cues for online audio-
visual target speaker extraction and proposed viseme-guided ex-
traction as a lightweight and interpretable alternative to conven-
tional mouth-cue/VSR-pretrained representations, enabled by
an online viseme recognizer trained via cross-modal distilla-
tion. Building on this front-end, we developed a fully causal
A V-TSE system. Experimental results show that the proposed
system achieves competitive or better separation performance
than the evaluated baselines while using the lowest MAC count
among them.
