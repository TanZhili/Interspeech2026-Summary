Abstract
Emirati Arabic, a widely spoken Gulf dialect, is severely under-
represented in speech resources, limiting dialectal ASR and NLP
applications. We introduce Hamsa, a manually annotated Emi-
rati Arabic speech-to-text corpus containing 11 hours of con-
versational recordings across multiple emirates. All speech was
transcribed and validated by native speakers, capturing natural
pronunciation and regional variation. Challenges included infor-
mal speech, dialectal diversity, and background noise. We ﬁne-
tuned state-of-the-art multilingual Automatic Speech Recogni-
tion (ASR) models on Hamsa and achieved signiﬁcant perfor-
mance improvements, with Word Error Rates reducing from over
40% to below 25% for Whisper-v2. Hamsa enables dialect-
aware ASR, machine translation, automatic subtitling, LLM
training, and commercial applications, such as call center au-
tomation, ﬁlling a critical gap in Emirati Arabic annotated
speech resources.
Index Terms: Emirati Arabic, Speech-to-Text, Dialectal ASR
1. Introduction
Arabic is spoken by nearly 500 million people across diverse
regions1 . The language is marked by diglossia and signiﬁcant
dialectal variation [1, 2]. While Modern Standard Arabic (MSA)
is used in formal writing, everyday communication occurs in
spoken dialects that diﬀer widely in phonology, morphology,
and lexicon [3, 4]. This variability presents major challenges
for speech technologies such as Automatic Speech Recognition
(ASR), machine translation, and LLMs, which often perform
poorly on dialectal speech when trained primarily on MSA [5].
Emirati Arabic, which is the primary spoken dialect in the
UAE, exhibits distinct phonological and lexical features com-
pared to other Gulf dialects, which it shares geographical prox-
imity with [6, 7]. For instance, the letterﺝis often pronounced
asيtransforming words likeسلجم toسليم Lexical diﬀerences
also exist; for example,ﺭوطف refers to a Ramadan meal rather than
breakfast, which is instead calledقويﺭ Furthermore, the dialect
is highlighted with nuanced variations across the seven emirates.
These distinctions make Emirati Arabic a linguistically rich yet
underrepresented variety in annotated speech resources.
Despite recent eﬀorts in Arabic speech data collection, such
as the MGB Challenge [8, 9], QASR [10], and DART [11], these
datasets primarily cover MSA, Egyptian, or Levantine dialects.
Other attempts, such as [6], attempt to capture the modern evo-
lution of the Emirati dialect with English code switching. To our

In this work, we present Hamsa (ةسمه meaning ”whisper-

subtitling, and voice-based services. The goal is to advance the
representation of Emirati Arabic in speech technology and ad-
dress a critical gap in Arabic NLP research. Beyond computa-
tional applications, Hamsa also contributes to the preservation of
Emirati oral heritage and supports the development of culturally-
aware tools for education and accessibility.
2. Related Work
Arabic speech resources, dialectical and otherwise, have ad-
vanced considerably in recent years, yet the overwhelming ma-
jority still target MSA resource reﬁnement. Early eﬀorts such
as the Arabic broadcast news transcription system of [12] and
GlobalPhone’s 35-hour MSA corpus [13] both established foun-
dational ASR benchmarks. Furthermore, larger public initia-
tives including Mozilla Common Voice [14] and MASC [15]
expanded coverage to 15 and 1,000 hours, respectively. Despite
their scale, these resources capture formal speech that poorly
represents the diversity of everyday spoken Arabic.
Recognizing this limitation, subsequent eﬀorts targeted di-
alectal Arabic. The MGB-2 [8] and MGB-3 [9] challenges in-
troduced multi-dialect broadcast speech, and QASR [10] pro-
vided rich annotations for both MSA and dialectal content in-
cluding dialect identiﬁcation and punctuation restoration. How-
ever, these datasets predominantly cover Egyptian and Levantine
varieties, leaving Gulf Arabic largely unaddressed in the speech
resource landscape.
Within Gulf Arabic, resources remain scarce. The SAA VB
corpus [16, 17] provides 96 hours of Saudi dialect recordings,
representing the most substantial Gulf Arabic speech resource to
date, yet its coverage does not extend to Emirati Arabic. The only
prior attempt at an Emirati speech resource is Mixat [6], which
provides 15 hours of transcribed podcast recordings. However,
Mixat was designed primarily to study code-switching phenom-
ena between Emirati Arabic and English, rather than to sup-
port monolingual dialectal ASR development. Its podcast-style
recordings and code-switching focus make it ill-suited as a train-
ing resource for conversational Emirati ASR.

3. Dataset Description
The Hamsa corpus comprises audio samples sourced from pub-
licly available UAE-based online content through web scrap-
ing techniques. The collection demonstrates substantial diver-
sity in content, encompassing regional discussions, formal dis-
course, casual conversations, poetry, songs, and social commen-
tary. Quality ﬁltering ensured retention of only coherent speech
samples suitable for ASR training.
To ensure compliance with content ownership policies, we
are in the process of securing formal redistribution agreements
with the original content providers. In accordance with stan-
dard practice for corpora derived from third-party sources, the
public release of Hamsa will provide transcription annotations
alongside metadata and collection scripts, enabling researchers
to independently retrieve the original audio. The dataset will
be released for non-commercial academic research use under a
license consistent with the terms of the source material.
Audio samples are either monologues or conversations in-
volving up to three participants. Audio samples that contain
overlapping speech segments were systematically excluded to
maintain transcription clarity. The ﬁnal collection comprises
4,174 audio samples with an average duration of 1 minute and 3
seconds. All samples were subjected to rigorous annotation and
quality assurance procedures, which are detailed in subsequent
sections. The examples in Table 1 illustrate four categories of
recurring errors rooted in Emirati Arabic’s phonological and or-
thographic properties.
Phonological substitution (ﺝ/ﻕ alternation). Emirati speakers
pronounceقasﺝwhile the correct form retainsقMSA-trained
models fail to resolve this mapping.
Dialect-specific negation particles . Emirati Arabic usesبم 
rather than the gulf dialect-adjacentوم a distinction absent from
training data. This leads to substitution errors and distorted sur-
rounding context.
Feminine morphology. Emirati Arabic marks feminine forms
with a terminalﺝrather than MSA ’sكModels consistently
default to MSA forms produce substitution errors when encoun-
tering these tokens acoustically.
Orthographic normalization errors . Models overcorrects
Emirati-speciﬁc spellings such asلياسﺭ orيشلاهل back toward
MSA forms, producing outputs that are phonetically plausi-
ble but orthographically incorrect. This partially explains
Whisper-v2’s disproportionately high zero-shot CER of 71%.
These 4 error categories compound one another in the ASR
pipeline most severely in mms-1b-all, whose outputs are
largely incoherent beyond isolated function words, underscoring
the necessity of dialect-matched training data such as Hamsa.
4. Annotation Process
To ensure accurate representation of Emirati Arabic, we em-
ployed a rigorous manual transcription process, guided by
dialect-speciﬁc rules and supported by custom tooling. The aim
was to capture the spoken dialect authentically without introduc-
ing MSA forms or orthographic normalization.
Manual Transcription: The annotation workload was dis-
tributed among ﬁve native Emirati Arabic speakers, each respon-
sible for approximately 2.2 hours (2̃ ,370 audio segments) of tran-
scription. Annotators listened carefully to each audio ﬁle and
transcribed the speech manually, preserving the dialect as spo-
ken. Emphasis was placed on phonological and lexical ﬁdelity to
Emirati Arabic. Following that, one annotator conducted a full
secondary review of 5 hours of audio (approximately 45% of the
corpus) cross-checking transcriptions against the dialect-speciﬁc
guidelines and correcting inconsistencies where found. In total,
the corpus accumulated approximately 16 hours of human an-
notation eﬀort across the primary transcription and secondary
review phase.
Annotation Guidelines: Clear guidelines were developed to
standardize transcription and avoid drift toward MSA or other
Gulf dialects. Annotators were instructed to capture words as
they are commonly spoken in Emirati Arabic. A reference ta-
ble was provided, highlighting distinctions between dialectal and
MSA forms. For example, the feminine second-person pronoun
ِتنﺃ is often pronounced the same in both registers but written
asيتنﺃ in the Emirati dialect. These rules ensured consistent
spelling and preserved the linguistic authenticity of the tran-
scripts. A summary of the guidelines is provided in Table 2.
Challenges Encountered: There were several challenges, in-
cluding variable audio quality, background noise, and regional
variation within the Emirati dialect. Another complexity was
code-switching, where speakers occasionally mixed Emirati
Arabic with MSA or English. To handle such cases, rules were
established for when to transcribe mixed-language utterances
or omit them, ensuring consistency across the dataset. It is
worth noting that since annotators worked on non-overlapping
segments, no formal inter-annotator agreement metric was com-
puted. The secondary review pass served as the primary quality
assurance mechanism. We acknowledge this as a limitation and
recommend that future extensions of Hamsa incorporate over-
lapping annotation on a held-out subset to enable rigorous con-
sistency measurement.
Tooling and Workflow: Annotation and review were facili-
tated using Python-based scripts and a custom Streamlit inter-
face, which allowed annotators to eﬃciently listen to audio, edit
transcripts, and ﬂag problematic segments.

5. Dataset Statistics
This section presents a statistical analysis of the HAMSA corpus
across both training and test splits. The training split comprises
11,000 samples totaling 10.74 hours, while the test split con-
tains 861 samples spanning 51.8 minutes. Both splits exhibit
similar temporal characteristics with average durations of 3.51
and 3.61 seconds respectively, and consistent ranges from 2.00 to
approximately 13.7 seconds. The corpus contains 93,354 words
total, with comparable sentence complexity (7.85 vs 8.15 aver-
age words per sentence). However, the test split shows greater
sentence length variability, with a maximum of 109 words com-
pared to 47 in training, potentially indicating longer conver-
sational segments. Figure 1 show the distribution of sentence
lengths in number of words of train and test splits.
Vocabulary statistics reﬂect expected distributions, with
23,350 unique words in training versus 3,459 in test. Speech
rate analysis demonstrates consistency across splits at approxi-
mately 0.44-0.45 seconds per word. The training split exhibits
a strong duration-word correlation of 0.740, while the test split
shows a moderate correlation of 0.544, suggesting greater vari-
ability in speech density. Table 3 summarizes the key character-
istics. These statistics validate corpus quality and consistency,
with balanced representation across temporal, textual, and lin-
guistic dimensions suitable for robust ASR development.

The model was ﬁne-tuned using the Hugging Face Trainer Li-
brary, which provides a ﬂexible and eﬃcient code-base for
sequence-to-sequence tasks. As summarized in Table 4, we used
a batch size of 32, a learning rate of1∗10−5, and applied mixed-
precision training (FP16) to optimize memory usage and train-
ing speed. A short warmup phase of 5 steps was included, and
the model was trained for 3 epochs with generated sequences
capped at a maximum length of 225 tokens. Training was per-
formed on one NVIDIA H100 GPU node, allowing for faster
computation and support for memory-eﬃcient features, such as
gradient checkpointing. To ensure fair comparison and isolate
the eﬀect of the training data, identical hyperparameters across
all models were used.

7. Limitations
While Hamsa represents an important step toward Emirati Ara-
bic speech technology, several limitations should be noted. At 11
hours, the corpus is modest by large-scale ASR standards, but it
reﬂects a substantial manual collection and validation eﬀort for
low-resource, monolingual conversational Emirati Arabic. Geo-
graphic coverage is intentionally limited, meaning dialectal vari-
ation in border regions and rural communities is not captured.
The training and test splits are not strictly speaker-independent,
although the independent Mixat evaluation provides an external
signal across diﬀerent speakers and recording conditions. For-
mal inter-annotator agreement metrics were not computed; qual-
ity assurance instead relied on detailed written guidelines and
a secondary manual review pass covering 45% of the corpus.
Future releases will include speaker metadata and overlapping
annotations for formal IAA reporting. Finally, audio redistribu-
tion depends on formal licensing agreements with the original
content providers, and the public release will initially provide
transcriptions and collection scripts rather than raw audio ﬁles.
8. Conclusion and Future Work
This paper introduced Hamsa, the ﬁrst large-scale manually an-
notated monolingual Emirati Arabic speech corpus, comprising
11 hours of conversational recordings across the UAE. Our ex-
periments demonstrate that ﬁne-tuning on Hamsa yields con-
sistent improvements across all evaluated ASR models, with
Whisper-v2 reducing WER from 42.25% to 24.46% and
Whisper-v3 from 29.69% to 23.04%, with gains generalizing to
the independent Mixat benchmark. These results conﬁrm that
dialect-matched ﬁne-tuning on a care
