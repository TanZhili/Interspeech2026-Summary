Auditory Contrast Network for Text-Free Prominence Detection
Kosuke Shimizu1, Keiichi Zempo2,∗∗
1 College of Media Arts, Science and Technology, University of Tsukuba, Japan
2 Institute of Systems and Information Engineering, University of Tsukuba, Japan

Abstract
We introduce the Auditory Contrast Network (ACN), a model
with up to 238 trainable parameters that encodes three psychoa-
coustic principles — pairwise contrast, cue independence, and
forward dominance — into an interpretable detector of word-
level perceived prominence. Using only three acoustic features
and no text, ACN achievesr= 0.412on cross-corpus trans-
fer (Helsinki Prosody→Emphases), matching a frozen 94.6M-
parameter wav2vec2 baseline (r= 0.409) at120×lower la-
tency; adding text features raisesrto0.451. The learned
weights suggest forward-dominant context use (over 96% pre-
ceding across all cues), strict locality (±1word), and the cue
hierarchy duration>energy>spectral≫F0, consistent with
established findings while preserving a direct architectural read-
out. The acoustic-only result makes ACN suitable for on-device
applications where text is unavailable, such as hearing aids and
real-time pronunciation monitoring.
Index Terms: prominence detection, psychoacoustics, auditory
contrast, cross-corpus transfer, prosody
1. Introduction
This paper addresses automatic detection ofword-level per-
ceived prominence ratingsin read English speech. This target
is related to, but not identical with, lexical stress or sentence-
level focus/pitch-accent assignment: pitch accents often mark
information structure and can make a focused word highly
prominent, whereas our labels are continuous word ratings from
corpus-level prominence annotation [1, 2, 3, 4, 5]. Within this
operational target, perceived prominence is shaped not only by
absolute acoustic properties but also by how a wordsoundsrel-
ative to its neighbours — that is, by contrasts in duration, in-
tensity, and spectral shape between adjacent words [6, 7]. For
example, a 300 ms word sounds unremarkable in slow speech
but stands out when surrounded by 150 ms words. Promi-
nence is also shaped by a word’s lexical role: content words
are more likely to carry prominence than function words, partly
because they tend to be acoustically more salient and lexically
less predictable [7, 8]. Despite this well-established relational
view, current state-of-the-art detectors, from mel-spectrogram
CNNs [5] to self-supervised models such as wav2vec2 [9], pro-
cess each word through generic architectures with no explicit
mechanism for comparing a word’s acoustic realisation against
that of its neighbours.
Psychoacoustic research provides specific guidance for de-
signing a model that encodes this relational structure. The
Weber–Fechner law establishes that perceived change scales
with the logarithm of the stimulus ratio [10, 11, 12], which

means that a nonlinear contrast function is more appropri-
ate than simple subtraction. Ulanovsky et al. [13] showed
that auditory cortical neurons exhibit stimulus-specific adap-
tation (SSA), responding more strongly to stimuli that deviate
from theprecedingcontext, which predicts an asymmetric role
for forward and backward neighbours. Furthermore, duration,
loudness, and spectral shape are processed along functionally
distinct pathways before integration [12], which implies that
per-cue contrast should precede cross-cue combination. To-
gether, these three findings define the computational primitives
that a principled prominence model should implement.
We translate these principles into the Auditory Contrast
Network (ACN), a model with up to 238 trainable parameters.
For each acoustic cue, a small multi-layer perceptron (MLP)
computes a nonlinear contrast score between the target word
and the preceding word, and separately between the target word
and the following word; learned attention weights then set the
balance between these two directions. A second MLP aggre-
gates all per-cue contrast scores, the raw cue values, and op-
tional text features into a single prominence estimate. On cross-
corpus transfer from Helsinki Prosody [14] to Emphases [5],
acoustic-only ACN (r= 0.412) matches a frozen 94.6M-
parameter wav2vec2 baseline (r= 0.409) without requiring
text input — a property critical for on-device applications such
as hearing aids and real-time speech monitoring. Adding text
features raises performance tor= 0.451, comparable to a lo-
gistic regression baseline (r= 0.444), confirming that high-
capacity models are not necessary for this task. The key differ-
ence is interpretability: the learned weights show that forward
attention exceeds 96% across all cues, that context beyond±1
word does not improve performance in our setting, and that the
cue ordering (duration>energy>spectral≫F0) is consistent
with Kochanski et al. [15].
2. Related Work
Word-level prominence detection has progressed substantially
in both labelling methodology and modelling approaches. Suni
et al. [4] proposed continuous wavelet transform (CWT) based
prominence labels, and the Helsinki Prosody Corpus [14] scaled
these annotations to 36,802 words from 50 speakers. Morri-
son et al. [5] collected crowdsourced human ratings in the Em-
phases corpus and reportedr= 0.534with a mel-spectrogram
CNN under supervised in-corpus training. SSL models such as
wav2vec2 [9] encode prominence information at intermediate
layers [16], and knowledge distillation [17] has been explored
for compression. However, all of these systems treat promi-
nence as a generic regression target and provide no insight into
what computation drives the prediction.

Including raw cue values alongside contrast scores allows the
model to mark a word as prominent either because it differs
from its neighbours or because it is extreme in absolute terms
(e.g. a very long word regardless of surrounding context). The
ReLU hidden layer lets the aggregation capture cross-cue in-
teractions: for instance, a word that is simultaneously longer
and louder than the preceding word may be disproportionately
prominent, an effect that a linear combination would miss.
We use three acoustic cues (C= 3): log word dura-
tion, mean MFCC0 (approximately proportional to log energy),
and standard deviation of MFCC 2 within the word. The third
feature captures within-word variability of spectral curvature,
serving as a proxy for spectral balance [6]. MFCCs are ex-
tracted with a 25 ms window, 10 ms hop, and 13 coefficients
using librosa; word boundaries come from the Montreal Forced
Aligner. All features arez-normalised per speaker. As optional

Each contrast MLP has 33 trainable parameters. With three
cues and two attention logits per cue, the contrast stage totals
3×33+6 = 105. The aggregation MLP maps 9 inputs through
12 hidden units, contributing 133 parameters. The complete

4. Experimental Setup
We train on the Helsinki Prosody Corpus [14], which contains
36,802 words from 50 English speakers in LibriTTS labelled
with continuous CWT prominence scores [4]. We evaluate on
the Emphases corpus [5], which provides crowdsourced human
prominence ratings for 69,714 words from 17 different Lib-
riTTS speakers; no speakers overlap between the two corpora.
Training minimises MSE on CWT labels using Adam [22]
(lr= 0.003, L2 regularisationλ= 10 −3, batch size 4096, early
stopping with patience 40 on a held-out speaker split). To re-
duce seed variance, we train 20 random initialisations and re-
port the mean of the top 3, approximating practitioner model
selection.
We compare against five systems spanning the complex-
ity spectrum. The SSL baseline is wav2vec2-base [9] (94.6M
parameters) with frozen encoder and layer-9 pooled embed-
dings [16] fed into a feedforward head (768→256→128→1,

5.2. Learned Model Properties
Because each ACN component maps onto a testable claim, the
trained weights serve as experimental findings.
All three cues exhibit forward dominance: each assigns
over 96% of the attention weight to the preceding word de-
spite symmetric initialisation (Duration: 0.98/0.02, Energy:
0.97/0.03, Spectral: 0.96/0.04). This forward bias is reminis-
cent of SSA in auditory cortex [13], though it may also reflect
the fact that the preceding word is simply a stronger statistical
predictor of prominence than the following word.

This suggests that prominence perception depends on immedi-

Third, the learned weights expose a consistent cue hi-

by energy and spectral features; the tested F0 summaries con-

(mean/std/slope) capture pitch range rather than fine-grained in-
tonation contours or pitch-accent shape, which may underesti-
mate F0’s true role in sentential prominence and focus marking.
Surprisal receives the highest weight overall [20, 8]. Permu-
tation feature importance (PFI) confirms the ranking: surprisal
(∆r= 0.24), duration (∆r= 0.08), contrast features (0.008–

solute channels, because L1-norm does not account for input
scale. Both methods agree on the acoustic ordering (duration
>energy>spectral≫F0), where we use MFCC2 standard
deviation as a proxy for spectral tilt [6].
6. Discussion
The parity between ACN and the SSL baseline suggests that,
for this word-level prominence-rating task, much of the rele-
vant information in the speech signal can be captured by three
explicit contrast operations rather than by learning generic rep-
resentations from hundreds of hours of unlabelled audio. The
overlapping confidence intervals (r= 0.412[.406,.418] vs.
r= 0.409[.403,.415]) are consistent with the interpretation
that the two systems extract similar predictive information on
this benchmark, yet ACN uniquely provides a built-in readout
of directional bias (over 96% forward attention), locality (K=1
suffices), and cue hierarchy — properties that are invisible in-
side a frozen wav2vec2 encoder.
The forward dominance pattern is qualitatively reminiscent
of SSA [13], albeit at longer timescales (∼300 ms words vs.
∼100 ms tones); the preceding word may also be a stronger
statistical predictor, and the two explanations are not mutually
exclusive. In the acoustic-only condition, contrast contributes
∆ = +0.041, closing the gap with the SSL baseline; with
text, the contribution shrinks to∆ = +0.005, indicating that
lexical expectation and acoustic contrast carry overlapping in-
formation. This pattern suggests that contrast is most valuable
precisely in the on-device scenario where text is unavailable.
ACN’s 238 parameters yield a120×latency advantage
(1.6 ms vs. 194 ms per word on a single CPU thread, excluding
shared word segmentation; RTF 0.006 vs. 0.58). This makes
ACN viable for real-time subtitle prosody markup, pronuncia-
tion feedback in language learning, or lightweight TTS front-
ends, and aligns with the goal of bringing speech technology to
resource-constrained clinical and educational settings. The ar-
chitecture is also extensible: additional acoustic cues (e.g. voice
quality, rhythmic regularity) or alternative text features can be
incorporated by adding contrast channels or aggregation inputs,
without changing the core design.
Limitations include evaluation on a single corpus pair (read
English), reliance on external word boundaries, and weight-
based rather than perceptual evidence for directional bias; the
low contribution of F0 may also reflect our simplified feature
set (mean/std/slope) rather than a genuine absence of F0, pitch-
accent, or focus-related effects.
7. Conclusion
The Auditory Contrast Network shows that three acoustic fea-
tures with pairwise contrast are sufficient to match a frozen
94.6M-parameter SSL baseline on cross-corpus word-level
prominence-rating prediction, without requiring text input. At
120×lower latency, ACN is suitable for on-device deployment
where text is unavailable. When text features are added, perfor-
mance rises further, but the acoustic-only result indicates that
a compact contrast-based representation is a strong operating
point for this task. Future work will add richer F0 contour and
pitch-accent features, extend ACN to spontaneous speech and
additional languages, and use targeted perception experiments
to validate the directional asymmetry predicted by the model.
