Streaming Open-Vocabulary Keyword Spotting via Role Swapping in
Cross-Attention
Xi Chen, Haichuan Bai, Liming Song
Samsung R&D Institute China-Beijing, Beijing, China
{xi2.chen, haichuan.bai, lm0518.song}@samsung.com
Abstract
In recent years, attention-based multi-modal open-vocabulary
keyword spotting (KWS) has attracted attention, yet its stream-
ing deployment on device with such a design remains under-
explored. We identify a data flow mismatch in traditional
frameworks: speech as Key/Value requires global context but,
in streaming, provides only local frames; enrollment repre-
sentation as Query supplies only local information, despite
its inherent global semantics. To resolve this mismatch, we
propose a role-swapping streaming open-vocabulary KWS ap-
proach: streaming speech becomes Query (carrying frame-level
local info) and enrollment representation as Key/Value (encod-
ing global semantics), enabling streaming processing. With-
out loss of generality, we instantiate this framework with a
text-registered model (∼0.8M parameters) and evaluate it on
LibriPhrase, achieving an EER/AUC of 6.82%/97.95% on easy
negative subset and 28.21%/79.19% on hard negative subset.
Index Terms: human-computer interaction, open-vocabulary
keywords spotting, cross-attention, multi modal
1. Introduction
In the era of large language models, attention to human-
computer interaction through voice continues to increase.
Among various approaches to initiating voice interaction, voice
wake-up technology has garnered significant industry focus.
This technology is typically implemented through keyword
spotting (KWS) tasks, which fall into two categories: prede-
fined KWS [1] and open-vocabulary KWS [2].
Open-vocabulary KWS is designed to detect pre-registered
keywords from continuous speech, where the keywords may be
unseen during training. It has gained widespread attention in re-
cent years [3, 4, 5, 6, 7] due to its ability to support personaliza-
tion. Benefiting from advances in multi-modal semantic align-
ment, registration for open-vocabulary KWS has evolved from
single-modal [2, 8] to multi-modal paradigms [7, 9]. More-
over, the integration of large-scale pre-trained models (e.g.,
BERT [10] for text, Whisper [11] for speech) has significantly
improved the quality of enrollment representations, providing
richer semantic and acoustic features for alignment [9, 12].
Among these approaches, Cross-Attention has proven par-
ticularly effective in enabling semantic alignment between en-
rollment representations and speech frames, providing inter-
pretable and clear distinctions between positive and negative
samples. However, most existing Cross-Attention based meth-
ods operate at the segment level, assuming the entire speech ut-
terance is available prior to alignment—an assumption that does
not hold in streaming scenarios. For on-device applications,
where low latency and real-time processing are critical, stream-
ing KWS systems are essential yet remain underexplored.
Recent approaches to open-vocabulary KWS can be
broadly grouped into Connectionist Temporal Classifica-
tion (CTC)-based methods [6, 13] and a similarity-based
method [7]. CTC-based methods force-align keyword frames
from streaming speech, enabling streaming operation; however,
their alignment confidence drops on mismatched text-speech
pairs, requiring extra tuning for on-device deployment. The
similarity-based method computes frame-level similarity be-
tween enrollment and speech embeddings, making it applica-
ble to open-vocabulary tasks, but it relies on heuristic post-
processing for acceptance decisions.
To bridge this gap, we argue that the key to stream-
ing open-vocabulary KWS lies in rethinking the data flow of
Cross-Attention. While the proposed framework generalizes
to various enrollment modalities, this paper focuses on a text-
registered instantiation where streaming speech is assigned as
Query and registered text as Key/Value, enabling frame-by-
frame processing while preserving the semantic richness of pre-
trained text encoders. However, this frame-by-frame design
leads to performance loss. To address this issue, we optimize
the model with the following strategies:
•Multi-modal Embedding Enhancement: Integrate modal
embedding information into the Pattern Discriminator. The
Discriminator combines pattern learning with frame-level bi-
nary classification decisions to improve keyword detection
accuracy;
•Contrastive Learning: Compute InfoNCE loss [14] using
text and audio as anchors to assist audio-text embedding
alignment;
•Staged Learning: Complete semantic alignment in the first
stage by combining text information with attention outputs.
In the second stage, use pattern fusion to optimize decision-
making with audio information;
•Hard Sample Generation: Generate challenging samples
online via time-domain masking to enhance model robust-
ness.
Compared to existing streaming solutions CTC-
aligned Audio-Text (CTCAT) keyword detector [6] and
SYNASPOT-AT [7], this work:
•Eliminates CTC alignment requirements, reducing de-
ployment complexity;
•Replaces heuristic post-processing with network-based
decisions, enabling end-to-end optimization and data aug-
mentation tailored to error types;
•Incorporates an autonomous learning mechanismthat
handles challenging samples directly from data, improving
robustness.

2. Proposed Method
This paper builds upon the cross-modal correspondence de-
tector (CMCD) [3] framework with two key distinctions: (i)
a redesigned Cross-Attention data flow that assigns streaming
speech as Query and registered text as Key/Value, enabling
frame-by-frame processing; and (ii) a two-stage training strat-
egy that incorporates text representations in the first stage and
speech representations in the second stage to assist decision-
making. The overall architecture is illustrated in Figure 1.
The remainder of this section is organized as follows. Sec-
tion 2.1 details the training phase, covering both encoder train-
ing and the sequence discriminator design. Section 2.2 then
describes the streaming inference process.
2.1. Training Phase
We adopt a multi-task learning strategy with the total loss:
Ltotal =L f +Lp +Lc,(1)
where
•L f is the frame-level binary classification loss,
•L p is the phoneme matching loss,
•L c is the audio-text contrastive loss.
The encoders are trained usingL p andL c to facilitate se-
mantic alignment, as detailed in Section 2.1.1. The frame-level
binary classification lossL f optimizes the entire network (en-
coders and sequence discriminator) for frame-wise prediction,
with its configuration and two-stage training strategy described
in Section 2.1.2.

The text encoder follows the design of CMCD. To satisfy the
causality constraint of streaming processing, the audio encoder
consists of two causal convolutional layers followed by two
GRU layers; its detailed structure is shown in Figure 2.

2.1.2. Sequence Discriminator
The Cross-Attention mechanism produces two important ten-
sors: the Affinity MatrixA∈R Ts×Tt (whereTs is the number
of audio frames andTt is the length of the text token sequence),
and the Attention OutputO∈R Ts×m, which is the weighted
sum of text token embeddings. To leverage both sources of in-
formation for the final decision, we design a Pattern Discrimi-
nator consisting of a GRU followed by a dense layer.
Two types of inputs are considered:
•Attention-based input: The Attention OutputOis concate-
nated with the text token embeddingsEt along the time axis,
producing a sequence[E t, O]∈R (Tt+Ts)×m that aligns
with the audio frames.
•Affinity-based input: The Affinity MatrixAis concatenated
with the audio embeddingsE s along the feature dimension,
forming[A(t,:);E s(t)]∈R Tt+m at each time stept.
Experimental observations reveal that the model converges
more easily when using the Affinity-based input, but it may
learn incorrect decision patterns. In contrast, the Attention-
based input guides the model toward the desired pattern shown
in Figure 4. To combine the benefits of both, we adopt a staged
learning strategy:
•First stage: Train the Discriminator using the Attention-
based input. This stage establishes a correct decision bound-
ary.
•Second stage: Continue training using the Affinity-based in-
put, which provides clearer and more decision-friendly pat-
terns, further improving performance.
Figure 4 visualizes the typical patterns of the Affinity Ma-
trix for positive and negative samples after training.
2.2. Inference
In the enrollment phase, the text encoder (pre-trained) extracts
raw token embeddingsE t, which are linearly projected to key
vectorsK t(i) =W K Et(i). During inference, the streaming
speech is processed frame by frame: the audio encoder runs
causally, producingE s(t)∈R m at timet, which is projected
toQ s(t) =W QEs(t)while the unprojectedE s(t)is retained
for concatenation.
The Affinity MatrixAis updated incrementally: the ele-
mentA(t, i)is computed as the dot product of the projected
embeddings:
A(t, i) =Q s(t)·K t(i).(3)
This computation depends only on the current audio frame,
ensuring that the attention stage introduces no latency.
Consistent with the Phase II training input (as shown in Fig-
ure 1), at each time steptthe newly computed rowA(t,:)is
concatenated with the unprojected audio embeddingE s(t)and
fed into the Pattern Discriminator (a GRU layer and a Dense
layer). The discriminator outputs a decision (wake-up or not)
for that frame. This process is repeated frame by frame, en-
abling real-time streaming detection.
3. EXPERIMENTAL
3.1. Datasets
Dataset: We evaluate the proposed method using the
LibriPhrase dataset [3], where the training set is extracted from
the train-clean-100 and train-clean-360 subsets of LibriSpeech,
and the test set is drawn from the train-others-500 subset
of LibriSpeech. LibriPhrase categorizes negatives into two
classes: easy negatives (LPE) and hard negatives (LPH), a clas-
sification standard adopted here.
Preprocessing: Frame-level labels are provided at a sampling
rate of 50 Hz, which matches the output rate of the network.
The final 5 frames of keywords are labeled 1, while all other
frames are labeled 0 to generate frame-level annotations. These
labels are then padded to a uniform length for consistency. We
simulate noisy environments using the babble noise subset of
MS-SNSD [15] as the noise source. The SNR is uniformly dis-
tributed across [-5 dB, 15 dB], and amplitude perturbation is
applied to audio signals to ensure the magnitude is uniformly
distributed across [-40 dB, 0 dB].
3.2. Setup
The proposed streaming model is trained using TensorFlow
with the Adam optimizer.
For the audio Encoder, the first convolutional layer outputs
a filter size of 128, with kernel size set to 5, strides set to 2,
and padding mode set to causal; the second convolutional layer
outputs a filter size of 256, with kernel size set to 5, strides set
to 1, and padding mode set to causal. The hidden dimensions
of the 2 GRU layers and a Dense layer are set to 128. The text
Encoder is inherited from the CMCD text Encoder. The GRU
18
and Dense hidden dimensions of the Discriminator are the same
as its inputs.
During training, noisy input data undergoes time-frequency
masking: two masks are applied in both the time and frequency
domains, with a time mask width of 5 frames and a frequency
mask width of 10 frequency bins. Hard samples are generated
by masking the first 600 ms of audio features. These hard sam-
ples only participate in the calculation ofL f andLc, whileL p
does not utilize hard samples in its calculation.

for deployment in real-world production environments where
complex acoustic interference is common.

streamable. The absence of explicit CTC alignment require-

pared to CTCAT-like models. To empirically validate this real-
time efficiency, we conducted single-thread latency tests on an
AMD EPYC 9654 platform, achieving a total RTF of 0.065
(Feat+AE: 0.05; Attention+DIS: 0.015). Notably, this latency
was measured at float32 precision without platform-specific op-
timization; with further techniques such as quantization, fixed-
point arithmetic, and instruction-level acceleration commonly
applied in edge deployment, our approach shows potential for
streaming-friendly on-device applications.

4. Discussion
We have presented a streaming text-registered open-vocabulary
KWS model based on a redesigned Cross-Attention data flow.
The core idea—assigning streaming speech as Query and
registered text as Key—enables frame-by-frame processing
while preserving the semantic richness of pre-trained text en-
coders. To mitigate performance loss inherent in local frame-
level information, we introduced four optimization strategies:
(i) multi-task learning with contrastive and phoneme matching
losses, (ii) a staged learning scheme for the Pattern Discrim-
inator, (iii) online hard sample generation via time-frequency
masking, and (iv) multi-modal feature concatenation to enrich
discriminator inputs.
Experimental results on LibriPhrase demonstrate that our
method achieves competitive performance, particularly on
hard negatives (LPH), outperforming both SYNASPOT-AT and
CTCAT in AUC. The elimination of CTC alignment simplifies
deployment, and the learnable Pattern Discriminator replaces
heuristic post-processing, making the system more adaptable to
real-world false alarm patterns.
Future work will explore more sophisticated hard sample
generation strategies tailored to specific error types, including
leveraging multi-modal information (e.g., verifying text simi-
larity credibility through speech representations). We will also
investigate model compression techniques (e.g., CNN and GRU
pruning) to further reduce the parameter footprint for resource-
constrained devices.
