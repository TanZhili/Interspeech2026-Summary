Abstract
Speech separation models trained on raw waveforms achieve
high performance but come with substantial computational
costs. Neural Audio Codecs (NACs) offer an efficient alterna-
tive by operating in compressed latent spaces. However, the ex-
isting codec-based method aggregates per-layer Residual Vec-
tor Quantization (RVQ) vectors into a single vector, collapsing
the coarse-to-fine acoustic hierarchy. We propose RVQ-Grid
to explicitly preserve this hierarchy by stacking per-layer RVQ
vectors into a 3D grid and processing it using dual-axis recur-
rent blocks. On WSJ0-2Mix, RVQ-Grid achieves a +3.6 dB
SI-SDRi improvement over the prior codec-based method. Our
evaluation on the Automatic Speech Recognition task shows
that RVQ-Grid achieves an 8.6% WER, demonstrating compa-
rable performance to the SepFormer baseline under the same
codec condition. During inference, RVQ-Grid achieves a 6x
reduction in MACs compared to SepFormer.
Index Terms: speech separation, residual vector quantization,
neural audio codec, computational efficiency
1. Introduction
Speech separation, often referred to as the cocktail party prob-
lem [1, 2], is the task of isolating individual speakers from
overlapping voice mixtures. Modern speech separation meth-
ods [3, 4, 5, 6] are trained on raw waveforms to optimize
the Scale-Invariant Signal-to-Distortion Ratio (SI-SDR) [7].
Although these models achieve impressive performance, they
come with substantial computational costs because operat-
ing directly on raw audio at a high sample rate results in
long sequence lengths. While efficient separation architectures
have been proposed through lightweight designs, asymmetric
encoder-decoder structures, and efficient sequence modeling
[8, 9, 10], practical deployment scenarios such as transmitting
audio through a codec or running on resource-limited devices
motivate performing separation directly in compressed repre-
sentations.
Neural Audio Codecs (NACs) have emerged as a promis-
ing method for such compressed-domain speech separation ap-
proaches [11, 12]. NACs commonly use an encoder-decoder
architecture with a Residual Vector Quantization (RVQ) com-
ponent, which converts continuous representations into discrete
codes using multiple codebook layers arranged in a residual
manner [13, 14, 15]. This architecture captures acoustic infor-
mation in a coarse-to-fine hierarchy and provides high temporal
compression, enabling separation models to process shorter se-
quences at lower computational cost.
Building on these codec architectures, Codecformer [11]
was the first to apply speech separation in the codec latent
space. It adopts the SepFormer [4] architecture but operates
in the neural audio codec domain rather than on waveform rep-
resentations. When the RVQ setting is enabled, Codecformer
aggregates all RVQ layers into a single embedding to process in
the separator, which eliminates the per-layer structure of RVQ.
Prior works in audio codec design and codec-based audio gen-
eration [16, 17, 18] suggest that explicitly modeling RVQ layer
structure can improve the output quality. Different RVQ lay-
ers provide complementary cues, such as coarse acoustic infor-
mation in early layers and fine details in later layers [13, 19].
Consequently, modeling cross-layer interactions may enable the
codec-based speech separation model to leverage information
across the full RVQ hierarchy.
In this paper, we propose RVQ-Grid, a codec-based speech
separation model that treats per-layer RVQ vectors as a struc-
tured representation. Unlike prior work that collapses all RVQ
layers into a single embedding, RVQ-Grid preserves the hier-
archical structure of RVQ by processing each layer explicitly.
Specifically, we stack per-layer quantized vectors into a 3D grid,
where the three dimensions correspond to the number of code-
book layers, the embedding dimension and the temporal frames.
This grid is then processed by dual-axis recurrent blocks that al-
ternately model cross-layer dependencies and temporal context.
We evaluate RVQ-Grid on the standard WSJ0-2Mix [20] bench-
mark and show that it outperforms Codecformer in speech sep-
aration performance. Furthermore, since NACs are optimized
for perceptual reconstruction quality rather than exact sample-
level waveform matching [14, 15], standard metrics such as
SI-SDR/SDR do not fully reflect the actual quality of codec-
reconstructed audio. We perform further benchmarks using
perceptual metrics and downstream tasks such as Automatic
Speech Recognition (ASR). Our results demonstrate that RVQ-
Grid achieves highly competitive Word Error Rate (WER) com-
pared to SepFormer [4].

2.3. Codec-based separation: Codecformer
Codecformer [11] extends the mask-based separation frame-
work to the NACs domain. It applies the encoder-mask
estimator-decoder pipeline in the codec’s latent space: encodes
the mixture via the codec encoder, applies a separator to esti-
mateˆzs, and decodes to waveformsˆss = DEC codec(ˆzs). Be-
cause the codec provides high temporal compression, Codec-
former processes much shorter sequences than waveform-based
methods, achieving significant computational savings.
However, in realistic edge-cloud deployments, codec la-
tents must be quantized via RVQ for transmission. In the RVQ-
enabled setting, Codecformer applies (2) to sum all layer out-
puts intoˆzbefore passing it to the separator, thereby discarding
the per-layer structure. The authors report that enabling RVQ
degrades the performance by 2-3 dB on SI-SDR metric.
3. Methodology
3.1. RVQ Vector Stacking
Our work addresses the Codecformer’s limitation by explicitly
modeling per-layer RVQ vectors to exploit their hierarchy. In-
stead of summing RVQ layer outputs as in (2), we propose to
stack them into a 3D grid representationZ grid∈R N×D×T :
Zgrid(i,d,t) =Z i(d,t). (4)
This preserves the hierarchical structure of RVQ layers, en-
abling explicit layer-wise modeling.
3.2. RVQ-Grid Separator
As illustrated in Figure 1, our proposed system consists of a
frozen pre-trained codec and a trainable separator. The mixture
is encoded and quantized through the frozen codec, then the
per-layer RVQ outputs are stacked to formZ grid.
The stacked representation is first projected from dimension
Dto hidden dimensionCvia a learnable Conv2D layer, pro-
ducingH 0∈R N×C×T . The projected features are then pro-
cessed byLdual-axis recurrent blocks, which are inspired by
alternating-axis processing in dual-path models [3], but applied
to codebook and temporal dimensions. Each block consists of
two sequential paths:
1.Cross-layer path:A BiLSTM is applied along theNaxis to
model dependencies across RVQ layers, producingH cross∈
RN×C×T .
2.Temporal path:A BiLSTM is applied along theTaxis to
capture the temporal context, producingH temp∈R N×C×T
fromH cross.
Finally, each block includes residual connections and layer
normalization:
Hl =LayerNorm(H l−1 +H temp). (5)

4. Experimental Setups
4.1. Datasets
We use the common WSJ0-2Mix [20] dataset for all of the train-
ing and evaluation. It contains mixtures of two speakers de-
rived from the WSJ0 corpus [22], with 20000 training mixtures
(30h), 5000 validation mixtures (10h) and 3000 test mixtures
(5h). Both mixtures and ground truth are sampled at 8kHz. We
resample audio to match the codec’s native sample rate during
encoding, and resample outputs back to 8 kHz for evaluation to
ensure fair comparison across all methods.
4.2. Models and Baselines
We implement two RVQ-Grid variants using different codecs:
•RVQ-Grid (DAC):Uses DAC [15] codec withN= 12
codebook layers andD= 1024. We use the same DAC vari-
ant as Codecformer for a fair comparison. The separator has
L= 6blocks with hidden dimensionC= 512and LSTM
hidden size of 512. Following DAC’s internal activation, we
use Snake activation in the mask head.
•RVQ-Grid (EnCodec):Uses EnCodec [14] codec withN=
32andD= 128. The separator hasL= 16blocks with hid-
den dimensionC= 128and LSTM hidden size of 128. Fol-
lowing EnCodec’s internal activation, we use ELU activation
in the mask head.
Following the evaluation setup of Codecformer, we compare
against the following baselines:
•Codecformer:The first audio codec-based speech separa-
tion model. We use the RVQ-enabled setting provided in their
official codebase.
•SepFormer:A widely adopted waveform-based model that
uses a transformer-based architecture. It serves as a strong
reference for both separation quality and computational com-
plexity.
•SepFormer (Codec):To evaluate robustness to codec distor-
tion, we pass the mixture audio through the codec and feed
the reconstructed output to SepFormer. We use the original
pretrained SepFormer without retraining in order to simulate
a deployment scenario where a pre-deployed model encoun-
ters compressed input.

4.4. Evaluation Metrics
We evaluate the speech separation quality using SI-SDR im-
provement (SI-SDRi) and SDR improvement (SDRi), both
measured in dB. We also report the number of multiply-
accumulate operations (MACs) of the separator computed on
audio segments at 8 kHz using pytorch-OpCounter1. Since neu-
ral audio codecs are optimized for perceptual quality, we ad-
ditionally measure wideband Perceptual Evaluation of Speech
Quality (PESQ) [24] and Short-Time Objective Intelligibility
(STOI) [25]. PESQ and STOI are widely used in speech syn-
thesis and speech enhancement to assess perceived audio qual-
ity and intelligibility. PESQ scores range from−0.5 to 4.5 and
STOI ranges from 0 to 1. Finally, for downstream ASR evalua-
tion, we compute WER using Whisper large-v3-turbo [26].

5.2. Perceptual Quality for Downstream Tasks
Although codec-based methods show lower SI-SDR due to
lossy reconstruction, we observe that the separated audio retains
high perceptual fidelity. To demonstrate this, Table 2 reports
PESQ, STOI and WER between our proposed method and base-
lines. RVQ-Grid achieves perceptual scores close to SepFormer
with codec passthrough while delivering competitive WER,
indicating that the separated audio is both natural-sounding
and usable for downstream speech recognition. Compared
to Codecformer, it shows substantial improvements across all
three metrics. For qualitative inspection, we provide audio sam-
ples on our project page2.
5.3. Ablation 1: Computational Efficiency
Figure 2 shows GPU memory consumption during inference
without any audio chunking techniques. SepFormer’s mem-
ory usage grows quadratically with sequence length due to self-
attention operations, exceeding the memory limit at around 15-
second audio and making long audio processing impractical. In
contrast, codec-based methods consume much lower memory
because neural audio codecs greatly reduce the sequence length
the separator must process.
This compression also enables training on longer audio se-
quences within the same memory budget. To evaluate this,
we create longer mixtures by concatenating utterances from the
WSJ0 corpus and generating new mixtures following the WSJ0-
2Mix procedure. Using the RVQ-Grid EnCodec variant, we
observe that training with 10-second audio improves SI-SDRi
from 8.6 dB to 8.9 dB, and training with 20-second audio fur-
ther improves it to 9.0 dB.

In practical deployment, bandwidth constraints may require
lower codec bitrates, so we examine how the codec’s target bi-
trate affects RVQ-Grid separation quality. We use the RVQ-
Grid EnCodec variant with bitrate configurations provided by
the official EnCodec codebase. Table 4 shows that performance

reconstruction quality itself becomes the bottleneck, with 3.0
kbps and 1.5 kbps achieving 3.3 dB and 0.6 dB respectively.
6. Conclusion
In this work, we introduced RVQ-Grid, a codec-based speech
separation model that preserves the hierarchical structure of
RVQ layers by stacking per-layer RVQ vectors into a 3D grid
and processing them with dual-axis recurrent blocks. RVQ-Grid
achieves a +3.6 dB SI-SDRi over the prior codec-based method
and an 8.6% WER competitive with waveform-based baselines,
while requiring 6x fewer MACs. Our ablation studies show
that using more RVQ layers consistently improves the separa-
tion quality and that performance degrades gracefully at lower
bitrates.
One limitation of our work is that RVQ-Grid uses frozen
pre-trained codecs that are neither designed for separation nor
trained on the target dataset, which may limit the achievable
separation quality. Future work could explore designing au-
dio codec architectures specifically optimized for speech sepa-
ration. Additionally, extending RVQ-Grid to other downstream
tasks such as continuous speech separation and speaker diariza-
tion is a promising direction.
