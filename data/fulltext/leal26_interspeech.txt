Abstract
Spontaneous speech recognition remains a key challenge for
conversational AI, where disfluencies such as fillers, hes-
itations, and truncations are common. Although recent
Transformer-based ASR systems perform well on read speech,
their effectiveness under spontaneous Brazilian Portuguese con-
ditions remains limited, partly due to the lack of diverse bench-
marks. In this work, we introduce Tarsila ASR, a new bench-
mark with more than 70 hours of spontaneous speech aggre-
gated from multiple public datasets to better reflect real world
conversational variability. Using this benchmark, we systemat-
ically evaluate open source ASR models and improve strong
baselines through fine tuning on diverse conversational data,
achieving new state of the art (SOTA) performance. Our results
highlight the importance of domain specific adaptation and pro-
vide publicly available resources to support future research on
conversational ASR in Brazilian Portuguese.
Index Terms: speech recognition, Brazilian Portuguese, bench-
marking dataset
1. Introduction
Recent advances in automatic speech recognition (ASR),
largely driven by deep learning methods and Transformer-based
architectures, have significantly improved the performance of
applications that rely on automated transcription. These in-
clude virtual assistants, voice chatbots, automatic meeting sum-
marization, accessibility technologies, and customer service so-
lutions for call centers [1, 2]. As conversational AI systems
become increasingly prevalent, accurate transcription of sponta-
neous speech has emerged as a critical requirement. Unlike read
speech, spontaneous speech contains fillers, hesitations, trunca-
tions, and other disfluencies that reflect natural human interac-
tion. Preserving these characteristics is particularly important
for training spontaneous-style text to speech systems [3] and
becomes even more critical for emerging real time speech to
speech duplex models [4, 5]. Improving ASR robustness under
spontaneous speech conditions is therefore not only a recog-
nition challenge, but also a key step toward more natural and
reliable conversational AI.
However, for Brazilian Portuguese (BP), the effectiveness
of these systems remains limited, particularly in spontaneous
speech scenarios. Such settings introduce additional challenges,
including regional accents, hesitations, acoustic variability, and
word truncation [6, 7]. A key factor contributing to this gap
is the lack of specialized and diverse benchmarks that enable
standardized and reliable comparisons across models.
In this context, [6] introduced the CORAA ASR corpus,
a diverse dataset comprising dialogues, spontaneous speech,
and multiple regional accents. CORAA ASR integrates five in-
dependent projects, ALIP, C-ORAL BRASIL, NURC-Recife,
SP2010, and TEDx, covering speech from three Brazilian states
and totaling 290 hours of audio. The authors also released a
public ASR model based on the Wav2Vec 2.0 XLSR-53 ar-
chitecture, fine tuned on this corpus. Subsequently, [8] evalu-
ated additional architectures, namely Wav2Vec2-XLSR-53 and
Distil-Whisper, fine tuned on 239 hours of speech from the
NURC-SP Audio Corpus. Although this work expanded the
range of evaluated models, it provided less acoustic diversity
than [6], as the dataset focuses primarily on the paulistano ac-
cent from the city of S ˜ao Paulo. More recently, [7] introduced
MuPe Life Stories, a spontaneous speech corpus for Brazilian
Portuguese consisting of 365 hours of life story interviews. The
dataset includes speakers with diverse characteristics, such as
variations in sex, age, education level, and regional accents
across 17 Brazilian states. Leveraging this resource, the au-
thors fine tuned a Distil-Whisper model originally pre-trained
by [8], revealing performance biases related to age and edu-
cation and emphasizing the need for more diverse benchmark
datasets. Since their study focused on a single architecture, a
broader evaluation involving multiple state of the art models re-
mains necessary to identify the most effective approaches.Ta-
ble 1summarizes Brazilian Portuguese public available ASR
datasets.
These studies highlight a recurring limitation in the lit-
erature, namely the absence of a standardized benchmark for
Brazilian Portuguese that jointly captures diverse linguistic and
acoustic characteristics while enabling consistent evaluation
across multiple models in realistic, real world scenarios.
To address these challenges, we introduce Tarsila-ASR,
a spontaneous speech benchmark designed specifically for
Brazilian Portuguese and built by unifying multiple public
datasets. The benchmark spans a wide range of speaking condi-
tions, from prepared stage presentations such as TEDx talks to
spontaneous dialogues featuring accents from S˜ao Paulo, Minas
Gerais, Pernambuco, and 14 additional Brazilian states, captur-
ing the linguistic and acoustic diversity of spoken Portuguese
in Brazil. In addition, the inclusion of varied speech genres,
including interviews, lectures, read speech, and informal con-
versations, enables a rigorous evaluation of model generaliza-
tion under conditions that depart from controlled studio envi-
ronments. Leveraging this benchmark, we evaluate multiple

Until mid 2020, Brazilian Portuguese faced significant lim-
itations due to the scarcity of publicly available speech data,
with only a few dozen hours accessible for training automatic
speech recognition models. Early open datasets were relatively
small, totaling roughly 60 hours of audio. Over the past five
years, however, this landscape has evolved considerably. As
summarized inTable 1, large public corpora1 designed for ASR
development or commonly used for evaluation now amount to
approximately 1,521 hours of Portuguese speech. Nevertheless,
fewer than 894 hours correspond to spontaneous speech, pri-
marily from CORAA ASR 1.1, NURC-SP, and MuPe, high-
lighting the continued need for diverse conversational bench-
marks.
Several widely used datasets have contributed to this
growth. The Multilingual LibriSpeech (MLS) corpus [12] is
a large scale multilingual dataset derived from LibriV ox audio-
books2 and includes recordings in eight languages. The Brazil-
ian Portuguese subset contains 131 hours of read speech from
54 speakers and is mainly suited for low noise recording condi-
tions. The BRSDv2 dataset [13] combines the CETUC dataset
[14], originally designed for both speech synthesis and recogni-
tion tasks, with four smaller datasets, including Sid, V oxForge,
and LapsBM, three of which are freely available 3, totaling 158
hours of speech. The Multilingual TEDx corpus [15] was intro-
duced to support research in automatic speech recognition and

in pronunciation, speech rate, vocabulary, and grammatical us-

when developing and evaluating ASR systems.
3. Tarsila-ASR Test Dataset
TarsilaASR was constructed by compiling the test subsets of
the public datasets in previous Brazilian ASR work [7], listed
inTable 1. We developed scripts for audio downloading, nor-
malization, and merging. To ensure data integrity across diverse
metadata structures, only thetext,duration, andaudiocolumns
were retained. Two additional columns were added: anorigin
column indicating the original source subset, and agendercol-
umn with values estimated via thevoice-gender-classifier[19].
All audio files were standardized to a 16kHz sampling rate.
The resulting dataset comprises 62,094 samples, totaling
72.46 hours. The distribution across subsets is detailed inTa-
ble 2. Regarding gender balance, the dataset contains 48% fe-
male and 52% male voices.
4. Evaluating open-source models
To better understand the current limitations of Brazilian Por-
tuguese ASR, we evaluate three representative open source
models: MuPe-ASR, a Distil-Whisper model fine tuned for
Brazilian Portuguese; Whisper-large-v3, one of the most widely
adopted open weights ASR systems; and the 7B version of Om-
nilingual model, a recent large scale multilingual architecture.
The performance breakdown across the different subsets of the
benchmark is reported inTable 3.
The results show that although Whisper and Omnilin-
gual achieve strong performance on subsets dominated by
read speech, their accuracy drops substantially in spontaneous
speech conditions. This degradation occurs for two reasons:
(a) spontaneous speech presents challenges such as disfluen-
cies, speaker variability and acoustic diversity; (b) MuPe-ASR
training corpus uses a similar shares similar annotation with the
remaining corpora (for example, acronyms and numbers repre-
sentation). These identified limitations can be mitigated through
fine tuning with more diverse spontaneous speech data, as ex-
plored in Section 5.

All fine tuning experiments are conducted on a single
NVIDIA H100 GPU with 80GB of VRAM, with the longest
training run, Whisper-large-v3, lasting approximately five days.
Performance evaluation is performed on both an NVIDIA H100
and an RTX 4070 GPU with 12GB of VRAM. All check-
points are released on Hugging Face, and the training scripts
together with hyperparameter configurations are available at

5.1. Results and Discussion
To provide a comprehensive evaluation, we report a diverse set
of metrics capturing transcription accuracy, efficiency, and se-
mantic fidelity. Word Error Rate (WER) and Character Error
Rate (CER) measure lexical accuracy at the word and character
levels, respectively, while Real Time Factor (RTF) quantifies
inference efficiency as the ratio between processing time and
audio duration. To complement lexical metrics, we also eval-
uate semantic similarity using BERTScore [24], which aligns
contextual token embeddings between reference and hypothe-
sis, and SeMaScore [25], which analyzes layered semantic rep-
resentations to better capture meaning preservation and hallu-
cination sensitivity. Our SeMaScore implementation adapted
the author’s repository, replacing the DeBERTa-large backbone
with mDeBERTa-V3-base to improve multilingual support.
Table 4highlights a clear trade off between recognition ac-
curacy and computational efficiency on the Tarsila ASR bench-
mark. Across all model families, non adapted checkpoints ex-
hibit substantially higher WER and CER, whereas fine tuning
on diverse spontaneous speech consistently reduces error rates
to the 15 to 19 percent range. This consistent improvement ex-
poses a strong mismatch between spontaneous Brazilian Por-
tuguese speech and standard pretraining distributions, empha-
sizing that competitive performance in realistic conversational
settings requires targeted in domain adaptation.
Among all evaluated systems, Whisper large-v3 with di-
verse spontaneous speech fine tuning achieves the strongest lex-
ical accuracy. In particular, Whisper-large3-ft-75k attains the
lowest WER and CER together with the highest BERTScore,
establishing a new performance reference on the benchmark.
These gains, however, come at a higher computational cost, as
its average real time factor is roughly three to four times slower
than the Distil-Whisper variants. In contrast, Distil-whisper-ft-
200k represents a more deployment oriented operating point,
achieving WER within 1.2 absolute points of the best system

While BERTScore largely follows the trends observed in
WER and CER, SeMaScore provides a complementary view
of semantic preservation. The highest SeMaScore is achieved
by omniASR-LLM-1B-ft-9K, albeit by a small margin over
the best Whisper configuration. This suggests that OmniASR
adapted models may better preserve semantic structure or ex-
hibit greater robustness to meaning level distortions, even when
lexical differences are minimal. Such behavior highlights the
importance of complementing lexical metrics with semantic
evaluation when assessing transcription quality.
Another important observation is that performance does
not improve monotonically with additional fine tuning steps.
For several model families, intermediate checkpoints outper-
form longer training runs, such as Distil-Whisper FT-200K
compared to FT-750K and Whisper large-v3 FT-75k compared
to FT-450K. This pattern is consistent with overfitting or in-
creased sensitivity to subset specific characteristics, indicating
that adaptation to heterogeneous datasets such as Tarsila re-
quires careful checkpoint selection guided by validation perfor-
mance rather than training duration alone.
A key qualitative finding concerns robustness across speak-
ing conditions. As shown inTable 3, the untuned Whisper
large-v3 model performs strongly on read speech but degrades
significantly on spontaneous speech subsets, revealing a pro-
nounced domain and style mismatch. After fine tuning with
more diverse conversational data, performance becomes more
balanced across speaking styles. Although a modest regression
is observed on read speech, substantial gains appear in spon-
taneous and conversational scenarios, resulting in a model that
is better aligned with real world deployment conditions. These
findings reinforce the importance of evaluating ASR systems on
heterogeneous speech and designing adaptation strategies that
reflect intended application domains rather than relying solely
on read speech benchmarks.

6. Conclusions and Future Works
In this work, we introduced Tarsila ASR, a benchmark for eval-
uating automatic speech recognition under diverse spontaneous
speech conditions in Brazilian Portuguese. By unifying multi-
ple public datasets, we built a heterogeneous evaluation frame-
work that better reflects real world conversational scenarios.
Our experiments revealed a performance gap between standard
pretrained models and those adapted with diverse spontaneous
speech data, highlighting the importance of domain specific fine
tuning. Our models achieved SOTA results, improving robust-
ness across speaking styles while maintaining competitive ef-
ficiency and enabling more reliable transcription of conversa-
tional phenomena such as fillers, word truncation, hesitations,
and disfluencies. By releasing the benchmark, models, and
training resources, this work promotes more realistic evalua-
tion practices and supports reproducible research for Portuguese
ASR. Future work will focus on expanding the benchmark, im-
proving transcript normalization, balancing speaking styles, and
exploring multilingual adaptation and evaluation protocols that
jointly capture accuracy, efficiency, and semantic fidelity.
