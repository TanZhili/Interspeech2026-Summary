Abstract
Digital speech measures are a promising approach for assess-
ing progression in Huntington’s disease (HD). Reading tasks
allow evaluation of both motor and cognitive dysfunction due
to the high cognitive load involved. In this study, speech during
passage reading was automatically detected and analyzed in 89
patients with HD and 82 matched controls in three languages
(English, Polish, and Spanish). Bursts of voiced activity were
identified through an adaptive thresholding method to estimate
speech features. Significant differences in temporal features
were observed between the HD and control groups. Features
were then standardized to the language-matched controls and
averaged to yield a composite temporal speech index (CTSI).
Strong correlations were observed between CTSI and compos-
ite, cognitive, and motor scores from the Unified Huntington’s
Disease Rating Scale, with the highest correlation for the Sym-
bol Digit Modality Test, which measures cognitive processing
speed and attention.
Index Terms: Huntington’s disease, automated speech analy-
sis, multilingual composite speech biomarker, reading task
1. Introduction
Huntington’s disease (HD) is a rare hereditary neurodegenera-
tive dysfunction associated with motor, cognitive, and behav-
ioral impairments [1]. Heterogeneous speech alteration in HD
is related to the neurodegeneration of the caudate nucleus, puta-
men, and globus pallidus of the basal ganglia [2]. Digital mea-
sures of speech have the potential to be quantitative markers for
identifying HD progression [3]. Since subtle changes of cog-

nitive function could emerge many years before the HD diag-
nosis [4], passage reading (PR) tasks, which require relatively
high cognitive load, may provide a more sensitive measure of
alterations in speech than simpler speech tasks [5]. In HD,
passage reading has been investigated using automated speech
analysis and quantification of speech features to evaluate differ-
ences between HD and control participants, and features have
been shown to be correlated with the Unified Huntington’s Dis-
ease Rating Scale (UHDRS) motor [5, 6] and cognitive scores
[7, 8, 9, 10].
In rare diseases, such as HD, the ability to combine data sets
across multiple languages is an important consideration for clin-
ical trials. To date, automated assessment of PR tasks across
different languages in HD has been reported only in Fahed et al.
[6]. However, given the effect of language, speech features from
passage reading were excluded when combining the datasets for
comparison against clinical scores.
The aim of this study was to develop a multilingual composite
temporal speech index that captures speech alterations during a
passage reading task in HD, suitable for use across different lan-
guages. By automatically analyzing speech during PR in three
different languages (English, Polish, and Spanish), the compos-
ite index was developed to examine the relationship between
temporal speech features and cognitive and motor scores from
the UHDRS.

2.2. Pre-processing and Bursts of V oiced Activity Detection
A zero-phase fourth-order Butterworth band-pass filter between
10 Hz and 5 kHz was applied to the speech recordings. Then,
the filtered signals were down-sampled to 44.1 kHz, and the
mean was subtracted. Bursts of energy within voiced activity
during the reading task were automatically detected using a dy-
namic adaptive threshold, applied to the Teager-Kaiser Energy
Operator (TKEO) [17, 18] envelope of the speech signal (Figure
1). In order to obtain the TKEO envelope, the TKEO signal was
rectified and smoothed with moving-average filters, followed by
a zero-phase second-order low-pass filter with a 10 Hz cutoff.
To define the adaptive threshold, the TKEO envelope was seg-
mented into 50 ms overlapping windows with 75% overlap, and
the Otsu’s method [19] was applied to each window. An onset
was detected when the TKEO envelope exceeded the adaptive
threshold, and an offset was determined when the TKEO enve-
lope fell under the adaptive threshold. A burst was defined as
an increase in the TKEO envelope of the speech signal above
the adaptive threshold lasting longer than 50 ms. To exclude
false positives in the detected segments, bursts with an area less
than 130% of the area of a corresponding length of noise were
excluded. The accuracy of the detection method was confirmed
by visual inspection. The same automated analysis method was
applied to each signal for all three languages.

2.3. Feature Extraction
To capture temporal patterns, speech fluency, and pausing be-
havior, 10 temporal speech features were estimated:
• Total Speech Time (TST): duration between the onset of the
first detected burst to the offset of the last;
• Total Pause Time (TPT): sum of individual pauses between
the offset of a burst and the onset of the next;
• Net Speech Time (NST): TST excluding pauses;
• Mean Burst Duration (MBD): mean duration of bursts esti-
mated between the detected burst onset and offset;
• CoV of Burst Duration (CoV-BD): standard deviation of
burst duration divided by the MBD;
• Mean Pause Duration (MPD): mean duration of pauses esti-
mated between offset of one burst and onset of the next;
5734
• CoV of Pause Duration (CoV-PD): standard deviation of
pause duration divided by the MPD;
• Net Burst Rate (NBR): number of bursts divided by NST;
• Pause Ratio (PR): TPT expressed as a percentage of TST;
• Number of Pauses (nP): total number of pauses during TST.
Features were estimated using custom Python (v3.11.9) scripts.
2.4. Statistical Analysis
Linear mixed models [20] were implemented in RStudio
(v4.5.1) to investigate differences between controls and HD
groups. For each feature, language and sex were included as
fixed effects, and the intercept for participants was included as
a random effect, as each subject performed multiple repetitions
of the task. P-values were obtained using analysis of variance
(ANOV A) on the fitted mixed-effects model. After checking
for the effect of language between groups, a post-hoc analysis
was performed to examine differences between groups within
each language. Multiple comparisons were corrected using the
Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate [21]. Values less than
0.05 for the corrected p-value were considered significant.
2.5. Composite Temporal Speech Index
To summarize the differences in speech features between HD
and control participants, a composite temporal speech index
(CTSI) was developed by combining features that differed sig-
nificantly between control and HD participants across all lan-
guages. Each feature was standardized using the mean and stan-
dard deviation of the language-matched control group to enable
data combination across languages. Standardized features from
multiple task repetitions were averaged within subjects before
combining. For features in which severity was associated with
lower values, the sign of z-scores was reversed to ensure consis-
tent directionality. Finally, the z-scores of each selected feature
were averaged to obtain the CTSI.

3. Results
3.1. Differences in Temporal Features Between HD and
Control Participants
Linear mixed models revealed significant differences in all 10
speech features between controls and HD participants. Exam-
ining the interaction between groups and languages, the follow-
ing speech features showed statistically significant interaction
(p <0.001for all): TST, NST, TPT, and nP. No significant in-
teraction between group and language was observed for MPD
(p = 0.91), CoV-PD (p = 0.27), PR (p = 0.62), MBD (p = 0.44),
CoV-BD (p = 0.10), and NBR (p = 0.25).
Post-hoc analysis showed that 6 speech features differed signif-
icantly between HD and control participants consistently across
languages (Figure 2): TST (English, Spanish:p <0.05, Pol-
ish:p <0.001), NST (English, Spanish:p <0.05, Pol-
ish:p <0.001), TPT (English, Spanish:p <0.05, Pol-
ish:p <0.001), MBD (English:p <0.01, Polish, Span-
ish:p <0.001), NBR (English:p <0.01, Polish, Spanish:
p <0.001), and nP (English, Spanish:p <0.05, Polish:
p <0.001).
The composite temporal speech index was then calculated, con-
sidering only the 6 features statistically different between HD
participants and controls in each language (TST, NST, TPT,
MBD, NBR, and nP), to ensure robustness to language effects.
3.2. Relationship Between Composite Temporal Speech In-
dex and Clinical Scores
Significant correlations were observed between the CTSI and
the cUHDRS (r = -0.74,p <0.001), the SDMT (r = -0.77,
p <0.001), the SWRT (r = -0.70,p <0.001), and the TMS
(r = 0.64,p <0.001), with highest correlation observed for
SDMT and lowest correlation with the TMS (Figure 3).

4. Discussion
In this study, a fully automated method for detecting and an-
alyzing paragraph reading across three languages in Hunting-
ton’s disease is presented and applied to examine differences
between HD and control participants. Energy of bursts within
voiced activity was detected using an adaptive threshold based
on Otsu’s method. Significant differences were observed in all
temporal speech features between control and HD participants.
Patients took longer to read the paragraph [5, 6], with a greater
number of pauses. Net speech rate decreased [7], and both mean
bursts duration of voiced activity and pauses were longer over-
all in HD participants [9, 10]. After examining the interaction
between groups and languages, post-hoc analysis showed that
6 out of 10 temporal speech features differed significantly be-
tween HD participants and controls for each language (Figure
2). From these 6 speech features, a composite temporal speech
index was assessed to summarize the alterations in speech fea-
tures across languages, standardized to language-matched con-
trol data, and to investigate the relationship between the CTSI
and 4 clinical measures (cUHDRS, SDMT, SWRT, and TMS).
Strong correlations were observed between the CTSI and the
clinical measures, with the highest correlation with SDMT,
which measures cognitive decline in HD (Figure 3).
Similar correlations with clinical metrics were observed by Ti-
teux et al. [23] when coupling speech features from counting
and spontaneous tasks, extracted by speech pathologists and the
Whisper*ASR model, and demographic information, reflecting
disease stage. Kouba et al. [9] also combined speech features
from sustained phonation, syllable repetition, and passage read-
ing in HD in German-speaking population to obtain a single
speech index; however, composite speech indices have not yet
been applied in a multilingual cohort to our knowledge. The
proposed method is fully automated and can be applied to dif-
ferent languages and paragraphs of different lengths. Here,
rather than developing machine learning models to predict each
clinical metric, the focus was on assessing the relationship be-
tween a new measure of HD speech dysfunction, the CTSI,
and clinical measures related to brain volume (cUHDRS), mo-
tor (TMS) and cognitive function (SDMT, SWRT). The CTSI
can potentially, therefore, exhibit differences in speech that the
clinical scores are not sensitive to.
5. Conclusions
Driven by the need for digital biomarkers of speech that re-
flect changes in motor and cognitive functions in HD, and
to combine datasets across multiple languages for clinical tri-
als, a method to automatically identify differences in tempo-
ral speech features between control and HD participants is pro-
posed. The speech features, standardized by language-matched
controls, were combined into a composite temporal speech in-
dex, which was highly correlated with both motor and clini-
cal measures. The proposed composite temporal speech index
could potentially be used to monitor changes in speech with dis-
ease progression. In addition, as cognitive changes can occur
many years before motor changes and diagnosis, it may provide
means to detect subtle changes in early stages of HD.
