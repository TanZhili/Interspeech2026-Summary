Abstract
Speech Language Models (SLMs) extend large language mod-
els to spoken interaction, but this multimodal integration intro-
duces modality-specific vulnerabilities. While prior work has
typically addressed speech and text safety in isolation, our work
uncovers a shared safety subspace where benign, harmful, and
jailbreak representations exist regardless of input modality. We
identify this subspace via a PCA projection, revealing a well-
defined boundary separating refusal from jailbreak compliance.
Causal intervention along this boundary shifts model behavior
between refusal and compliance across modalities and attack
types without additional training. For example, a refusal vector
derived from an audio attack reduces text jailbreak success from
76.5% to 2.2%, while a refusal vector derived from the text at-
tack reduces audio jailbreak success from 79.0% to 3.1%. We
demonstrate these results on Qwen2-Audio and GLM-4-V oice,
and confirm transferability across five different attacks. 1
Index Terms: Speech Language Models, AI Safety, Inter-
pretability, Jailbreak, Steering
1. Introduction
Speech Language Models (SLMs) add audio input to large lan-
guage models, enabling interaction through both speech and
text and capturing cues such as timing and emphasis that are
absent from text alone [1, 2, 3, 4]. However, adding speech
also expands the safety attack surface: in addition to text jail-
breaks, SLMs are exposed to audio-based attacks that can in-
duce unsafe behavior through carefully constructed spoken in-
puts, compounding jailbreak risk across modalities. Jailbreaks
are also diverse within the audio modality: attacks can exploit
paralinguistic cues [5], embed hidden instructions in the wave-
form while keeping the surface transcript benign [6], or use
optimization-based perturbations [7, 8].
Prior defenses use a range of approaches, including input
filtering, adversarial training on perturbed audio, and post-hoc
activation interventions [5, 9]. While these approaches can re-
duce measured attack success rates, they often treat safety as an
outcome metric to be optimized rather than characterizing the
internal mechanism that jailbreaks bypass or how adding audio
changes the model’s safety state. In parallel, mechanistic in-
terpretability studies on text-only language models argue that
refusal is controlled by compact structure in activation space:
for example, refusal can be modulated by intervening on a small
number of residual-stream directions, and related work suggests
multiple separable refusal or safety directions rather than a sin-
gle control signal [10, 11, 12]. However, it remains unclear

whether these mechanistic conclusions extend to SLMs. This
raises a key question: do audio jailbreaks exploit a modality-
specific failure mode introduced by speech, or do they bypass
the same internal safety mechanism through a different input
channel?
Recent work in text-only LMs has begun to suggest that
safety behavior may admit shared structure across attacks: for
example, shaping safety boundaries can yield interventions that
generalize across jailbreak methods [13], and refusal-feature
approaches exploit compact refusal representations in the resid-
ual stream [11]. Notably, suppressing refusal features does
not necessarily induce harmful compliance, suggesting that re-
fusal features capture an important component of safety behav-
ior but may not fully explain jailbreak-induced compliance, es-
pecially outside the text-only setting [11]. Once the model ac-
cepts speech as well as text, it is therefore unclear whether the
same internal features or boundary-shaping interventions will
transfer: audio inputs can introduce transcript-preserving per-
turbations and non-linguistic cues that may interact with safety
mechanisms differently.
We therefore ask whether multimodal jailbreaks still con-
verge on a shared low-dimensionalsafety subspacein the resid-
ual stream, despite the diversity of attacks and the shift from
text to speech. In this work, we test this hypothesis by mapping
safety behavior in residual-stream activation space and inter-
vening at inference time. We use dimensionality reduction al-
gorithm, Principal Component Analysis (PCA), to reveal a low-
dimensional structure in which benign, harmful, and jailbreak
queries become separable, and we construct simple steering di-
rections from the resulting subspace. Injecting these directions
into the residual stream at inference time produces targeted be-
havioral flips between refusal and compliance without retrain-
ing.
A key empirical result is that thelearned directions are
modality-agnostic: vectors derived from audio attacks transfer
to text and vice versa, with large reductions in jailbreak success
across multiple attack families. For example, applying a refusal
direction derived from an audio-based AdvWave attack reduces
the success rate of the AutoDAN-text jailbreak from 72.69% to
2.82%, and the reverse transfer is similarly effective. We fur-
ther observe strong generalization across multiple audio attack
families and consistent improvements over random directions.

3. Experimental Setup
We conduct all experiments on 2 open-source speech language
modelsQwen2-Audio[3] andGLM-4-Voice[20] which accept
either text or audio as input and generate text responses.
Datasets.Throughout our experiments, we will send be-
nign, harmful, and jailbreak inputs respectively, in both text
and audio modalities, to the model. For benign text prompts,
we randomly sample 200 instructions from Alpaca [21]; mean-
while, for benign audio prompts, we use 200 samples from Air-
Bench [22], both of which contain general utility queries. For
harmful queries, we use AdvBench [23] in text form. To con-
struct harmful audio inputs, we use synthesized audio versions
of AdvBench prompts, similar to [9]. This allows us to keep
the underlying harmful intent consistent across modalities while
changing only the input channel.
Attacks.As discussed earlier, we consider both audio and
text modality based jailbreak attacks as part of this paper. For
audio, we use AdvWave [7], AMSE [24], and CA V A [25]; Ad-
vWave and AMSE are sourced from JALMBench [26]. For text-
based attacks, we use AutoDAN [27] and BEAST [28], which
are jailbreak methods originally developed for text LLMs and
have also been reported to transfer to SLMs in recent work [7].
Evaluation.To quantify the model’s safety under jailbreak
attempts, we use theAttack Success Rate(ASR), defined as
the percentage of adversarial inputs for which the model actu-
ally complies to the input, and produces harmful outputs: ASR
= Nharm
N ×100%, whereNis the total number of inputs and
Nharm is the number of inputs that elicit harmful content (i.e.,
successful jailbreaks). A lower ASR indicates that the model is
refusing more often or otherwise avoiding harmful generations
under attack. We determineN harm using an LLM-as-a-judge
protocol: we useGPT-5[29] to label each generated response
as harmful or not. The judge prompt can be found in the at-
tached code.

Table 1 summarizes the results. Subtracting the steering di-
rection from jailbreak inputs reduces ASR to below 7% across
all five attacks on both models. For example, AdvWave drops
from 97.93% to 0.52% on Qwen2-Audio and from 84.97% to
2.59% on GLM-4-V oice. Text jailbreaks show the same pattern,
with AutoDAN falling from 72.69% to 2.82% and from 81.48%
to 3.24% on the two models, respectively. Crucially, the Audio
Vector is equally effective on text jailbreaks and vice versa, em-
pirically validating the cross-modal transferability predicted by
Proposition 1. Applying the positive direction to refused harm-
ful inputs produces the opposite effect, with ASR increasing
from below 6% to 56-90% across both models and modalities.
Random vectors of matched norm fail to produce comparable
shifts, confirming that the steering effect is directional rather
than an artifact of perturbation magnitude
Together, these results provide causal evidence for our cen-
tral claim: a unified safety subspace exists in Speech Language
Models, and a single steering direction is sufficient to control
refusal behavior across modalities, attack families, and models
without any additional training.
5. Conclusion
We uncover a unified safety subspace in Speech Language Mod-
els that holds simultaneously across speech and text modalities
and across diverse jailbreak attacks, linking spoken and writ-
ten prompts through the same internal mechanism. We provide
a formal theoretical analysis explaining why such cross-modal
safety directions should arise in SLMs, and we design experi-
ments that empirically verify that these predicted directions re-
liably arise in practice. While prior work identified safety direc-
tions that generalize across jailbreak strategies in text models,
we show that the same geometric structure also governs cross-
modal safety behavior. We validate this structure across five
diverse jailbreak attacks and identify a compact direction in
the residual stream that separates refusal from jailbreak com-
pliance. From this subspace, we derive Audio and Text steering
vectors that consistently modulate model behavior at inference
time without retraining, thereby suggesting a common geomet-
ric boundary rather than modality-specific vulnerabilities.
Future work includes extending this analysis to additional
input modalities and architectures and developing training-time
methods that explicitly regularize or align this refusal subspace
to improve robustness against jailbreak attacks.
