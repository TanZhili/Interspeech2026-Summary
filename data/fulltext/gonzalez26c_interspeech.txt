Abstract
Automatic Speech Recognition errors emerge from interac-
tions across linguistic dimensions. While prior work corre-
lated errors with external features, it remains unclear whether
these patterns hold when linguistic features are measured from
ASR outputs. We analysed substitution errors on the FLEURS
dataset using Whisper and Seamless, computing phonetic,
morphological, syntactic, and lexical similarity directly from
ASR audio and transcriptions. Results revealed that semantic
preservation emerges from systematic cross-dimension interac-
tions: morphological and syntactic features operated interde-
pendently while phonological accuracy intensified when syn-
tax was preserved. Interaction patterns differed across archi-
tectures: Seamless showed better baseline performance, while
Whisper showed heightened error sensitivity but leveraged mor-
phological information more effectively, showing that ASR ar-
chitectures integrate linguistic information through fundamen-
tally different mechanisms.
Index Terms: Automatic Speech Recognition, Multilingual,
Linguistic Interactions, Error Analysis, FLEURS Dataset
1. Introduction
Automatic Speech Recognition (ASR) systems are increasingly
used in multilingual and cross-linguistic applications [1, 2]. Un-
derstanding how linguistic structure shapes ASR errors is criti-
cal for both evaluation and improvement [3, 4]. Substitution er-
rors occur when an ASR system replaces a reference word with
an incorrect word in the recognised output [5]. The impact of
substitutions differs across linguistic properties (e.g., phonetic,
semantic), yet standard ASR evaluation metrics fail to capture
these nuances, most notably, word error rate (WER) [6].
ASR evaluation has therefore traditionally relied on global
accuracy measures that treat all errors as equally costly and
largely ignore their linguistic consequences [7]. While WER re-
mains a standard benchmark, numerous studies have shown that
it correlates imperfectly with human judgements of intelligibil-
ity and meaning preservation, particularly in multilingual and
morphologically-rich languages [8, 9]. To address these limita-
tions, prior work has explored linguistically-informed analyses
of ASR errors. At the phonetic level, studies have examined
confusability patterns based on distinctive features and articu-
latory similarity, demonstrating that substitution errors differ in
their perceptual impact, rather than being uniformly disruptive
[10, 11]. At higher linguistic levels, research has shown that
morphological and syntactic structure can systematically influ-
ence recognition difficulty, particularly in languages with com-
plex inflectional systems or flexible word order [12, 13]. Se-

meaning preservation beyond exact word matching, revealing
cases where high WER sentences nonetheless retain substantial
semantic content [14, 15].
On the language diversity front, multilingual ASR research
has increasingly emphasised cross-linguistic variation and ty-
pology. Large-scale analyses have demonstrated that language
family, phonological inventory size, and morphological com-
plexity affect ASR performance in systematic ways [16, 17].
Recent work has also leveraged typological databases such as
World Atlas of Language Structures (W ALS) [18] to relate ASR
error patterns with abstract linguistic properties, enabling cross-
language generalisation but at the cost of relying on aggregated,
non-token-level features [19].
Most directly related to the present study, [4] analysed ASR
errors across linguistic dimensions using typological features
derived from W ALS, comparing multiple languages. Their re-
sults suggested that Whisper places greater emphasis on struc-
tural linguistic properties, while Seamless adopts a more holis-
tic strategy that yields higher accuracy based on WER. How-
ever, because the linguistic features in that work were typologi-
cal proxies and language-level rather than derived directly from
ASR outputs, it remains unclear whether the observed patterns
reflect properties of the recognition errors themselves or broader
correlations encoded in typological databases.
2. Aim of Study
The present study advances this line of research by calculat-
ing phonetic, morphological, syntactical, and semantic similar-
ity measures directly from ASR substitution errors, enabling
a token-level, multidimensional characterisation of recognition
patterns across languages and ASR systems. A central aim is
to move beyond WER, which treats all errors as equally disrup-
tive, and instead evaluate how linguistic dimensions impact the
degree to which semantic content is preserved between ground-
truth transcripts and ASR outputs.
This shift is motivated by the observation that downstream
applications, such as information retrieval, dialogue systems,
and spoken language understanding, are sensitive not to isolated
word errors but to the preservation of meaning. By modelling
how multiple linguistic dimensions jointly shape semantic sim-
ilarity, we focus on the functional consequences of ASR errors.
These measures are integrated within a mixed-effects modelling
framework that captures both additive and interaction effects
while explicitly accounting for language and system-specific
variability. This approach provides a more fine-grained and
linguistically grounded evaluation of multilingual ASR perfor-
mance, offering deeper insight into how different ASR systems
weight and preserve linguistic information across languages.

3. Methodology
3.1. ASR Systems
We selected two widely used multilingual ASR systems, Whis-
per and Seamless, to examine system-specific patterns in substi-
tution errors and to assess whether linguistic interaction effects
differ across ASR architectures. This comparison also allowed
us to examine whether systems encode linguistic structure in
distinct ways. Whisper (Open AI, 2022) is a Transformer-
based speech-to-text model trained in a supervised multitask
setting for speech recognition, translation and language identi-
fication on large-scale multilingual audio-text data [20]. Whis-
per has been shown to produce outputs with relatively high se-
mantic accuracy, even in cases where surface-level accuracy is
imperfect [20]. In contrast, SeamlessM4T is a unified mul-
tilingual speech-text model that supports speech recognition,
speech translation, text-to-speech and speech-to-speech trans-
lation across over 100 languages, with a particular emphasis
on phonetic robustness and cross-lingual consistency (Seam-
lessM4T Team, 2023). Its architecture integrates large-scale
self-supervised speech representations with explicit multilin-
gual alignment objectives, which have been shown to improve
phonetic accuracy and cross-language generalisation, especially
for under-resourced and typologically diverse languages [21].
3.2. Speech Dataset
We used the FLEURS dataset [22], a large-scale multilingual
speech corpus with transcribed speech in approximately 100
languages, spanning a wide range of language families. It con-
sists of recordings from speakers reading texts that elicit di-
verse phonetic and morphological patterns, syntactic construc-
tions, and semantic content. The broad coverage and standard-
ised recording conditions make FLEURS well suited for cross-
linguistic ASR evaluation. For the present study, we selected 42
languages with sentences for which reliable alignment between
the ASR output and the reference transcription could be estab-
lished, allowing us to focus specifically on substitution errors.
It includes over 48,000 audio files, with each language repre-
sented by an average of 1,157 audio files (SD=402). The total
duration of the selected data exceeds 154 hours, with a mean
total per language of 3.7 hours (SD=1.4).
3.3. Metrics
We conducted a detailed annotation of substitution errors
across linguistic dimensions while capturing cross-linguistic
and cross-system variation. ASR outputs were aligned with ref-
erence transcriptions, and each alignment was processed using
universal dependency parsing via theStanza toolkit[23]. Our
analyses rely on currently available, standardised tools, such as
Stanza, and our findings are interpreted with this limitation in
mind. For each aligned instance, we extracted both the refer-
ence and ASR-predicted word sequences, which formed the ba-
sis for subsequent similarity analyses. To ensure correct word-
pair comparisons, we limited this study to substitution errors,
excluding deletions and insertions identified during alignment.
3.3.1. Sentence-level Semantic Similarity as the Main Re-
sponse Variable
The response variable in the analysis was built at the sentence-
level semantic scale, which measured the degree of semantic
similarity between reference and ASR transcriptions. Sentence-
level semantic similarity [SENT] was calculated as a nor-
malised score between reference and ASR outputs (0=no over-
lap, 1=identical meaning). We used thesentence-transformers
python library [24] to encode sentences into dense seman-
tic embeddings using the pretrainedparaphrase-multilingual-
MiniLM-L12-v2model, after which similarity was calculated
directly between reference and hypothesis embeddings.
3.3.2. Establishing Independent Variables
For the independent variables, we computed a set of four lin-
guistically informed similarity measures targeting distinct lin-
guistic dimensions. Similar to [25, 26], phonetic similar-
ity [PHN] was computed as normalisedLevenshtein Distance
between reference and predicted words (0=identical, 1=com-
pletely dissimilar). Phonetic transcriptions were obtained for
all words in the dataset using thephonemizerlibrary [27], which
interfaces with theeSpeak-ngtext-to-speech synthesis system.
For each word, thephonemizefunction converted orthographic
text to phonetic representation based on the word’s correspond-
ing language code. The transcription process used language-
specific phonological rules.
Morphological [MOR] and Syntactic [SYN] similarity
were operationalised as a normalised difference between parts-
of-speech and syntactic dependency tags, respectively, reflect-
ing whether substitutions preserved or altered grammatical and
syntactical categories, with values again ranging from 0 (identi-
cal) to 1 (different). Semantic similarity [SEM] was the seman-
tic distance between reference and predicted words using dis-
tributed word embeddings, with lower values indicating greater
semantic overlap. We employed pretrainedfastText[28] (Face-
book AI Research) multilingual word embeddings to capture
graded semantic relatedness beyond surface-form variation. Ta-
ble 1 shows an example of the types of differences measured.

3.4. Statistical Modelling
The analytical framework was developed in four stages to quan-
tify the relative contributions of the linguistic dimensions of
interest to sentence-level ASR performance. This staged ap-
proach allowed us to identify interaction effects among these
dimensions that shape substitution errors, and to determine
whether such interactions vary systematically across languages
and ASR systems. All analyses were restricted to substitution
errors in order to isolate patterns of lexical replacement, rather
than effects driven by insertion or deletion phenomena.
In the first stage, (Baseline Model), we built a baseline
model to estimate the main effects of each linguistic dimension
on overall sentence-level semantic similarity. We employed
mixed-effects beta regression using theglmmTMB[29] pack-
age in R [30], which is well suited for proportion-like data and
allows simultaneous modelling of fixed and random effects, and
dispersion [31]. The baseline model included fixed effects for
PHN, MOR, SYN, and SEM similarity. To account for repeated
measurements and hierarchical structure in the data, random in-
tercepts were included for both language and ASR system.
In the second stage, (Inter-dimension Interactions), we ex-
amined inter-dimension interactions by extending the baseline

Including these terms allowed us to test whether linguistic di-

In the third stage, (Effect of ASR System), we examined
whether the strongest interaction effects differed across ASR
systems by introducing ASR as a moderator of both main effects
and pairwise linguistic interactions. This was implemented by
adding three-way interaction terms between linguistic similarity
dimensions and ASR system, while retaining language as a ran-
dom intercept. Likelihood-ratio tests comparing ASR-specific
and ASR-agnostic models were used to evaluate whether allow-
ing interaction effects to vary by system significantly improved
model fit, thereby indicating system-dependent error dynamics.
In the fourth stage, (Effect of Language), we examined
cross-linguistic variation by fitting a language-sensitive model
with language interacting with PHN, MOR, SYN, and SEM
similarity, while keeping ASR systems as random intercepts.
This captures language-specific differences in how individual
linguistic dimensions (and their interactions with language)
contribute to SENT preservation under ASR errors.
4. Results
4.1. Baseline Model Results
Results from the base model revealed that all predictors were
significant (p<.001). PHN had the strongest negative effect
(β=-0.31), followed by smaller negative effects of SYN (β=-
0.03). In contrast, SEM similarity showed a strong positive
effect (β=0.84) followed by smaller positive effects of MOR
(β=0.03) and, indicating that preserving word-level meaning
helps maintain overall sentence-level semantic similarity de-
spite surface-form errors.
Random effects indicated substantial cross-language vari-
ability (SD=0.33), and ASR-system variability (SD=0.28). A
likelihood-ratio test comparing the pairwise interaction model
(i.e., second stage) to the main-effects model (i.e., baseline)
revealed a significant improvement in fit (χ 2=165.54, df=6,
p<2.2e-16), indicating that interactions among linguistic di-
mensions contribute meaningfully to explaining sentence-level
semantic preservation of the text. This also shows that SENT
similarity is highly conditional on how linguistic dimensions
interact, rather than on their sole independent effects.
4.2. Inter-dimension Interaction Results
We further examined the contribution of each interaction
term by performing likelihood-ratio tests for single-term dele-
tions from the full interaction model (See Figure 1). Ex-
cept for PHN:MOR and SYN:SEM, all other interactions
were statistically significant (p<.05), indicating that most pair-
wise combinations similarity jointly influence SENT simi-
larity. The largest effects were observed for MOR:SYN
(χ2=113.02, p<.001), followed by PHN:SEM (χ 2=21.7,
p<.001), MOR:SEM (χ 2=8.62, p<.003), and PHN:SYN
(χ2=7.4, p<.006).
The strong MOR:SYN interaction indicates that preserv-
ing both grammatical features produces greater sentence sim-
ilarity than expected from their independent effects, suggest-
ing they function as an integrated system for meaning compre-
hension. The PHN:SEM interaction suggests that successful
phonological recognition amplifies ASR’s ability to preserve
semantic similarity. These results therefore indicate that ASR
systems have higher functional accuracy by leveraging a com-
bination of multiple linguistic dimensions, rather than only iso-
lated dimension-specific features.
4.3. ASR System Effect Results
To examine whether the effects of linguistic dimensions and
their interactions differ across ASR systems, we extended the
interaction model to include interactions with the ASR system.
A likelihood-ratio test confirmed that including ASR effects
significantly improves model fit (△AIC=9,987, p<.001),
indicating that some linguistic effects vary between systems.
Seamless outperformed Whisper at baseline (β=-0.20,
p=0.011), but interactions revealed systematic differences.
Two-way ASR interactions showed Whisper benefits more
from morphological preservation (β=0.14, p<.001) but suffers
greater penalties from phonological (β=-0.29, p<.001),
semantic (β=-0.29, p<.001), and syntactic (β=-0.07, p=0.003)
degradation, showing more sensitivity to linguistic divergence.
Linguistic features interacted across both systems:
MOR:SYN (β=0.24, p<.001) demonstrated interdependent
preservation effects, PHN:SYN (β=-0.22, p<.001) showed
phonological accuracy becomes critical when syntax is
preserved, and SEM:MOR (β=0.09, p=0.047) revealed
modest coupling. PHN:SEM was non-significant (p=0.727).
Three-way interactions exposed system-specific sensitivities.
PHN:SEM × ASR (β=0.45, p<.001) indicates Whisper
recovers meaning better when both phonological and semantic
features are accurate. Negative MOR:SYN × ASR (β=-0.12,
p<.001) and SEM:MOR × ASR (β=-0.28, p<.001) interac-
tions show Whisper’s morphological advantage diminishes in
combination with other preserved features, while PHN:SYN

4.4. Language Effect Results
Random effects revealed substantial cross-linguistic variation,
reflecting differences in phonemic inventories, morphological
complexity, and training data representation (See Figure 2).
Vietnamese (PHNβ=1.29, SEMβ=1.44) and Dutch (PHN
β=0.55, SEMβ=0.56) consistently outperform averages, while
Czech shows marked deficits (PHNβ=-0.89, MORβ=-0.34).
Language-specific effects interact with linguistic dimensions.
Czech shows severe PHN errors (β=-0.89) and reduced MOR
(β=-0.34) yet maintains positive SEM (β=0.51) and SYN
(β=0.25), indicating compensation at higher levels. Vietnamese
excels comprehensively (PHNβ=1.29, SEMβ=1.44), while
Hungarian shows moderate PHN deficits (β=-0.40) buffered by
strong SEM (β=0.97) and SYN (β=0.27) preservation.
Sentence-level outcomes reveal complex integration ef-
fects. Spanish (β=0.52) and Ukrainian (β=0.33) achieve
strong performance through balanced dimension preservation.
Vietnamese shows surprisingly modest sentence-level results
(β=-0.26) despite exceptional dimension scores, while Czech
(β=0.42) demonstrates robust compensation despite PHN
deficits. Urdu shows the poorest sentence-level performance
(β=-0.42) despite moderate dimension preservation, suggest-
ing integration difficulties. This reinforces that sentence-level
meaning emerges from complex cross-dimension interactions
rather than isolated linguistic strengths.
5. Discussion
The results from this study demonstrate that sentence-level se-
mantic preservation is based on systematic interactions across
linguistic dimensions rather than isolated error types. Morpho-
logical and syntactic features operate interdependently (β=0.24,
p<.001), with their joint preservation giving disproportionate
gains in meaning retention. Phonological accuracy plays a
context-dependent role: its contribution intensifies when syn-
tactic structure is intact (β=-0.22, p<.001), suggesting that
phonological and syntactic information are processed in com-
bination during ASR decoding. Of particular relevance, re-
sults show that phonological and semantic dimensions operate
largely independently (p=0.73), suggesting distinct processing
pathways for these features.
A critical finding is that these interaction patterns differ sys-
tematically across ASR architectures. Seamless shows better
baseline performance (β=-0.20, p=0.01) and greater stability
under linguistic degradation. Whisper, while more sensitive to
phonological (β=-0.29, p<.001), semantic (β=-0.29, p<.001),
and syntactic (β=-0.07, p=0.003) errors, leverages morpholog-
ical information more effectively (β=0.14, p<.001) and shows
better sensitivity to multi-level feature preservation.
Three-way interactions reveal that Whisper derives greater
benefit when phonological and semantic features align (β=0.45,
p<.001) or when phonological and syntactic accuracy co-
incide (β=0.26, p<.001), but its morphological advantage
diminishes when combined with other preserved features
(MOR:SYNxASR:β=-0.12, p<.001; SEM:MOR×ASR:β=-
0.28, p<.001). This shows that ASR architectures integrate
linguistic information through fundamentally different mecha-
nisms that are not captured by aggregate accuracy metrics.
Substantial language-specific variation (βrange: -0.89
to 1.44) revealed systematic interactions between typological
properties and error patterns. Vietnamese and Dutch consis-
tently showed strong performance across dimensions, while
Czech showed high phonological (β=-0.89) and morphologi-
cal (β=-0.34) deficits. We also found that dimension-specific
strengths did not directly predict sentence-level outcomes: Viet-
namese achieved high phonological and semantic preservation
even when having lower sentence-level performance (β=-0.26),
while Czech showed positive sentence-level similarity (β=0.42)
despite multiple dimension deficits. Urdu is a clear example
of this, which has individual dimension moderate preservation
(phonologyβ=0.31, semanticsβ=0.42) but very low sentence-
level similarity (β=-0.42), potentially reflecting script or syn-
tactic complexity underrepresented in training data. These
findings demonstrate that typologically diverse languages en-
counter qualitatively different error profiles, and that sentence-
level meaning emerges from complex integration processes be-
yond summing dimension-specific accuracies.
6. Conclusions
Recognising that our results are interpreted within the limita-
tions of the selected dataset and ASR models, this study demon-
strated that ASR error patterns varied systematically across ar-
chitectures and languages. Whisper showed more sensitivity
to phonological, semantic, and syntactic errors but leveraged
morphological information more effectively, while Seamless
maintained stable performance under degradation. Morpho-
logical and syntactic features operated interdependently, with
phonological accuracy intensifying when syntax was preserved.
Language-specific analyses revealed substantial variation.
Dimension-specific accuracies did not predict sentence-
level outcomes: Urdu maintained moderate dimension preser-
vation but poor sentence-level similarity, while Czech achieved
positive performance despite deficits, indicating complex inte-
gration processes beyond simple aggregation.
These findings highlight the value of language-guided anal-
ysis for understanding how ASR errors affect meaning, par-
ticularly for low-resource and typologically-diverse languages.
