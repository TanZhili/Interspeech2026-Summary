Abstract
Bio-acoustic health signals are increasingly vital for remote di-
agnostics, yet efficient transmission remains a bottleneck for
clinical adoption. While audio codecs offer strong compres-
sion for general audio, they preserve diagnostically critical in-
formation in bio-acoustic signals is unclear. To address this
gap, we introduce BACH, the first systematicBenchmark for
evaluatingAudioCodecs on bio-acousticHealth tasks. BACH
assesses eight codecs across five bio-acoustic health tasks, uti-
lizing a unified framework covering original audio, compressed
representations, and reconstructed audio. Our central finding
reveals a critical misalignment between reconstruction fidelity
and downstream task performance: codecs excelling at sig-
nal reconstruction do not always retain task-relevant semantic
cues. This highlights a fundamental challenge for future re-
search: codecs for medical applications must be co-designed to
optimize for both perceptual quality and diagnostic information
preservation.
Index Terms: bio-acoustic health, audio codec, benchmark, re-
construction fidelity, semantic information
1. Introduction
In recent years, the rapid development of remote healthcare
has transformed how patients interact with medical profession-
als, enabling continuous monitoring beyond clinical settings[1].
Bio-acoustic health signals, such as heartbeats, snoring, and
lung sounds, carry critical diagnostic information for early
screening and long-term monitoring [2]. However, the vast vol-
ume and high dimensionality of bio-acoustic health signals pose
challenges for transmission and real-time analysis. Reliable
transmission and automated classification are crucial to remote
screening and continuous monitoring.
A central challenge is how to compress bio-acoustic health
signals while preserving critical diagnostic cues. Audio codecs
offer a new opportunity: they achieve remarkable success in
compressing audio at low bitrates while maintaining high fi-
delity. Such codecs encode audio into discrete tokens via an
encoder and quantizers to achieve compression, and then re-
construct high-quality signals by a decoder. In the past few
years, neural audio codecs have made significant advancements.
DAC [3] and EnCodec [4] employ multi-layer residual vec-
tor quantization (RVQ) proposed for high-fidelity audio recon-
struction. Building upon these foundations, more recent codecs
have explored different strategies to balance bitrates, fidelity,
and semantic richness. For instance, WavTokenizer [5] utilizes
a single quantizer to improve reconstruction quality, achiev-
ing higher compression. SemantiCodec [6] introduces a dual-

encoder framework that decouples audio into acoustic and se-
mantic representations. The evaluation of codecs is shifting
from purely signal quality [7, 8] to the ability to preserve infor-
mation useful for downstream tasks. Benchmarks like Codec-
SUPERB [9] illustrate this shift, but they remain focused on
reconstructed audio. ARCH [10] and AudioCodecBench [11]
probe audio representation from codecs in speech tasks, but
their utility in bio-acoustic health remains underexplored.
Bio-acoustic health signals pose unique challenges. Unlike
speech, they are often short, sparse, and irregular [12]. A codec
that achieves high perceptual scores on speech, audio, and mu-
sic may fail to preserve subtle but clinically critical cues. Prior
work on heart sound classification or respiratory sound analy-
sis has largely used handcrafted features or spectrogram-based
convolutional neural networks, without considering compres-
sion constraints [13]. Yet no systematic study has examined the
performance of codecs on compressed bio-acoustic health sig-
nals.
In this work, we introduce BACH, the first benchmark for
systematically evaluating neural audio codecs in bio-acoustic
health. BACH covers five bio-acoustic health datasets and as-
sesses eight representative codecs within a unified framework.
We consider boththe original domain, where the audio is
not processed by any codecs,the compressed domain, where
audio representation derived from codec’s quantizer is used
for downstream tasks, andthe reconstructed domain, where
the reconstructed audio is evaluated. In addition, we measure
signal-level reconstruction quality. These evaluations provide
a comprehensive view of the trade-offs among bitrates, recon-
struction quality, and downstream tasks performance.
The contributions of this article are summarized as follows:
• We propose BACH, which, to the best of our knowledge, is
the first systematic benchmark to evaluate audio codecs on
bio-acoustic health tasks.
• We introduce a three-view evaluation framework that as-
sesses bio-acoustic health tasks on original audio, audio rep-
resentation from the codec, and reconstructed audio, enabling
a holistic analysis of codec task-preserving ability.
• We benchmark a diverse set of neural audio codec models
and provide new insights into the trade-offs among bitrates,
reconstruction fidelity, and downstream tasks performance.
2. Benchmark
We introduce BACH to provide a fair and comprehensive eval-
uation for audio codecs in bio-acoustic health tasks. The bench-
mark consists of three core elements: (1) A unified evaluation
pipeline. (2) A diverse set of codecs that cover distinct architec-
tures. (3) A variety of bio-acoustic health datasets. The follow-
ing subsections detail each element.

2.1. Evaluation Pipeline
Our evaluation framework adopts a unified architecture to en-
sure fair comparisons across different audio codecs. As illus-
trated in Fig. 1, the pipeline consists of three complementary
evaluation paths: original domain, compressed domain, and re-
constructed domain.
Original domain:This path evaluates the raw audio with-
out any codec processing, serving as a baseline for the other
two domains. It reflects the maximum performance achievable
when no information is lost through compression.
Compressed domain:We directly use the audio represen-
tation produced by the codec quantizer to assess the codec’s
ability to extract audio features. This provides insights into
whether compressed tokens retain semantic information useful
for downstream bio-acoustic health tasks.
Reconstructed domain:The input audio is first com-
pressed and reconstructed by the audio codec. Prior work [14]
has shown that HuBERT [15] effectively captures bio-acoustic
characteristics owing to its robust self-supervised representa-
tions. Accordingly, the reconstructed audio is passed to a pre-
trained HuBERT to extract audio features, which are fed into
the downstream classifier for health condition prediction. This
path measures how well the codec preserves task-relevant infor-
mation in the reconstructed audio.
For both evaluation paths, we employ the same classifier
architecture to ensure fairness: a linear mapping layer for di-
mensionality alignment, followed by a Transformer Encoder to
model contextual dependencies and a feed-forward layer for fi-
nal classification.
To enable fair and consistent evaluation, we adopt stan-
dardized metrics for both classification and reconstruction qual-
ity. For classification, we report Accuracy (Acc) and F1-Score
(F1). For signal reconstruction quality, we utilize Perceptual
Evaluation of Universal Speech Quality Model Optimized for
MOS (UTMOS) [16], Perceptual Evaluation of Speech Quality
(PESQ) [17], and Short-Time Objective Intelligibility (STOI)
[18], providing a comprehensive measure of the fidelity of
codec outputs. We encourage future studies to follow these stan-
dards to ensure fair and reproducible comparisons.

2.2. Neural Audio Codecs
We evaluate several classical codecs at extremely low bitrates
(around 1 kbps) on bio-acoustic health datasets. The se-
lected models span multiple design paradigms: multi-codebook
codecs (DAC, EnCodec), single-codebook codecs (WavTok-
enizer, BigCodec), decoupling-based codecs (SpeechTokenizer,
FACodec), and semantic-based codecs (UniCodec, Semanti-
Codec). Detailed information about these codecs is provided
in Table. 1.
DAC [3]is a high-fidelity universal neural audio compres-
sion model based on an improved RVQ, along with adversarial
and reconstruction losses, and codebook utilization strategies,
which achieve remarkable compression rates while maintaining
audio quality across various types of audio data.
EnCodec [4]has become a widely used baseline due to its
robust performance across speech and music domains. As a
multi-RVQ codec, EnCodec emphasizes accurate waveform re-
construction and has inspired numerous follow-up models.
WavTokenizer [5]is designed for extreme compression ef-
ficiency, reducing a second audio into only 75 tokens through a
single quantizer. It uses an expanded vector quantization space,
extended temporal context windows, and an attention-based de-
coder to improve reconstruction quality.
BigCodec [19]achieves substantial advancements in low-
bitrate speech coding by effectively scaling up model size and
optimizing key components such as vector quantization and
temporal dependency modeling.
SpeechTokenizer [20]disentangles semantic and acoustic
aspects of speech by RVQ. It leverages self-supervised learning
(SSL) based speech representation models to distill semantic in-
formation into the first-layer codebook. The subsequent code-
books are devoted to capturing acoustic details. This decompo-
sition enables specialized tokenization that separates linguistic
content from acoustic variation.
FACodec [21]is a factorized neural speech codec that can
convert complex speech waveforms into disentangled subspaces
representing speech attributes of content, prosody, timbre, and
acoustic details. It also reconstructs high-quality speech wave-
form from these speech attributes.

SemantiCodec [6]is a novel dual-encoder architecture that
integrates both semantic and acoustic representations. The se-
mantic encoder leverages a self-supervised pre-trained Audio
Masked Autoencoder, while the acoustic encoder captures the
residual details needed for faithful audio reconstruction.
2.3. Datasets and Tasks
To evaluate codec performance across a range of health-related
acoustic signals, we use five diverse datasets. A detailed sum-
mary of each dataset is provided in Table. 2.
Snoring [23]dataset contains 500 snoring samples and 500
non-snoring samples. Since no official split is provided, we di-
vide the dataset into training and testing sets with an 80:20 ratio.
HeartSound [24]dataset, acquired using an electronic
stethoscope, consists of one normal and four abnormal cate-
gories which respectively are aortic stenosis, mitral regurgita-
tion, mitral stenosis, and murmur occurring in the systole inter-
val. Following [24], we use an 80:20 train–test split.
ICBHI [25]dataset consists of 920 lung sound recordings
from 126 participants, covering diseases such as asthma, pneu-
monia, bronchitis, bronchiectasis, COPD, and respiratory infec-
tions. It is officially split into 539 training and 381 test samples,
respectively.
MSTI [26]dataset contains 6,661 samples from 25 com-
mon medical categories, such as emotional distress, stomach
ache, knee pain, and feeling cold. It is officially divided into
15,570 training, 1,860 validation, and 3,594 test samples, re-
spectively.
V ocalSound [27]dataset consists of 21,024 recordings of
laughter, sighs, coughs, throat clearing, sneezes, and sniffs. The
V ocalSound dataset is officially split into training, validation
and test sets with 15,570, 1,860, and 3,594 samples, respec-
tively.
3. Experiments and Results
In this section, we present the experimental setup and analyze
the benchmark results from multiple perspectives.
3.1. Experimental Setup
To ensure fair comparison, we train all models under consistent
conditions. For both classifiers, we use the AdamW optimizer
with an initial learning rate of 5e-4. The batch size is configured
to 32, and the number of epochs is set to 50.
3.2. Benchmark Results
We benchmark eight representative codecs on five bio-acoustic
health datasets. Each codec is evaluated in three domains: orig-
inal, compressed, and reconstructed domain for downstream
tasks, and further assessed for reconstructed audio quality us-
ing signal-level metrics in Table. 3 and Table. 4.
Compressed domain:Across datasets, the compressed do-
main performance is always lower than that of the original do-
main. We attribute this gap to an objective mismatch: audio
codecs are mainly optimized for perceptual fidelity. The origi-
nal audio is processed with the pretrained HuBERT model [15],
a self-supervised architecture designed to learn semantic repre-
sentations by training on a large corpus. While the compressed
audio representations are directly extracted from codec, which
prioritizes audio reconstruction rather than semantic informa-
tion. The quantization bottleneck may also discard diagnostic
cues. Notably, designs that inject semantic (e.g., SpeechTok-
enizer, FACodec) narrow the gap, showing the benefit of se-
mantically guided quantizers in the compressed domain.
Reconstructed domain:Compared with the original do-
main baseline, reconstructed audio performance shows a mod-
est decline in downstream tasks, implying that the codec
compression-reconstruction pipeline not only discards fine
acoustic details, but also partially suppresses task-relevant se-
mantic information.

codecs, SpeechTokenizer and FACodec, both based on de-

SpeechTokenizer excels in task preservation by leveraging a
HuBERT-guided first-layer codebook that distills semantic in-
formation, consistent with prior work showing HuBERT’s ef-
fectiveness in capturing bio-acoustic cues [14]. FACodec also
performs strongly, benefiting from its factorized VQ design,
which explicitly separates content from other attributes, and
from its large parameter scale that enables fine-grained repre-
sentation learning. In contrast, UniCodec and SemantiCodec
incorporate semantic cues but generalize less effectively, likely
due to limited exposure to bio-acoustic health signals during
training. These findings highlight the need for codecs with
stronger adaptability to bio-acoustic and other non-speech do-
mains.
Trade-off between semantic preservation and recon-
struction:Our experimental results reveal that none of the eval-
uated codecs can simultaneously achieve strong reconstruction
quality and preserve task-relevant semantic information. At
comparable bitrates, DAC and EnCodec achieve high recon-
struction fidelity, but perform poorly on downstream tasks due
to the lack of explicit semantic modeling. In contrast, Speech-
Tokenizer attains lower reconstruction scores while achieving
high accuracy on downstream tasks. To maintain a comparable
bitrate of around 1 kbps, SpeechTokenizer is evaluated using
only three codebooks: the first one dedicated to extracting se-
mantic information and the other two designed to capture acous-
tic details. However, relying on only two acoustic codebooks is
insufficient to achieve strong reconstruction quality. This re-
veals that superior reconstruction quality does not always lead
to better downstream performance. Reconstruction quality re-
mains important, but it alone is insufficient to guarantee clas-
sification success. This observation highlights a critical chal-
lenge for future audio codec development: designing codecs
that jointly optimize perceptual fidelity and semantic preserva-
tion, particularly for bio-acoustic and healthcare applications.
3.3. Impact of the Number of Codebooks
As shown in Fig. 2, we evaluate DAC, EnCodec, and Speech-
Tokenizer to study the impact of the number of codebooks. We
do not vary this for BigCodec, UniCodec, and WavTokenizer
because they are single-codebook designs, nor for Semanti-
Codec and FACodec, whose codebooks operate in parallel.
Increasing codebook depth reduces information loss and im-
proves reconstruction fidelity, but yields limited gains in down-
stream performance. This may reflect the brevity of the Snor-
ing clips (1 second on average) and the lower inherent quality
of stethoscope-acquired recordings in HeartSound and ICBHI,
which constrains the benefit of increasing the number of code-
books. SpeechTokenizer mainly extracts semantic information
in the first codebook, so increasing the number of codebooks
has little effect on downstream tasks performance across all
datasets. Overall, our results demonstrate that although deeper
codebooks generally improve reconstruction fidelity, their ben-
efits for downstream tasks remain highly dependent on both
dataset characteristics and codec architecture.
4. Conclusion
In this work, we introduce BACH, a unified benchmark for eval-
uating audio codecs on bio-acoustic health tasks. BACH covers
five bio-acoustic health datasets and eight representative audio
codecs, with a standardized pipeline to ensure fair and repro-
ducible comparison. Our results demonstrate that the evaluated
codecs in the bio-acoustic health domain struggle to balance
reconstruction fidelity with downstream classification perfor-
mance, revealing a fundamental trade-off between perceptual
fidelity and semantic preservation. We hope that future codecs
can be effectively applied to medical applications. Future work
will extend BACH to more bio-acoustic health tasks to investi-
gate codec robustness in real-world scenarios.
