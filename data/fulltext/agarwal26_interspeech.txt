Grounding Whisper: Audio Anchor-Based Approach for Hallucination
Mitigation and Throughput-Efficient ASR
Prateek Agarwal1, Saurabh Kumar1, Priyanka Bhatt1
1 Walmart Global Tech
prateek.agarwal@walmart.com,saurabh.kumar1@walmart.com, priyanka.bhatt@walmart.com
Abstract
Whisper exhibits robust ASR performance but suffers from
hallucinations on silence and non-speech audio, generating spu-
rious transcriptions that undermine production reliability. We
introduceanchor audio, a simple input-level technique that
prepends a short audio phrase with near-zero domain occur-
rence to the input, enabling hallucination detection without
modifying or fine-tuning the model. Using this mechanism,
we evaluate five inference approaches ranging from single-call
augmentation to batch concatenation with automatic fallback,
exploring accuracy–latency–throughput trade-offs. Our batch
strategy leverages Whisper’s fixed 30-second window to con-
catenate multiple short utterances while maintaining fidelity
through anchor-based validation. This reduces overall WER
from 32.18% to 13.23%, achieves 0.14% Hallucination Error
Rate on non-speech audio, and preserves latency comparable to
baseline V AD pipelines, demonstrating an effective, practical
solution for hallucination mitigation.
Index Terms: speech recognition, hallucination mitigation, an-
chor audio, ASR, Whisper
1. Introduction
Whisper [1] achieves strong automatic speech recognition
(ASR) performance across diverse domains due to large-scale
training on 680k hours of speech data. While variants exist
[2, 3, 4, 5], whisper-turbo-large-v3 offers optimal performance-
latency trade-offs. Despite this robustness, Whisper exhibits
a persistent failure mode: hallucinations [6] on silence and
non-speech audio, where the model produces unwarranted, ran-
dom transcriptions. These hallucinations degrade accuracy and
hinder deployment in real-time systems. Previous strategies -
V AD filtering, confidence thresholds, token suppression, or ar-
chitectural changes - reduce but do not effectively mitigate these
errors [7, 8, 9, 10, 11].
In this work, we introduce a simple, input-level technique
that enables systematic hallucination detection without modify-
ing or fine-tuning Whisper. The key idea is to prepend a short,
phonetically clearanchor audioto all inputs. The text of this
audio acts as a lightweight ground-truth marker: when Whisper
correctly recognizes it, subsequent transcription can be trusted
to be correct.
Beyond hallucination mitigation, anchor audio enables safe
concatenation of multiple short utterances within Whisper’s
fixed 30-second window. Short conversational turns underuti-
lize this window, leading to compute wastage. By inserting an-
chor phrases as delimiters between segments, we reliably split
Whisper’s output back into individual transcripts, unlocking
batching efficiency without sacrificing correctness. We evaluate
multiple concatenation strategies under varied load conditions
using a GPU inference framework.
Our contributions are:(1) We introduce anchor audio as
a light-weight, dual-purpose mechanism for both hallucination
suppression and throughput optimization using anchors as de-
limiters to safely concatenate multiple short utterances within
Whisper’s 30-second window. (2) We analyze accuracy and la-
tency under real-world workloads, showing that anchor audio
mitigates hallucinations while maintaining low latency.
2. Related Work
2.1. Streaming ASR and Throughput Optimization
Whisper excels in offline transcription, but its fixed 30-second
chunk design conflicts with real-time requirements. Several
streaming approaches have emerged [12, 13, 14], along with
optimization strategies using CTranslate2 or TensorRT, quanti-
zation and knowledge distillation [2].
These methods reduce per-utterance latency but do not ad-
dress the throughput loss caused by Whisper’s fixed window,
which is underutilized in conversational ASR. Our work com-
plements these efforts by enabling safe batch concatenation of
multiple audio segments using anchor audio as a delimiter, im-
proving throughput without modifying the underlying model.
2.2. Hallucination in Large ASR Models
Whisper is known to hallucinate text on silence and environ-
mental noise, studied in multiple papers [15, 16, 17, 18, 19]. [7]
characterised the patterns in Whisper, while [8] attributed it to
Whisper’s training on weakly-labelled data. Careless-whisper
[9] examined its technical and moral ramifications.
Although prompting Whisper model has been explored for
domain adaptation and improving performance [20, 21, 22, 23,
24, 25], it has not been studied as a method of hallucination
reduction. We perform an ablation study to quantify its impact.
V oice Activity Detection [26, 27] serves as a fundamental
pre-processing step in ASR pipelines, which is used in Whis-
perx [10] to reduce hallucination. Other strategies apply post-
processing error correction [28], but fail when Whisper assigns
high confidence scores to hallucinated output. Calm Whis-
per [11] proposed fine-tuning specific attention heads to reduce
hallucinations, but requires model modification, making direct
comparison without reproducing their procedure infeasible.
Our technique complements rather than replaces V AD:
V AD provides coarse filtering, while anchor audio enables fine-
grained hallucination detection for segments that pass V AD
screening.

3.2. Anchor Audio Generation and Design
The choice of anchor phrase is critical for robust separation. We
filtered candidates based on three criteria: (1)Acoustic Dis-
tinctivenessfor clear recognition; (2)Domain Orthogonality
(near-zero natural occurrence) to prevent false splitting; and (3)
Token Efficiencyto minimize latency. We rejected common
prompts (e.g., “Start”, “hello”) due to frequent collision with
input audios.
We compare two empirically selected candidates:
•“Mongolia”: Chosen for phonetic robustness (strong nasals
/m/, /n/ and plosive /g/) and token efficiency (1–2 tokens). As
a proper noun semantically distant from our retail domain, it
minimizes false positives.
•“The present user said”: A multi-word phrase optimized
for semantic uniqueness, acting as a highly specific delimiter
despite a larger token footprint.
Anchor audios were synthesized via TTS (male/female) and
validated across three amplitude levels (1.0, 0.5, 0.25) to ensure
the anchor is detected without overpowering the primary audio
signal.
• We fixed the silence delimiterδat 2.0 seconds, empirically
determined to be sufficient for the model’s attention mecha-
nism to reset between the anchor and the target speech with-
out incurring excessive latency.
• We exclusively employ prefix-based anchoring: by success-
fully decoding the anchor, the autoregressive decoder is con-
ditioned on producing faithful transcriptions before encoun-
tering the target audio. Without an anchor, silence or noise
at the input onset can trigger self-reinforcing hallucination
loops; the anchor provides grounding tokens that constrain
subsequent generation towards the actual acoustic content.
3.3. Inference Approaches
We describe five inference approaches for integrating anchor
audio into the transcription pipeline, as shown in Figure 3.
A1: Vanilla Whisper:Pre-V AD baseline; A2–A5 all use Silero
V AD as a pre-processing step.
A2: Only V AD:Uses V AD to filter non-speech segments before
they reach Whisper, processing only the detected speech por-
tions, a common production practice to reduce hallucinations.
A3: V AD with Anchor.We define an acoustic anchorA(e.g.,
“Mongolia”) and a silence bufferδ. For an input utteranceU,
we construct the augmented inputX ′ = [A,δ,U]. The model
generates a raw transcriptY. We attempt to decomposeYinto
a prefixpand contentcsuch thatY=p+c, wherepmatches
the anchor text. If the match succeeds, the final transcription is
ˆT=c. If the anchor text is absent (match fails) - indicating
potential speech overlap masking the anchor - we fallback to
ˆT=Yto prevent data loss, accepting the risk of hallucination
in favour of recall.
A4: Naive Batching:To maximize throughput, we exploit
Whisper’s capacity to process 30-second contexts by physically
concatenating multiple discrete short inputs (U 1,...,U n) into
a single audio buffer. A 2.0s silence (δ) follows each utterance;
with the anchor, the inter-utterance delimiter is (δ+A+δ), giv-
ingU batch = [A,δ,U 1,δ,A,δ,U 2,...]. Post-inference, we re-
construct individual transcripts by splitting the output string us-
ing the anchor text as a delimiter. This approach assumes a strict
1:1 mapping where thek-th text segment corresponds to audio
Uk. While highly efficient, it is “naive” because it lacks error
recovery: if the model fails to transcribe an intermediate anchor
(e.g., due to noise overlap), the delimiter count mismatches the
input count (N out ̸=N in), causing transcript misalignment or
merging.
A5: Batching with Fallback:To address the alignment failures
of Approach 4, we introduce a validation logic based on token
cardinality. LetNbe the number of inputs in the constructed
batch. We perform inference to obtainY batch and count the
occurrences of the anchor text phrase to findk. Ifk=N,
the mapping is unambiguous, and we assign the resulting split
segmentss 1...s N to inputsU 1...U N . Ifk̸=N(indicat-
ing a missing or hallucinated delimiter), the batch is flagged as
invalid. In this failure case, the system discardsY batch and au-
tomatically falls back to processing each constituent inputU i
individually using Approach 3. This hybrid strategy maintains
the high throughput of batching for the majority of cases while
guaranteeing the robustness of single-call augmentation when
complex audio compromises batch structural integrity.

4. Experimental Setup
4.1. Data
To ensure relevance to real-time conversational ASR use cases,
we filter all datasets to include only segments with duration be-
tween 1-5 seconds, resulting in 33k test audio samples.
Primary Evaluation Dataset:A privately licensed retail
customer-service speech corpus with anonymized data.
Non-Speech Audio Source:We use UrbanSound8K [29]
for non-speech evaluation, excluding the “children playing” cat-
egory to avoid segments containing human vocalisations (6,614
clips retained). Ground truth is set to empty; any non-empty
output—including sound-event descriptions (e.g., “*music*”,
“*crickets*”)—is counted as a hallucination. Figure 1 shows
the most common hallucinated outputs on this data.
Other Open-source Datasets:AMI meeting corpus and
the LibriSpeech datasets [30] enable standardized baselines to
assess the transcription accuracy of our approach.

A1 (Only Whisper):Vanilla Whisper suffers catastrophic
hallucinations (72.2% HER on Urban8k), demonstrating the ne-
cessity of mitigation. Performance on genuine speech datasets
(Retail: 16.4%, AMI: 21.66%) acts as our baseline.
A2 (Only V AD):V AD reduces overall WER to 16.67%,
driven by hallucination mitigation on Urban8k (18.19% HER

(Retail: 16.52%, AMI: 22.1%), probably because of classifying

A3 (V AD with Anchor):Prepending anchor audio to every
segment provides a decisive breakthrough. Hallucinations on
Urban8k are virtually eliminated (0.12% HER). Surprisingly,
speech performance also improves WER (Retail: 16.1%, AMI:
21.86%). Latency (P95: 579ms) remains comparable to the
V AD baseline (571ms), indicating no computational penalty.
A4 (Naive Batching):Achieves latency reduction (P95:
487ms) by processing multiple segments per call. However, it
suffers a catastrophic failure rate of 8.45% when the expected
anchor count is not found during transcript splitting, making it
unusable in production (AMI WER: 33.65%).
A5 (Batch with Fallback):Maintains the accuracy of A3
(overall WER: 13.23%, Urban8k: 0.38%) with zero failures.
Latency is comparable to A3 but higher than A4 due to fallback
re-processing; however, the batching design enables higher
throughput when validation succeeds. This graceful degrada-
tion provides efficiency gains without sacrificing reliability.
Ablation Study.To verify if text conditioning could mit-
igate hallucinations, we evaluated a text-only baseline using a
promptinitial prompt=”Transcript:” first only on vanilla
Whisper, then using it in conjunction with V AD. While this re-

6. Conclusion
We demonstrated that anchor audio achieves systematic hallu-
cination mitigation in Whisper. Vanilla Whisper suffers from
95.16% WER on environmental audio (Urban8k), with V AD
improving it to 16.58%. Our anchor audio approach virtually
eliminates hallucinations to 0.38% with no latency overhead.
While prepending audio adds a theoretical compute cost,
the observed latency impact is negligible due to GPU paral-
lelization efficiency. A3 (V AD with Anchor) suits latency-
critical applications (13.21% WER, P95: 580ms); A5 (Batch
with Fallback) suits throughput-critical services, maintaining
accuracy (13.23% WER) while enabling batch processing when
validation succeeds. The dual-purpose design provides both
hallucination detection and safe concatenation through a single
input-level technique requiring no model modification, making
this a viable strategy for real-time systems.
7. Limitations
While anchor audio effectively mitigates hallucinations, several
limitations apply. Anchor phrase selection requires domain-
specific tuning; phrases optimal for retail may not generalise to
other domains. Batch concatenation benefits depend on utter-
ance length distribution, with diminishing returns for utterances
exceeding 10 seconds. Although the technique operates purely
at the input level and is applicable in principle to any encoder-
decoder ASR model, our evaluation is limited to Whisper; vali-
dating across architectures remains future work. Similarly, our
evaluation focused on English; multilingual deployment may
require additional validation.
