Abstract
Automatic dysarthria severity assessment is crucial for clini-
cal monitoring yet hindered by data scarcity and pronounced
speech heterogeneity. While self-supervised models such as
WavLM provide strong representations, adapting them to low-
resource medical domains remains challenging. We propose a
Clinically-Supervised Hierarchical LoRA-MoE framework that
freezes the WavLM backbone and introduces shared LoRA
adapters in lower layers for general acoustic modeling, together
with dynamically routed expert LoRAs in deeper layers for
severity-specific patterns. A clinically-supervised router se-
lectively activates expert parameters conditioned on utterance
characteristics. Experiments on the UA-Speech corpus in 2-
and 5-class settings demonstrate consistent improvements over
full fine-tuning, achieving F1 scores of 94.54% and 61.55%, re-
spectively.
Index Terms: Dysarthria severity prediction, Hierarchical ar-

1. Introduction
Dysarthria is a motor speech disorder caused by neurological
damage, such as stroke or Parkinson’s disease, leading to im-
paired articulation, abnormal prosody, and reduced speech intel-
ligibility [1, 2, 3]. Accurate severity assessment is essential for
clinical rehabilitation, therapy planning, and longitudinal mon-
itoring. An automatic severity classification system can further
serve as a front-end module to enhance the robustness of down-
stream speech technologies for atypical speech [4, 5].
However, automatic dysarthria assessment remains chal-
lenging due to the scarcity of labeled pathological speech data
and the substantial inter- and intra-speaker variability inherent
in motor speech disorders [6, 7]. Early approaches relied on
handcrafted acoustic features or conventional deep neural net-
works trained from scratch [8, 9]. While these methods estab-
lished foundational baselines, their generalization ability is lim-
ited under small-scale datasets and highly heterogeneous acous-
tic conditions [10].
Self-supervised learning (SSL) foundation models, such as
HuBERT [11] and wav2vec 2.0 [12], have demonstrated pow-
erful representation learning capabilities by leveraging large-
scale unlabeled speech corpora. Nevertheless, directly fine-
tuning large SSL models on low-resource pathological datasets

is prone to overfitting and catastrophic forgetting, and often in-
curs substantial computational cost [13].
Parameter-efficient fine-tuning (PEFT) methods provide a
practical alternative by updating only a small subset of model
parameters [14, 15]. Among them, Low-Rank Adaptation
(LoRA) [16] has shown strong adaptation capacity. How-
ever, standard LoRA applies a uniform low-rank transforma-
tion across all inputs, which may be insufficient to model the
pronounced acoustic heterogeneity across different dysarthria
severity levels [17].
To address this limitation, we introduce a Clinically-
Supervised Hierarchical LoRA-MoE framework based on
WavLM [18], integrating Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) [19] rout-
ing into LoRA modules. Leveraging annotated clinical severity
features, the framework enables input-conditioned expert acti-
vation for severity-aware adaptation. Specifically, we adopt a
hierarchical design where standard LoRA is applied in lower
layers to preserve general acoustic representations, while MoE-
enhanced LoRA is deployed in deeper layers to capture fine-
grained, severity-dependent characteristics.
We conduct extensive evaluations under both 2- and 5-class
severity classification settings, demonstrating that the proposed
framework achieves state-of-the-art performance with minimal
parameter updates while maintaining computational efficiency.
The main contributions are summarized as follows:
• We propose a hierarchical MoE-enhanced LoRA framework
for parameter-efficient adaptation of SSL speech models to
dysarthria speech assessment.
• We introduce severity-aware, input-conditioned expert rout-
ing to address the acoustic heterogeneity of dysarthric
speech.
• We demonstrate strong performance across multiple clinical
granularities with substantially reduced trainable parameters.
2. Methods
We present the Clinically-Supervised Hierarchical LoRA-MoE
framework for dysarthric speech detection. The design ad-
dresses two key challenges: scarce training data and substan-
tial acoustic heterogeneity across severity levels. As shown
in Figure 1, the framework builds on a frozen WavLM self-
supervised speech backbone and introduces a hierarchical,
input-conditioned routing mechanism that selectively activates
lightweight adaptation modules.

2.1. Model Architecture
The proposed architecture separates general acoustic feature ex-
traction from severity-specific pathological modeling. Given an
input speech waveform, a trainable CNN feature extractor is
fine-tuned to capture the domain-specific low-level acoustic ar-
tifacts of dysarthria. These representations are then fed into a
multi-layer Transformer encoder. While the CNN front-end is
updated, the backbone parameters throughout all Transformer
layers remain strictly frozen; adaptation is achieved by injecting
LoRA modules into both projection and feed-forward matrices.
The network is logically divided into two stages. The bot-
tom layers form a shared representation extractor that captures
general acoustic patterns common to both healthy and patholog-
ical speech. The top layers form a MoE ensemble of severity-
specific adaptation modules. A clinically-supervised router dy-
namically determines which expert module should be activated
for a given input utterance based on its severity-related charac-
teristics.

2.6. Robust Feature Augmentation with Temporal Variabil-
ity
Given the limited size of pathological speech datasets, prevent-
ing overfitting is essential. In addition to conventional stochas-
tic acoustic perturbations such as noise injection and time-scale
modification, we introduce temporally structured augmenta-
tions that simulate pathological characteristics.
Specifically, randomly selected temporal segments are
masked during training to mimic articulatory interruptions and
irregular pauses commonly observed in dysarthric speech. This
encourages the temporal attention pooling mechanism to rely on
broader contextual information rather than overfitting to specific
local artifacts.
3. Experiments
3.1. Dataset
We used the UA-Speech [23] corpus, a standard English dataset
for dysarthric speech research. The corpus contains recordings
from 15 speakers with dysarthria and 13 age-matched healthy
controls. Each participant produced three blocks of 255 iso-
lated words (765 utterances per subject), consisting of repeated
common words (digits, letters, command words, and common
Brown-Corpus words) and unique uncommon words, yielding
455 distinct lexical items in total. Recordings were captured
with a seven-channel microphone array and transcribed by five
native American English speakers.
Following standard clinical protocols, speaker intelligibil-
ity was determined through transcriptions provided by medical
professionals. The assessments were mapped to four severity
levels: very low (0-25%), low (26-50%), medium (51-75%),
and high (76-100%), forming the clinical ground truth for our
framework. Table 1 details the distribution. The dataset exhibits
class imbalance–notably, the low and medium severity groups
contain only three speakers each, so we report macro-averaged
accuracy and macro F1 to ensure fair evaluation across classes.

To evaluate generalization to unseen speakers, we follow
a speaker-independent (SID) protocol in which test speakers
are strictly disjoint from the training set. For UA-Speech, we
adopt a One-Speaker-per-Severity (OSPS) configuration [24].
The OSPS configuration can assess the model’s generalization
to unseen speakers while ensuring that all severity levels are
represented in both the training and test sets. In this setting,
each severity level in the test set is represented by a distinct
speaker, and no speaker included in the test set appears in the
training set. This design guarantees strict speaker independence
while maintaining balanced severity coverage across splits.
Following the OSPS configuration, we performed five ran-
dom splits, resulting in five different training and test sets. In
each split, 11 speakers with dysarthria and 12 healthy controls
were used for training, while 4 speakers with dysarthria and 1
healthy control were reserved for testing. The speaker identi-
ties of the test sets for all five splits are listed in Table S1 of the
supplementary file testsplits.pdf.
3.2. Experimental Settings
Model Configuration.Our framework is built upon the pre-
trained WavLM-Large model 1, which consists of 24 Trans-
former layers with a hidden dimension of 1024. To ensure
parameter efficiency, all backbone parameters remain frozen
throughout training except CNN encoder. LoRA modules are
injected into the query, key, value, and output projection ma-
trices, as well as the two feed-forward sublayers within each
Transformer block. The LoRA rank is set tor= 16, with a
scaling factorα= 32and a dropout rate of 0.3.
The hierarchical routing mechanism divides the Trans-
former into a shared bottom and an expert top. Specifically,
the lowerNlayers employ a shared LoRA adapter, while the
upperL−Nlayers are equipped with a MoE configuration
consisting of five expert-specific LoRA modules. Through ab-
lation analysis, we determineN= 9to yield the best valida-
tion performance. The Clinically-Supervised Router is inserted
after theN-th layer and applies temporal self-attention pool-
ing to aggregate the1024-dimensional hidden representations
into a256-dimensional intermediate embedding before produc-
ing routing probabilities over experts.
Implementation Details.All recordings from the UA-
Speech corpus are resampled to 16 kHz and truncated or zero-
padded to a maximum duration of 10.0 seconds. To mitigate
overfitting under limited data conditions, we adopt a double-
randomness augmentation strategy during training. With a
probability of 30%, each utterance undergoes stochastic acous-
tic perturbations, including Gaussian noise injection withµ= 0
andσ= 0.005, time stretching with rates of 0.8 or 1.2,
and pitch shifting by four semitones. Independently, tempo-
ral masking is applied with a probability of 50%, where a ran-
domly selected contiguous segment between 0.05 s and 0.8 s
is replaced with silence. This augmentation simulates articula-
tory interruptions and irregular pauses commonly observed in
dysarthric speech.
The model is optimized using AdamW with a weight decay
of0.01. Training is conducted for20epochs with a batch size
of32. A linear learning rate schedule is adopted, peaking at
4×10 −5 after2,000warm-up steps. For the multi-task objec-
tive, the weighting coefficients are set toλ 1 = 0.3for router
supervision andλ 2 = 0.1for load balancing.
To address class imbalance across severity levels, class
weights are computed using a balanced heuristic. Additional
penalty multipliers of 1.7 and 1.5 are applied to the medium and
severe categories, respectively, together with a label smoothing
factor of 0.1. All experiments are conducted using six NVIDIA
RTX 3090 GPUs.
Evaluation Metrics.Given the substantial class imbalance
in pathological speech severity classification, overall accuracy
alone may not adequately reflect model performance. We there-
fore adopt Macro-Averaged F1 score as the primary evaluation
metric to ensure equal weighting across severity levels. In addi-
tion to Macro F1, we report overall accuracy, macro precision,
and macro recall. For the binary detection task distinguishing
healthy and dysarthric speech, we further report the Area Under
the Receiver Operating Characteristic Curve. All results are re-
ported under the strict OSPS cross-validation protocol to reflect
realistic clinical generalization performance.

3.3. Results and Analysis
3.3.1. Main Results
Table 2 reports the performance comparison between full fine-
tuning, standard LoRA adaptation, and the proposed Clinically-
Supervised Hierarchical LoRA-MoE framework on both the 5-
class dysarthria classification task (healthy + 4 severity bins)
and the 2-class dysarthric speech detection task.
Comparison with Full Fine-Tuning and Standard
LoRA.As shown in Table 2, standard LoRA consistently un-
derperforms full fine-tuning across both SSL backbones. For
example, on the 5-class task with WavLM, standard LoRA
achieves a macro F1-score of 55.02% and an accuracy of
58.49%, compared to 57.94% and 62.30% obtained by full fine-
tuning. A similar trend is observed with HuBERT.
This performance gap suggests that a uniform LoRA
applied across all layers may be insufficient to model the
pronounced acoustic heterogeneity associated with different
dysarthria severity levels. While LoRA substantially reduces
the number of trainable parameters, its shared low-rank struc-
ture appears to limit representational flexibility under fine-
grained pathological variability.
Effectiveness of the Hierarchical LoRA-MoE Frame-
work.By incorporating severity-aware expert routing, the pro-
posed LoRA-MoE framework not only mitigates the perfor-
mance degradation introduced by standard LoRA but also sur-
passes full fine-tuning.
On the 5-class classification task, the WavLM-based LoRA-
MoE achieves 64.32% accuracy and a macro F1-score of
61.55%, outperforming full fine-tuning by 2.02% and 3.61%,
respectively. With the HuBERT as pre-trained model, LoRA-
MoE increases the macro F1-score by 4.31% compared to full
fine-tuning.
Consistent improvements are also observed on the binary
detection task. The WavLM-based LoRA-MoE model attains
95.14% accuracy, 96.59% AUC, and 94.54% F1-score. These
results indicate that dynamic routing of utterances to severity-
specific expert adapters enhances modeling capacity without
increasing the number of active parameters during inference.
Overall, the consistent gains across both backbones and tasks
demonstrate the benefit of combining hierarchical feature de-
composition with expert specialization in parameter-efficient
adaptation settings.

ing approximately 9–12 layers to the shared bottom yields the
most effective balance between generalized acoustic represen-
tation and expert-level specialization. Based on validation per-
formance, we selectN= 9as the final configuration for all
subsequent experiments.
4. Conclusion
We proposed the Clinically-Supervised Hierarchical LoRA-
MoE, a parameter-efficient framework for dysarthric speech as-
sessment. By combining shared LoRA in lower layers with dy-
namically routed MoE-LoRA in upper layers of a WavLM back-
bone, our method effectively disentangles general phonetic fea-
tures from severity-specific distortions. Evaluations on the UA-
Speech corpus show our architecture significantly outperforms
full fine-tuning and standard PEFT, achieving Macro-F1 scores
of 94.54% for binary detection and 61.55% for 5-class severity
classification. These results highlight the critical advantage of
incorporating clinical priors into expert routing. Future work
will explore continuous severity scoring and multimodal inte-
gration to further enhance diagnostic robustness.
