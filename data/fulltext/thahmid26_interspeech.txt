Layer-wise Probing of Whisper’s Encoder Representations for Bengali
Phone-like Units
Munim Thahmid1, Sadia Sharmin1
1 Department of Computer Science & Engineering, Bangladesh University of Engineering and
Technology (BUET), Bangladesh
munimthahmid2@gmail.com, sadiasharmin@cse.buet.ac.bd
Abstract
We investigate where Bengali phone-like units become lin-
early separable across the encoder layers of OpenAI’s Whisper.
Phone-like segments are obtained via MMS forced alignment
on uromanized transcripts, and lightweight linear probes are
trained per layer under speaker-disjoint evaluation. Whisper-
small and Whisper-medium both peak in the mid-to-late layers,
whereas Whisper-large-v3 forms a broad late-layer plateau with
markedly less final-layer degradation. A self-supervised base-
line (wav2vec2-XLSR) shows a far steeper late-layer decline,
suggesting that supervised ASR training preserves phonetic de-
tail deeper into the encoder. Per-class F1 analysis reveals that
aspirated stops and affricates are separable from early layers,
while nasals and sibilants drive mid-layer gains. We further
validate these findings with ABX discriminability, alignment-
confidence filtering, duration stratification, and an English MFA
cross-lingual baseline.
Index Terms: Whisper, probing, Bengali, forced alignment,
interpretability
1. Introduction
Multilingual speech encoders are increasingly adopted as
general-purpose feature extractors, yet the distribution of pho-
netic information across their layers remains poorly understood
for most languages. Prior probing studies have demonstrated
that mid-to-late layers of self-supervised encoders are most in-
formative for phonetic tasks in English [1], but relatively little
work extends this analysis to supervised ASR encoders or to
South Asian languages.
We address this gap for Bengali, the seventh most spoken
language globally and one with limited interpretability research
on modern speech models. Our target encoder is Whisper [2], a
supervised multilingual encoder-decoder, and our central ques-
tion is:at which encoder layers are Bengali phone-like units
most linearly separable, and how does this profile change with
model scale?
Phone-level supervision is derived from the MMS forced
aligner [ 3] applied to uromanized transcripts. We train
lightweight probes on frozen encoder representations under
speaker-disjoint evaluation—a more stringent protocol than
utterance-level splits. Our contributions are:
1. A reproducible probing pipeline for Bengali Whisper en-
coders with speaker-disjoint evaluation.
2. A scaling analysis across Whisper-small (12 layers), -medium
(24), and -large-v3 (32) showing that phone-like unit separa-
bility peaks in mid-to-late layers, with late-layer degradation
decreasing as model size grows.
3. A self-supervised baseline (wav2vec2-XLSR [4]) and an En-
glish cross-lingual baseline (MFA [5] on LibriSpeech [6]).
4. Per-class F1 analysis revealing class-specific depth profiles,
duration-stratified probing, and ABX discriminability on
Bengali-relevant contrasts (aspiration, retroflexion).
5. Extensive robustness controls: alignment-confidence filtering,
label-shuffle baselines, text-disjoint splits, and probe-recipe
ablations.
2. Related Work
Layer-wise probing has been applied extensively to speech en-
coders. Pasad et al. [ 1] established that phonetic information
concentrates in mid layers of wav2vec 2.0 and HuBERT, with
a decline toward upper layers. Subsequent work has probed
Finnish wav2vec2 [7], explored cross-layer agreement in mul-
tilingual SSL representations [8], and investigated probing for
accent identification [9] and speech emotion recognition [10].
Whisper [ 2] has primarily been studied for ASR perfor-
mance, including on Bangla [11], but layer-wise phonetic prob-
ing on Bengali remains unexplored. Recent work probes Whis-
per’s intermediate representations for clinical tasks such as
dysarthria detection [ 12], demonstrates late-layer specializa-
tion under LoRA adaptation for emotion recognition [13], and
adapts Whisper representations toward psychological objec-
tives [14]. We additionally contextualize Whisper against self-
supervised encoders, using wav2vec2-XLSR [4] as a direct base-
line and referencing published findings on HuBERT [ 15] and
wav2vec 2.0 [16]. For phonetic supervision, we use MMS [3] for
Bengali and the Montreal Forced Aligner (MFA) [5] for English.
Forced-alignment quality directly affects the probing targets.
HMM-based systems such as MFA can outperform CTC-based
methods in boundary accuracy [ 17], while CTC aligners may
produce overly “peaky” outputs that label-prior methods can im-
prove [18]. Self-supervised approaches such as TIPAA-SSL [19]
provide text-independent alignment, and multilingual MFA ex-
tends precise alignment to related low-resource languages [20].
We therefore treat alignment as a practical proxy and explicitly
evaluate robustness to its noise (Section 5).

3.2. Forced alignment and phone segments
Bengali (MMS).We run the MMS forced aligner [ 3] (TorchAu-
dio bundle) over normalized uromanized transcripts [ 22]. For
each utterance, MMS returns uroman tokens with spans and
token scores. We define aphone-like unitas the interval-level
target obtained by greedily merging adjacent token spans accord-
ing to Table 1: label = merged string after vowel collapse, span =
union of token spans, and confidence = mean token score. These
units are reproducible grapheme-cluster alignment targets, not
a canonical phoneme inventory. Our claims concern how such
targets become linearly separable across encoder depth, not the
phonological status of each label.
Because universal romanization collapses several distinc-
tions, we restrict linguistic interpretation to broad contrasts stable
under transliteration (e.g., aspiration, retroflexion; Section 5).
English (MFA).We use the Montreal Forced Aligner to
obtain phone boundaries and directly use its phone labels for
probing [5].
3.3. Feature extraction and frame mapping
We extract encoder hidden states (embedding layer + per-layer
transformer outputs) from the frozen HuggingFace checkpoints
openai/whisper-small, -medium, and -large-v3,
with output hidden states=True and no dropout. Whis-
per’s encoder produces a 50 Hz frame grid (i.e., 20 ms per
frame) [2]. We map each phone segment to model frames by
maximum temporal overlap. Frames where the dominant phone
covers less than 70% of the frame duration are marked ambigu-
ous and excluded. For each segment we keep only the central
third of frames and average them to form a single representation
per phone per layer; for segments spanning fewer than three
frames, this degenerates to the single center frame. Segments
with no remaining valid frames are dropped. Because the same
frame indices apply across layers, the usable segment count is
constant for a given model.
3.4. Probing setup
For each encoder layer, we fit a shallow classifier on frozen
segment embeddings and evaluate it on held-out speak-
ers; the speech encoder itself is never fine-tuned.Linear
probe:multinomial logistic regression ( C=0.1, L2; lbfgs;
max iter=2000; tol=10−4), with per-layer z-score stan-
dardization computed on the training split. We check solver
convergence and report accuracy and Macro-F1.MLP abla-
tion:one hidden layer (256 units, ReLU, dropout 0.1), batch
size 256, 30 epochs, Adam (lr=10−3), with the same standard-
ization.Splits:Bengali uses speaker-disjoint splits; the smaller
English subset uses utterance-disjoint splits because speaker-
disjoint evaluation is underpowered at 200 utterances. All results
report mean±std over three random seeds.
4. Experiments
We evaluate Whisper-small (12 encoder layers), -medium (24),
and -large-v3 (32) [ 2]. Each phone class is capped at 2 000
samples and classes with fewer than 50 samples are dropped.
Two conditions are tested: (i) no confidence filtering, and
(ii) min score=0.7. Code and artifacts will be released pub-
licly.
Although 2 000 utterances is modest by ASR training
standards, we are probing fixed representations, not training
a model. After filtering and balancing, the shared Bengali
subset yields 7 669 phone segments from 493 speakers
spanning 30 phone-like labels (a, b, bh, ch, dd, ddh,
dh, e, gh, h, i, k, kh, l, m, n, ng, nn,
p, ph, r, s, sh, ss, t, th, tt, tth, u, y ).
The most frequent label ( a) is capped at 2 000 samples; the
smallest retained class has 52. With min score=0.7, 6 015
segments from 485 speakers spanning 27 labels are retained.
Speaker-disjoint splits (test size=0.2) yield approximately
6.1k train / 1.5k test segments ( ≈395 / 99 speakers) at no
filtering, and 4.8k / 1.2k (≈386 / 99 speakers) at 0.7.
Aligned segments are short: the median duration is 20 ms
and 76% of segments map to≤2 frames at 50 Hz. This motivates
central-third pooling; our ablation confirms the qualitative layer
trends are robust to the pooling choice. Lexical overlap across
speaker-disjoint splits is minimal (4–6 overlapping transcripts
per seed,≈0.2%), so the speaker-disjoint setting is effectively
text-disjoint.
5. Results
As a cross-lingual anchor, English MFA probing on 200 Lib-
riSpeech test-clean utterances peaks at layer 10/12 for Whisper-
small (Macro-F10.900±0.016). This places the Bengali peak
in the same mid-to-late region despite noisier uroman/MMS tar-
gets, so the Bengali depth profile is not solely an artifact of the
label construction.
5.1. Scaling across Whisper sizes
Table 2 and Figure 1 summarize the main scaling result. Whisper-
small peaks at layer 8/12 (Macro-F10.837±0.017) and Whisper-
medium at layer 15/24 ( 0.858±0.003 ), placing the peak at a
similarrelativedepth (≈0.65) despite doubling encoder depth.
Whisper-large-v3 peaks at layer 26/32 (0.860±0.003 ) but al-
ready reaches within 1.3% of its peak by layer 21/32 ( ≈0.66
depth). Crucially, large-v3 remains within ≈2% of its peak
through the final layer (layer 32/32: 0.842), whereas medium
drops≈4%. This suggests that scaling reduces the late-layer
trade-off between phonetic detail and decoder-facing representa-
tions.

supervised multilingual encoder with 24 transformer layers. On
the shared 2 000-utterance subset, XLSR peaks at layer 15/24
(Macro-F1 0.801±0.010 ), consistent with mid-layer phonetic
concentration reported for English [1]. However, XLSR exhibits

{tt, tth, dd, ddh, nn, ss} vs.{t, th, dh, n,

5.4. ABX discriminability
Classifier probing depends on optimization and class balance.
As a complementary, classifier-free metric, we compute ABX-
style discriminability [23, 24] on the same segment embeddings
using cosine distance and 5 000 sampled trials per condition.
We balance the two classes and use a standard ABX setup: A
and B are tokens from the same speaker but different classes,
and X matches A’s class (within-speaker: same speaker; across-
speaker: different speaker). We do not enforce phonetic context,
so scores reflect natural-context discrimination.

5.5. Robustness to alignment confidence
Filtering by MMS confidence (min score=0.7) consistently
improves Macro-F1: Whisper-small reaches 0.891±0.008
(layer 8/12), medium 0.902±0.006 (layer 15/24), and large-v3
0.904±0.001 (layer 28/32). Importantly, peak-layer rankings
are preserved, confirming that depth trends are not artifacts of
noisy alignment boundaries.
5.6. Frame-majority threshold sensitivity
Our frame mapping excludes frames where the dominant phone
covers <70% of the frame. Sweeping this threshold on a 100-
utterance subset (13-label allowlist) shows that the qualitative
depth trend persists: peaks remain mid-to-late (layer 11/12 at
0.5; layer 10/12 at 0.7 and 0.9; Figure 3).
5.7. Per-class F1 across layers
To identify which phone classes drive the aggregate mid-layer
peak, we compute per-class F1 at every Whisper-small layer
(Figure 4; 15 classes retained after min-count filtering; speaker-
disjoint).
Classes exhibit distinct depth profiles. The aspirated stops
th and bh, the affricate ch, and the open vowel a reach
F1≥0.90 by layer 4–5 and remain high, indicating early lin-
ear separability from spectral cues alone. In contrast, nasals (n,
nn) and sibilants (ss) rise steeply through mid layers and peak

5.8. Controls and ablations
Baselines and splits.Label-shuffle collapses Macro-F1 to near-
chance, and a text-disjoint split yields nearly identical peaks:
layer 8/12 for small ( 0.834±0.023 ), 16/24 for medium, and
24/32 for large-v3. The English MFA anchor above provides a
cross-lingual check.
Probe and pooling variants.An MLP on a 500-utterance
subset gives the same peak (layer 8/12; Macro-F1 0.885 vs. 0.884
for linear); solver, standardization, and class-weight variants
preserve the curve shape. Median pooling is nearly identical to
mean (peak layer 9/12; Macro-F1 0.841±0.024 ). The largest
peak-layer confusions are n↔nn (13%/10%), s→sh (16%),
anddd→tt(19%).
Alignment and segment robustness.A Bengali MFA audit
(191 utterances; median 70 ms; 92% usable-frame retention vs.
26% for MMS) preserves the mid-layer peak (layer 9/12; Macro-
F1 0.632±0.015 ; 22 classes). Short (≤3 frames) and long (≥4
frames) segments both peak at layer 8/12 (Macro-F1 0.872 vs.
0.875), and raw non-canonicalized labels preserve the same peak
(Macro-F1 0.813; 25 classes).
6. Analysis
Across all three Whisper sizes, Bengali phone-like unit separabil-
ity rises sharply into mid-to-late layers. Small and medium show
an inverted-U profile, whereas large-v3 forms a broad plateau
with minimal final-layer loss. The wav2vec2-XLSR comparison
sharpens this interpretation: XLSR loses 14 pp from peak to
final layer, compared to 2 pp for Whisper-large-v3, consistent
with English SSL findings [1] and with ASR training preserving
phonetic information deeper into the encoder. Per-class analysis
(Figure 4) shows that the aggregate peak is not monolithic: as-
pirated stops and the affricate ch separate early, while subtler
dental/retroflex nasal and sibilant distinctions drive mid-layer
gains. A pre-specified 2/3-depth-to-final contrast confirms the
scale effect: Whisper-medium declines reliably (layer 16→24:
mean−0.039; 95% CI [−0.069,−0.008] ), while large-v3 does
not (layer 21→32: mean−0.007; 95% CI[−0.041,+0.028]).
Limitations.Our labels are coarse uromanization proxies,
not canonical phonemes; fine-grained phonological conclusions
require caution. Segments are short (median 20 ms), though du-
ration stratification and multi-frame filtering preserve the trends.
We compare against one SSL encoder and use a small Bengali
MFA audit (191 utterances); broader SSL baselines and context-
controlled ABX remain future work.
7. Conclusion
We probed Whisper’s encoder layers for Bengali phone-like units
under speaker-disjoint evaluation: small and medium peak mid-
to-late, large-v3 forms a broad plateau, and wav2vec2-XLSR
declines more steeply, supporting the hypothesis that ASR train-
ing preserves phonetic separability deeper into the encoder.
