Not all language switching is equal: Language brokering and code-switching
are associated with working memory and inhibitory control in young adults

Abstract
We examined whether distinct forms of bilingual language use
are differentially associated with executive function (EF) in
young adults. Shifting, working memory, and inhibition were
modelled as correlated latent variables. Motivations for code-
switching were derived via exploratory factor analysis. Overall
switching frequency was unrelated to EF. Lexically motivated
switching was positively associated with updating and inhibi-
tion, whereas frequent language brokering was negatively asso-
ciated with these domains. Results suggest that associations be-
tween bilingual speech and EF depend on communicative func-
tion rather than switching frequency alone.
Index Terms: bilingualism, code-switching, executive function
1. Introduction
Speaking two languages requires dynamic control processes
that adapt to communicative demands. Bilingual language use
is not a unitary experience but encompasses a range of inter-
actional practices that vary across speakers and contexts. Be-
cause bilingual speakers must coordinate monitoring, planning,
and inhibition across languages during speech production, it has
been proposed that bilingualism may engage domain-general
executive control processes. However, behavioural evidence
for such advantages has been inconsistent, particularly among
young adults whose executive performance is at its develop-
mental peak (e.g., [1, 2]). One explanation for these mixed
findings is that bilingual language use is heterogeneous: not all
forms of bilingual speech impose equivalent cognitive demands.
Code-switching, which is the alternation between lan-
guages within or across spoken utterances, illustrates this het-
erogeneity. In many bilingual communities, interlocutors share
more than one language, allowing speakers to insert lexical
items from one language into another, or shift languages across
clauses. Under inhibitory control accounts of bilingual produc-
tion [3], switching requires selection of the intended language
and suppression of competing alternatives. However, empiri-
cal findings linking switching frequency to executive function
(EF) have been mixed (see [4] for a review). A potential reason
is that switching is prompted by diverse communicative pres-
sures. Speakers may switch due to lexical retrieval difficulty,
pragmatic efficiency, affective emphasis, or identity expression.
These drivers likely differ in the extent to which they recruit
language control processes (e.g., [5]). For example, switch-
ing in response to lexical need may reflect reactive control un-
der retrieval constraint, whereas identity motivated switching

may serve expressive goals without comparable inhibitory de-
mand. Prior work has distinguished lexical-need and expressive
switching [6], yet motivational distinctions may be more fine-
grained. Collapsing across them may obscure associations be-
tween specific forms of switching and executive performance.
Evidence that language switching can relate to executive
performance comes from studies of professional interpreters
and interpreting students, who coordinate rapid language al-
ternation under time pressure. Such groups often outperform
control participants on tasks indexing cognitive flexibility and
attentional control (e.g., [7]). However, these findings are dif-
ficult to interpret causally. Professional interpreters differ from
controls not only in language experience but also in education,
training, and self-selection into competitive programs [8]. It
remains unclear whether observed differences reflect the ef-
fects of sustained interpreting practice, or pre-existing execu-
tive strengths. The present study adopts a complementary ap-
proach by examining within-sample variation in everyday bilin-
gual speech practices among young adults, thereby reducing
confounds related to professional selection.
1.1. The measurement problem in bilingual advantage re-
search
The detection of language-related differences also depends on
the sensitivity of the EF measure employed. Behavioural ad-
vantages associated with bilingualism are more consistently re-
ported in older adults than in young adults, whose EF is near
its developmental peak [9]. In contrast, neuroimaging studies
have identified structural and functional differences in bilin-
guals even when behavioural differences are not observed (e.g.,
[10, 11]). This dissociation suggests that commonly used be-
havioural tasks may lack the precision required to detect small
effects in high-performing populations.
One explanation is task impurity: single executive tasks
capture not only the target construct but also substantial task-
specific variance. When expected differences are subtle, as in
young adults, measurement noise may obscure meaningful as-
sociations. Latent variable approaches offer a more sensitive
alternative. By modelling EFs as the shared variance across
multiple tasks, latent modelling statistically isolates common
executive components while reducing task-specific error. Such
approaches may be better suited to detecting subtle associations
between bilingual speech experience and EF.

2. Method
2.1. Participants
A total of 697 undergraduate psychology students from a large
Australian university participated for course credit as part of a
broader study on bilingual language experience and executive
functioning. Eligibility criteria required normal or corrected-
to-normal hearing and vision, no self-reported colour blindness,
and no history of neurological disorder or head injury.
Participants who did not complete the survey, did not pro-
vide consent, or did not complete any EF tasks were excluded.
Latent EF variables were derived from participants with com-
plete data across all EF tasks (N= 375,M age = 23.0 years,SD
= 7.2; 79.5% female). Code-switching analyses were restricted
to participants who spoke at least one language in addition to
English and reported code-switching at least Rarely (≥2) on a
seven-point Likert scale ranging fromNever(1) toAlways(7;
N= 271). Of these, 209 had EF factor-score data and were in-
cluded in zero-order EF correlations and uncontrolled EF mod-
els. Covariate-adjusted regressions used complete cases across
EF scores, language-use predictors, age, dominance, oral profi-
ciency, and SES (N= 176).
The study was approved by the university’s Human Re-
search Ethics Committee and conducted in accordance with the
Declaration of Helsinki.
2.2. Executive function measures
EFs were assessed using nine tasks indexing task shifting,
working memory updating, and response inhibition (hence-
forth, shifting, updating, and response inhibition), following
the unity/diversity framework [12]. Shifting was indexed by
Colour–Shape [13] and Global–Local [14] switch costs ex-
pressed as inverse efficiency scores (IES), and Number–Letter
switch costs expressed as response time (RT) [15]. Updating
was indexed by arcsine-transformed error rates on Letter Mem-
ory [16], Spatial 2-back [17], and Non-verbal Keep Track [14].
Response inhibition was indexed by arcsine-transformed Anti-
saccade errors [18], IES on the Sustained Attention to Response
Task [19], and the Simon interference effect expressed as IES
[20].
Switch costs were computed as performance on switch mi-
nus stay trials, and the Simon interference effect as incongru-
ent minus congruent trials. IES (RT/accuracy) were used where
speed–accuracy trade-offs were observed. All indicators were
standardised prior to modelling.
EFs were modelled using a three-factor confirmatory factor
analysis (CFA) reflecting correlated latent constructs of shifting,
updating, and response inhibition [12]. The model was esti-
mated using maximum likelihood inlavaan[21]. Factor scores
were estimated using the regression method and used in subse-
quent analyses to reduce task impurity and measurement error.
2.3. Language measures
Overall code-switching frequency and language brokering fre-
quency were assessed using single seven-point frequency items
(NevertoAlways). Because these items indexed graded fre-
quency across seven ordered response categories, they were
treated as approximately continuous predictors [22]. Analyses
were restricted to participants reporting switching≥2(Rarely).
Motivations for code-switching were assessed using 14
items from the Assessment of Code-Switching Experience
Survey (ACSES [6]), rated on seven-point agreement scales
(Strongly disagreetoStrongly agree). Items indexed reasons for
switching including lexical retrieval difficulty, communicative
efficiency, identity expression, and enjoyment. Because items
were ordinal, exploratory factor analysis (EFA) was conducted
using polychoric correlations. Maximum likelihood extraction
with oblimin rotation was applied. Factor retention was guided
by parallel analysis and model fit comparisons. Factor scores
were estimated using the Ten Berge method and used in subse-
quent regression analyses.
Language brokering was assessed using a single item index-
ing the frequency of translating or interpreting for others. Con-
ceptually, brokering differs from conversational code-switching
in that it involves responsibility for cross-language meaning
transfer in socially consequential contexts, potentially impos-
ing greater monitoring and control demands.
Participants reported current speaking and comprehension
proficiency in English and their most frequently used non-
English language on five-point scales (Non-existenttoGood).
Oral proficiency was calculated as the mean of speaking and
comprehension ratings per language. A directional dominance
index was computed as non-English minus English oral profi-
ciency, such that positive values indicated non-English domi-
nance and negative values indicated English dominance. Over-
all oral proficiency was calculated as the mean of English and
non-English oral proficiency scores.
Socioeconomic status (SES) was estimated using the
Hollingshead Four-Factor Index [23] and included as a covari-
ate in regression models.
2.4. Statistical analysis
Associations between language experience and EFs were exam-
ined using linear regression. Separate models were estimated
for each EF latent variable. Three model sets were conducted:
1. Switching frequency models, controlling for age, language
dominance, overall oral proficiency, and SES.
2. Motivational models including EFA-derived motivation fac-
tor scores and the same covariates.
3. Brokering models controlling for the same covariates.
A comprehensive model including switching frequency,
motivations, and brokering simultaneously was estimated to as-
sess the independence of effects. Variance inflation factors were
examined to assess multicollinearity. No formal multiplicity
correction was applied; p-values are interpreted cautiously as
exploratory evidence across related, theory-guided model fami-
lies. Model-levelFstatistics,R 2, and p-values are reported.

3. Results
3.1. Executive functioning CFA fit
A three-factor confirmatory factor analysis was conducted to
model shifting, updating, and inhibition as correlated latent con-
structs. Model fit was acceptable,χ 2(24) = 47.82,p=.003,
RMSEA=.051, CFI=.91. All indicators loaded significantly on
their intended latent factors (allps<.001). The latent factors
were moderately correlated (rs = .30 – .53), consistent with
the unity/diversity framework of executive function [12]. Factor
scores were estimated using the regression method and used in
subsequent analyses.
3.2. Code-switching EFA
An exploratory factor analysis was conducted on 14 ordinal
items assessing motivations and beliefs about code-switching
using polychoric correlations and maximum likelihood extrac-
tion with oblimin rotation. Parallel analysis suggested reten-
tion of four factors. Both three- and four-factor models were
examined. The three-factor solution yielded interpretable di-
mensions corresponding broadly to identity-affiliative motiva-
tions, pragmatic/functional motivations, and lexical retrieval
difficulty. However, model fit was suboptimal (RMSEA = .13,
TLI = .82). The four-factor solution demonstrated substantially
improved fit (RMSEA = .075, 90% CI [.058, .094], TLI = .94,
BIC = -125.31 vs 0.60 for the three-factor model). Inspection
of the loading pattern indicated that the additional factor distin-
guished intrinsic enjoyment of switching from broader identity-
related motivations, while retaining separate pragmatic and lex-
ical factors (Table 1). This suggests that positive affect toward
switching may represent a distinct dimension of bilingual lan-
guage use rather than a simple extension of cultural affiliation.
Factors were modestly to moderately correlated, with correla-
tions ranging fromr=−.06tor=.61. Given the parallel
analysis recommendation, improved fit indices, and conceptual
clarity, the four-factor solution was retained for subsequent re-
gression analyses.
3.3. Everyday code-switching
We first examined whether overall frequency of everyday code-
switching was associated with latent EFs. Regression models
predicting shifting, updating, and inhibition included switching
frequency as the focal predictor and controlled for age, language
dominance, oral proficiency, and SES (N= 176). Model-
level statistics are reported in Table 2. Switching frequency
was not significantly associated with shifting, updating, or re-
sponse inhibition (allps> .30). Thus, general frequency of
code-switching was not reliably related to EF after accounting
for demographic and language proficiency covariates.
3.4. Motivations for code-switching
We next examined whether distinct motivational dimensions
of code-switching were associated with EF. Regression models
predicting shifting, updating, and response inhibition included
the four motivations factor scores (identity, enjoyment, prag-
matic, lexical) as focal predictors and controlled for age, lan-
guage dominance, oral proficiency, and SES (N= 176).
Model-level statistics are reported in Table 2. Lexical mo-
tivation was positively associated with updating and response
inhibition (bothps = .007), whereas identity, enjoyment, and
pragmatic motivations were not significant predictors (allps
>.09). No motivational factors were associated with shifting.
3.5. Language brokering
We next examined whether frequency of language brokering
was associated with EF. Regression models predicting shifting,
updating, and response inhibition included brokering frequency
as the focal predictor and controlled for age, language domi-
nance, oral proficiency, and SES (N= 176).
Model-level statistics are reported in Table 2. Brokering
frequency was not associated with shifting (p=.08), but was
negatively associated with updating (p < .001) and response
inhibition (p=.007).
3.6. Comprehensive model
In the comprehensive model (Table 2), lexical motivation re-
mained positively associated with updating and response inhi-
bition (ps≤.004), whereas brokering frequency remained neg-
atively associated with these domains (ps≤.004). Switch-
ing frequency remained non-significant across all executive do-
mains. Comprehensive-model variance explained was modest
(R2 =.068–.174).
4. Discussion
The present findings suggest that different forms of bilingual
speech production may relate differently to executive control.
Overall frequency of everyday conversational code-switching
was unrelated to any EF domain, even when EFs were mod-
elled as latent constructs. This pattern is less consistent with lin-
ear accounts predicting that greater switching frequency yields
stronger executive control and is more consistent with context-
dependent models of bilingual language regulation.
Under inhibitory control accounts of bilingual production
[24], lexical selection requires suppression of competing repre-
sentations. However, dense conversational switching may re-
flect routinised, efficient control processes that no longer im-
pose measurable behavioural costs or benefits. Consistent with
the Adaptive Control Hypothesis [5], interactional context alone
may be insufficient to predict executive outcomes if control
processes have stabilised through prolonged use. In contrast,
switching motivated by lexical retrieval difficulty was positively
associated with updating and inhibitory control. Lexically mo-
tivated switching may reflect moments of heightened cross-
language co-activation during speech production, where time-
constrained lexical selection may require rapid repair. Evidence
from bilingual production research indicates parallel activation
of lexical representations across languages (e.g., [25]), poten-
tially requiring competition resolution and interference suppres-
sion. Repeated experience with lexical production pressure may
therefore relate more directly to updating and inhibitory pro-
cesses than conversational switching frequency alone.
Language brokering demonstrated the opposite pattern:
more frequent brokering was associated with poorer updating
and inhibitory control. Brokering differs fundamentally from
conversational code-switching in that it involves real-time spo-
ken mediation between interlocutors who do not share a lan-
guage, with demands for semantic fidelity, sustained monitor-
ing, and role maintenance. Research on family language bro-
kering (e.g., [26]) highlights its embeddedness in social re-
sponsibility and emotional labour. Unlike trained interpreting,
family-based brokering may impose sustained communicative
burden without structured consolidation of control processes.
Within adaptive control frameworks, such contexts may there-
fore be associated with persistent executive engagement under
load rather than efficiency gains.

Selective associations with updating and inhibition but not
shifting are theoretically informative. Updating supports the
maintenance and rapid replacement of active semantic represen-
tations during speech reformulation, while inhibition supports
suppression of competing lexical candidates and regulation of
irrelevant or emotionally salient content. These processes are
consistent with models of bilingual speech production that posit
non-selective lexical activation and competition during produc-
tion [27, 28]. In contrast, shifting tasks index discrete task-set
alternation, whereas lexical repair and brokering may rely more
on sustained monitoring within continuous speech. Together,
these findings suggest that executive associations may depend
on the functional characteristics of speech contexts rather than
switching frequency alone.
4.1. Implications for the Bilingual Advantage Debate
The results refine contemporary models of bilingual control.
Switching frequency alone was unrelated to EF performance,
which is difficult to reconcile with simplified “more switching
= better control” accounts. Instead, associations were specific
to forms of language use involving lexical retrieval and speech
mediation. This pattern is consistent with the Adaptive Control
Hypothesis [5], which proposes that distinct interactional con-
texts may engage partially dissociable control processes. The
present findings further suggest that communicative role and
production pressure may be important dimensions beyond in-
teractional category alone. Conversational switching, lexical re-
pair, and informal interpreting are qualitatively distinct speech
contexts that may involve different real-time control demands.
From a speech science perspective, these findings empha-
sise that bilingual speech should not be treated as a homo-
geneous experience. Conversational switching, lexical repair,
and mediated interpreting represent qualitatively distinct speech
contexts with different real-time control demands. Modelling
bilingual experience at the level of communicative function may
therefore be essential for understanding cognitive adaptation.
4.2. Measurement and limitations
Detecting these associations required latent modelling of EF,
underscoring the importance of measurement sensitivity in
high-functioning young adults. Aggregating across heteroge-
neous forms of language use or relying on single-task EF mea-
sures may obscure context-specific effects.
Several limitations warrant consideration. The cross-
sectional design precludes causal inference. Frequency of code-
switching and language brokering were assessed via single-item
self-report measures and should be interpreted as coarse indica-
tors. Future work should use more detailed measures of broker-
ing experience. Language dominance was operationalised as a
directional proficiency difference and did not incorporate age of
acquisition (AoA) or language history. As this sample included
heritage bilinguals, sequential migrants, and late L2 learners,
this measure may conflate qualitatively distinct bilingual pro-
files. Future work should model dominance jointly with AoA
and language history to better capture the multidimensional na-
ture of bilingual speech experience. Finally, socioeconomic sta-
tus was indexed using the Hollingshead measure [23], which
may not fully capture SES in contemporary society.
