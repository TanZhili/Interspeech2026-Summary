Abstract
Online speaker diarization—determining “who spoke when” in
real time—is critical for meeting transcription, yet scaling to many
speakers remains challenging. Within the clustering paradigm, we
propose an online diarization system with no explicit speaker-count
cap. Its core module, Delayed-Commitment Online Speaker
Tracking (DC-OST), labels each embedding immediately but defers
new-speaker registration until sufficient evidence accumulates.
DC-OST also incorporates an adaptive distance threshold to
suppress spurious registrations. Under system voice activity
detection, the proposed system achieves 9.53% and 9.12% DER
at 0.5 s latency on V oxConverse and V oxSRC-23—up to 21 and 28
speakers, respectively—outperforming both DIART and Sortformer.
Moreover, a per-speaker-count analysis shows that our system
maintains consistent accuracy as the number of speakers grows,
unlike baseline systems whose performance degrades.

1. Introduction
Speaker diarization answers “who spoke when” in an audio stream
and is a fundamental component of meeting transcription and
voice applications [1, 2]. While traditionally performed offline on
complete recordings, the growing demand for real-time transcription
has motivatedonlinespeaker diarization, where the system must
assign consistent speaker labels with bounded latency as the session
unfolds. Unlike offline pipelines [3, 4, 5, 6, 7] that can process all
speech segments after observing a complete recording, an online
system must decide incrementally whether each new segment
belongs to an already-observed speaker or signals the arrival of a
new one—and such decisions are difficult to revise once committed.
How a system manages these incremental speaker-identity
decisions largely defines its capabilities and limitations; existing
approaches can be broadly characterized by how they handle
these decisions.Clustering-basedmethods [8, 9, 10, 11] compare
incoming speaker embeddings against accumulated centroids and
register new speakers on demand, placing no architectural limit
on the speaker count.Fixed-capacitymethods [ 12, 13] instead
jointly classify frame-level multi-speaker activities through a fixed
set of output nodes, naturally handling overlapped speech but
restricting the number of simultaneous speakers to a small constant
(typically four). Recentcapacity-extendedmethods build upon the
same fixed-capacity paradigm, raising the speaker ceiling through
global and local attractors [14], online attractor decoders [ 15],
or target-speaker conditioning [16]; however, the fundamental
constraint remains—the number of speakers a model can reliably
handle is still bounded by per-block limits or implicitly constrained
by the speaker-count distribution seen during training.
Despite these advances, existing approaches struggle to main-
tain accuracy as the number of speakers grows substantially. Fixed-
capacity and capacity-extended methods are structurally bounded as
noted above, while clustering-based methods, though architecturally
open-ended, have not been specifically designed or evaluated to
maintain consistent accuracy as the speaker count increases. Real-
world meetings frequently involve far more than four participants,
and the number is typically unknown in advance. This gap is exacer-
bated by the evaluation practice of prior work: as shown in Figure 2,
the widely adopted DIHARD III benchmark [17] concentrates 61.4%
of sessions in the two-speaker bin, making it difficult to assess many-
speaker robustness. Prior online systems have largely been evaluated
on benchmarks dominated by low speaker counts, leaving their ro-
bustness under broad speaker-count distributions and realistic system
voice activity detection (V AD) conditions underexplored.
To bridge this gap, we propose an online diarization system
whose core isDelayed-Commitment Online Speaker Tracking
(DC-OST), a centroid-based speaker tracking module designed to
remain accurate even as the number of speakers grows. DC-OST
addresses two intertwined difficulties of online centroid tracking.
First, adelayed-commitmentbuffer defers centroid construction
until multiple candidate embeddings accumulate, producing
high-quality centroids from multiple observations. Second,
anadaptive distance thresholdthat scales with the number of
registered speakers suppresses the spurious speaker registrations
that empirically arise under a fixed threshold as more speakers
accumulate. Unlike conventional online clustering, which registers
a new speaker as soon as a single embedding’s distance exceeds
the decision boundary, DC-OST commits a centroid only after
aggregating multiple observations while still emitting a label for
every embedding immediately, marking the key distinction from
the closest prior work. Our main contributions are:
• We present DC-OST, a centroid-based online speaker tracking
module that maintains consistent diarization accuracy across
a wide range of speaker counts, without imposing any explicit
speaker-count cap.
• On V oxConverse [18] and V oxSRC-23 [ 19], our system
achieves 9.53% and 9.12% DER at 0.5 s latency, substantially
outperforming DIART [8] and Sortformer [13].
• We provide a detailed per-speaker-count analysis on many-
speaker datasets (up to 21 and 28 speakers) under system V AD
conditions, together with a consistency metric (Std(∆)), demon-
strating that the proposed system maintains substantially more
uniform performance across speaker counts than the baselines.

2.2. Delayed-Commitment Online Speaker T racking
Given a stream of speaker embeddings, the DC-OST module must
assign each embedding a speaker label in real time (Algorithm 1).
This task poses two intertwined challenges: (1)new-speaker
detection—deciding when an embedding belongs to a speaker not
yet observed, where a fixed decision boundary becomes increasingly
prone to over-segmentation as the number of registered speakers
grows; and (2)centroid quality—constructing a reliable centroid for
a newly detected speaker, given that a single noisy embedding can
corrupt all subsequent distance comparisons. This risk compounds
with the speaker count: more speakers require more centroid
constructions, raising the cumulative probability that a corrupted
centroid cascades errors into future assignments. A naive remedy
for centroid quality is to enlarge the embedding extraction window,
but this directly increases latency and the risk of capturing multiple
speakers within one window.
Our key insight is todecouple label output from speaker
registration: every embedding receives a speaker label immediately
upon arrival, while the internal centroid construction is deferred
until sufficient evidence accumulates. This decoupling resolves
the tension between the two challenges—the system can demand
strong evidence before committing a centroid (improving quality)
without delaying the output label (preserving latency). Figure 1(b)
illustrates this behavior: each embedding is immediately assigned
a speaker label (“Speaker labels” row), while the buffer progress
dots track the internal centroid construction toward the “commit”
point. We address challenge (1) with an adaptive distance threshold
(Section 2.2.1) and challenge (2) with a delayed-commitment buffer
(Section 2.2.2). The two components are presented in algorithmic
order; as the ablation in Section 4.3 shows, the delayed-commitment
buffer is the larger contributor to the overall improvement.

centroids using cosine distance (Algorithm 1). If the minimum
distance falls below the thresholdτ, the embedding is assigned to an
existing speaker; otherwise, it is treated as a potential new speaker.

Empirically, we observe that a fixed threshold leads to an increasing
rate of spurious speaker registrations as the number of registered
speakersN grows. We therefore employ an adaptive threshold that
scales linearly withN, requiring progressively stronger evidence
before creating another speaker:
τ=min(τ base+λ·N,τmax).(1)
Here,τmax imposes an upper bound that prevents the threshold from
growing indefinitely. When an embedding is assigned to an existing
speaker, the output label is determined via online AHC-based
label mapping [9], which groups similar centroids that may have
formed for the same speaker over time, and selects the label of
the centroid that has accumulated the most embedding assignments
in the matched group. The selected centroid is then updated via
a cumulative moving average with L2 re-normalization.
2.2.2. Delayed Commitment
When the distance exceedsτ, rather than immediately creating a
new centroid from this single (potentially noisy) embedding, we
place it in a buffer and defer centroid construction untilK candidate
embeddings accumulate (Algorithm 1). Crucially, the label output
isnotdeferred: every buffered embedding is immediately assigned
the provisional labelN+1, where N is the current number of
committed speakers. Once the buffer reaches K embeddings,
it yields a single committed centroid, and the provisional label
becomes the permanent identity of the new speaker. In Figure 1(b),
embeddingse1–e5 each receive provisional label 1 as they are
buffered; once the buffer has accumulatedK embeddings, the
centroid is committed and subsequent embeddings from the same
speaker (e.g.,e6, e10) receive committed label 1. Meanwhile,
e7–e9 exceed the adaptive threshold and begin a new buffer for
speaker 2 with provisional label 2. This separation of label output
from centroid construction ensures that the system outputs a speaker
label for every embedding upon arrival, while internally demanding
K observations before committing a centroid, thereby obtaining
a high-quality representation without any output delay.

3. Experimental Setup
3.1. Datasets and Evaluation Metrics
To evaluate whether online systems maintain consistent accuracy
regardless of speaker count, we select V oxConverse [18] and
V oxSRC-23 [19], which contain in-the-wild audio with up to 21 and
28 speakers, respectively. As shown in Figure 2, both datasets dis-
tribute their sessions broadly across speaker counts, with 15.5% and
12.6% of sessions containing 11 or more speakers, respectively. In
contrast, DIHARD III [17] concentrates 61.4% of its sessions in the
two-speaker bin, making it less suited for assessing many-speaker
robustness; we therefore focus on V oxConverse and V oxSRC-23.
All evaluations use system V AD rather than oracle boundaries,
reflecting realistic deployment conditions. Both benchmarks feature
many speakers with moderate levels of overlapping speech; our
study therefore targets such many-speaker, moderate-overlap condi-
tions, and we do not claim coverage of heavily overlapped settings.
Following the official evaluation protocols of V oxConverse and
V oxSRC-23, we report Diarization Error Rate (DER)—decomposed
into False Alarm (FA), Miss (MS), and Speaker Confusion (SC)—
and Jaccard Error Rate (JER), both with a standard 0.25 s collar.
3.2. Implementation Details
Our V AD module employs a convolutional recurrent neural network
(CRNN) with a sliding-window onset/offset decision mechanism,
identical to that described in [20]. To extract speaker embeddings,
we employ a 256-dimensional embedding model trained on
V oxCeleb1&2 [21] with angular margin softmax, as described
in [22]. Embeddings are extracted with a 1.5 s window and 0.5 s step.

3.3. Baselines
We are not aware of prior online diarization studies reporting results
on V oxConverse or V oxSRC-23 under system V AD conditions. We
compare against two representative online diarization systems—one
clustering-based and one fixed-capacity—whose authors have made
well-documented implementations publicly available, enabling
reproducible evaluation under identical test conditions:
• DIART[ 8]: A clustering-based online system that combines
pyannote-based local segmentation with incremental clustering
for global speaker tracking. We use the official open-source
implementation [23] with the authors’ recommended parameters
optimized for V oxConverse (τactive = 0.576, ρupdate =
0.915,δnew=0.648), operating at 0.5 s latency.
• Sortformer[13]: A fixed-capacity streaming system that jointly
models up to four speakers per chunk. We use the official
Hugging Face checkpoint [24], whose training data includes
V oxConverse, in a 1.04 s low-latency configuration.
Given these ties to V oxConverse, neither baseline is at a disadvantage
in this evaluation. Since each system integrates its own front-end
models (V AD and speaker embeddings), the comparison reflects
full system-level performance. To isolate the contribution of our DC-
OST module from the V AD front-end, we additionally report results
using the publicly available pyannote segmentation model [25, 26]
for V AD (denoted “Ours (pyn. V AD)” in Table 1). We also
include the best single system (Ahc2 in Table 4 of [27]) from the
V oxSRC-23 Challenge winner as an offline upper-bound reference.
In contrast to our system, DIART, Sortformer, and the offline
reference system employ explicit overlap detection mechanisms.

4.2. Robustness Across Speaker Counts
While Table 1 shows strong overall performance, the primary
objective of this work is to assess whether the improvement remains
consistent as the number of speakers increases. To quantify this,
we group sessions by their reference speaker count and compute a
per-bin DER. We define∆ as the difference between each system’s
per-bin DER and its own global DER, and use the standard deviation
of ∆ across 11 bins (1 to 11+) as a within-system consistency
measure: a lower Std(∆) indicates more uniform accuracy across
speaker counts.
As shown in Table 2, both baselines show∆ values that trend
upward with the speaker count, indicating progressive accuracy
degradation in many-speaker sessions. In contrast, our system’s∆
remains relatively flat across bins. On V oxConverse, this is reflected
in a Std(∆) of 2.04 for our system, compared to 4.14 for DIART and
9.14 for Sortformer, and the same trend holds on V oxSRC-23. These
results confirm that DC-OST effectively scales to many-speaker
sessions, and underscore the importance of evaluating diarization
systems across diverse speaker-count distributions.

4.3. Ablation Studies
Component Analysis:Table 3 isolates the contribution of each DC-
OST component, starting from a baseline that uses a fixed distance
threshold with immediate commitment (K=1 ). Each component
individually reduces DER on both datasets: Delayed Commitment
improves centroid quality by buffering multiple embeddings before
selecting a representative, while Adaptive Thresholding suppresses
excessive speaker registrations. Combining both yields the lowest
DER (9.53% and 9.12%), consistent with their complementary
roles: delayed commitment governshowa centroid is formed, while
adaptive thresholding governswhena new speaker is detected.
Effect of Buffer Size (K):Table 4 examines the sensitivity to
the buffer sizeK. With an overly smallK (e.g.,K=1 ), the system
creates a centroid from a single, potentially noisy embedding, yield-
ing DERs of 10.59% and 10.62%. Conversely, with an excessively
largeK (e.g.,K≥13 ), the prolonged buffering period increases
the risk of mixing embeddings from different speakers, degrading
centroid purity (DER rises to 9.84% on V oxSRC-23 atK=13 ). The
results show thatK in the range 5–11 consistently yields the lowest
DER on both datasets; we adoptK=5 as the default since it pro-
vides strong performance while minimizing the buffer requirement.
4.4. Real-time Analysis
On all 642 recordings from the V oxConverse and V oxSRC-23 test
sets (∼103 h), the pipeline achieves an average Real-Time Factor
(RTF) of 0.043 on a single GPU; the DC-OST module itself runs en-
tirely on CPU and adds no latency beyond the 0.5 s processing step.
5. Conclusion
We presented DC-OST, a centroid-based online speaker tracking
module whose adaptive distance threshold and delayed-commitment
buffer jointly address the intertwined challenges of new-speaker
detection and centroid quality in many-speaker online diarization,
decoupling label output from centroid construction so that every
embedding receives a speaker label immediately upon arrival.
Evaluated on V oxConverse and V oxSRC-23 (up to 21 and 28
speakers, respectively) under system V AD, our system achieves
9.53% and 9.12% DER—roughly halving the error of both DIART
and Sortformer, driven largely by reduced speaker confusion.
Per-speaker-count analysis reveals highly consistent accuracy
across all speaker counts (Std( ∆) = 2.04 and 2.60), while the
baselines degrade sharply in many-speaker sessions. Current
limitations include the absence of explicit overlap handling and the
single-buffer assumption, which future work will address through
overlap detection and multi-buffer extensions.
