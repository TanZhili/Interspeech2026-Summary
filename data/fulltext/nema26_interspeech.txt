Abstract
Modern end-to-end automatic speech recognition (ASR) sys-
tems achieve strong overall transcription accuracy but remain
vulnerable to proper nouns and long-tail vocabulary. Such er-
rors are often phonetically plausible yet lexically incorrect, de-
grading downstream systems that rely on accurate entity in-
terpretation. We propose a post-ASR proper noun ground-
ing framework that links noisy mentions to ranked candidates
from a predefined lexicon without retraining or modifying the
ASR model. The method integrates complementary similarity
views: fine-grained phonetic representations via grapheme-to-
phoneme (G2P), coarse phonetic encoding using Soundex, and
context-aware semantic embeddings, with normalized score fu-
sion for unified ranking. Multi-view fusion increases Recall@1
over vanilla exact match by 36.4 and 23.6 percentage points
for Whisper-large-v3 and Qwen3-ASR-1.7B, reaching 74.67%
and 59.82% respectively, with up to 87.36% Recall@10 in the
strongest configuration.
Index Terms: proper noun recognition, entity grounding, pho-
netic similarity, grapheme-to-phoneme, contextual embeddings
1. Introduction
End-to-end neural automatic speech recognition (ASR) sys-
tems have achieved substantial gains in transcription accuracy
due to advances in neural architectures and large-scale train-
ing data [1]. Unlike traditional hybrid pipelines with explicit
acoustic, pronunciation, and language modeling components,
modern end-to-end systems rely on implicit language model-
ing and open-vocabulary decoding. Despite their success, they
continue to exhibit systematic errors when transcribing proper
nouns and long-tail vocabulary items that are under-represented
in training corpora. These errors are often phonetically plausi-
ble but lexically incorrect, degrading downstream applications
that depend on accurate entity interpretation [2]. Mitigating
such errors often involves domain adaptation of the ASR model
through fine-tuning strategies such as supervised fine-tuning
(SFT) or parameter-efficient fine-tuning (PEFT), which require
domain-specific data and additional computational resources.
Also a range of post-ASR mitigation strategies has been ex-
plored to address such errors without retraining the underlying
ASR model. Prior work includes phonetic-aware post-editing
approaches [3], retrieval-based correction via datastore lookup
mechanisms [4], and methods incorporating auxiliary entity de-
scriptions to reduce ambiguity [5]. More recently, large lan-
guage models have been investigated for generative post-ASR
correction using dictionary prompting and retrieval augmenta-
tion [6, 7, 8], as well as sequence-to-sequence spelling correc-
tion and contextual biasing techniques [9, 10]. While effective
in specific settings, many approaches rely on a single similar-
ity signal, require strong contextual cues, or assume integration
within the decoding process.
In practice, proper noun errors in ASR outputs are rarely
random. Substitutions typically follow structured phonetic con-
fusion patterns, where rare or domain-specific entities are re-
placed by more frequent but acoustically similar alternatives.
This observation suggests that noisy ASR surface forms often
remain close to their canonical entities in phonetic space, even
when lexically incorrect. Motivated by this structure, we for-
mulate the task as post-ASR proper noun grounding rather than
direct transcript correction. Given an ASR transcript, the ob-
jective is to link potentially noisy entity mentions to a ranked
list of candidates from a predefined lexicon. This formulation
treats ASR outputs as noisy observations and leverages struc-
tured similarity signals to recover likely canonical entities, en-
abling downstream indexing, decision support, or human veri-
fication without modifying the base ASR system.We propose a
multi-view retrieval framework that integrates complementary
similarity signals: (i) fine-grained phonetic similarity derived
from grapheme-to-phoneme (G2P) representations, (ii) coarse
phonetic encoding via Soundex, and (iii) context-aware seman-
tic embeddings. View-specific similarity scores are normalized
and fused to produce a unified ranking over candidate entities.
The framework operates solely on transcript text and is fully
ASR-agnostic. We evaluate the approach on medication name
grounding using transcripts generated by Whisper-large-v3 [11]
and Qwen3-ASR-1.7B [12]. Under a closed-vocabulary pro-
tocol, multi-view fusion yields substantial and consistent im-
provements in entity-level Recall@K across both ASR systems,
without modifying the original transcripts.

3. Proposed Methodology
We propose a post-ASR proper noun grounding framework
that operates purely on transcript text. Given a noisy entity
mention e extracted from transcript T , the objective is to re-
trieve and rank canonical entities from a predefined lexicon
L ={w1,...,w M}, whose construction is described in Sec-
tion 4. The pipeline consists of three components: (1) ASR
transcription, (2) entity extraction, and (3) multi-view phonetic–
semantic retrieval overL. The framework does not modify the
underlying ASR model and requires no access to acoustic fea-
tures or decoder internals.
3.1. ASR Transcription
Speech is first converted into text using an end-to-end ASR
system. In our experiments, we evaluate transcripts gener-
ated by Whisper-large-v3 [11] and Qwen3-ASR-1.7B [12]. The
grounding framework is ASR-agnostic and can operate on tran-
scripts generated from any ASR system.
3.2. Entity Extraction
Given an ASR transcript, candidate proper noun mentions are
identified using a named entity recognition (NER) model. We
employ GLiNER [14] in a zero-shot configuration to extract
medication entities from transcripts. The NER module serves
solely to identify candidate spans for grounding and is not op-
timized within this work. Alternative extraction mechanisms
may be substituted without altering the grounding pipeline.
3.3. Multi-View Phonetic–Semantic Retrieval
For each extracted entity mention e within transcriptT , we re-
trieve candidate entitiesw∈L and compute view-specific sim-
ilarity scores, which are aggregated to produce a final ranking,
as formalized in Algorithm 1.
3.3.1. Coarse Phonetic View (Soundex)
The coarse phonetic view applies Soundex encoding [15] to
both the mention e and each lexicon entry w ∈ L. For ex-
ample, aspirin is mapped to a Soundex code such as A21650.
Similarity simsx(sxe,w ) is computed via normalized edit dis-
tance between Soundex codes. This view captures broad pho-
netic proximity but may conflate acoustically similar entities.
3.3.2. Fine-Grained Phonetic View (Grapheme-to-Phoneme)
The fine-grained phonetic view converts e and w ∈ Linto
phoneme sequences using grapheme-to-phoneme (G2P) con-
version [16, 17]. For instance, aspirin may be represented as
AE S P R IH N . Similarity simg2p(ge,w ) is computed us-
ing normalized edit distance over phoneme sequences, enabling
more precise modeling of pronunciation correspondence.
3.3.3. Semantic View (Contextual Embeddings)
The semantic view captures contextual compatibility between
the mention e and lexicon entries w ∈ L. A dense embed-
ding se = fsem(e,T ) is computed using the mention together
with its transcript context. For each w∈L , a contextual em-
bedding sw = fsem(w,C w) is computed using the canoni-
cal entity together with its associated context Cw. Similarity
simsem(se, sw) is measured via cosine similarity.
3.3.4. Multi-View Fusion
Each similarity view retrieves top-N candidatesw∈L , form-
ing a merged candidate set C as defined in Algorithm 1. For
each w ∈ C, view-specific similarities are normalized using
min–max normalization overC to obtain SG2P (w), SSX (w),
and SSEM (w). These normalized scores are aggregated via
weighted summation to compute the final score S(w), which
determines the ranking of candidates.
Here, fg2p, fsx, and fsem compute phonetic and contex-
tual representations. The retrieval operators R(·) return top-N
candidates fromL under each view. Phonetic similarities use
normalized edit distance, semantic similarity uses cosine simi-
larity, andα +β +γ = 1.

4.1. Dataset
We use the United-MedSyn (UWC) dataset [13], an English
medical speech corpus with paired audio and ground-truth tran-
scripts and a predefined train–test split. The training split is
used exclusively to construct the canonical medication lexicon,
while the test split is reserved for grounding experiment evalu-
ation.
4.2. ASR Transcription
Audio samples are transcribed using two end-to-end ASR sys-
tems: Whisper-large-v3 2 and Qwen3-ASR-1.7B3. The ground-
ing framework operates solely on transcript text and is applied
identically to outputs from both ASR systems. For each audio
sample, we obtain: (i) the ground-truth transcript and (ii) the
ASR-generated transcript.
4.3. Entity Extraction and Alignment
Medication entity mentions are extracted from both ground-
truth and ASR transcripts using GLiNER [14] in a zero-shot
configuration. The NER module is used only to identify spans
for grounding and is not jointly trained with the retrieval model.
Entity-level evaluation is constructed as follows:
• Entities are independently extracted from ground-truth and
ASR transcripts.

4.4. Canonical Lexicon Construction
A canonical lexicon is constructed from medication entities ex-
tracted from ground-truth transcripts in the training split. This
establishes a closed-vocabulary evaluation protocol and pre-
vents test-set leakage. During retrieval, candidates are drawn
exclusively from this training-derived lexicon. Test entities ab-
sent from the lexicon are treated as out-of-vocabulary cases.
4.5. Multi-View Representations
For each extracted entity mention e within transcript T , we
compute three representations:
• Fine-Grained Phonetic (G2P): A pretrained neural
grapheme-to-phoneme model [16, 17] converts text into
phoneme sequences. Similarity is measured via normalized
edit distance.
• Coarse Phonetic (Soundex): A six-character Soundex-style
code [15] is computed for each entity, and similarity is mea-
sured via normalized edit distance over codes.
• Semantic (Contextual Embeddings): Dense embed-
dings are computed over the entity mention together
with its transcript context. We evaluate multiple
embedding models including, text-embedding-3-large[18],
text-embedding-3-small[18], MedCPT Query Encoder[19],
Qwen3-Embedding-0.6B[20]. Canonical lexicon entries are
embedded in the same space, and cosine similarity is used for
retrieval.
4.6. Candidate Retrieval and Fusion
Each similarity view retrieves the topN = 50 candidates from
the canonical lexicon. Candidate sets are merged and scored
using the normalized weighted aggregation defined in Algo-
rithm 1. Unless otherwise specified, equal weights ( α = β =
γ = 0.33) are used, tuned on a validation split derived from the
training set. Candidates are ranked by fused score.
4.7. Evaluation Protocol
Grounding performance is measured using Recall@K (forK∈
{1, 3, 5, 10}). A prediction is considered correct if the ground-
truth canonical entity appears within the top- K ranked candi-
dates. All results are reported under the closed-vocabulary set-
ting described above.

5.1. Baseline ASR Entity Performance
Before applying grounding, we measure raw entity-level exact-
match performance for both ASR systems under the closed-
vocabulary setting. As shown in Table 3, Whisper-large-v3
achieves 38.27% exact entity match, while Qwen3-ASR-1.7B
achieves 36.27%. These results highlight the difficulty of proper
noun transcription in domain-specific speech and establish the
baseline for post-ASR grounding.
5.2. Entity-Level Grounding Performance
Table 3 reports entity-level Recall@K (K∈{ 1, 3, 5, 10}) un-
der the protocol described in Section 4. Results are shown for
individual similarity views as well as multi-view fusion across
multiple embedding models.
5.2.1. Single-View Retrieval
Among phonetic-only approaches, the fine-grained G2P view
consistently outperforms the coarse Soundex view at Recall@1
for both ASRs (68.83% vs. 68.27% for Whisper; 55.99%
vs. 55.25% for Qwen3), demonstrating the importance of
phoneme-level modeling for structured pronunciation errors.
Embedding-only retrieval yields lower Recall@1 compared to
phonetic views but shows competitive improvements at higher
K, indicating its ability to retrieve contextually related candi-
dates even when phonetic alignment is imperfect. Performance
varies across embedding models, with text-embedding-3-large
providing the strongest standalone semantic retrieval results.
5.2.2. Multi-View Fusion
Fusion consistently improves over all single-view methods
across both ASR systems. Using text-embedding-3-large,
multi-view fusion achieves 74.67% Recall@1 and 87.36%
Recall@10 for Whisper-large-v3, and 59.82% Recall@1 and
71.86% Recall@10 for Qwen3-ASR-1.7B. Similar trends are
observed across alternative embedding backbones. Relative
to the raw ASR baseline, multi-view fusion improves Re-
call@1 by +36.40 percentage points for Whisper and +23.55
points for Qwen3. These gains demonstrate that structured
phonetic–semantic retrieval substantially enhances proper noun
grounding without modifying the original ASR transcripts.
5.2.3. View Complementarity
Phonetic similarity dominates performance at Recall@1, re-
flecting the structured pronunciation-driven nature of ASR sub-
stitutions. In contrast, semantic embeddings contribute more
prominently at higherK, retrieving contextually plausible alter-
natives even when strict phonetic alignment is imperfect. Multi-
view fusion leverages this complementary behavior to stabilize
ranking across heterogeneous error types.
5.3. Error Analysis
Residual errors reveal complementary limitations across simi-
larity views. Phonetic representations (G2P and Soundex) per-
form well when errors are more pronunciation-centric; yet,
similar-homophonous entities with very similar phoneme se-
quences are hard to distinguish. On the other hand, seman-
tic embeddings learn contextual compatibility but might con-
fuse entities with similar clinical usage or semantic neighbor-
hoods despite having different surface representations. Multi-
view fusion mitigates many of these failure modes by com-
bining pronunciation-based and contextual signals, yet highly
similar phonetic forms and semantically related entities remain
challenging cases. These observations suggest that future work
may benefit from adaptive weighting strategies or tighter inte-
gration with decoding-time constraints to further disambiguate
difficult instances.
6. Conclusions
We presented a post ASR proper noun grounding framework
that fuses multi granular phonetic similarity (G2P and Soundex)
with contextual semantic embeddings under a unified scoring
strategy. Operating purely on transcript text, the method is ASR
agnostic and requires no modification of the underlying ASR
model. Across Whisper large v3 and Qwen3 ASR 1.7B, multi
view retrieval substantially improves entity level Recall@K un-
der a closed vocabulary protocol with a fixed canonical lex-
icon, showing that systematic phonetic confusability in ASR
outputs can be effectively addressed through structured pho-
netic–semantic retrieval. Rather than altering transcripts, the
framework returns a ranked set of plausible canonical entities
supporting downstream indexing, decision support, or human
verification. Although evaluated on medication entities, the ap-
proach extends to any specialized lexicon exhibiting structured
phonetic confusability.
Future work includes tighter integration with decoding
time mechanisms, where retrieved candidates act as lexical
constraints during second pass inference, along with adaptive
weighting of similarity views and improved disambiguation of
near homophonous entities.
