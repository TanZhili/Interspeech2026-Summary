Stutter: A Zoom-In Nonword Study

Abstract
As speech production and perception share neural mechanisms,
people who stutter (PWS) may also exhibit atypical speech
perception. The present study used a nonword continuum
and a zoom-in design concentrating stimuli around the cate-
gory boundary to analyze categorical perception of voice on-
set time (VOT) in Mandarin-speaking PWS. In identification,
PWS showed a trend-level rightward boundary shift, requiring
longer VOTs to perceive aspirated consonants. Unlike fluent
controls, who displayed typical response delays near the bound-
ary, PWS showed a reduced asymmetry, indicating inefficient
access to phonemic categories. In discrimination, accuracy and
reaction times did not differ between groups; however, more
severe PWS responded faster to within-category stimuli. As
accuracy remained stable, this likely reflects right hemisphere
compensatory processing. Thus, PWS do not show impaired
categorical perception per se but exhibit atypicality in forming
and accessing category mappings.
Index Terms: stuttering, categorical perception, VOT
1. Introduction
Speech production and perception are closely linked. Liber-
man et al. [1] laid the theoretical foundation for this link with
their motor theory, which argues that the true objects of speech
perception are a speaker’s intended articulatory gestures. More
recently, Hickok et al. [2, 3] revealed a dorsal stream net-
work where temporal-lobe auditory representations and frontal-
lobe motor representations interact bidirectionally, demonstrat-
ing that these two processes share a common neural basis.
Stuttering is a speech fluency disorder characterized by in-
voluntary repetitions, prolongations, and blocks [4]. Although
traditionally regarded as a disorder of motor execution, given
that production and perception share neural mechanisms, then
people who stutter (PWS), who inherently experience difficul-
ties in production, may also exhibit atypicalities in perception.
This view is supported by Max et al. [5] and Chang and Guen-
ther [6], who applied the DIV A [7] and GODIV A [8] models
to stuttering, respectively; stuttering is now theorized as a dis-
order of sensorimotor integration. One manifestation of such
atypicality is the production of voice onset time (VOT). VOT is
a critical temporal cue for distinguishing phonemic categories
such as aspirated versus unaspirated stops [9], and PWS have
been consistently reported to exhibit atypical VOTs compared
to people who do not stutter (PWNS), even during perceptually
fluent speech, across multiple languages [10–12]. In this re-
gard, VOT represents an ideal test case for examining atypical
auditory–motor integration in PWS.
The categorical perception (CP) paradigm provides a be-
havioral means of investigating how the brain maps continuous
acoustic signals onto discrete phonemic categories [13]. Sev-
eral studies have applied the CP paradigm to VOT perception in
PWS, but the findings remain inconsistent. Neef et al. [14] ex-
amined German-speaking PWS using bilabial and alveolar stop
continua and reported shallower identification slopes and a ten-
dency for category boundaries to shift toward longer VOT val-
ues. In contrast, studies with Cantonese-speaking PWS found
only prolonged reaction times or a trend toward slower pro-
cessing speed, with no significant differences in perceptual acu-
ity [15, 16]. Shao et al. [17] subsequently introduced backward
masking to increase task demands and reported reduced cate-
gorical perception ability in PWS. However, Chen [18], study-
ing Mandarin-speaking PWS, observed difficulties in VOT cat-
egorical perception even under quiet conditions. Phillips and
Myers [19] also confirmed that English-speaking PWS show
significantly shallower identification slopes for VOT.
Previous studies have three main gaps. With the excep-
tion of Neef et al. [14], all previous studies [15–19] (1) used
real words as stimuli, leaving open the possibility that phone-
mic perception was conflated with lexical processing, and (2)
utilized a relatively coarse spacing of 7 to 9 steps across the
continuum, which may have been insufficient to detect subtle
between-group differences without external noise—a condition
that confounds low-level auditory and higher-level phonologi-
cal deficits [17]. Last, although Neef et al. [14] increased task
difficulty without external noise, they did not administer a dis-
crimination task, leaving the overall profile of categorical per-
ception in PWS incomplete.
The present study addresses these limitations and extends
previous findings in three ways. First, we used nonword stim-
uli to isolate purely phonemic perception. Second, we ad-
ministered both identification and discrimination tasks using
a “zoom-in” design: a preliminary experiment established na-
tive speakers’ boundary locations, concentrating stimuli around
those boundaries to maximize task sensitivity without external
noise. Third, to test whether atypical VOT perception general-
izes across places of articulation, we used a velar stop contin-
uum rather than bilabial or alveolar ones [14–19]. Under these
conditions, where lexical influences are eliminated and task sen-
sitivity is maximized, we explore whether there are differences
in categorical perception between PWS and PWNS. Addition-
ally, we investigate the relationship between stuttering severity
and perceptual measures to clarify how atypical auditory pro-
cessing relates to the clinical profile.

2.2. Stimuli
A VOT continuum was created between /ko55/ and /k ho55/,
both nonwords in Mandarin Chinese. The source syllable
/kho55/, recorded by a male native Mandarin speaker, was nor-
malized to 500 ms in duration and 60 dB in intensity using
Praat. The syllable was segmented into a release burst (4 ms),
an aspiration portion (90 ms), and a vowel /o/ portion (406 ms).
The aspiration portion was then varied in equal steps from 0
to 80 ms, producing an 11-step continuum. A preliminary ex-
periment involving identification and discrimination tasks was
conducted with 20 Mandarin native speakers who did not par-
ticipate in the main experiment. Probit analysis placed the cate-
gory boundary at step (s) 3.69. Using the linear transformation
zm = 3si − 2, step 3.69 on the original 11-step scale corre-
sponds to z = 9.07 on a 31-step scale. For the main experi-
ment, a 31-step continuum was synthesized following the same
procedure, and the 10 stimuli centered on z = 9.07 (i.e., z5
throughz14) were selected as experimental tokens.
2.3. Procedure
Following previous work [16, 17], a letter decision task was ad-
ministered to rule out differences in basic motor speed between
groups. Participants pressed the key corresponding to a visually
presented letter (X or O) as quickly and accurately as possible.
In the identification task, the 10 stimuli were each presented
8 times in random order for a total of 80 trials. Participants in-
dicated whether they heard /ko/ or /kho/ (presented as “go” and
“ko” in Mandarin’s Pinyin) within a 5-second response window.
In the discrimination task (AX paradigm), participants
judged whether two successively presented sounds were the
“same” or “different.” To manipulate difficulty, stimulus pairs
were constructed at three step-size levels: 6 steps (low diffi-
culty; e.g.,z5–z11), 5 steps (medium; e.g.,z5–z10), and 4 steps
(high; e.g.,z5–z9). Forty pairs, including identical and reverse-
order pairs, were repeated three times for a total of 120 trials.
Task order was counterbalanced across participants.
2.4. Data Analysis
For the letter decision task, independent-samples t-tests were
used for reaction times (RT) and Mann–Whitney U tests for ac-
curacy, based on normality checks.
In the identification task, probit analysis was performed for
each participant to derive the boundary position (50% crossover
point) and boundary width (distance between the 25% and 75%
crossover points). Participants whose boundary position fell
outside the stimulus range ( z5–z14) or whose boundary width
was anomalous (≤ 0 or> 20) were excluded. This criterion led
to the exclusion of four participants from the PWS group; how-
ever, the exclusion rate did not differ between groups (Fisher’s
exact test, p = .133), leaving 18 participants per group for
analysis. As both boundary position and boundary width de-
viated from normality, between-group comparisons were con-
ducted using Mann–Whitney U tests.
In the discrimination task, d′ scores ( = Z(hit rate) −
Z(false alarm rate)) were computed for each participant at each
step size (4, 5, 6) and stimulus unit, following signal detection
theory [21]. For both tasks, RT outliers ( < 50 ms or beyond
±3 SD of each participant’s mean) were removed, and the re-
maining values were log-transformed. Each trial was classified
on the basis of the individual’s boundary position. In the iden-
tification task, the two stimuli adjacent to the boundary were
labeled Between and the rest Within. In the discrimination task,
stimulus pairs straddling the category boundary were classified
as between and all others as within. Data from step sizes in
which only between units were available were excluded from
category-level analyses. Log RT and d′ scores served as de-
pendent variables in linear mixed-effects models (LMEs) with
Group, Category, and Step (discrimination only) as fixed ef-
fects and participant as a random intercept. Significant inter-
actions were followed up with Tukey-adjusted post hoc com-
parisons. Within the PWS group, the relationship between stut-
tering severity (%SS) and each perceptual measure was exam-
ined using LMEs. Given the limited sample size, within-group
analyses are regarded as exploratory.

4. Discussion
This study investigated VOT category perception in Mandarin-
speaking PWS using a nonword velar stop VOT continuum and
a zoom-in design. The results highlight subtle between-group
differences in real-time access to speech categories rather than
a global deficit. Four main findings emerged.
First, PWS showed a tendency toward a rightward bound-
ary shift on the velar /k/–/kh/ continuum with a moderate effect
size. This is consistent with Neef et al. [14] using bilabial and
alveolar stops and Shao et al. [17] using bilabial stops, suggest-
ing that atypical perceptual decision strategies for VOT in PWS
may not depend on place of articulation. However, unlike pre-
vious studies [14, 17–19], no significant between-group differ-
ences or trends were found in measures of categorical percep-
tion ability on the identification task, such as boundary width.
This discrepancy may reflect both the specificity of the velar
place of articulation and the task design. Because velar stops
cross-linguistically have longer VOTs with a wider range of val-
ues than bilabial and alveolar stops [24], listeners may tolerate a
wider temporal window during categorization. Yet the zoom-in
design demanded fine-grained identification from both groups
against that inherently broad window. This likely drove both
groups toward a comparable level of difficulty, as also reflected
in the non-monotonicity nearz8 in the identification curves for
both groups (Figure 1).
Second, on identification task RTs, PWNS displayed the
typical categorical perception pattern of slowed RTs near the
boundary [22, 23], whereas PWS did not show this asymme-
try. Two mechanisms may underlie this finding. One is re-
duced efficiency of access to phonemic categories. Neurons in
the left superior temporal gyrus (left STG) encode speech cate-
gorically [25], and reduced left STG activation has been widely
documented in PWS [26,27]. STG dysfunction may destabilize
categorical processing, thereby obscuring the typical RT slow-
ing near the boundary. The other concerns the stability of ac-
cess to phonemic categories. PWS show heightened articulatory
variability in velar–vowel sequences, among others [28]. Under
sensorimotor accounts of stuttering [4, 5], this motor instability
could act as noise that sustains erroneous learning in the internal
model responsible for generating auditory predictions from mo-
tor commands. Because such predictions are compared against
incoming auditory input during perception [29], noise carried in
these predictions could blur category boundaries at the percep-
tual level as well. Therefore, the attenuated RT asymmetry in
PWS points to atypical auditory–motor integration.
Third, on the letter decision task, PWS showed significantly
lower accuracy, yetd′ scores derived from the nonword percep-
tion task were equivalent across groups. A general weakness
in inhibitory control has been reported for PWS [30, 31], and
the letter decision results are in line with this account. Because
d′ is a measure of sensitivity that accounts for response bias,
however, it is robust against overall response inaccuracy. The
present results therefore support the view that executive func-
tion is fragile in PWS while indicating that categorical percep-
tion ability at a purely phonemic level does not differ between
PWS and PWNS. This raises the question of why earlier dis-
crimination studies found between-group differences [16–18].
A key factor is likely the lexical status of the stimuli. Weak-
nesses in lexical access in PWS have been documented in sev-
eral studies [32, 33]. In those earlier studies, which used real
words, top-down lexical facilitation would have advantaged
PWNS, potentially inflating apparent perceptual differences at-
tributable to lexical factors. As for discrimination RTs, the dis-
crimination task permits responses based on acoustic compari-
son without necessarily engaging category-level access, so the
atypical category mapping revealed in the identification experi-
ment would not have surfaced at this processing level.
Finally, higher stuttering severity was associated with sig-
nificantly shorter within-category RTs without loss of accuracy.
This appears to contradict Phillips and Myers [19], who found
shallower identification-curve slopes with greater severity, but
the two findings can be reconciled by considering the differ-
ent processing levels they tap and the compensatory role of the
right hemisphere. The identification slope reflects the sharpness
of category classification—that is, temporal and phonological
processing driven primarily by the left-hemisphere STG [25].
Within-category RT in the present study, by contrast, reflects
processing speed for acoustically distinct stimuli falling inside
the same category. To compensate for left-hemisphere dysfunc-
tion, PWS show enhanced neural synchronization in the right
auditory cortex [34] and increased auditory-evoked response
(P200) amplitudes [35], both of which correlate positively with
severity. Thus, as left-hemisphere STG impairment deepens,
boundary classification becomes less sharp, but the right hemi-
sphere, which excels in spectral acoustic processing [36], en-
hances efficiency for within-category stimuli. The divergence
between the present results and Phillips and Myers [19] there-
fore offers a behavioral parallel to the two-sided neural dynamic
that neuroimaging studies have consistently identified in stutter-
ing: left-hemisphere dysfunction coupled with compensatory
right-hemisphere reorganization [37, 38].
5. Conclusion
This study examined VOT categorical perception in Mandarin-
speaking PWS, using nonword stimuli and enhancing task
sensitivity by employing a zoom-in method. Results sug-
gest that while there was no difference in categorical per-
ception ability between PWS and PWNS at a purely phone-
mic level, PWS demonstrated atypical acoustic-to-phonemic
mapping and altered real-time access to these representations.
Additionally, higher stuttering severity correlated with facil-
itated within-category processing, likely reflecting compen-
satory right-hemisphere acoustic processing. To address the
limitations of this study, future studies should incorporate larger
sample sizes, a wider range of speech features (especially vow-
els and tones), and neurophysiological techniques (e.g., EEG)
to investigate the auditory processing system in stuttering from
both behavioral and neural perspectives.
