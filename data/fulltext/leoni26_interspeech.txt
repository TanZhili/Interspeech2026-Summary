Abstract
This paper introduces an Indigenous-led approach for creating a
high-quality text-to-speech (TTS) model for the M¯aori language
(the Indigenous language of Aotearoa, New Zealand). The pa-
per focuses on data curation, collection, and quality assurance,
and the decisions made throughout that process, as examples
of how this process could be replicated for other Indigenous,
endangered, or minority languages. Despite the common as-
sumption that the more data a model has, the better it will per-
form, this study demonstrates that meticulous attention to detail
produces high-quality data and better results for Indigenous lan-
guages.
Index Terms: text-to-speech recognition, Indigenous language,
data sovereignty
1. Introduction
Natural language processing (NLP) and automatic speech
recognition (ASR) technology enable people to use their de-
vices for everyday functions. Text-to-speech (TTS) technology
is a branch of NLP that converts text to audio and reads it aloud
with a computer-generated or synthetic voice. TTS is positively
impacting the world by increasing human efficiency and con-
nection. For people who cannot read, write, or speak, TTS im-
proves access to information and services by eliminating the
accessibility barriers people face [1]. It helps people listen to
emails, text messages, and other forms of written communica-
tion. It can assist people with their learning needs, especially
those with reading disabilities, dyslexia, or other challenges.
And, it improves device functionality for people to use naviga-
tion apps and voice assistants [2]. TTS technology also has the
potential to be an essential tool in language revitalisation and
reclamation [3] because of its ability to supplement “text-based
language learning tools with audio” and as an “interim solution
pending recordings from first language speakers” [4].
High-resource languages have reaped the benefits of having
large datasets available to support the development of this type
of technology [1, 5, 6]. For TTS, the method involves compil-
ing a corpus of recorded speech paired with the corresponding
text to train the model. The amount of data needed depends on
whether the goal is to build a base model from scratch (could
require 20-50 hours) [7] or to fine-tune a basic voice model
on another voice (could require as little as 30-60 minutes) [8].
Although mainstream technology often promotes multilingual
ASR capabilities, it has consistently failed to produce high-
quality TTS outputs for low-resource languages, particularly
Indigenous languages [9, 10, 11]. The large corpora typically
required are inaccessible, non-existent, or poorly curated, re-
sulting in TTS systems that are often inaccurate or error-prone.
For example, open-source datasets and benchmarks that state
that they include te reo M ¯aori (the Indigenous language of
Aotearoa, New Zealand), are of terrible quality, fail to recognise
the unique characteristics of the language, and do not reflect the
diversity of speakers from the M¯aori language community [9].
Current TTS voices also disregard the many accents that
may exist within a single language. The English voices cur-
rently available on iOS devices are from the United States of
America, the United Kingdom, Scotland, Ireland, South Africa,
India and Australia. Google Assistant currently offers English
voices from Australia, the United Kingdom and the United
States of America. With more than 1.5 billion English speak-
ers worldwide, across over 65 countries, these seven accents
do not adequately represent people and their voices. Further-
more, Indigenous communities have their own type of English
accent, which is also disregarded in the TTS space. In New
Zealand, there are two legally official languages (M ¯aori and
New Zealand Sign Language) and one de facto official language
(English). There are currently no publicly available voices on
devices that adequately represent the unique accents and voices
of these languages. There is no M¯aori voice and the current En-
glish voices are listed previously. Whilst many people around
the world think that New Zealand and Australia are similar, they
are not, and have many linguistic and accentual differences [12].
The current absence of M ¯aori and New Zealand English
voices, or even a voice capable of speaking both languages, is
problematic for several reasons. From a technical perspective,
there is no foundational M ¯aori voice model to build upon. Te
reo M ¯aori has a distinct orthography and phonetic system, in-
cluding consonant combinations such as ”ng” and ”wh” which
are single phonemes. These are the only adjacent consonants
in the language, and all words end in a vowel. As a result, us-
ing a synthesiser designed for another language often produces
“near unintelligible reproduction” of M¯aori [12]. Ethically, and
most importantly, people who rely on synthetic voices for com-
munication are not adequately or appropriately represented. It
also affects those who simply wish to use technology in their
own language or accent but cannot because the available voices
do not reflect how they speak. In Aotearoa, New Zealand,
bilingualism and codeswitching are naturally part of the lan-
guage communities’ vernacular; many M ¯aori place names and
words have been widely adopted into New Zealand English,
and many English words lack M ¯aori equivalents. Without ded-
icated M¯aori or New Zealand English voices, accurate and cul-
turally appropriate pronunciation of this shared vocabulary re-
mains unattainable.
This paper describes the process for creating high-quality
TTS training and benchmark datasets for the M ¯aori language
and New Zealand English. It focuses on the methods and
methodologies of data curation, collection, quality assurance,
and decision-making throughout the process, before briefly de-

2. Te Reo M ¯aori TTS
For over 10 years, our organisation, Te Reo Irirangi o Te Hiku
o te Ika (Te Hiku Media), a tribal radio station in Aotearoa
New Zealand, have been tasked by our elders to create ethi-
cally sound and culturally appropriate tools for M¯aori and other
Indigenous languages. This includes NLP technology that con-
tributed to the creation of M¯aori language speech-to-text (STT),
pronunciation assessment and speech synthesis [13, 16]. Te
Hiku Media places strong emphasis on upholding ethical stan-
dards in data collection, curation, and use [14]. Recognising
that high-quality data can produce superior tools, without ex-
pansive quantities of data often assumed to be necessary, is what
ultimately contributes to the success of the ASR and other tech-
nology [9].
A TTS model would be invaluable for the M ¯aori language
community. TTS technology would support the inclusion of
current M ¯aori language speakers in society, enabling them to
hear and interact with technology in te reo M ¯aori. It is also
a step towards re-indigenising the sound of new speakers by
providing access to an authentic, native pronunciation model
[15]. For people and organisations wishing to use or include the
M¯aori language in their services, even for something as simple
as place names, content must currently be recorded by a human.
No M¯aori-language synthetic voice exists that can compete with
high-resource language TTS systems. This limitation also ex-
tends to New Zealand English and to the absence of a bilin-
gual voice capable of moving naturally between English and
M¯aori, reflecting how the languages are used together in every-
day life in Aotearoa. For example, on public transport such as
trains, audio announcements ensure passengers receive the in-
formation they need. Relying on pre-recorded messages limits
flexibility. If a stop or station becomes unavailable, a synthetic
voice could instead be provided with updated text and immedi-
ately deliver a revised announcement. However, without a high-
quality M¯aori-first voice, and ideally a bilingual voice suited to
the New Zealand context, this remains unattainable.
The creation of a M ¯aori language TTS model would also
support M¯aori language organisations with their different work-
streams. For example, a key component of Rongo, our M ¯aori
language pronunciation app, was having voice talent record sen-
tences for users to repeat. Users have since requested the ability
to enter their own text and have it read aloud to mimic, and
there is an appetite to expand content across other contexts and
regional variations. While discussions around validity, quality
and ethics would be needed, a synthetic voice could mitigate
some of these issues.
3. The Process
While a codeswitching voice is ultimately needed, developing a
M¯aori language voice was considered the essential first step. We
decided that if the TTS would contribute to the restoration of the
M¯aori language, it should reflect the qualities of a native speaker
and incorporate a style of prosody that second-language learn-
ers of M¯aori would want to emulate [9, 16, 17]. Careful ethical
reflection was essential when determining whose voice would
be used for the M ¯aori TTS. From the organisation’s perspec-
tive, there was not enough existing M¯aori language data from a
single fluent speaker that had been recorded and produced to a
standard suitable for model training. It was also crucial to ob-
tain direct consent from the individual whose voice would be
selected [9], reflecting the need to maintain respectful relation-
ships.
The senior leadership team at Te Hiku Media determined
that the voice talent needed to demonstrate advanced profi-
ciency in te reo M ¯aori, given its central importance to the de-
velopment of the tool. It was resolved that there was a staff
member who met this criterion and who would participate fully
in decisions relating to the use of their voice. This individual
also brought broadcasting experience, enabling the production
of high-quality recordings suitable for model training. Selecting
someone from within the team allowed any concerns or issues
with recordings to be addressed efficiently. The staff member is
also genealogically linked to and is an active member of several
of the tribes the organisation represents, an important consider-
ation in upholding cultural and linguistic responsibilities to the
community.
It was fitting that the first synthetic voice be modelled
on someone with a deep understanding of the project and the
broader implications involved in creating such a resource. Al-
though these tools are developed with good intentions to im-
prove accessibility or to support language revitalisation, exter-
nal users have been known to misuse voices, such as in deep
fakes, manipulative content, or inappropriate messages [18]. Te
Hiku Media maintains that our ethical practices are grounded in
Indigenous principles and approaches and that we are actively
working to indigenise NLP. This approach involves carefully
controlling how the voice is deployed, where it can be used,
and under what conditions. Assigning the role to a single in-
dividual with the necessary skills and cultural knowledge illus-
trates the prioritisation of ethical integrity. These standards are
then upheld throughout the processes of data collection, cura-
tion, quality assurance, and model training, an important facet
of data sovereignty [15, 9].
To create a balanced TTS model, we also sought female
voice talent, maintaining the same priorities of strong genealog-
ical ties to the community, relevant broadcasting or vocal expe-
rience, and a trusted relationship with the organisation. Having
already navigated many of the technical and ethical consider-
ations through the development of the male voice, it was less
critical for the second voice to be based within the organisation.
At this stage, we also possessed a deeper understanding of the
potential risks and ethical challenges associated with TTS and
AI technologies, which allowed for more informed and trans-
parent discussions with the female voice talent.
3.0.1. Data Curation
Fostering relationships through whakawhanaungatanga (the
M¯aori custom of fostering relationships) has been central to re-
ceiving permission to use texts from authors or their families
for the ASR and TTS model development. By using only data
for which explicit consent has been granted, we reinforce our
ethical standards and core values and continue to embody data
sovereignty.
A key part of the data curation was carefully selecting
suitable texts for model training. Both fiction and non-fiction
sources were included to provide a diverse range of words,
2864
grammatical structures, and contexts, ensuring the model cap-
tured a wide variety of sounds. Priority was given to materials
authored by M¯aori, for M¯aori and chosen for their cultural rele-
vance, rather than generic or poorly translated content, drawing
on previous analyses of M ¯aori language use in open datasets
[9]. Incorporating texts grounded in M ¯aori knowledge remains
central to the development of M¯aori TTS. This ensures the TTS
system can authentically represent the language and accurately
convey concepts and topics that matter to the M¯aori community.
We received texts in various formats, including PDFs,
Word documents, and e-reader files. After testing different ap-
proaches, the team found that organising sentences in a spread-
sheet and automatically splitting them at punctuation worked
best for initial processing. Our internal language specialists,
who belong to the tribes the organisation represents and have
combined decades of experience in M ¯aori language revitali-
sation, manually reviewed and edited each sentence. This is
a time-intensive but essential step to ensure data quality and
prevent errors from affecting the TTS output. Texts converted
automatically or electronically often contain mistakes, such as
words split incorrectly due to typewriter formatting or punctua-
tion, such as decimal points misread as full stops. The special-
ists also updated orthography, corrected missing or misplaced
macrons, and verified proper nouns with elders to maintain cul-
tural accuracy and respect. We then segmented sentences to
an appropriate length, considering punctuation, rhythm, and
word count, and removed very short fragments, such as one-
or two-word titles, which could disrupt reading flow or pro-
vide limited phonetic context. The sentences were transferred
to slideshows, and each sentence was presented on a separate
slide, which allowed the voice talent to focus on one sentence
at a time while moving smoothly between slides. A final spe-
cialist review caught any remaining errors, ensuring consistent,
high-quality data suitable for training an accurate and natural-
sounding TTS model.
3.0.2. Data Collection/Recording
The team used several approaches to gather data throughout the
project. We initially recorded approximately six hours of ma-
terial at Te Reo Irirangi o te Hiku o te Ika’s radio studio us-
ing Adobe software, though this proved time-consuming due
to technical limitations. We also incorporated around 30 min-
utes of studio-based radio interviews that the male voice talent
had completed over the last few years. Our language special-
ists transcribed these interviews using M ¯aori ASR technology
[13] to ensure consistency and maintain a high level of accu-
racy. The female voice talent recorded in her own studio with an
experienced producer. The producer also has genealogical con-
nections to the tribal radio station, strong M¯aori language profi-
ciency, and expertise in tone and intonation. The male voice ac-
tor subsequently recorded in the same studio with the same pro-
ducer. This collaboration improved both efficiency and record-
ing quality, as the producer and voice talent worked together to
capture the strongest possible version of each sentence, some-
times requiring multiple takes. As models were trained and fail-
ure modes were identified, more sentences were curated and
recorded to fill any gaps, such as numbers, acronyms or specific
contexts like health. As a result, we now have approximately 24
hours of male voice recordings (nearly 11 hours M¯aori and over
13 hours English) and over 8 hours of female voice recordings
(over 3 hours M¯aori and nearly 5 hours of English).
3.0.3. Data Quality Assurance
After completing the recordings, the audio files were sent back
to the language specialists for detailed review alongside the
prepared text. The specialists listened to each recording and
provided feedback on pronunciation and cadence where nec-
essary. This was more common in the earlier recordings for
the male voice talent when the process was new and unfamil-
iar. As the team completed more recordings, the process was
smoother and needed less fixing. The specialists then decided
whether a sound file should be approved, re-recorded because
of errors, or fixed (perhaps it was cut off early, or was too
quiet). Another option was to amend the text to match what was
recorded, where grammatical adjustments could improve clarity
while retaining meaning. This was a much easier change than
getting something re-recorded. Once satisfied with the record-
ings, the specialists confirmed the sound files for approval, and
the producer compiled and delivered the final files in bulk to
the data scientists for testing and model training. Drawing on
our linguistic expertise, familiarity with the curated materials,
and clear understanding of the intended output, the language
specialists ensured the recordings met a high standard before
entering the training pipeline. This proactive review process
strengthened overall data quality, contributed to a high-quality
foundation and reduced the likelihood of errors or unintended
consequences emerging after the model had been trained.
3.0.4. Model Training
In a previous paper, we described the training process for a re-
lated TTS model, including its optimisation for deployment on
smaller devices [12]. That work detailed the use of International
Phonetic Alphabet (IPA) phonemisation, the rationale for lever-
aging Spanish training data due to phonetic similarities between
the languages (as Spanish covers most te reo M ¯aori phonemes
while differing in features such as vowel length and the rolled
/r/), the incorporation of fine-tuning data from male and female
M¯aori voice talent, and the computational resources used during
training [12]. It also reported findings from initial experiments
conducted on a smaller training corpus.
3.0.5. Benchmark
Whilst NLP and ASR success relies heavily on word error rate
(WER) and other types of quantitative testing, this is hard for
TTS, and subjective human review is a much better source of
analysis [4]. It is particularly hard for a M ¯aori language voice
that does not conform to generic standards [3].
After each model is trained, the language specialists are
consulted to review the qualitative output of the synthetic voice.
This subjective human analysis is how the quality of the voice
is assessed. The issue of objectivity is raised with this method
because people have diverse preferences and sensitivities to
pronunciation, intonation and naturalness. However, as data
specialists, we have an innate knowledge of the language and
understand that the end goal is for the TTS output to exem-
plify a M¯aori native speaker sound and secondly a natural New
Zealand English voice. This is why, first and foremost, our anal-
ysis of the quality of the output is most important.
During the early reviews, numerous technical issues needed
to be addressed, such as the speed of the voice and the sound
quality, and several noticeable linguistic failure modes emerged.
For example, early models produced connected sounds with a
robotic, segmented quality, whereas later models more closely
reflect the fluency of a native speaker. For the language spe-

cialists, it was unsurprising that the TTS model struggled with
proper nouns because it was impossible to build a dataset that
covered the expansive number of proper nouns in the M¯aori lan-
guage. This has been an ongoing issue across M¯aori and Indige-
nous language ASR development.
Initially, the models were analysed by using an assortment
of sentences from Te Hiku Media’s data or the team select-
ing sentences to add to the synthesiser. The lack of consis-
tency made it difficult to judge different iterations of the model,
and so M ¯aori and bilingual benchmark datasets were sought.
Our paper on benchmarking M ¯aori ASR [9] demonstrates how
none of the well-known benchmarks could adequately analyse
M¯aori voices because of the uniqueness of the language. This
is the same for TTS, and so our language specialists created
two benchmark datasets to test the voice against. We carefully
selected sentences that were grammatically correct and cultur-
ally and ideologically appropriate to ensure the benchmark, and
therefore the TTS model, is reputable and maintains our cultural
integrity [9].
The M ¯aori benchmark includes 90 M ¯aori language sen-
tences and covers all possible sounds in the M ¯aori vocabulary.
Some of these sounds are very rare, and may only have a few
words in the entire vernacular that use them - several of these
are proper nouns. By including these sounds in the benchmark,
the language specialists can determine whether the voice is ad-
equate at producing them, even if they haven’t been used in the
training dataset due to their rarity. The bilingual benchmark
was harder to construct, as there are few truly M ¯aori/English
bilingual code-switching examples available [9]. The language
specialists identified a recording from our archive of a meeting
in which a male speaker discusses M ¯aori customs in both En-
glish and M ¯aori, which had previously been transcribed. This
transcript was used to select 100 bilingual codeswitching sen-
tences with a range of M¯aori and English sounds.
A benchmarking report is then produced so that the lan-
guage specialists can analyse the output. It includes the target
sentence, followed by audio from the current model and au-
dio from the proposed iteration. The metrics considered are
the pronunciation of vowels and consonants, the pitch, prosody,
intonation and rhythm of the words, as well as clarity and nat-
uralness of the voice. Because of the language specialists’ fa-
miliarity with the curated data, we are well-equipped to offer
advice on where the issues might lie. The language specialists
move through the report to identify any specific issues or failure
modes with the TTS model. Our team, including the language
specialists and the data scientists develop a plan together to rec-
tify these, such as ensuring certain sounds are included in fur-
ther training datasets. Specific examples include getting more
recordings of numbers and acronyms, adjusting pre- and post-
processing features and doing further production on the audio.
