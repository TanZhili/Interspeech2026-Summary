Abstract
Most of us feel discomfort when we hear our recorded voice.
This is because our voice is filtered by head vibrations during
natural speech. Characterizing this filter may therefore recreate
natural self-voice. Previous attempts relied on spectral equal-
ization and failed to converge on a universal filter, likely due
to unguided search across vast acoustic spaces. We introduce
SELFIX, an interactive system that constrains this search with
perceptually motivated dimensions, such as pitch and vocal-
tract control. In a preliminary study (N = 25), participants re-
peatedly adjusted four randomized, unlabeled sliders. Consis-
tent adjustments with high match and confidence ratings sup-
port SELFIX’s validity. While all participants boosted low fre-
quencies, males also lowered pitch and increased vocal-tract
length, indicating that self-voice approximation extends beyond
mere spectral equalization. These findings establish a structured
acoustic basis for scalable, personalized self-voice modeling.
Index Terms: self-voice reconstruction, voice transformation,
perceptual parameterization, low-dimensional modeling
1. Introduction
Many people cringe when they hear a recording of their own
voice. The voice sounds thinner, higher, or simply “not like
me” [1]. Self-voice discomfort is a well-known phenomenon
documented already in the 1960s [2], when voice recordings
were gradually becoming more common in everyday life. To-
day, in an era of voice messages, video conferencing, podcast-
ing, and AI voice tools, individuals are increasingly exposed to
recorded versions of themselves, making this mismatch a prac-
tical and widely discussed issue. The question of why we dis-
like our recorded voice repeatedly appears in mainstream me-
dia [3, 4, 5, 6], reflecting sustained public interest. Yet de-
spite decades of attention, there is still no established method
for transforming self-voice recordings into versions that reliably
match how speakers perceive themselves during natural speech.
Self-voice recordings sound unnatural because the acous-
tic signal we hear while speaking is not the same as the signal
captured by a microphone. When we speak, sound reaches the
inner ear not only through the air but also through vibrations
that travel through the skull and surrounding tissues. This path-
way, commonly referred to as bone conduction, involves the
mechanical transmission of vibrations through bone, cartilage,
soft tissue, and inner-ear fluids [7, 8, 9]. Acoustic measure-
ments show that this internal transmission reshapes the signal
in a frequency-dependent way, typically increasing the relative
contribution of lower frequencies and altering resonant patterns
compared to an air-conducted recording. Bone-conducted con-

tributions are especially pronounced in mid frequencies (several
hundred Hz to 2 kHz) [10, 11] and vary with articulation, as cer-
tain vowels and nasals engage internal vibrations more strongly
[11]. Importantly, these findings describe the mechanical filter-
ing properties of biological tissues, but do not establish which
acoustic differences drive the perception of unnaturalness.
To address the perceptual aspect of the problem, several
studies developed interactive voice-manipulation interfaces in
which participants adjusted recordings until they sounded more
like their own voice. The general strategy was to provide a
graphical interface with sliders controlling spectral gains or pre-
defined transfer functions and to infer, from participants’ ad-
justments, the acoustic properties associated with natural self-
voice. Shuster and Durrant (2003) used a multiband equalizer
that allowed listeners to boost or attenuate specific frequency
bands [12]. They reported a preference for low-pass filtered
versions of self-voice recordings; however, the exact spectral
shapes varied substantially across individuals. Vurma (2014),
working with singers, evaluated several fixed transfer functions
and reported a preference for a trapezoid-shaped filter that at-
tenuated both low and high frequencies while preserving energy
approximately between 1.7 and 3.2 kHz [13]. Won et al. (2014)
implemented an eight-band equalizer interface to estimate indi-
vidual transfer functions and observed a tendency toward band-
pass characteristics centered roughly between 300 Hz and 1.2
kHz [14]. Kimura and Yotsumoto (2018) compared predefined
spectral manipulations – low-pass, band-pass, and step-like fil-
ters – with freely adjusted filters generated via a graphical inter-
face [15]. They reported substantial interindividual differences
and explicitly concluded that no universal filter can approximate
natural self-voice for all listeners.
Overall, previous studies approached the problem primarily
by reshaping the spectral envelope of recordings through filter-
ing. However, they did not converge on a clear solution and
consistently highlighted large variability in responses. A likely
contributor to this heterogeneity is the vastness of the acoustic
search space: infinitely many combinations of gains and filter
shapes can be applied to a voice recording, making unguided ex-
ploration unlikely to converge. More fundamentally, equalizer
bands and predefined filters may not correspond to perceptually
meaningful dimensions of voice representation. If the sliders do
not align with how voices are encoded perceptually, participants
may struggle to map their internal self-voice percept onto these
spectral manipulations.
Research on voice identity may provide more principled
guidance regarding which acoustic features are perceptually rel-
evant. Experimental studies have shown that listeners rely on a
relatively small set of acoustic features when recognizing and
discriminating voices [16]. These features can be represented
within a low-dimensional space in which Euclidean distances

Motivated by these considerations, we developed SELFIX
(Self-voice Estimation through Low-dimensional Feature In-
tegration and eXploration), an interactive voice-manipulation
tool that integrates acoustic features derived from (1) previous
spectral-shaping approaches, (2) source-filter theory, and (3)
psychoacoustic models of voice quality. The goal of SELFIX is
to identify the acoustic properties that make a self-voice record-
ing sound natural to the speaker. After recording their voice,
users can transform the signal by manipulating one or more
graphical sliders that control predefined acoustic features (e.g.,
F0, formant values, harmonic structure, or specific filter types).
We evaluated SELFIX in a proof-of-concept user study with
25 participants, focusing on two filter-based features inspired
by earlier work and two identity-related features grounded in
source-filter theory. Sliders were presented in randomized order
with randomized initial positions and neutral-reference shifts,
and their acoustic function was not disclosed to participants. By
analyzing how participants adjusted these features to approx-
imate their natural speaking voice, we compared the relative
importance of different acoustic dimensions and examined both
intra- and between-participant consistency in feature usage.
2. Method
2.1. SELFIX System
SELFIX is a browser-based interactive voice transformation
system that allows users to manipulate acoustic parameters of
their recorded voice in real time (Fig. 1). The client runs in a
standard web browser and communicates with a Python back-
end implemented using FastAPI. Audio is sent to the Uvicorn
server, processed, and returned as a transformed waveform for
immediate playback. All interactions (parameter changes, tim-
ing, ratings) are logged automatically and stored server-side.
The audio processing pipeline combines source-filter ma-
nipulations implemented using Parselmouth [24] with spectral
filtering implemented using SciPy [25]. Incoming audio is con-
verted to mono and analyzed to estimate global pitch. Pro-
cessing follows the following pipeline: (1) pitch and vocal-
tract-length (VTL) changes are applied using PSOLA (Pitch-
Synchronous Overlap and Add) resynthesis, (2) the signal is
decomposed into source and filter components using linear pre-
dictive coding (LPC), (3) the source envelope (harmonic struc-
ture) can optionally be modified (e.g., harmonics-to-noise ratio
(HNR) or jitter; not used in the present study), and (4) spectral
filtering is applied using a set of parametric filters (e.g., low-
shelf and trapezoid-shaped responses). Transformations are ap-
plied only when the corresponding slider deviates from its neu-

clipped to ensure stable playback levels. All parameters are con-
strained to predefined physiologically plausible ranges.
Although SELFIX implements 35 manipulable acoustic
features, this proof-of-concept study used four: pitch (F0) shift,
VTL scaling, a 600 Hz low-frequency shelving filter (LS), Vur-

outside it [13]. Table 1 reports the units and ranges of the cho-
sen sliders.
2.2. Participants and Apparatus
Twenty-five participants (13 male, 12 female) took part in the
study. Each participant produced the utterance “Hi, how are
you?” which was recorded and used as the manipulation source.
The experiment was conducted on a Windows laptop (Lenovo
21ML008YMZ) using the integrated digital microphone array
and built-in speakers. No dedicated studio microphone or head-
phones were used, to test whether systematic self-voice approx-
imation effects can be observed with basic laptop hardware sim-
ilar to everyday self-voice exposure.
The study was conducted following the guidelines of the
Ethics Committee of the Faculty of Arts and Social Sciences at
the University of Zurich (registration number 25.05.13).
2.2.1. Experimental Procedure
The experiment consisted of two parts. In both parts, partici-
pants adjusted sliders to transform the recording into their natu-
ral speaking voice and could speak at any time for comparison.
They were instructed to focus on perceived similarity to their
spoken voice, not on loudness, clarity, pleasantness, or how they
wished to sound. It was emphasized that there was no correct
answer and that self-voice perception is inherently individual.
2.2.2. Part 1: single-slider manipulation
In Part 1, one slider was shown per trial and participants were
unaware of its acoustic function. On each trial, they moved the
slider until the recording matched their natural speaking voice
as closely as possible (Fig. 2). Upon saving the final slider ad-
justment, participants rated the perceived acoustic match and
confidence in their response (0–100) on two visual analog scales
(V AS). Time spent, number of adjustments, and range exten-
sions were logged automatically. There were 20 trials (four pa-
rameters repeated five times) presented in a randomized order.

2.2.3. Part 2: multi-slider manipulation
In Part 2, participants could freely manipulate all four sliders
within each trial, thereby combining their effects. As in Part 1,
the functional meaning of the sliders was not disclosed. How-
ever, participants could now listen to the original recording at
any time and compare it to the current transformation produced
by the active slider configuration. They were also able to re-
set individual or all sliders and restart the adjustment process.
The purpose of Part 2 was to examine whether increased system
complexity improves perceived acoustic match and whether it
has a detrimental effect on response consistency. There were
three trials with randomized slider order.
2.2.4. Statistical Analysis
For Part 1, the primary dependent variable was final slider dis-
placement relative to the randomized starting value. Mean dis-
placements were tested against zero using one-sample t-tests.
To compare parameters expressed in different units, standard-
ized effect sizes were computed. Repeated-measures ANOV As
with Slider (Pitch, VTL, LS, Trapezoid) and Gender (Female,
Male) examined differences in adjustment magnitude, time
spent, number of changes, and V AS ratings. Changes in behav-
ior over time were assessed using linear regression with trial
number (1-20) as predictor. Within-subject consistency was
quantified as the standard deviation across repetitions per pa-
rameter, and between-subject consistency as the standard devi-
ation of participant means. Relationships between parameters
were examined using Pearson correlations and principal com-
ponent analysis (PCA).
For Part 2, paired t-tests compared ratings to Part 1. Con-
sistency across parts was evaluated by correlating participant-
wise mean parameter values between parts. Stability across the
three multi-slider trials was assessed using within-subject cor-
relations of parameter configurations. Correlation matrices and
PCA of the four-parameter vectors (Pitch, VTL, LS, Trapezoid)
were used to examine systematic co-adjustment patterns.
All analyses were conducted in Python using NumPy [26],
SciPy [25], statsmodels [27], and scikit-learn [28]. Plots of all
results, SELFIX screenshots, and one participant’s log file are
available athttps://osf.io/6nxzw/.

3.2. Part 2
In Part 2, allowing simultaneous manipulation of all four fea-
tures led to higher subjective evaluations. Match ratings were
significantly higher than in Part 1 (t(24) = 4.36,p < .001,
d= 0.87), and confidence showed a similar trend (t(24) =
1.96,p=.061,d= 0.39), indicating that increased pa-
rameter availability facilitated self-voice approximation. Im-
portantly, participant-wise mean slider values correlated signif-
icantly between parts, demonstrating stable individual prefer-
ences across task formats (Pitch:r= 0.50,p=.011; VTL:
r= 0.61,p=.001; LS:r= 0.65,p < .001; Trapezoid:
r= 0.59,p=.002). Parameter configurations were also con-
sistent across the three multi-slider trials despite randomized
order (Fig. 4; all pairwisers≥0.75, allps<.001), indicating
stable individual target configurations.
A PCA on standardized subject-wise preferences showed
a more distributed variance structure in Part 2 (PC1 = 34.8%,
PC2 = 32.4%, PC3 = 23.0%), with pitch and low-frequency
gain loading in opposite directions on PC1 (0.76,−0.49), while
trapezoid and low-frequency gain loaded primarily on PC2
(0.68, 0.62), and VTL defined PC3 (0.77). Notably, although
pitch and VTL were correlated, low-frequency gain covaried
with pitch but not with VTL. This indicates distinct contribu-
tions of spectral and vocal-tract cues and supports the addition
of identity-related features beyond spectral filtering alone.
4. Discussion
The goal of this study was to validate SELFIX as an interactive
tool for approximating natural self-voice. Earlier work relied
mainly on equalizers or predefined filters and often reported
high variability without clear convergence. In this proof-of-
concept study, SELFIX constrained exploration with percep-
tually motivated dimensions and recorded systematic user be-
havior. Validation is supported by several findings: partici-
pants showed (1) stable slider displacements across repeated
randomized trials controlled for starting-position and visual-
centering biases, (2) consistent slider use across task formats,
(3) high match and confidence ratings, and (4) comparable
intra- and between-participant variability. These results indi-
cate that SELFIX captures reproducible self-voice approxima-

Acoustically, low-frequency amplification was the only ef-
fect shared across participants, supporting the idea that recorded
self-voice is perceived as lacking low-frequency energy [12]. In
contrast, the trapezoid filter [13] did not show a consistent group
effect, arguing against a single fixed spectral transfer function
[15]. Pitch and VTL were also systematically adjusted and re-
mained stable across task formats. Their coordinated use with
low-frequency gain suggests that self-voice approximation in-
volves identity-relevant source-filter dimensions in addition to
spectral shaping. This supports the inclusion of voice-identity
features in systems like SELFIX and shows that self-voice ap-
proximation extends beyond spectral filtering alone.
Gender differences emerged for pitch and VTL, with sys-
tematic shifts in males but not females. Although participants
were instructed to approximate their speaking voice, identity-
related adjustments may partly reflect how individuals prefer to
hear themselves. Future work should experimentally disentan-
gle perceptual familiarity from aspirational self-representation.
This study was conducted with a relatively small sample
and a single utterance using standard laptop hardware. Future
work should test larger and more diverse samples, explore ad-
ditional acoustic dimensions available in SELFIX, and evaluate
online deployment for large-scale data collection and different
hardware. Overall, the present findings provide initial empirical
validation of SELFIX as a controlled and scalable framework
for investigating natural self-voice approximation.
Future applications could include perceptually calibrated
AI voice cloning [29, 30] and real-time voice transformation,
where modifying self-voice may influence emotion, identity,
and behavior [31, 32, 33, 34, 35]. It could facilitate investigation
of altered self-voice processing in clinical contexts, including
gender-affirming hormone therapy [36, 37, 38], auditory-verbal
hallucinations [39, 40, 41], autism [42], brain lesion [43], brain
and voice surgery [44, 45, 46, 47, 48], and neuroprosthesis [49].
