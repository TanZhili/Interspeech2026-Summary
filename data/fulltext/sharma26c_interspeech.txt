Abstract
We propose a V ocos-based bandwidth extension model that en-
hances audio at 8-48 kHz by generating missing high-frequency
content. Inputs are resampled to 48 kHz and processed by a
neural vocoder backbone, enabling a single network to sup-
port arbitrary upsampling ratios. A lightweight Linkwitz-Riley-
inspired refiner merges the original low band with the generated
high frequencies via a smooth crossover. On validation, the
model achieves competitive log-spectral distance while running
at a real-time factor of 0.0001 on an NVIDIA A100 GPU and
0.0053 on an 8-core CPU, demonstrating practical, high-quality
BWE at extreme throughput.
Index Terms: bandwidth extension, neural vocoder, audio
super-resolution, speech enhancement
1. Introduction
Bandwidth extension (BWE) aims to recover or hallucinate
missing frequency components of audio signals captured with
limited bandwidth, such as legacy recordings or telephony
speech. Traditional signal processing methods, including
interpolation-based upsampling and spectral shaping, offer
high efficiency but often fail to reconstruct convincing high-
frequency details. Recent learning-based approaches have re-
formulated BWE as a conditional generation task, predicting
missing high-band content from the observed low-band signal.
State-of-the-art diffusion-based models such as Au-
dioSR [1], have demonstrated exceptional generative quality.
However, their iterative sampling process is often too compu-
tationally expensive for real-time or large-scale deployment.
Conversely, GAN-based approaches offer substantial speed ad-
vantages and share key similarities with our work, as they fre-
quently operate in the Fourier domain and utilize ConvNeXt [2]
backbones. Among these, AP-BWE [3] relies on an interleaved
magnitude and phase prediction block tailored to fixed in-
put/output sample-rate pairs (e.g., 16 kHz→48 kHz), restrict-
ing its flexibility. While recent extensions like MS-BWE [4]
target arbitrary-rate and faster processing, they introduce intri-
cate pipelines with specialized multi-scale networks. In con-
trast, our framework vastly simplifies this paradigm by predict-
ing complex STFT coefficients simultaneously within a single
unified head. By pairing this joint prediction with an initial
input-resampling step to 48 kHz and a Linkwitz-Riley-inspired
frequency refiner, our model completely eliminates both ratio-
specific architectures and multi-scale complexities.
In this paper, we propose a versatile BWE system that
supports arbitrary input sample rates from 8–48 kHz within
a single network. We build upon the V ocos [5], leveraging
its high-fidelity Fourier-domain neural vocoder architecture to
synthesize missing high frequencies. By resampling all in-
puts to a 48 kHz and employing a V ocos-style generator, our
model maintains consistency across various upsampling ra-
tios. To further enhance fidelity, we introduce a lightweight
frequency-domain refiner inspired by the Linkwitz-Riley in-
spired crossover [6]. This refiner smoothly merges the orig-
inal resampled low-band signal with the generated high-band
components, ensuring a smooth transition at the crossover fre-
quency.
Experimental results on the VCTK corpus [7] demon-
strate that our proposed model matches or outperforms strong
baselines in terms of Log-Spectral Distance (LSD) while of-
fering orders-of-magnitude higher throughput than diffusion-
based methods.
The primary contributions of this work are as follows:
• We introduce the first V ocos-based BWE model to our knowl-
edge, utilizing a neural vocoder to generate high-frequency
content for arbitrary input sampling rates.
• We propose a Linkwitz-Riley inspired frequency-domain re-
finer that improves perceptual quality by seamlessly combin-
ing the original low band with the synthesized high band.
• We demonstrate a superior quality-to-speed trade-off, achiev-
ing a Real-Time Factor (RTF) of0.0001on an NVIDIA
A100 and maintaining considerably faster-than-real-time per-
formance on a standard 8-core CPU.
2. Proposed Method
2.1. Problem formulation
Letx∈R T denote a waveform at 48 kHz, and letx↓r denote a
band-limited, downsampled observation captured at sample rate
r∈{8,16,24}kHz(and more generally anyr∈[8,48] kHz).
The goal of BWE is to estimate a high-band enhanced wave-
formˆxat 48 kHz that is perceptually and spectrally close to
x, while being consistent with the low-band content implied by
x↓r.
The proposed model first resamples the input to 48 kHz us-
ing sinc interpolation to obtain a baseband waveformy∈R T
that preserves the available low-band information but lacks true
high-frequency detail. A generatorG θ then predicts an en-
hanced waveform˜x=G θ(y), after which a frequency-domain
refiner produces the final outputˆx.
2.2. Model architecture
The generator operates on a time–frequency representation
(mel-spectrogram) derived from the 48 kHz resampled input
and produces a complex STFT representation inverted to the
waveform via iSTFT.
Feature extraction.We compute a mel-spectrogram with
80 mel bins,n fft = 2048, and hop length 512 at 48 kHz. This

Output head and waveform synthesis.The linear head

to generate missing high-frequency content rather than merely
reconstructing the input band.

2.4. Training objectives
The proposed model is trained using a combination of spectral,
perceptual, and adversarial losses to ensure both structural ac-
curacy and high-frequency realism.
Multi-resolution STFT loss (MRSTFT).STFT losses are
computed atn fft∈{512,1024,2048}with hop sizesn fft/4.
This multi-scale approach encourages accurate reconstruction
across different time–frequency resolutions, capturing both
fine-grained temporal events and long-term spectral envelopes.
Mel-spectrogram loss.We apply anL 1 loss between the
mel-spectrograms of the ground truth and the generated audio.
This is computed at 48 kHz with 128 mel bins andnfft = 2048,
focusing the model on perceptually relevant frequency bands.

Multi-Resolution Discriminator (MRD).The MRD [8]
consists of multiple sub-discriminators that operate on the com-
plex STFT of the waveform at different scales. By analyzing the
signal in the frequency domain with varying window sizes, the
MRD can simultaneously penalize artifacts in high-frequency
transients (short windows) and preserve the harmonic structure
of voiced speech (long windows). This prevents the ”phase-
smearing” common in time-domain-only discriminators.

3. Experimental Setup
3.1. Dataset
We train on the VCTK corpus (approximately 44 hours
of speech), using roughly 60,000 audio segments randomly
cropped to 1.28, 2.56, or 3.2 s.
3.2. Degradation process
Clean 48 kHz audio is randomly downsampled tor∈
{8,12,16}kHz using sinc, ZOH, or linear interpolation. Op-
tional quantization noise is added to make the model more ro-
bust. The degraded signal is resampled back to 48 kHz before
being fed to the generator.
3.3. Baselines
Baselines include:
•Sinc upsampling– standard interpolation.
•AP-BWE– APNet2 inspired GAN trained for specific ratios.
•NVSR[9] – neural GAN vocoder based super-resolution
baseline.
•AudioSR– diffusion-based audio super-resolution.

ViSQOL.ViSQOL [10] compares patches of the refer-

SI-SDR.(Scale-Invariant Signal-to-Distortion Ratio) eval-
uates the quality of an audio signal by comparing it to a clean
reference in the time domain. Unlike frequency-domain metrics
like LSD or ViSQOL, it explicitly accounts for phase quality.
Reported in decibels (dB), higher values indicate better audio
reconstruction or source separation.

4.2. Performance Analysis
The experimental results in Table 1, Table 2, and Table 3
demonstrate the effectiveness of our proposed model across
various upsampling scales, evaluated through objective spec-
tral distortion, perceptual quality, and time-domain waveform
fidelity metrics.
Discussion:The proposed model achieves the lowest LSD
across all scenarios, reaching0.85at8→48kHz, outperform-
ing AudioSR (1.61) and NVSR (1.22). Perceptually, it matches
AP-BWE at8→48kHz (3.51) and remains competitive at
16 kHz (3.69). While our SI-SDR (18.02dB) slightly trails AP-
BWE’s interleaved, dual-backbone architecture, it significantly
outperforms NVSR (14.68 dB). By predicting complex STFT
coefficients simultaneously via a single backbone and linear
head, our single-stream network trades a minor phase optimiza-
tion delta for extreme simplicity and an order-of-magnitude
CPU speedup (see Section 4.5). Crucially, our unified frame-
work supports arbitrary input sampling rates at a fraction of
the computational cost, delivering a superior quality–efficiency
trade-off.
4.3. Robustness to Out-of-Domain Sample Rates
While the model was primarily trained on common sampling
anchors (8, 12, 16kHz, etc.), a key advantage of our architecture
is its ability to handle arbitrary input bandwidths. To evaluate
this ”zero-shot” generalization, we tested the model on various
out-of-domain (OOD) sample rates.
OOD Generalization.As shown in Figure 2, LSD de-
creases monotonically with higher input rates, with unseen out-
of-domain (OOD) rates tracking the same linear trend as in-
domain training anchors.
This robustness stems from two factors: (1) pre-resampling
inputs to 48 kHz casts BWE as a fixed-grid spectral comple-
tion task for the ConvNeXt backbone, and (2) the Linkwitz-
Riley-inspired refiner dynamically anchors the generated high
band via a smooth frequency-domain crossover, eliminating the
spectral discontinuities and metallic artifacts typical of fixed-
rate systems.

5. Conclusion
We presented a V ocos-based bandwidth extension model that
supports any input sample rate from 8–48 kHz while achiev-
ing extreme throughput. By resampling inputs to 48 kHz,
leveraging a ConvNeXt-based V ocos generator, and applying
a lightweight Linkwitz-inspired frequency-domain refiner, the
proposed model attains competitive spectral and perceptual
quality relative to strong baselines. Future work includes eval-
uation across music, noisy conditions, and exploring adaptive
refiners for different tasks.
