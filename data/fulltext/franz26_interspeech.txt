Abstract
Objective voice quality measures are sensitive to reverbera-
tion, which limits their robustness in clinical applications. This
study investigates whether blind diffusion-based dereverberation
enables room-independent assessment of voice quality using
Smoothed Cepstral Peak Prominence (CPPS). Anechoic voice
samples from two databases were convolved with impulse re-
sponses of real therapy rooms and subsequently dereverberated.
Effects on CPPS level and the rank order of voice qualities were
analyzed. For low-reverberation recordings, no systematic CPPS
bias was introduced by dereverberation. In reverberant condi-
tions, CPPS reductions were compensated after processing. For
continuous speech, voice quality rankings were reconstructed,
and CPPS values approached their anechoic reference levels
across databases. These findings indicate that blind derever-
beration can effectively mitigate room-acoustic distortions in
objective voice quality assessment of continuous speech.

1. Introduction
In speech and language therapy (SLT), diagnosis and progress
monitoring of voice disorders frequently rely on therapists’ per-
ceptual judgments. These SLT assessments are well established
and practice-oriented (e.g.,GRBAS[ 1] orCAPE -Vscale [ 2]),
although ratings may vary depending on contextual factors and
individual raters [3], affecting inter- and intrarater reliability for
both GRBAS and CAPE-V [4, 5, 6]. To complement perceptual
evaluation, objective and quantitative measures of voice qual-
ity like multiparametric acoustic indices are gaining increasing
importance, particularly with regard to reliability, validity, and
comparability [7, 8].
A key criterion of such measures is robustness to recording
conditions. Microphone type and placement can influence acous-
tic measures [9], and background noise, early reflections, and
reverberation can systematically alter the speech signal, affect-
ing automated assessment. Smoothed Cepstral Peak Prominence
(CPPS) [10] is widely used for objective voice quality assessment
[11]; however, recent evidence indicates systematic bias. Con-
volution with real room impulse responses (IRs) reduces CPPS
and related measures, with stronger effects for less pathological
voices and higher reverberation [12]. Thus, room acoustics may

Since reverberation and ambient noise cannot be avoided in
practice, two strategies may compensate: (i) regression-based
calibration of acoustic parameters, or (ii) signal-level dereverber-
ation. Parameter-level compensation requires a correct, general-
izable model incorporating relevant parameters (e.g., reverbera-
tion, microphone type/position), which are typically unavailable
in routine clinical recordings. Moreover, such models may be
domain-specific and do not directly correct signal distortions
caused by reverberation.
Against this background, we investigate signal-level dere-
verberation to make CPPS-based voice quality assessment more
room-independent. Although widely studied in speech enhance-
ment, its effect on objective voice quality measures under real-
istic SLT room acoustics remains largely unquantified. Many
established approaches, such as weighted prediction error (WPE)
[13], rely on explicit room IR estimation, while supervised neu-
ral methods require paired clean–reverberant data. These pre-
requisites are typically unavailable in clinical settings, making
standard approaches difficult to apply.
We therefore evaluate the diffusion-based blind dereverber-
ation method BUDDy [14], which is open source and operates
fully unsupervised and does not require measured IRs or clean
reference signals, making it well suited for practical clinical use.
Specifically, we apply BUDDy to weakly and strongly reverber-
ant recordings from two independent voice databases (VDBs) to
obtainpseudo-anechoicreference signals. These references are
then convolved with real-world IRs to simulate controlled acous-
tic degradation and quantify how reverberation and subsequent
dereverberation affect CPPS.
This design allows us to test three sets of hypotheses: (H1)
the effect of the first dereverberation on CPPS, (H2) the impact
of convolution with room IRs on CPPS levels and relative voice
quality rankings, and (H3) the extent to which a second derever-
beration step restores CPPS levels and ranking consistency. The
complete signal-processing workflow is shown in Figure 1.
This paper is structured as follows: Section 2 describes the
analyzed voice databases and their preprocessing, details the
generation of the four signal conditions and CPPS extraction,
and provides the formal specification of the hypotheses together
with the corresponding statistical tests. Section 3 presents and
discusses the empirical findings. Section 4 summarizes the main
results and implications.

2. Methodology
2.1. Voice Samples and Preprocessing
Two German-language voice VDBs were used for the analy-
ses. TheSaarbr ¨ucken Voice Database (SVDB)[ 15] comprises
physiological and pathological voices recorded under controlled,
low-reverberation conditions (sampling frequency fs= 50 kHz ).
From 274 randomly selected subjects, two speech samples were
extracted: the sustained vowel /a/ and the sentence “Guten Tag,
wie geht es Ihnen?” (English: ”Good day, how are you?”).
The data collected in theReST study[ 16] (fs= 22.05 kHz )
provides recordings of teachers before and after a vocal load-
ing task (VLT) [ 17]. For each speaker, a sustained vowel /a/
and a read text (“Der Nordwind und die Sonne”, engl. “The
North Wind and the Sun”) were recorded on site in schools in
acoustically untreated rooms under field conditions, resulting in
variable reverberation and occasionally low levels and unfavor-
able direct-to-reverberant ratios. From this dataset, 133 subjects
were randomly selected, providing a practice-oriented contrast
to the low-reverberation SVDB samples.
For further processing, all voice samples were resampled
to fs= 16 kHz in Python using the resampler provided by the
librosa [18] library. This reduces computational cost during
dereverberation and enables the use of the pretrained BUDDy
modelVCTK 16k 4s time-190000.pt[19].
2.2. Blind Unsupervised Dereverberation with Diffusion
Models
As a first step, all original recordings (weakly and strongly rever-
berant) were processed with BUDDy without additional prepro-
cessing to obtainpseudo-anechoicreference signals.
In a second step, these pseudo-anechoic voice samples were
convolved with 35 IRorig measured in real SLT rooms [12] and
subsequently dereverberated again. This enables a systematic
analysis of IR parameters on CPPS, as well as a direct com-
parison between the initially dereverberated signals and those
processed after convolution and second dereverberation.
As a result, four signal conditions were obtained: the original
recordings, the first dereverberation (pseudo-anechoic reference),
convolution with real world IRs, and the second dereverberation.

Shapiro–Wilk tests on random subsamples (n= min(N,5000) )
for large datasets. As systematic deviations from normality were

As no established clinical thresholds for meaningful CPPS
differences exist, the tolerance parametersδ were defined as a
heuristic but robust, data-driven measure of practically relevant
effects. Relevance limits were anchored to empirically observed
IR-induced measurement variability rather than arbitrary cutoffs.
For each voice sample, the interquartile range of CPPS
across all IR conditions,IQRIR(CPPS), was computed to quan-
tify acoustic fluctuation. Given non-normal distributions, the
IQR was chosen as a robust measure of dispersion. To rep-
resent substantial yet typical variability while limiting the in-
fluence of extreme cases, the upper quartile of the IQR dis-
tribution was used, and δ was defined as half of this value:
δ= 0.5·Q 0.75(IQRIR(CPPS)).
For H1b and H3b, the resulting margin δ1 refers to CPPS
values after the second dereverberation; for H2b, δ2 refers to
values after convolution.

4. Conclusions
This contribution demonstrated that realistic room acoustics alter
CPPS-based objective voice quality assessment, even at desir-
able reverberation times of about0.2–0.3 s, and can distort voice-
quality rankings, consistent with evidence that simulated room
acoustics impair automated voice health assessment [23]; impor-
tantly, it further shows that blind signal-level dereverberation can
mitigate these effects.
The results show that the blind dereverberation method
BUDDy effectively compensates for reverberation, particularly
for continuous speech. After dereverberation, CPPS values shift
toward the unreverberated reference for both mildly and strongly
reverberant conditions, and the original ranking of voice qual-
ities is largely restored for continuous speech. This indicates
that reverberation-induced, stimulus-dependent distortions are
substantially reduced.
For sustained vowels, dereverberation effects are markedly
weaker, plausibly due to their temporal and spectral characteris-
tics and because the model was not trained on sustained vowel
material, limiting its suitability for these signals.
Overall, the findings support dereverberation as a robust pre-
processing step for objective voice assessment based on CPPS
and related spectral/cepstral measures. Importantly, the strong
performance observed for continuous speech is particularly rel-
evant in practice, since speech—not sustained vowels—is the
primary signal in everyday communication. If voice quality can
now be estimated robustly from connected speech even under
room-acoustic influences, this enables new diagnostic and moni-
toring scenarios, e.g., continuous assessment of a patient’s voice
quality during natural conversation or throughout therapy ses-
sions.
Future work includes evaluating the approach on additional
voice databases to assess generalizability, and investigating
whether room impulse response estimates obtained from con-
tinuous speech can also improve dereverberation of sustained
vowels. In the longer term, we aim to develop a robust system
for continuous voice-quality assessment from connected speech
in realistic clinical and everyday settings.
