AccentDrift: Real-time Streaming Accent Conversion
via Sparse Speech Tokenization

Abstract
Although recent speech AI advances have enabled real-time
speech understanding and generation, real-time accent conver-
sion (AC) for second-language (L2) speakers in real-world in-
teractions remains underexplored. In this paper, we present
AccentDrift, a real-time streaming AC system that preserves lin-
guistic content and speaker identity while transforming speech
into a target accent with low latency. From an information bot-
tleneck perspective, we adopt sparse speech tokenization to ex-
tract linguistic information from continuous speech. An accent
adapter injects accent style into sparse semantic tokens, fol-
lowed by a timbre adapter that hierarchically generates speaker-
specific acoustics. To support streaming, we adopt a cache-
aware streaming architecture and parallel streams, achieving
520 ms latency. AccentDrift outperforms prior parallel AC
models, and demonstrates the feasibility of generating native-
like accents for L2 speakers. Audio samples are available at
https://accentdrift.github.io/demo/.
Index Terms: accent conversion, streaming audio generation,
real-time system, sparse speech tokenization
1. Introduction
The rapid advances in generative models such as language
models and diffusion models have revolutionized speech under-
standing systems including automatic speech recognition (ASR)
[1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6] and speech generation systems including text-
to-speech (TTS) [7, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12] and voice conversion (VC)
[13, 14, 15, 16, 17, 18]. Real-time streaming speech understand-
ing and generation systems have also been actively investigated
for real-world applications such as full-duplex spoken dialogue
systems [19, 20, 21]. However, despite these advances, real-time
streaming accent conversion (AC) systems have not yet been
sufficiently explored. AC aims to preserve linguistic content and
timbre while transforming speech into a target accent. In partic-
ular, real-time streaming AC is crucial for second-language (L2)
speakers in real-world interactive scenarios, where low latency
and naturalness are essential for seamless communication.
However, existing AC systems [22, 23, 24, 25, 26, 27, 28]
suffer from several critical limitations: 1) a strong reliance on
parallel datasets, which are costly to obtain; 2) parallel architec-
tures that hinder real-time streaming interaction; 3) insufficient
generative capability, resulting in degraded audio quality; 4)
inflexible architectural designs that restrict fine-grained control-
lability over accent and timbre.
To address these limitations, we propose AccentDrift, a real-
time streaming accent conversion system. First, we adopt sparse
speech tokenization to extract only linguistic representations
from speech. To this end, we analyze the conventional audio
and speech tokenizers and carefully design a sparse semantic

For real-time streaming applications, AccentDrift is built

2. Speech Tokenization
To convert the style of speech, disentangling speech attributes
is essential. Typically, VC models have investigated effective
speech disentanglement methods to extract linguistic information
using information bottlenecks (IB) [13, 39, 40, 41] or informa-
tion perturbation [ 42, 43, 44, 45]. In this work, we adopt an
IB approach based on vector quantization (VQ) to disentangle
speech representations. Following [13, 37, 38], we categorize the
audio/speech tokenizers into acoustic and semantic tokenizers
according to the objectives, and further subdivide the semantic
tokenizer into dense and sparse variants based on the IB size.
2.1. Acoustic Tokenizer
In general, neural audio codecs can be viewed as acoustic tok-
enizers. Most existing models, such as EnCodec [33] and DAC
[34], are trained with self-reconstruction objectives including
reconstruction losses and audio feature-based GAN losses. As a
result, acoustic information inevitably leaks into the VQ-based
codebooks, regardless of the IB size. SpeechTokenizer [46] and
Mimi [19] leverage self-supervised representation (SSL) distil-
lation to enhance semantic capacity. However, we observe that
the reconstruction-based acoustic tokenizer fails to disentangle

2.2. Semantic Tokenizer
Unlike acoustic tokenizer, CosyV oice [35] first introduced a fi-
nite scalar quantization (FSQ) [47]-based supervised semantic
speech (S3) tokenizer. S3 tokenizer does not employ recon-
struction objectives and instead relies solely on ASR-based text
supervision to obtain semantic tokens. However, when config-
ured with a relatively wide IB, the semantic tokens may contain
unnecessary attributes. Although they intentionally preserve
prosody to support robust downstream tasks, this design can hin-
der style conversion. In our observations, suchdense semantic
tokenizersare not well suited for AC. To address this limitation,
we introduce asparse semantic tokenizerin the next section.
3. AccentDrift
3.1. Sparse Semantic Tokenizer
Following [37, 38], we adopt a sparse semantic tokenizer with a
narrower IB to suppress prosodic leakage. We identify several
key conditions for achieving sparse linguistic representations: 1)
high-level ASR or SSL representations, 2) highly compressed
quantization, and 3) supervised learning.
For streaming speech encoding, we adopt a pre-trained
streaming ASR encoder from Nemotron Speech ASR1, which
consists of the cache-aware [48] FastConformer [3] with 24 lay-
ers. Specifically, the encoder employs feed-forward at 12.5 Hz
from the downsampled representations of the Mel-spectrogram
(100 Hz) and supports a flexible lookahead attention with dy-
namic chunk sizes including 80ms, 160ms, 560ms, and 1,120ms.
For an efficient real-time application, we investigate the repre-
sentations across the encoder layer and prune the encoder layer
to 17 layers.
For IB, we adopt an iFSQ [49], which can guarantee both

With a narrow iFSQ and supervised learning, sparse super-

minimal paralinguistic detail. In addition, we introduce a hi-

by adapting accent style and timbre in a hierarchical manner,
unlike [38], which converts entire voice style from these sparse
semantic representations. The detailed accent adapter and timbre
adapter are described in the following subsections.
3.2. Accent Adaptation
To inject target accent characteristics while preserving the lin-
guistic information of sparse semantic tokens, we introduce an
accent adapter that reconstructs dense semantic tokens from
sparse representations. As the target dense semantic tokens, we
use CosyV oice 3 tokens that contain both linguistic and prosodic
information. Accordingly, we apply a causal transposed convo-
lutional layer to upsample the sparse semantic representation of
the iFSQ outputs (12.5Hz) by a ratio of 2 to match the target
resolution (25Hz). We concatenate the upsampled representa-
tion with accent style embedding extracted by CommonAccent
(XLS-R) [50] for accent adaptation. Then, the accent adapter
predicts the target accent tokens.
With the ASR encoder frozen, we jointly optimize the sparse
semantic tokenizer (including the iFSQ layer and CTC decoder)
and the accent adapter. The quantized representations are fed
into the CTC decoder for phoneme-based supervised learning
and into the accent adapter for dense semantic token prediction
from sparse semantic tokens. The final objective combines the
CTC lossl ctc and the cross-entropy lossl CE .
However, it is critical to prevent the CE loss gradients from
directly influencing the sparse tokens, as this may reintroduce
prosodic information into the representations. To mitigate the
potential information leakage, we further employ a gradient
reversal layer (GRL) [51, 52] instead of a stop-gradient before
the accent adapter, using a small reversal scale αgrl to avoid
training collapse. The final objective isLctc +λ ceLce. We set
λce = 1andα grl = 0.005for GRL.
For streaming accent adaptation, we adopt a fully causal
Transformer with rotary positional embeddings (RoPE) and a
limited context window of 6 seconds. We further employ a looka-
head convolutional layer to improve the robustness in streaming
scenarios.

3.3. Timbre Adaptation
After adapting the accent style, we subsequently adapt the tim-
bre style following CosyV oice 3 [31] which generates a Mel-
spectrogram using a causal diffusion Transformer (DiT) [ 53]
conditioned on timbre embeddings extracted by CAM++ [54].
First, the embedding of the predicted dense semantic tokens are
fed to a lookahead convolutional layer with a lookahead size
of 3 tokens (120ms) for robust streaming Mel-spectrogram gen-
eration. The upsampled representations are concatenated with
{xt,x ctx,s} before the DiT layers, where the noisy samplext
is obtained via a linear interpolation between noisex0 and the
target data point x1, xctx is a masked x1 as a prompt, and s
denotes the timbre embedding. Then, DiT estimates the vector
field, and we employ an Euler solver with 10 steps.
The generated Mel-spectrogram chunks are fed into a causal
neural vocoder for waveform synthesis. We use the pre-trained
HiFTNet [55] of CosyV oice 3 which uses four-frame lookahead
(80ms) for robust streaming waveform generation.
4. Experiment and Result
4.1. Dataset
We utilize LibriTTS [56], VCTK [57], and GLOBE V3 [58] to
learn diverse English accents. Although VCTK and GLOBE-V3
provide accent labels for approximately 164 English accents,
we do not rely on these potentially inaccurate annotations. In
our preliminary experiments, we failed to transfer the accent
style when using the predicted accent label. Instead, we leverage
hierarchical style adaptation with accent embeddings to enable
zero-shot AC without requiring explicit accent labels.
4.2. Training
We jointly train the sparse tokenizer and the accent adapter with
a learning rate of 1×10−4 and a batch size of 64 for 1.1M steps
(2 days) on eight NVIDIA A6000 GPUs. For efficient training,
we filter the dataset up to 10 seconds and use a limited context
windows of 6 seconds. We utilize the representations from the
17th layer of NeMo-ASR for effective speech disentanglement.
Also, we investigate the iFSQ configuration to find the optimal
IB size. In addition, we explore the streaming configuration,
including attention chunk sizes of 80ms, 160ms, 560ms, and
1,120ms for FastConformer, lookahead sizes of 0, 1 (80 ms),
2 (160 ms), and 3 (240 ms) for causal Transformer. For the
ablation study, we train all models for 50k steps. For Whisper,
Mimi, and NeMo-ASR, we train all models for 1.1M steps.
4.3. Evaluation
We conduct subjective evaluation using 480 samples of L2
speakers (20 samples for each 24 non-native speaker) in the
L2-ARCTIC [59]. The evaluation is performed by 20 listeners
(Amazon Mturk) using 5-scale. Due to the limited target classes
of CommonAccent, we evaluate objective metrics using 376 In-
dian samples in the VCTK. We use the target accent embeddings
extracted from the target L1 speakers (US) and use the target
timbre embeddings extracted from the source L2 speakers to
only convert the accent by preserving timbre style.

4.4. Accent Conversion
We compare our model with Vevo-Style, a publicly available
AC model. Vevo 1.5 [62] requires text for conversion so we do
not use it. As no open-source streaming AC model is currently
available, we instead compare demo samples of [28].
Table 2 demonstrates that our model achieves robust AC
performance in terms of accent accuracy and similarity by pre-
serving timbre information (SPK-SIM of 0.72). Our streaming
model exhibits significantly lower WER of 6.27 than the paral-
lel baseline Vevo-Style (WER of 13.8). Furthermore, despite
operating in a streaming manner, our model has comparable
performance in subjective evaluation including both naturalness
MOS (NMOS) and accent similarity MOS (AMOS).
In addition, sparsity in semantic tokens is critical for style
conversion, whereas a dense semantic tokenizer (CosyV oice 3)
fails to convert the accent. However, given that VC is primarily
designed to preserve the source intonation, this observation does
not necessarily indicate the overall superiority of the proposed
model.
4.5. Ablation Study
4.5.1. Layer-wise Analysis of ASR Encoder Representations
SSL representations such as Wav2Vec 2.0 [ 1, 42] have been
shown to encode different types of information across layers.
Specifically, earlier layers capture more acoustic information,
middle layers primarily encode linguistic context, and later layers
represent higher-level contextual information. We also conduct
a representation comparison across NeMo-ASR encoder layers.
Table 3 demonstrates a similar tendency in that earlier layers
capture more acoustic information, such as accent, middle layers
exhibit lower paralinguistic capacity that facilitates accent con-
version, and the final layer fails to accurately predict the dense
semantic tokens. We select the 17th layer representations as an
input to the sparse semantic tokenizer, considering the trade-off
between WER and AC performance.

4.5.2. FSQ and iFSQ
Table 4 demonstrates that a narrower IB of FSQ and iFSQ re-
duces paralinguistic capacity but also degrades speech intelli-
gibility, whereas a wider IB enhances the speech intelligibility
but tends to leak accent information. Furthermore, iFSQ shows
better capacity under the same settings. We adopt iFSQ with a
dimension of 128 and a scale of 9 (d128s9).
4.5.3. Stop Gradient and GRL Layer
It is essential to rely solely on supervised learning to prevent
paralinguistic information leakage. Without the stop-gradient
operation (Stop Grad.) applied before accent adapter, Table 5
shows that accent leakage occurs in the quantized representations.
Applying Stop Grad. blocks the gradients from audio-related
objectives (dense semantic token prediction), mitigating accent
leakage. Furthermore, applying GRL further reduces accent
leakage, achieving better attribute disentanglement.

4.5.5. Latency
We evaluate the robustness under different latency using different
ASR streaming chunk sizes and accent adapter lookahead sizes.
Table 7 shows that the lookahead layer is essential for robust
token prediction. We find that the ASR streaming chunk of
[70,1] and the accent adapter lookahead of 2 tokens provide a

4.5.6. Audio Encoder
We compare our model using different front-end encoders, in-
cluding a parallel ASR encoder (Whisper), a streaming acous-
tic codec (Mimi), and a streaming ASR encoder (NeMo-ASR).
When applying a strong FSQ of [5,5,3,3] to Whisper embed-
dings, parallel encoder achieves strong speech intelligibility with
robust AC. In contrast, an acoustic codec (Mimi) shows strong
attribute entanglement resulting in accent leakage at both the first
semantic VQ token and the pre-quantized representation (Pre-
quant.). Considering both streaming feasibility and performance,
we employ the NeMo-ASR in our proposed system.
5. Limitations
While our work achieves lower latency (0.5s) and a more efficient
cache-aware structure compared to chunk-wise generation of
[28] (0.8s latency), there is still room for improvement in real-
time interaction. Decoder latency can be reduced by integrating
the Mel decoder and vocoder via StreamFlow [29]. In addition,
MeanFlow [63, 32] can be explored for one-step generation.
6. Conclusion
This work presents AccentDrift, a streaming AC system via
sparse semantic tokenzation and a hierarchical style adaptation
framework. We carefully leverage sparse semantic tokenization
to prevent paralinguistic leakage, and analyze the effectiveness
of supervised learning and the risk of audio-related objectives
through the stop gradient operation and the GRL layer. Our
model successfully achieves real-time streaming AC with a min-
imal latency of 0.5s using efficient streaming architectures.
