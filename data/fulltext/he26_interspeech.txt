Abstract
Binaural audio can deliver an immersive listening experience,
and it is valuable for virtual reality, gaming, and creative con-
tent production. While recent text-to-audio models can generate
high-fidelity audio, they still struggle to generate binaural sig-
nals with directional control and accurate spatial perception. To
address this issue, we propose TTBA, a text-to-binaural audio
generation model. TTBA combines a cross-arranged discrete
representation with a transformer-based autoregressive back-
bone to jointly model semantic content and spatial cues. We
further design a spatial prompt encoder that maps explicit spa-
tial prompts into a conditioning representation, which is fused
with text features to guide binaural token generation. We use
a monaural generation model as guidance and emphasize both
content accuracy and inter-channel discrepancy. Experiments
on the BEWO-1M dataset show that TTBA can generate binau-
ral audio with spatial awareness and controllable directionality.
Index Terms: binaural audio generation, transformer, spatial
perception, spatial audio
1. Introduction
Spatial audio is an advanced audio technology that creates an
immersive auditory experience by simulating the propagation
and positioning of sound in three-dimensional space. Compared
to traditional monaural or stereo audio, its primary advantage
lies in its ability to accurately reproduce the direction, distance,
and even movement of sound sources, significantly enhancing
the realism and sense of presence in audio content [1, 2]. This
technology is widely used in virtual reality (VR), augmented
reality (AR), 360° video and professional sound design, play-
ing a pivotal role in shaping next-generation immersive media
experiences.
Binaural audio is a key form of spatial audio [3]. It uses spe-
cially recorded or algorithmically simulated two-channel sig-
nals to deliver subtly different audio to the listener’s left and
right ears, creating a realistic three-dimensional sound field il-
lusion in the brain [4, 5, 6, 7]. However, the traditional process
of producing high-quality binaural audio is both complex and
costly. It typically requires specialized recording equipment or
a sound engineer to perform tedious tasks such as sound source
positioning, mixing, and effects processing within a digital au-
dio workstation (DAW) [8]. This complexity severely limits
its scalability and widespread application. Therefore, exploring
methods that can rapidly and automatically generate binaural
audio from simple instructions while satisfying spatial require-
ments is of great research significance and strong practical po-
tential.

Numerous studies have been conducted on the synthesis of
mono-to-binaural audio. However, these works are not primar-
ily focused on binaural audio generation. Instead, they convert
existing monaural audio into binaural audio by leveraging hu-
man sound perception and incorporating positional information
of the sound source [9, 10, 11, 12] or video frame characteristics
[13, 14, 15, 16, 17]. In essence, this is a binaural audio render-
ing task. As a result, the synthesized binaural audio content is
limited and often fails to meet the demands of many practical
applications.
In recent years, advancements in deep learning have led to a
paradigm shift in generative audio models, particularly in Text-
to-Audio (T2A) generation. Existing models are now capable
of generating high-fidelity and diverse audio samples from nat-
ural language descriptions. These improvements largely rely on
powerful architectures such as transformers [18, 19, 20, 21] and
diffusion models [22, 23, 24, 25, 26].
Despite these significant advancements, a key limitation
persists in mainstream T2A systems: the generated audio is al-
most exclusively mono. Although some works [25] can produce
stereo audio, they still lack controllable spatial information. Re-
cent studies have begun to explore conditional, controllable spa-
tial audio generation. Rishit et al. proposed SEE-2-SOUND,
which first generates monaural audio from visual elements in
an image and then spatially positions it to create spatial audio
[27]. In contrast, Sun et al. employ the end-to-end diffusion
model to generate controllable binaural audio [28], and Heydari
et al. generate controllable first-order ambisonics (FOA) [29].
However, these methods can still produce spatial drift and lo-
calization errors, which reduce spatial controllability and desta-
bilize binaural cues.
To address these challenges, we propose TTBA, an end-to-
end spatial prompted text-to-binaural audio generation model.
To our knowledge, it is the first discrete-token-based framework
for binaural audio generation. The contributions are summa-
rized as follows:
• We propose a cross-arranged scheme for the left and right
channels, enabling the model to capture semantic content and
inter-channel spatial relationships within a unified discrete
representation space.
• We introduce a spatial prompt encoder that encodes explicit
source spatial prompts into conditioning features and fuses
them with text prompts, providing more effective guidance
for controllable binaural generation.
• To stabilize content generation, we leverage a monaural gen-
eration model for content guidance and employ a multi-loss
training strategy that emphasizes both content accuracy and
inter-channel discrepancy.

2. Methodology
2.1. Overall structure
The proposed text-driven binaural audio generation framework
consists of four main components: a text encoder, an audio
encoder-decoder, a spatial prompt encoder and a transformer-
based audio language model, as illustrated in Fig. 1. Given a
natural language description with spatial informationText, our
goal is to first use the Text Encoder to convert the text into a
semantic representationE text. We then employ a spatial prompt
encoder that takesE text together with compact source spatial
promptspas inputs, where each source is specified by its start
direction, end direction, and a scalar motion speed. The spa-
tial prompt encoder produces a joint conditioncondthat inte-
grates the textual description with the desired spatial behavior.
On the audio side, the binaural audio is discretely compressed
via the audio encoder, resulting in a sequence of discrete tokens
x= (x l,x r). The audio language model (ALM) then performs
conditional modeling in this discrete representation space and
outputs the predicted binaural audio tokens˜x= (˜xl,˜xr).

2.2. Binaural audio discretization
To effectively model binaural signals, we employ a pre-trained
monaural Encodec [30] neural encoder to convert waveforms
into discrete audio codebook representations. Unlike monau-
ral audio generation, binaural signals require capturing both the
differences and relationships between the left and right ears.
To address this, we introduce a cross-arranged strategy in the
design of the codebook: the codebooks for the two channels
are fused during the encoding stage, enabling the model to not
only learn the content features of each individual channel but
also model the inter-channel dependencies in the latent space.
Specifically, after the left and right channels of the input audio

2.4. Audio language model
The Audio Language Model (ALM) is the core component
of the entire framework. We adopt a Transformer-based au-
toregressive structure [18], which, with its powerful sequence
modeling capabilities, can capture long-range dependencies,
thereby generating coherent and semantically consistent audio
sequences. In this model, text features are injected as conditions
through a cross-attention mechanism, ensuring that the gener-
ated output is highly consistent with the input description.
For text encoding, we use the pre-trained T5-large [31]
model to obtain semantic representations. Given the variabil-
ity in input length for audio generation tasks, we adopt the
max lengthpadding strategy for T5, ensuring alignment of
batch inputs while avoiding positional encoding ambiguities.
This design improves training stability and minimizes biases
caused by length discrepancies during generation. By com-
bining T5 with the Transformer ALM architecture, our model
strikes a good balance between semantic understanding and se-
quence generation.
2.5. Training and inference strategy
In the task of binaural audio generation, accurately capturing
both semantic content and spatial information is challenging,
especially given the complexity of modeling the spatial aspects
of sound. To address this, we adopt a knowledge distillation
strategy. During the training phase, we use a pre-trained text-to-
monaural audio generation model as the teacher model [18], fix-
ing its parameters to provide guidance signals for content gen-
eration. By distilling knowledge from this teacher, the binaural
audio generation model can benefit from its pre-learned capa-
bilities for content generation, such as capturing the semantic
meaning of the text descriptions.
Next, we use only half of the layers from the teacher model
to obtain a smaller student model for text-binaural audio gen-
eration. The student model is then initialized with parameters
extracted from the teacher model and fine-tuned on a spatial au-
dio dataset. This enables the student model to simultaneously
learn both semantic content and spatial information, which is
crucial for accurately generating binaural audio.
Additionally, we introduce Classifier-Free Guidance (CFG)
to enhance the controllability of the generation process. Specif-
ically, during training, both conditional and unconditional pre-
dictions are performed to refine the model’s ability to handle
different input conditions. During generation, a coefficientcfg
is used to adjust the weighting between the two, striking a bal-
ance between content fidelity and spatial controllability, ensur-
ing that the generated audio aligns with the text description
while preserving accurate spatial cues.

discrepancy, we optimize TTBA with a two-term objective:

3. Experiments and Analysis
3.1. Datasets and implementation details
We use the text-audio subset of the BEWO-1M dataset [28]
for training and evaluation, which provides paired text captions
and spatial prompts for binaural audio, totaling over 1M au-
dio–caption pairs (about 2.8k hours). Following [28], we adopt
the official train/validation/test splits and evaluate on four sub-
sets: Single Static (SS, one stationary source), Double Static
(DS, two stationary sources), Single Dynamic (SD, one moving
source), and Mixed (Mix, one to four sources with both station-
ary and moving sounds).
We use EnCodec with4codebooks×2048bins for au-
dio tokenization. The text encoder is T5-large with max length
= 128, and the dimension is1536. Transformer-ALM is a
decoder-only autoregressive Transformer with 24 layers, dim
= 1536, and 24 heads. The teacher is an autoregressive
Transformer with 48 layers, dim= 1536, and 24 heads. We
train with Adam [32] using a learning rate of5×10 −5 and
β= (0.9,0.999).
3.2. Evaluation metrics
We conduct both quantitative and perceptual evaluations. For
quantitative assessment, we reportFD openl3 [33] and FAD [34]
computed between generated audio and ground-truth to mea-
sure audio similarity (lower is better),KL passt [35] to quan-
tify semantic correspondence via label-distribution divergence
(lower is better), andCLAP score [36] to evaluate text-audio
alignment (higher is better).
Since binaural generation also requires correct spatial cues,
we additionally compute interaural metrics between the left and
right channels, including interaural level difference (ILD), in-
teraural time difference (ITD), and interaural cross-correlation
(IACC). We then measure the error between the generated audio
and the ground truth using three spatial metrics DILD, DITD,
and DIACC [37, 38], defined as the MAE of the corresponding
ILD, ITD, and IACC. We compute ILD using an STFT with an
FFT size of 1024 and a hop size of 512, and we compute ITD
and IACC using a 20 ms window with 50% overlap.
For perceptual evaluation, we use Overall Quality (OVL)
for general audio quality [18], Text Relevance (REL) for how
well the audio matches the text content [18], and Spatialization
Quality (SPQ) for spatial accuracy. These metrics can provide
a comprehensive assessment of audio quality.

3.4. Ablation study
To validate the effectiveness of our proposed method, we con-
duct a series of ablation experiments, as shown in Table 1. All
experiments are evaluated after 10K training steps.
w/o distill denotes that the pre-trained monaural audio gen-
eration model is not used to guide content learning. The results
show that, under the same number of training steps, remov-
ing the guidance from the monaural audio generation model
prevents the network from learning accurate textual seman-
tic information, leading to worse performance across all met-
rics, demonstrating the effectiveness of our proposed training
strategy. w/o SPE denotes using text prompts only, without
any explicit source spatial prompts. The results show that
this results in much smaller changes in content-related metrics.
CLAPscore is nearly unchanged in most cases, suggesting that
textual prompts remain sufficient to determine the overall se-
mantic content. However, spatial metrics consistently worsen,
especially DILD, confirming that explicit spatial prompts are
crucial for accurate interaural level difference. Overall, these
results verify that monaural guidance primarily benefits content
learning, while SPE is key to improving spatial cue accuracy.
4. Conclusion
This paper proposes TTBA, an end-to-end framework for text-
to-binaural audio generation. By cross-arranging the discrete
encodings of the left and right channels, TTBA effectively cap-
tures both semantic content and spatial relationships between
the channels, enabling the generation of binaural audio directly
from text descriptions. We design a comprehensive training
strategy that integrates cross-entropy loss and knowledge dis-
tillation, further enhanced by classifier-free guidance, ensuring
high-fidelity and spatially controllable binaural audio genera-
tion. Experimental results on the BEWO-1M dataset show that
TTBA outperforms existing methods in multiple metrics, par-
ticularly under complex multi-source conditions, and achieves
outstanding results in both content accuracy and spatial percep-
tion. Future work will focus on further enhancing spatial real-
ism and extending the model to more complex audio environ-
ments.
