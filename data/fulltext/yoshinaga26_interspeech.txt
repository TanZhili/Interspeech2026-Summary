Abstract 
Sounds generated by turbulence at the glottis, such as those 
observed in breathy voice, affect voice quality. Although 
aerodynamic noise originating in the vocal tract has been 
incorporated in one-dimensional (1D) acoustic models, the 
accuracy of these models has rarely been examined. In this 
study, we evaluated the scaling factor used in a 1D noise model 
for vowel /a/ and /u/ by comparing with results from three -
dimensional (3D) flow simulations that explicitly computes 
sound generation from turbulent airflow. Results showed that, 
for normal phonation in the vowel /a/, the conventional scaling 
factor yielded good agreement with the 3D results. In contrast, 
in breathy phonation where the glottis did not fully close, and 
in configurations with supraglottal constrictions of vowel /u/, 
the optimal scaling factor changed from the conventional value. 
These findings suggest that turbulence noise modeling in 1D 
approaches needs to be adjusted to the flow configuration of the 
vocal tract. 
Index Terms: voice production, computational fluid dynamics, 
breathy voice, turbulence 
1. Introduction 
When humans produce voice, airflow from the lungs induces 
self-sustained oscillation of the vocal folds through interaction 
with their elastic properties. The resulting periodic vibration 
modulates the lung airflow into a pulsating flow, whose 
temporal variation acts as the primary sound source. Variations 
in this flow waveform modify the harmonic structure of the 
voice, thereby generating a wide variety of voice qualities [1]. 
In addition to this harmonic structure, voice also contains noise 
components originating from the turbulent airflow, which 
becomes particularly prominent in phonation types such as 
breathy voice. The goal of this study is to establish a model of 
this noise generation in voice production. 
Various numerical models have been proposed to describe 
vocal fold vibration and its acoustic consequences. One of the 
simplest approaches is the Rothenberg model [2], which 
directly prescribes the glottal flow waveform, where voice 
quality is controlled by parameters such as the open quotient or 
the slope of the flow waveform. However, this approach 
assumes complete source-filter separation and does not 
explicitly link the flow rate to vocal fold dynamics.  
Alternatively, the flow rate can be derived from vocal fold 
motion and the time-varying glottal area, providing a direct 
relationship between the vocal fold vibration and voice quality. 
When the flow is computed from the transglottal pressure 
without feedback to vocal fold vibration, this condition is 
referred to as the Level 1 interaction [3]. Models, in which vocal 
tract shape influences vocal fold vibration, are said to have the 
Level 2 interaction and become essential in conditions of strong 
source-filter coupling such as singing [4]. 
While these modeling approaches have provided significant 
insights into the relationship between vocal fold vibration and 
harmonic structure, they often neglect non -harmonic 
components. In particular, when voice disorder such as vocal 
fold paralysis causes persistent glottal gap, it is expected that 
turbulence-induced noise becomes significant due to increased 
flow rate. Fant [5] modeled this aerodynamic noise using the 
Reynolds number and an arbitrary scaling value based on 
Meyer-Eppler’s findings [6] for fricatives. This model has since 
been widely used in fricative-noise simulations [7-10] and has 
also been applied to synthesize breathy voice in 1D acoustic 
models [11]. In breathy phonation, similar to fricatives, a jet 
emerging from the glottal constriction is thought to generate 
noise when it impinges on the wall of the epiglottis or other 
pharyngeal structures. However, the flow during phonation is 
pulsating, which differs from the quasi-steady conditions of 
fricatives. In addition, previous studies [11] have not 
sufficiently examined the arbitrary parameter in Fant’s 
formulation, and its validity therefore requires further 
investigation. 
Recent advances in computational power have enabled the 
prediction of acoustic characteristics of fricatives by directly 
solving the Navier-Stokes equations, which describe flow 
motion including inertia and viscosity [12 -13]. However, 
although one previous study has analyzed glottal fricatives [14], 
very few studies have investigated noise generation during 
voice production or specifically in breathy voice. 
       Therefore, in this study, we examine the validity of the 
arbitrary parameter used in the noise model by comparing 3D 
airflow simulations with a 1D acoustic model incorporating 
Fant’s noise formulation for both normal phonation and breathy 
voice production in vowel /a/ and /u/. This work enables more 
accurate estimation of aerodynamic noise within simplified 
one-dimensional models, allowing efficient analysis with 
significantly reduced computational cost. 
2. Methods 
2.1. 3D voice production model

Stokes equations were performed to predict sound generated by 
airflow in both the glottis and the vocal tract. The Navier-Stokes 
equations are the governing equations of airflow motion that 
account for inertial and viscous effects, enabling prediction of 
sound generated by flow fluctuations. The volume penalization 
(VP) method [15], an immersed boundary method, was 
employed to incorporate Level 2 interaction between the 
airflow and vocal fold vibration. The Navier-Stokes equations 
with the VP method are written as

An overview of the computational setup is shown in Fig. 1. 
The simulation domain consists of a flow channel from a 
pressure chamber representing the lungs to the exit of the vocal 
tract. A constant pressure of 1200 Pa was imposed as an inflow 
boundary condition at the entrance of the pressure chamber, and 
the pressure propagation within the trachea was computed. The 
geometry of trachea was constructed based on the cross -
sectional areas measured by Story [19]. This subglottal pressure 
excites vocal fold vibration, and the generated sound propagates 
through the vocal tract and radiates from a baffle that represents 
the face. The vocal tract geometry was constructed as an 
axisymmetric flow channel based on the area functions of /a/ 
and /u/ [19]. The area functions are plotted in Fig. 2. For /a/, a 
narrow passage of approximately 0.3 cm2 extends from the 
epiglottis to the pharynx and expands toward the oral cavity to 
about 5 cm2. In contrast, for /u/, the area first widens to 
approximately 6 cm2 in the pharynx and then constricts to about 
0.2 cm2 between the tongue and palate, and expands again 
toward the lips. These geometries were used to examine how 
turbulence generated within the vocal tract affects voice quality.

2.2. 1D voice production model 
In the 1D flow model, acoustic analysis was performed using 
the glottal flow rate U3D obtained from the 3D flow simulation 
to assess the ability of the 1D model to predict turbulence -
induced sound. 1D acoustic models have been used in many 
studies because they allow vowel synthesis at significantly 
reduced computational cost. In this study, we employed the 
waveguide model [20], specifically the "Tube Talker" 
implementation [21-23]. As shown in Fig. 3, the volume 
velocity was prescribed at the inlet, and the forward - and 
backward-traveling waves were computed. The vocal tract was 
divided into 44 waveguide sections, based on the cross -
sectional areas shown in Fig. 2. Each section has a length of 
approximately 4 mm. Viscous losses were taken into account 
at the walls, and a radiation impedance corresponding to a 
piston with an infinite baffle was imposed at the outlet. 
One-dimensional flow models lack spatial dimensions to 
model the turbulent flows. As such, Fant [5] proposed a model 
with additive noise flow component:

3. Results and discussion 
3.1. Vowel /a/ 
First, we present the results of the 3D simulations for the vowel 
/a/. To modify the vibratory characteristics of the vocal folds, 
the initial glottal opening area Ag0 was varied from 1.0 mm2 to 
8.2 mm2. Figure 4 shows the waveforms of the glottal area and 
flow rate. For Ag0 up to 7.0 mm², the vocal folds achieved the 
complete closure during vibration, whereas the vocal folds 
could not maintain the complete closure  when Ag0 was 
increased to 8.2 mm². In each of the three cases, the glottis 
opened more rapidly than it closed, a characteristic that reflects 
the simplified geometry of the vocal folds and the physical 
parameter values chosen for these simulations.  The 
fundamental frequencies were 146, 145, and 131 Hz for Ag0 
=1.0, 7.0, and 8.2 mm2, respectively. The flow rate waveforms 
showed that, for Ag0	=1.0 mm2, the closed phase was longer 
than for Ag0=7.0 mm². At Ag0 =7.0 mm², the opening occurred 
earlier, and reverse flow was observed during the opening 
phase. Since the lung pressure was constant in all cases, the 
maximum glottal flow rate was nearly the same for all 
conditions. 
 
 
Figure 4: Glottal area and flow rate waveforms for /a/ 
in the 3D simulation. 
 
Figure 5 presents the instantaneous velocity field in the 
central plane of the vocal folds and vocal tract of /a/. The blue 
color indicates the relatively low flow velocity region, whereas 
the green and red regions indicate higher flow velocities. The 
small patterns indicate the turbulent airflow in the vocal tract. 
The highest velocity, approximately 50 m/s, appeared at the 
glottal exit, followed by another high velocity region in the 
pharynx. After the flow left in the pharyngeal region, the flow 
disturbances decayed, and the low-velocity airflow propagated 
toward the oral exit.

plane of vocal tract /a/. 
 
Using U3D obtained from the 3D model (Fig. 4), the 1D 
voice simulation was performed. The scaling factor 𝛼 in the 
noise model (Eq. (4)) was varied, and the resulting spectra were 
compared with the pressure spectrum at the mouth outlet in the 
3D simulation. The mean difference at each frequency between 
the 3D and 1D pressure spectra is shown in Fig. 6. The error 
bars indicate the standard deviation across eight realizations of 
the random noise signal. When Ag0=1.0mm², the smallest 
difference was achieved with 𝛼=6×10%’, whereas for Ag0=
5.0mm² and 7.0mm², the minimum difference was observed at 
𝛼=4×10%’. This is consistent with a previous study that 
adopted 𝛼=4×10%’ from the listening test [11]. In contrast, 
when Ag0	=8.2	mm² and the vocal folds did not achieve 
closure, the smallest difference was observed with 𝛼=0.

Figure 7 plots the spectra for the cases with Ag0 =1.0 and 
8.2	mm2. When the vocal folds vibrated with full closures (Ag0 
=1.0 mm2), harmonics were generated up to high frequencies, 
and the 1D model was able to predict these harmonics up to 
5000 Hz. The magnitude of the noise generated by turbulence 
downstream from the glottis appeared as the depth of the 
troughs, and the 1D model with 𝛼=0 underestimated the noise 
generation. By setting 𝛼=6×10%’, the noise band between 
2000 and 5000 Hz was amplified, resulting in good agreement 
of the spectral shape with the 3D model.  
In contrast, when Ag0=8.2 mm2, harmonics were limited to 
approximately 2000 Hz, and the sound above 2000 Hz were 
dominated mainly by aerodynamic noise. In the 1D model, even 
with 𝛼=0(i.e., without introducing the noise model), the 
resulting sound pressure level showed similar levels to those of 
the 3D model. These results suggest that, for vocal fold 
vibrations which achieved closure (i.e., normal phonation), the 
conventional arbitrary value of 4×10%’can appropriately 
estimate the noise component. In contrast, for vibrations 
without closure, using the same constant may lead to an 
overestimation of the noise generated in the vocal tract unless 
the parameter is adjusted. It should be noted as a limitation, the 
input of U3D to the 1D model already contains some 
disturbances generated within the vocal tract of the 3D model, 
and these disturbances may have been amplified within the 
vocal tract.

3.2. Vowel /u/ 
For the vocal tract of /u/, the initial glottal opening area Ag0 was 
varied from 0 mm2 to 1.0 mm2. When Ag0 was increased beyond 
1.0 mm², the self-oscillation could not be sustained and 
eventually ceased. Figure 8 shows the waveforms of the glottal 
area and flow rate for the vocal tract of /u/. In both cases, the 
vocal folds achieved complete closure during oscillation, and 
the closed period was slightly longer for Ag0 = 0 mm2 The 
fundamental frequencies were 160, 162 Hz for Ag0 = 0, and 1.0 
mm2, respectively. The maximum glottal flow rates were 
almost the same between the two cases. 
 
 
Figure 8: Glottal area and flow rate waveforms for /u/ 
in the 3D simulation. 
 
Figure 9 presents the instantaneous velocity field in the 
central plane of the vocal folds and vocal tract of /u/. The flow 
disturbance observed immediately downstream of the vocal 
folds for /a/ was also observed in the /u/ configuration. 
Meanwhile, because the cross-sectional area increased in the 
pharynx, the disturbance generated at the glottis temporarily 
dissipated. As the airflow passed through the constriction 
formed by the palate and tongue, the flow accelerated again and 
formed a jet in the oral cavity. The flow velocity in the mouth 
reached approximately 30 m/s. Although this velocity was 
lower than values typically reported for fricatives (roughly 40 
m/s) [14], it may act as an additional sound source. 
 
 
Figure 9: Instantaneous flow velocity at the central 
plane of vocal tract /u/. 
 
Using U3D obtained from the 3D model, the 1D voice 
simulation was performed, and the scaling factor 𝛼 in the noise 
model was varied in the same way as for vowel /a/. Figure 10 
shows the spectral difference in mouth pressure between the 1D 
and 3D models. For both cases of Ag0	=0 and 1.0 mm2, the 
smallest difference between the 1D and 3D models was 
achieved at 𝛼=14×10%’. This value was much larger than 
𝛼=4×10%’ for the vowel /a/, indicating that stronger 
aerodynamic noise is generated in the vocal tract of /u/ than in 
/a/.  
Figure 11 plots the spectrum for Ag0	=1.0	mm2. The 
generated sounds contained harmonics up to high frequencies 
in the same way as Ag0	=1.0	mm2 of vowel /a/, and the overall 
tendencies of the 1D and 3D models showed good agreement. 
The first formant, appeared at approximately 300 Hz, was 
consistent in both analyses. However, the second formant 
appeared at 1600 Hz in the 3D model and 1300 Hz in the 1D 
model. Since the second resonance frequency of the transfer 
characteristics without airflow was approximately 1290 Hz, the 
higher frequency observed in the 3D model suggests that jet 
flow modified the acoustic resonance characteristics, whereas 
this effect was not captured by the 1D model. 
When 𝛼=0 in the 1D model, the noise floor was 
underestimated, whereas setting 𝛼=14×10%’ improved the 
agreement with the 3D model. These results suggest that the 
noise scaling factor may need to be adjusted depending on the 
vocal tract geometry. Because a constriction is formed within 
the vocal tract for the vowel /u/, introducing an additional noise 
source within the oral cavity, which is similar to approaches 
used previous modeling for fricatives [9-10], may eliminate the 
need to adjust the scaling factor, and this will be investigated in 
future work.

4. Conclusions 
This study investigated the validity of a turbulence noise model 
in 1D acoustic simulations by comparing it with 3D 
compressible flow simulations of phonation. The 3D 
simulations, incorporating fluid-structure interaction and a two-
mass vocal fold model, enabled prediction of sound generation 
under different initial glottal openings, and the resulting glottal 
flow waveforms were then used as input to a 1D waveguide 
model, and the noise model was evaluated by adjusting its 
scaling factor. For vowel /a/ production with complete glottal 
closure, the conventional scaling factor provided good 
agreement between 1D and 3D models, indicating that the 
simplified model can reasonably estimate turbulence-induced 
noise. However, when glottal closure was incomplete in /a/ or 
when a supraglottal constriction was present in /u/, the optimal 
scaling factor differed from the conventional value, suggesting 
that the appropriate scaling depends on flow configuration. 
These findings suggest the need for vowel -dependent or 
spatially distributed noise modeling. Future work will focus on 
localized noise sources and the influence of constriction cross-
sectional shape on turbulence noise generation across a wider 
range of voice conditions.
