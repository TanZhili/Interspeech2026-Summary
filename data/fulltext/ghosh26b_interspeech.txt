Abstract
Lip-to-Speech synthesis aims to generate intelligible, natural-
sounding, and temporally synchronized speech from silent vi-
sual recordings of a speaker. Although recent end-to-end mod-
els have shown promising results, they require large amounts
of training data and often lack out-of-dataset generalization.
In this work, we introduce LipAdapter, a generic and efficient
modular framework that adapts an existing frozen pre-trained
Text-to-Speech model into a lip-synchronized speech generator.
LipAdapter bridges the modularity gap between frozen Visual
Speech Recognition and Text-to-Speech models by aligning
phoneme representation and lip embeddings via a novel text-
to-video alignment module. Our model achieves state-of-the-art
performance across multiple benchmarks in English while using
15× less training data than previous methods. We also demon-
strate the model’s zero-shot capabilities in multilingual lip-to-
speech synthesis in French, German, Spanish and Portuguese.
Code and demo are available at: https://lipadapter.
github.io/.
Index Terms: Lip-to-speech, video guided speech synthesis
1. Introduction
Lip-to-speech synthesis seeks to reconstruct intelligible,
natural-sounding, and lip-synced speech from silent visual
recordings of individuals speaking. It has applications in assis-
tive technologies for people with speech impairments, commu-
nication in noisy environments, and the restoration of speech in
corrupted or silent videos. Recent advances have led to pow-
erful end-to-end models for silent video-to-speech synthesis,
which directly map video frames to audio waveforms [1, 2, 3].
Although these systems have demonstrated good perfor-
mance, their speech quality often deteriorates when applied to
real-world conditions. The limitations may arise due to their
speech generation components being trained on datasets curated
for tasks such as Visual Speech Recognition (VSR), audiovisual
speech recognition (A VSR). These datasets lack high-quality,
clean speech samples on which modern Text-to-Speech (TTS)
models are trained, leading to lower naturalness and expressive-
ness in generated speech. In addition, directly feeding raw vi-
sual information into the speech synthesis stage can introduce
irrelevant variability and effectively act as noise, further degrad-
ing output quality. Other methods such as Lipvoicer [4] and
Accurate L2S [5] predict text from a silent video using a VSR
model, and train the speech generation pipeline by conditioning
on both visual and textual guidance. They achieve a lower word
error rate (WER) than video-based models but still suffer from
limited generalization as their speech component is trained from
scratch. Moreover, training both video-only and text-video Lip-
to-speech systems require over 430 hours of data, making it dif-

In contrast, recent TTS models [6, 7] already generalize well
across languages, styles and speakers. We propose treating lip-
to-speech not as a separate end-to-end problem but as adapt-
ing a pretrained TTS through a lightweight LipAdapter net-
work. Our adapter serves as an information bottleneck between
the pretrained frozen TTS and VSR modules by introducing
a novel Text-to-Video Alignment mechanism between the text
embeddings fed into the TTS model and lip embeddings ex-
tracted from silent videos. This provides phoneme-level du-
rations aligned with the lip movements, enabling the TTS to
generate speech with precise synchronization. Therefore, un-
like prior methods that train the full speech components from
scratch, we only focus on training the alignment, allowing us
to achieve robust performance with less than 30 hours of video
data. Our contributions are:
1. We adapt an off-the-shelf frozen pre-trained TTS model for
Lip-to-Speech using LipAdapter that utilizes a novel text-
to-video monotonic alignment network, which predicts lip-
guided phoneme durations enabling the TTS to generate
speech that is synchronized with the silent video.
2. Our experiments demonstrate that our proposed method
achieves competitive, and in some cases superior, perfor-
mance in terms of speech quality, word error rate (WER), and
lip-sync accuracy on English lip-to-speech tasks, using LRS3
and MultiVSR. It also demonstrates stable zero-shot transfer
to unseen languages in multilingual lip-to-speech synthesis,
while being trained on only 30 hours of LRS3 videos instead
of 430 hours.

These temporally regulated phoneme representations serve as
inputs to ZMM TTS, a state-of-the-art multilingual TTS model.
It comprises of two modules: Text2Vec and Vec2Wav. Text2Vec
converts the aligned phoneme representations into a sequence of
discrete code indices V derived from a pretrained multilingual
wav2vec 2.0 (XLSR-53) model. Subsequently, these indices are
mapped through the XLSR-53 codebookC to obtain the corre-
sponding discrete representations R, which serve as inputs to
the Vec2Wav module which transforms the discrete represen-
tations into an audio waveform. Thus, our novel text-to-video
alignment network effectively serves as a lip-guided phoneme
duration predictor, enabling TTS to synthesize speech that is
both content-accurate and temporally synchronized with the vi-
sual input. We train the alignment module using a monotonic
alignment likelihood objective [12] inspired by CTC [13], to-
gether with a path-concentration regularizer that sharpens soft
attention along the MAS-derived monotonic trajectory. To bet-
ter adapt the model, we also lightly fine-tuned the Text2Vec

3. Experimental Setup
Dataset: LipAdapter is compared against the baselines on
LRS3 [14], a highly challenging in-the-wild English dataset.
To evaluate cross-dataset generalizability, we test LipAdapter
and other state-of-the-art approaches on 500 English sam-
ples from MultiVSR [15], a recently released multilingual
dataset that includes videos sourced and transcribed from di-
verse YouTube content, making it particularly suitable for test-
ing out-of-distribution robustness. We use the MultiVSR testset
to assess the zero-shot multilingual performance of the model.
Metrics: We evaluate the effectiveness of our model using both
quantitative metrics (i.e. WER [16], LSE-C and LSE-D [17])
and human subjective assessments via Mean Opinion Scores
(MOS). To objectively quantify speech quality and intelligibil-
ity, we adopt the same metrics used in LipV oicer, specifically
DNSMOS [18] and STOI-Net [19].
4. Experimental Results
4.1. Baselines
We compare LipAdapter with prior methods that report re-
sults on LRS3 [14]. We consider two categories of models:
(i) video-only methods, which generate speech directly from
silent video input [10, 11, 3, 2, 1], and (ii) video and text meth-
ods [5, 4], which incorporate predicted text as additional guid-
ance alongside the visual input. Our approach belongs to the
latter category, as we leverage a TTS system guided by align-
ment with video-derived features. For evaluating V2SFlow [1],
DiffV2S [2], and Intelligible L2S [3], we directly use the gener-
ated samples available in the V2SFlow GitHub repository1. For
LipV oicer [4] and Accurate L2S [5], we perform inference on
the standard LRS3 testset comprising of 1321 samples.
4.2. Results in English
Table 1 summarizes our results. Notably, while prior mod-
els typically rely on 430 hours of training data, we achieve
comparable and in some cases superior performance with only
30 hours of data. In terms of speech quality and Word Er-
ror Rate (WER), our method achieves state-of-the-art results
among comparable systems. We also achieve strong lip-
synchronization performance, surpassing several previous ap-
proaches on LRS3. We observe that video-only models tend to
produce good synchronization scores, because their audio out-
put is directly conditioned on the visual speech signal. How-
ever, these methods frequently exhibit higher WERs because
mapping visemes to speech is inherently ambiguous, leading to
mispronunciations or incorrect words.
The most significant challenge for video-only architectures
arises when these models are applied to datasets like MultiVSR,
which are not seen during training. We take the best-performing
model from the Video-only set and the best performing model
from the Video+Text set to quantify the results. Table 1 shows
that although V2SFlow achieves perfect synchronization and
clear speech on this dataset, its WER has increased significantly
making the pipeline unsuitable for being used. We notice that
text modality greatly helps Lipvoicer to achieve comparable re-
sults to us. However even with just 30 hours of data we sur-
pass Lipvoicer in synchronization, speech quality and WER.
Although Lipvoicer is the closest alternative in terms of general-
izability, its diffusion backbone makes it slow, producing 5 s of
audio takes 50 s on an NVIDIA 2080 Ti, compared to 6 s with
our pipeline. Human Evaluation: We conduct a human sub-
jective study using Mean Opinion Scores (MOS) across three
dimensions: naturalness, intelligibility, and lip synchronization.

methods, along with the ground truth are shuffled and rated by

4.3. Zero-shot multilingual generalization
Our approach further demonstrates the ability to generalize to
unseen languages without additional training. As shown in Ta-
ble 3, our model trained on only 30 h of English videos general-
izes without any additional training in French, German, Por-
tuguese and Spanish. These findings indicate that the text-
to-video alignment module learns a robust cross-modal map-
ping between visual speech articulations and their correspond-
ing phonemes that generalize to language combinations not en-
countered during training. To the best of our knowledge, this
is the first lip-to-speech system to demonstrate stable zero-shot
multilingual transfer under such limited training data. For com-
parison, we also perform zero-shot multilingual inference using
LipV oicer and report the corresponding results. Despite em-
ploying a multilingual tokenizer, LipV oicer exhibits substan-
tially higher WER across all languages. WER exceeds 100%
in all cases, indicating that the number of insertion errors in the
ASR transcription of the synthesized speech surpasses the num-
ber of words in the reference transcript.
4.4. Ablation Study
Impact of Frozen TTS and Backbone Generalization: As
shown in Table 5, freezing the entire TTS backbone and train-
ing only the alignment module yields performance compara-
ble to end-to-end training, demonstrating that LipAdapter oper-
ates independently of the internal TTS parameters and modifies
only phoneme durations. To further validate backbone gener-
ality, we replace ZMM-TTS with a FastSpeech2-style length-
regulated TTS model trained on XPhoneBERT phoneme em-
beddings. LipAdapter integrates without architectural modi-
fication, supplying only phoneme durations to the new back-
bone. Although performance decreases relative to the stronger
ZMM-TTS setup, largely due to the simpler TTS model and
non-neural vocoder, the results confirm that LipAdapter re-
mains structurally compatible with different length-regulated
TTS backbones.

4.5. Error Analysis and Limitations
We analyze the principal failure modes of LipAdapter on in-
the-wild videos, where errors can accumulate across multiple
stages of the pipeline. These include (i) incorrect VSR tran-
scriptions, (ii) text-to-video misalignment and (iii) transcrip-
tion noise introduced during WER computation due to imper-
fections in the Automatic Speech Recognition model(Whisper-
Large [20]) used for evaluation. Table 7 reports the error break-
down of the VSR and ASR components in multilingual scenar-
ios. The ASR error is computed by running the ASR on the
ground truth audio. The high ASR error for these audio files in-
dicates that the measured WER is a conservative estimate, and
the true intelligibility of our outputs may be higher.
We further analyze how synchronization confidence varies
with input video length. As shown in Figure 3, very short utter-
ances (below 1 s) exhibit noticeably lower LSE-C scores, indi-
cating weaker lip-sync. This degradation is expected, because
short clips provide a limited temporal context for the monotonic
alignment module to reliably estimate phoneme durations. Per-
formance improves substantially for utterances longer than 2 s,
where the alignment becomes more stable and synchronization
confidence increases. This suggests that LipAdapter benefits
from sufficient temporal evidence and that handling extremely
short segments remains an open challenge.
5. Conclusion
We introduced LipAdapter, a modular and generalized lip-to-
speech framework that connects state-of-the-art visual speech
recognition and text-to-speech models via a novel text-to-video
alignment module. Experiments on LRS3 and MultiVSR show
that LipAdapter delivers strong results in terms of speech qual-
ity, intelligibility, and synchronization while using far less train-
ing data than prior works. The model is capable of performing
zero-shot multilingual lip-to-speech paving the way for future
research in low-resource languages. We believe that LipAdapter
opens a promising future direction towards video-guided speech
generation in accessibility, broadcasting, and human-computer
interaction. Additional analyses, including oracle text evalu-
ation, VSR error propagation, alignment robustness, extended
ablations and visual results are provided in the supplementary
material.
