Abstract
High-quality speech corpora require consistent alignment
between spoken and written text, yet the many-to-many map-
ping between them introduces ambiguity, leading to transcrip-
tion inconsistencies that ultimately degrade performance on
downstream tasks. To mitigate this inherent ambiguity, we pro-
pose a data-centric framework that leverages IPA-guided dual
transcription to achieve consistent spoken–written alignment.
The framework combines (1) a phoneme intermediate condi-
tional speech-to-text model that outputs both IPA and ortho-
graphic text, and (2) a fine-tuned large language model that gen-
erates consistent spoken–written pairs from orthographic and
phonetic inputs. By grounding normalization in phonetic real-
izations rather than text alone, the framework enables iterative
refinement of noisy or domain-specific corpora. Experiments
show that IPA-guided dual transcription yields substantial per-
formance improvements in text normalization and domain ASR
through corpus refinement.
Index Terms: speech recognition, phoneme intermediate con-
ditioning, dual text transcription, large language model
1. Introduction
The development of robust speech recognition and synthesis
systems depends on high-quality speech corpora with consistent
alignment between what is spoken and what is written. Con-
structing such corpora is difficult because speech and text are
linked by a complex many-to-many mapping: the same written
form can be realized in multiple spoken ways, and the same pro-
nunciation can be written in multiple ways. This spoken–written
ambiguity degrades both transcription quality and downstream
model performance, and complicates the curation and refine-
ment of large-scale speech data. We therefore frame the central
problem as data-centric speech corpus refinement: obtaining re-
liable spoken and written text pairs so that corpora can be im-
proved for recognition and synthesis.
Speech data require two complementary representations:
(1) spoken text, which captures the actual phonetic realization
(e.g., using the International Phonetic Alphabet, IPA), and (2)
written text, the standard orthographic form used for human
reading and system I/O. For instance, the written form “01/05”
may be spoken as “January fifth”, “zero one slash zero five”,
or “the fifth of January” depending on context; conversely, the
pronunciation/pli:z/may be written as “please”, “pls.”, or “plz.”
Resolving this ambiguity is essential not only for text normal-

Prior work has addressed this problem in different ways.
Modern end-to-end ASR uses hierarchical encoders from
speech through phoneme-level to word-level representations
[1], and intermediate conditioning with subword or phoneme
targets has been shown to improve ASR performance [2, 3, 4].
TN has been tackled with rule-based weighted finite-state trans-
ducers (WFSTs), WFST+LM scoring [5], and more recently
with large language models (LLMs) [6], which improve over
text-only TN but still operate without direct access to how the
input was actually pronounced. Ihoriet al.[7] proposed an
speech-to-text (STT) model that outputs both spoken and writ-
ten text via joint training and dual-text fine-tuning, demonstrat-
ing gains over cascaded systems; however, that pipeline does
not feedphoneticoutput from the recognizer into a downstream
model for disambiguation. A key distinction of our work is
that we use IPA produced by the STT system as direct input
to an LLM for spoken–written mapping. That is, the recog-
nizer’s phonetic stream is not only an auxiliary output but the
primary conditioning signal for the LLM, enabling disambigua-
tion that text-only TN cannot achieve and supporting a repeat-
able pipeline for corpus refinement.
In this paper we present a data-centric framework for
speech corpus refinement that resolves spoken–written ambi-
guity through IPA-guided dual transcription. The goal is high-
quality, consistent spoken–written pairs for corpus construction
and iterative data–model improvement; ASR, TN, and the LLM
are components that serve this goal. We combine (1) a phoneme
intermediate conditional (PIC) STT model that outputs both IPA
and orthographic text from audio, and (2) an LLM fine-tuned on
orthographic and phonetic inputs to produce consistent spoken–
written pairs. By grounding normalization in actual phonetic
realizations from the recognizer rather than in text alone, the
framework supports iterative refinement of noisy or domain-
specific corpora.
Our main contributions are:
• We design an STT model that generates both IPA and written
text from a single audio input via an intermediate phoneme-
level connectionist temporal classification (CTC) head. This
provides aligned phonetic and orthographic streams in one
forward pass, which the downstream LLM uses for disam-
biguation.
• We fine-tune a LLM on orthographic and IPA inputs to pro-
duce spoken–written pairs. Conditioning the LLM on STT-
derived IPA (rather than text only) is the key differentiator
and enables context-aware normalization and consistent dual
representations for corpus curation.

2.2. Dual Text Generation with LLM
The second stage of our framework leverages a LLM to gen-
erate dual textual representations—a spoken-normalized tran-
scription and an inverse-normalized transcription —using the
orthographic and phonetic outputs from the PIC-AE. Whereas
traditional TN focuses solely on written-to-spoken mapping,
our approach extends this paradigm to a bidirectional frame-
work. Such duality is essential for constructing large-scale cor-
pora, ensuring consistent alignment between spoken and written
text for both ASR training and clean corpus curation.
Given a raw orthographic sequenceXraw and its aligned IPA
sequenceX IPA, the objective of the LLM is to generate a nor-
malized output sequenceY:
ˆY= arg max

P(Y|X raw,X IPA;θ).(7)
Conditioned on the task instruction, the model outputs a
dual text representationYthat always contains both the writ-
ten and spoken forms, denoted as (Written)/(Spoken). In this
paper, we primarily evaluate the Written→Spoken direction,
while the same framework can also be prompted for the in-
verse Spoken→Written task using the same dual-text format.
We adopt Gemma-3 27B [12, 13] as the backbone model and
fine-tune it with the Unsloth framework [14], which supports
efficient LoRA adaptation [15] under 4-bit quantization. While
prompting-only approaches often fail on ambiguous cases (e.g.,
dates, numbers) and produce unstable outputs, our two-stage
fine-tuning strategy mitigates this: supervised fine-tuning (SFT)
aligns the model with paired raw-text/IPA examples, while
group relative policy optimization (GRPO) [16] improves ro-
bustness through reward signals for format and content consis-
tency. This combination yields more stable and deterministic
behavior across numerically rich and context-sensitive expres-
sions.
A natural alternative is to apply an LLM as a text-only
post-processor, e.g., by generative error correction or by con-
text biasing with a domain lexicon to better handle numbers

and proper nouns. Such approaches infer the intended (Writ-
ten/Spoken) form primarily fromtextualevidence (surround-
ing words, prompts, or bias terms) and therefore remain un-
derdetermined when the same written token admits multiple
spoken realizations (e.g., dates, digit groupings, acronyms) or
when multiple spellings share similar context. Our approach is
complementary: by conditioning the LLM on an IPA sequence
predicted from the speech signal, we provide phonetic ground-
ing that serves as an additional constraint on generation, al-
lowing the model to use contextual reasoning while being an-
chored to how the utterance was actually realized. In practice,
IPA grounding can be combined with context biasing (e.g., pro-
viding a domain term list) when both phonetic evidence and
domain priors are beneficial.
Each prompt explicitly conditions on both orthographic
and phonetic inputs, ensuring unchanged tokens remain in-
tact while IPA provides grounding for disambiguation. A rep-
resentative prompt is illustrated in Figure 1. We enforce a
fixed output schema and validate LLM generations with de-
terministic parsing; unparseable outputs are conservatively dis-
carded (or reverted) during corpus construction. This stage
complements decoding-level robustness methods by performing
context-aware spoken–written normalization anchored by IPA.
Although our experiments emphasize Written→Spo-
ken normalization, the same model can perform inverse TN.
For instance, the spoken input “one four three zero” maps
to the canonical written form “1430.” A representative am-
biguous case is date interpretation:Input:“The meeting is on
12/08.”Output:(12/08)/(December eighth) or (12/08)/(twelve
oh eight). Similar cases include digits, currency, and acronyms.
At training time, IPA sequences are obtained from phonem-
izers; at inference time, they are generated by the PIC-AE (Sec-
tion 2.1). This integration provides phonetic grounding without
raw audio input. While our main experiments are in English, we
further demonstrate multilingual applicability with Korean dual
transcription in Section 3.2.
3. Experiments
3.1. English Text Normalization
In this experiment, we compare our proposed dual transcrip-
tion approach based on PIC-AEs for TN with existing state-
of-the-art methods for converting written text to spoken text.
The comparing methods include: (a) WFST: Traditional rule-
based WFST approach [17], (b) WFST+LM: Enhanced WFST
method with LM-based scoring [5], and (c) Duplex: A neural
model for TN developed by NVIDIA [18]. For the experiments
of these baseline models we used the NVIDIA NeMo open-
source framework [19].
Our proposed TN method employs the following experi-
mental setup: STT Model Training: The STT model was trained
on the LibriSpeech corpus [20] train dataset, with vocabulary
sizes of subword units and IPA units are 8k and 125 respec-
tively. The AE architecture consists of 2048 hidden units with
an output dimension of 512. The model employs a total of
N=12 Conformer layers, where the intermediate layer k=9 is
trained to predict IPA sequences using CTC loss, while the fi-
nal layer N=12 is trained to predict subword sequences using
the same CTC loss formulation. LLM Fine-tuning: For SFT of
the LLM, we utilized 10k randomly sampled instances from
the GoogleTN train dataset [21]. LLM fine-tuning uses only
text/IPA pairs (IPA from a phonemizer) and does not use any
TTS-generated audio.
For the test, we randomly extracted 1,000 samples from
the GoogleTN test set. Since our method requires an acoustic
realization to extract IPA, we synthesize evaluation speech us-
ing a multi-speaker TTS system [22] with a VITS model [23]
trained on VCTK [24]. Crucially, the TTS input is therefer-
ence spoken formprovided by the dataset (ground-truth spo-
ken normalization), not the ambiguous written input. We ran-
domly sample one of 110 speakers per utterance to avoid single-
speaker artifacts. We then obtain the IPA context by applying
our STT model to these synthesized waveforms, yielding a pho-
netic stream aligned with the (known) spoken realization.
This protocol is intended as a controlled diagnostic eval-
uation of whether phonetic grounding (via IPA) helps re-
cover the correct normalization given an actual spoken real-
ization; it is not meant to model real-world ASR variabil-
ity. This approach was necessitated by the scarcity of pub-
licly available TN datasets that include paired natural speech
and their corresponding ground-truth spoken-written transcrip-
tions. We will evaluate the same framework on naturally spo-
ken TN data in future work. We employed four primary eval-
uation metrics: (1) sentence-level accuracy (SA), measuring
exact sentence-level recognition accuracy; (2) word error rate
(WER), quantifying word-level recognition errors; (3) F1 score,
evaluated at the token level, which measures the harmonic
mean of precision and recall between generated and refer-
ence text tokens. This balances the proportion of correctly
generated tokens (precision) against the proportion of refer-
ence tokens successfully captured (recall); and (4) digit error
rate (DER), specifically measuring error rates for numerical
expressions (digit category), which represent the most error-
prone category in TN evaluation. For DER calculation, we clas-
sified vocabulary items from the GoogleTN dataset contain-
ing the following 9 semantic tags as numerical expressions:
{”DATE”, ”CARDINAL”,”DECIMAL”, ”MONEY”, ”ORDI-
NAL”, ”TIME”, ”DIGIT”, ”TELEPHONE”, ”FRACTION”}.

3.2. Korean Speech Corpus Construction for Dental Do-
main Speech Recognition
Speech recognition models typically exhibit degraded perfor-
mance when applied to specialized domains that were not in-
cluded in their pre-training data. To address this limitation, do-
main adaptation through the construction of specialized speech
corpora and subsequent fine-tuning of pre-trained models has
proven to be an effective approach for performance improve-
ment. To explore this, we focus on the dental domain, where
publicly available speech data are extremely limited. In this
work, we construct a domain-specific corpus to support adap-
tation and evaluate the effectiveness of our proposed dual tran-
scription method.
The Korean Dental Speech Corpus (KDSC) is a 100-hour
collection of dental domain speech data sourced from YouTube
videos, constructed using semi-automatic transcription meth-
ods. This corpus contains numerous transcription errors, includ-
ing orthographic inconsistencies and domain-specific terminol-
ogy misalignments. We apply our dual transcription pipeline to
construct an improved KDSC V2 and compare fine-tuning on
KDSC vs. KDSC V2 using the same PIC-STT model. For ex-
ample, the original KDSC transcription recorded:

results demonstrate the practical effectiveness of our dual tran-
scription approach for domain-specific speech corpus construc-
tion and the importance of high-quality phonetic information in
the adaptation of a specialized domain.
Building on the performance gain from (c) to (d), our
framework naturally supports iterative refinement. Specifically,
the adapted model (d) (PIC+KDSC V2) can be used to re-
process additional in-domain audio and generate updated dual
(written/spoken) pairs, forming a further refined corpus KDSC
V3; this refined corpus can then be used to obtain a subse-
quent model (PIC+KDSC V3). Repeating this refinement-and-
adaptation loop can progressively improve both corpus quality
and domain robustness, and we leave a systematic multi-round
analysis to future work.
4. Conclusion
This paper presented an IPA-guided dual transcription frame-
work for data-centric speech corpus refinement that resolves
spoken–written ambiguity. Our dual transcription system ad-
dresses the fundamental challenge of mapping between spoken
and written text representations by leveraging IPA information
as an intermediate representation in speech recognition mod-
els. The integration of phonetic information through intermedi-
ate conditioning provides crucial disambiguation cues that are
absent in text-only normalization methods. Our approach en-
ables continuous quality improvement through iterative model
retraining, which makes it suitable for constructing high-quality
speech corpora for various applications and domains.
However, a key limitation is that our English TN evalua-
tion uses TTS-synthesized speech rather than public natural-
speech corpora, owing to the scarcity of datasets that pair nat-
ural speech with ground-truth spoken–written transcriptions;
this provides a controlled diagnostic but does not capture real-
world acoustic variability. Future work will therefore validate
the framework on naturally spoken data, and will extend the
approach to multiple languages and domains while optimiz-
ing computational efficiency. Additionally, we plan to investi-
gate the application of our dual transcription method to other
speech processing tasks such as speech synthesis and multilin-
gual speech recognition systems.
