Token-Independent Language Representations for Low-Latency Configurable
Multilingual Speech Recognition
Hongxu Zhu ID 1, Lahiru Samarakoon ID 1,∗∗, Ivan Fung1
1 Fano, Hong Kong SAR, China

Abstract
Configurable multilingual speech recognition enables a single
model to be tailored to any user-selected language combination
via language-specific modules (LSMs). While providing state-
of-the-art accuracy, it incurs high latency as decoder LSMs are
executed at every step, leading to extra computational costs that
scale linearly with the output token length. To eliminate this
per-token overhead, we propose token-independent language
representations that decouple language identity from the autore-
gressive decoding loop. We replace the neural LSMs in the
decoder with language representations enriched by one-time,
utterance-level encoder cues. This reduces the LSM’s per-token
complexity from quadratic to linear relative to the hidden di-
mension, while preserving utterance-level adaptability. Exper-
iments show that our method achieves accuracy comparable to
the original design while maintaining a near-constant LSM’s
overhead, reducing peak inference latency by over 90% for long
utterances.
Index Terms: multilingual speech recognition, token-
independent, language-specific module
1. Introduction
Monolingual automatic speech recognition (ASR) has matured
to deliver excellent accuracy for target languages [1, 2, 3].
However, the deployment overhead of managing separate mod-
els per language [4, 5] has driven a shift toward unified end-
to-end (E2E) multilingual ASR (MASR) [6, 7, 8, 9]. While
MASR systems eliminate the need for external classifiers, train-
ing on pooled data often leads to inferior performance due to
language confusion [10]—where phonetically similar sounds
cause misrecognition—and data bias toward high-resource lan-
guages [11]. To overcome these challenges, a common strategy
is to provide explicit language cues to the model, effectively
guiding it toward the correct phonetic and linguistic space.
A widely used signal for language guidance is the concate-
nation of a one-hot language ID (LID) or language token to
the model input [12, 13, 14, 15, 16, 17]. Recently, mixture-
of-experts (MoE)-based multilingual ASR [18, 19, 20, 21, 22]
has gained increasing attention by routing different languages
to specialized sub-networks. While MoE strikes a balance be-
tween parameter sharing and language specialization, it faces
significant scalability challenges: experts often approach the
size of a full model, making them difficult to scale to hundreds
of languages. More importantly, most MoE methods assume a
single target language per utterance and cannot support ‘config-
urable’ scenarios where users may wish to pre-select an arbi-
trary subset of languages for recognition.

The Configurable Multilingual Model (CMM) [23] ad-
dresses these challenges by introducing two key innovations:
weighted combinations of language-specific modules (LSMs)
for arbitrary subset activation, and residual modeling to keep
these modules parameter-efficient. By formulating the hidden
representation as a weighted sum of a universal model and sev-
eral lightweight LSMs, CMM achieves high performance with
a compact footprint. However, this configurability comes with
an unaddressed computational burden. In the original CMM
design, the LSMs in the decoder operate as neural layers that
must be re-executed at every autoregressive decoding step. This
couples language identity—a global property—to the per-token
decoding loop, resulting in an inference latency overhead that
grows linearly with the length of the output sequence.
A natural question arises: is it strictly necessary for LSMs
to operate in such a token-dependent manner? Cognitive stud-
ies suggest that humans identify language early in an utter-
ance, after which processing is guided by language-specific ex-
pectations rather than continuous re-identification [24]. Thus,
we hypothesize that language identity could be treated as a
global property that can be modeled independently of the de-
coding sequence. In this paper, we propose a strategy to decou-
ple language identity from the autoregressive process by mak-
ing the decoder’s language representation token-independent.
While we retain the neural LSMs in the encoder—which are
computed only once per utterance—we replace the decoder’s
per-token neural LSMs with static, token-independent language
vectors (CMM-S). This reduces the LSM’s per-token complex-
ity from a linear projection to a simple vector addition. To
bridge the gap between static vectors and the dynamic needs
of acoustic modeling, we further enhance these representations
with dynamic cues extracted from the Connectionist Tempo-
ral Classification (CTC)-supervised [25] top encoder layer’s
LSMs (CMM-D). Therefore, our method retains CMM’s high-
level language awareness while eliminating the linear scaling
of LSM’s decoding latency. The primary contributions of this
work are summarized as follows:
•Architectural Efficiency: We introduce token-independent
representations for the CMM decoder, reducing the per-step
LSM complexity from quadratic to linear relative to the hid-
den dimension size.
•Encoder-Informed Adaptation: We propose a method to
fuse static language vectors with utterance-level language
features from the top encoder layer, preserving accuracy
without adding per-token latency.
•Empirical Validation: We demonstrate on two multilingual
benchmarks that our approach achieves accuracy parity with
the original CMM while providing a near-constant inference
overhead regardless of output token length.

2. Configurable Multilingual Model
CMM [23] is designed to recognize any language combination
at inference time by using a user-defined language prompt to
activate lightweight language-specific modules (LSMs) along-
side a shared universal model. In this work, we implement this
design on a Conformer-Transformer backbone with joint CTC-
Attention decoding [26, 27, 28].

3. Encoder-informed Token-independent
Language Representation
The original CMM’s decoder LSMs recompute language pro-
jections at every token. However, language identity is a prop-
erty that should not require per-token re-estimation. We there-
fore propose a decoupled strategy to address this bottleneck.
Specifically, we first introduce static token-independent vectors
(CMM-S) to realize this global modeling for maximum com-
putational efficiency. Besides, we further describe a dynamic
encoder-informed representation (CMM-D) that enriches these
vectors with utterance-level cues.

4. Experiments
We evaluate our designs against the original CMM baseline to
verify their ability to maintain high recognition accuracy while
eliminating the LSM’s per-token decoding bottleneck.
4.1. Datasets and Experimental Setup
We use∼7K hours of speech from LibriSpeech (English)
[30] and seven non-English languages—Dutch (NL), French
(FR), German (DE), Spanish (ES), Italian (IT), Portuguese
(PT), and Polish (PL)—from the Multilingual Librispeech
(MLS) [31]. The size of training data for each language
varies from 25K (thousand) utterances to 425K, which are
shown in Table 2. This represents a data-imbalanced sce-
nario. Besides, we further construct a balanced 4-language cor-
pus—Cantonese (YUE), Mandarin (CMN), English(ENG), and
Malay (MS)—with 3,000 hours per language extracted from our
in-house dataset 1. Besides, this 4-language corpus is a more
rigorous testbed for language confusion: its languages are far
closer than those in MLS and contain code-switched segments,
partly reflecting code-switch performance.
We extract 80-dimensional log-Mel filterbank features us-
ing a 25ms window and 10ms shift. We apply global mean-
variance normalization on these features. We adopt a 2,000-
subword vocabulary for the MLS corpus due to its shared pho-
netic inventories. In contrast, we use 20,000 sentence pieces for
the 4-language dataset to support the inclusion of Chinese char-
acters. Specifically, Simplified/Traditioanl Chinese characters
are used for Mandarin/Cantonese. All models are built using
the ESPnet open-source toolkit [32] with 12-layer Conformer
encoder and a 6-layer Transformer decoder (d model = 512, 8
attention heads and 2048 feed-forward network units). LSMs
are integrated into the first and last layers of both the encoder
and decoder. We also use the relative position encoding to boost
the modeling of position information. For CMM-D, the adapter
uses a bottleneck dimension ofz= 128. We simulate user
uncertainty during training by randomly adding incorrect lan-
guage IDs to the prompt with probabilityp= 0.5. We op-
timize the model with a multi-task objective combining CTC
loss (weight 0.3) and attention cross-entropy loss (weight 0.7).
We use the Adam optimizer with gradient clipping and learning
rate warmup. All models are trained for 70 epochs.

(b) Allhot: This simulates a ”generalist” scenario where no
prior language information is available, forcing the model to
remain sensitive to all possible phonetic inventories simulta-
neously. This represents the lower bound of performance and
tests the model’s robustness against language confusion. As
an initial observation, Table 3 and Table 4 confirm that all
CMM variants significantly outperform the universal multilin-
gual baseline. This confirms that language-specific specializa-
tion is highly effective for multilingual ASR, justifying the in-
tegration of LSMs into the architecture.
From Token-Dependent to Static Vectors: the static
CMM-S leads to a slight but noticeable accuracy drop (e.g.,
from 8.70 to 8.80 on MLS allhot). This confirms our intuition
that purely static language vectors struggle to capture the fine-
grained acoustic variations across different utterances.
Capacity Recovery via CMM-D: By introducing encoder-
informed dynamic cues, CMM-D effectively bridges this per-
formance gap. As shown in Table 3, CMM-D achieves an aver-
age WER of 8.70 on MLS, identical to the original CMM. Sim-
ilar parity is observed on the 4-language corpus. These results
empirically validate that language modeling in the decoder does
not need to be token-dependent; a token-independent represen-
tation, if properly informed by utterance-level acoustic cues, is
sufficient for high-accuracy multilingual recognition.
Impact of Data Volume on Specialization: we observe
that the benefit of precise language configuration (onehot) is
highly dependent on the absolute volume of training data avail-
able for each language. On the MLS corpus, the onehot setting
yields minimal gains over allhot; for low-resource languages
such as Polish (PL) and Portuguese (PT), onehot even performs
slightly worse. This suggests that when data is scarce, the LSMs
are not sufficiently optimized to function as effective specialists.
In such scenarios, the model relies more on the robust, shared
representations of the universal backbone than on the under-
trained LSMs. In contrast, on the 4-language dataset—where
every language is backed by 3,000 hours of training data—the
onehot configuration consistently outperforms the allhot setting
across all languages. This confirms that while CMM architec-
ture provides the necessary capacity for specialization, the ac-
tual effectiveness of language-specific guidance requires a suf-
ficient data scale to fully optimize the LSMs.
4.3. Efficiency Analysis: Latency and Parameters
The core advantage of our decoupled representation is revealed
in the latency measurements. Beyond latency, we also analyze
the parameter footprint of each design.
Parameter Scalability: CMM’s decoder LSMs are neural
layers that require 0.13M parameters per language per layer.
Our CMM-S design reduces this footprint to just 512 parame-
ters (a singled model vector) per language, representing a 256x
reduction in language-specific parameters. While CMM-D in-
troduces a shared adapter (∼0.13M parameters), this adapter is
common to all languages. Consequently, with more languages,
the total parameter cost of CMM-D’s decoder LSMs would be
significantly lower than the original CMM, as the language-
specific component remains just a single vector.
Latency Win: As shown in Table 5, the original CMM
incurs a significant latency penalty that scales linearly with the
output token length, reaching +107.88s for long utterances. In
contrast, both CMM-S and CMM-D maintain a near-constant
overhead regardless of the sequence length. CMM-S adds only
∼2.5s, while CMM-D adds a stable overhead of∼3–7s due to
the one-time adapter computation. This confirms that our ap-
proach successfully decouples the computational cost of con-
figurability from the autoregressive process. As this gain is al-
gorithmic (quadratic to linear per token) rather than hardware-
specific, it holds across platforms, andCcan be increased to
recover accuracy at negligible added latency.
5. Conclusion
In this paper, we addressed the latency bottleneck in config-
urable multilingual ASR caused by token-dependent language
modeling in the decoder. We proposed a decoupled language
representation strategy that replaces repeating neural projec-
tions with token-independent language representations. By
combining static language vectors (CMM-S) with dynamic,
utterance-level cues from the CTC-supervised encoder (CMM-
D), we successfully transformed the per-step LSM complexity
from quadratic to linear (O

large language sets. This approach provides a practical path
for deploying highly efficient, configurable multilingual ASR
in latency-sensitive applications.
