Abstract
Understanding the dynamic relationship between 3D vocal
tract geometry and speech acoustics is central to speech produc-
tion modelling. Using a constrained Large Deformation Diffeo-
morphic Metric Mapping (LDDMM) framework, we combine
volumetric magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of sustained vo-
cal tract postures with midsagittal real-time MRI of short coar-
ticulated utterances to construct dynamic 3D vocal tract models
with time-varying acoustic responses. We demonstrate the ap-
proach on an [5ô5] utterance by an Australian English speaker.
Five acoustic landmarks define evaluation states in the refer-
ence recording, and intermediate meshes are sampled from an
rtMRI-parametrised deformation trajectory constrained by mid-
sagittal contours. Finite-element simulations estimate formants
from the resulting geometries. Simulated formants are system-
atically higher, yet track the reference formant trajectories, con-
sistent with hyperarticulation in sustained speech produced un-
der noisy conditions.
Index Terms: speech production, vocal tract, magnetic reso-
nance imaging, finite element method
1. Introduction
Speech production involves rapid and coordinated articula-
tory movements that dynamically reshape the configuration of
the vocal tract and associated time-varying acoustic response
[1, 2]. High-resolution volumetric Magnetic Resonance Imag-
ing (MRI) has long provided detailed information about 3D
vocal tract configurations during sustained articulations [3, 4],
while real-time MRI (rtMRI) primarily enables the observation
of two-dimensional (2D) midsagittal articulatory dynamics dur-
ing continuous speech [5, 6]. Combining data from these imag-
ing techniques to obtain dynamic 3D vocal tract models remains
an open challenge.
Detailed 3D vocal tract models are essential for accurate
acoustic modelling of speech production. One-dimensional
(1D) acoustic simulations typically represent the tract as a con-
catenation of tubes whose cross-sectional areas may be esti-
mated from midsagittal rtMRI data [7, 8]. These simplified
models, while computationally efficient, inherently neglect im-
portant three-dimensional information about vocal tract shap-
ing, especially during complex articulatory transitions such as
rhotics [9, 10].
Numerical acoustic simulation of 3D vocal tract models
is considerably more difficult than 1D modelling, as it must
resolve full spatial wave behaviour in anatomically complex,
branching, and often partially open domains. Existing 3D nu-
merical methods can be grouped by how they discretise the
wave equation. V olume-based methods, including the Finite El-
ement Method (FEM) [11, 12, 13] and Finite-Difference Time-
Domain (FDTD) schemes [14, 15], solve the wave/Helmholtz
equation throughout the air volume. Boundary-based methods,
like the Boundary Element Method (BEM), model the acoustic
field using surface integrals over tract boundaries and are well
suited to modelling radiation into unbounded exterior space
[16, 17]. Closely related time domain scattering formulations,
such as Digital Waveguide Mesh (DWM) [18, 19] and Trans-
mission Line Matrix (TLM) methods [20, 21], approximate
multidimensional propagation using interconnected waveguide
networks. Across these modelling paradigms, estimated for-
mant structure is highly sensitive to boundary/radiation choices
and to geometric uncertainties arising from segmentation and
the treatment of teeth and small cavities [11, 22, 23]. This sen-
sitivity is especially pronounced for rhotics, where the hallmark
lowering ofF3 can change substantially with small differences
in the location/degree of oral-palatal constrictions and the asso-
ciated front/back cavity shaping [9, 24].
Complementing the FEM-based acoustic framework, a
method is needed to generate smooth, anatomically consistent
deformations of vocal tract geometry across speech configura-
tions. Large Deformation Diffeomorphic Metric Mapping (LD-
DMM) [25, 26] offers a powerful framework to capture smooth
deformations of complex biological structures. Recent work
demonstrated that constraining LDDMM with rtMRI-derived
boundary contours improves the realism of vocal tract morph-
ing during speech transitions [27]. While such constrained mor-
phing can improve geometric coherence, there is still no estab-
lished validation framework for determining whether intermedi-
ate 3D shapes remain acoustically and anatomically plausible.
In this study, we investigate the relationship between dy-
namic 3D vocal tract shaping and acoustic outcomes during a
vowel-consonant-vowel (VCV) sequence [5-ô-5], focusing on
articulation of an intervocalic rhotic approximant and the acous-
tic response of the vocal tract at segmental targets and their coar-
ticulated transitions. We use the LDDMM framework proposed
in [27] to produce anatomically realistic 3D deformations of
the vocal tract, informed by real-time MRI contours. We in-
tegrate these deformed shapes into 3D finite element acoustic
simulations using COMSOL Multiphysics [28] to compare their
acoustic estimations with recorded speech.

2.1. V ocal Tract Morphing via Constrained LDDMM
High-resolution volumetric structural MRI was used to capture
the three dimensional configuration of the oral airway of the
vocal tract during sustained productions of [5:] and [ô:]. ITK-
SNAP [30] was used to identify 3D tissue boundaries following
the pipeline described in [29]. Companion rtMRI recordings
were used to track the change in midsagittal vocal tract con-
tours for the same speaker producing the utterance[5-ô-5]. Mid-
sagittal vocal tract postures were located in the rtMRI data and
co-registered into the volumetric coordinate system using the
procedure described in [27]. To generate a sequence of 3D vo-
cal tract meshes across the VCV transition [5-ô-5], we model
the transitions as two consecutive deformations, [5] → [ô] and
[ô]→ [5], each constrained by the corresponding rtMRI frame
sequence.

3. Numerical Experiments
To reduce computational burden, frequency-domain FEM simu-
lations were performed for five meshes sampled from the defor-
mation trajectory. We denote these five landmarks as [51], [5ô],
[ô], [ôa], and [52], corresponding to the first vowel target, the
[5→ ô] transition, the rhotic target, the [ô→ 5] transition, and
the second vowel target, respectively. Each mesh was simulated
using COMSOL, and F1–F3 were estimated from the transfer
response as dominant spectral peaks.
Landmarks were located independently in acoustic and
rtMRI data. Acoustic landmarks were identified in the out-of-
scanner [5ô5] recording, making reference to the time-aligned

and MRI data with the chosen landmarks.
3.1. Acoustic Alignment
To facilitate comparison on a common frequency axis, we ap-
ply a per-formant additive alignment to the FEM-derived for-
mants. A constant frequency offset is estimated for each for-
mant across all five landmarks (mean difference between out-
of-scanner measurements and FEM-derived values) and applied
uniformly across the trajectory. This reflects that systematic
resonance–formant biases in MRI-based FEM modelling can
be frequency dependent (and therefore not well captured by
a single global offset), due to posture differences and simpli-
fied boundary/radiation assumptions [11, 22, 23]. Importantly,
the offset alignment does not change the landmark-to-landmark
variation within each trajectory; agreement is evaluated from
the residual dispersion across landmarks after removing the
constant per-formant bias. The alignment is used only to place
curves on a common axis for visualisation, not as a predictive
calibration.
4. Results and Discussion
4.1. Endpoint Acoustic Plausibility Check
As a plausibility check on the segmented endpoint geometries
and the FEM boundary setup, we compared FEM formants for
the [5:] and [ô:] endpoint meshes against sustained record-
ings (Table 1). The differences between in-scanner and out-
of-scanner formant values are plausible given the combined ef-
fects of posture (supine vs. upright) on vocal tract acoustics
and practical limitations of in-scanner audio for formant estima-
tion [35]. Here, in-scanner formants refer to speech produced
supine inside the scanner, whereas out-of-scanner formants re-
fer to speech produced seated outside the scanner.
For the sustained [5:], the FEM estimates closely match the
upright token in F2 and F3, while F1 is elevated by approxi-
mately 110 Hz (Table 1). A similar pattern holds for sustained
[ô:], whereF2 andF3 again show close agreement with the up-
right measurements, accompanied by a consistent +110 HzF1
offset. As observed in prior work, absolute agreement between
FEM resonances and measured formants is not generally ex-
pected, even for MRI-derived geometries [11, 23].

4.3. Offset Aligned Landmark Comparison
After offset alignment, the FEM points track the reference land-
marks more closely in absolute frequency (Figure 4). Across the
five landmarks, the residual dispersion (audio - aligned FEM )
is 78 Hz for F1 and 74 Hz for F2, indicating that once global
bias is removed,F1 andF2 differences are relatively consistent
across the trajectory. In contrast,F3 shows a larger residual dis-
persion (163 Hz), driven primarily by the post-rhotic landmark
where the aligned FEM remains higher than the measured F3
by approximately 257 Hz. This pattern is consistent with the
fact that rhotic acoustics are particularly sensitive to small ge-
ometric differences that influence the characteristic loweredF3
of [ô] [24], and that post-rhotic acoustics can be strongly context
dependent in continuous speech. The remainingF3 discrepancy
may therefore reflect local geometric differences along the re-
lease portion of the deformation path that are not fully captured
by the selected meshes or by the underlying segmentation fi-
delity. A further plausible contributor is missing anterior detail
such as dentition. Controlled 3D comparisons with and with-
out teeth show that dentition can substantially alter the transfer
function and resonance structure. [40].
As an offset-independent check, we evaluated the relative
change in ∆(F3−F2) trajectories across the five landmarks
(r = 0.72, p = 0.17, 95%CI [−0.26, 0.97], n = 5). Given
the small number of landmarks, this is interpreted as qualitative
support for phonetically valid vocal dynamic tract modelling
rather than a formal statistical test.
5. Conclusion
This study acoustically validates an rtMRI-guided LDDMM
framework by linking MRI derived 3D vocal tract deforma-
tions to FEM simulations and comparing derived resonances
against reference audio in an intervocalic rhotic exemplar. Al-
though systematic resonance–formant offsets are expected in
MRI-based FEM modelling, the simulations reproduced key
phonetic properties of intervocalic rhotics, most notably robust
F3 lowering, supporting constrained LDDMM as a physiologi-
cally grounded generator of intermediate 3D geometries that re-
main acoustically interpretable. Results are shown for a single
speaker and should be interpreted as a proof-of-concept demon-
stration of the proposed morphoacoustic validation framework.
