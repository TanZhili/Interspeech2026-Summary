Abstract
Audio-visual speech recognition (A VSR) has achieved robust
accuracy by fusing lip motion with acoustic cues; however,
state-of-the-art systems still rely on high-resolution multimodal
sequences, imposing prohibitive computational costs on large
language models. Recent attempts to compress tokens remain
confined to single-task recognition. We address the open ques-
tion of whether the same compressed tokens can simultaneously
support speech recognition and speech-to-text translation with-
out retraining the encoder. We propose MTC-A VSR, a multi-
task model built upon minimal multimodal speech (MMS) to-
kens. The framework couples an MMS-style token compressor
with a prompt-conditioned LLM for single-stream inference,
introduces a lightweight language adapter module (LAM) that
maps compressed, source-aligned tokens into multilingual rep-
resentations, and presents the token-contrastive alignment loss
(TCAL) to align adapter outputs with a multilingual lexicon via
contrastive learning. Experiments show that our model achieves
a word error rate (WER) of 0.74% on LRS3 (on par with SOTA)
while setting new records on MuA ViC En-X tasks: English-
Spanish BLEU 28.1, French BLEU 26.3, and Portuguese BLEU
21.8, all state-of-the-art, validating the residual linguistic capac-
ity of compressed tokens under joint audio-visual speech recog-
nition and translation scenarios.

1. Introduction
Numerous researches have demonstrated that visual informa-
tion can provide effective cues to aid speech recognition, es-
pecially in such complex acoustic environments where the au-
dio is corrupted or unavailable. Therefore, Audio-visual speech
recognition (A VSR), incorporating video modality (e.g. lip mo-
tion and facial expression) into automatic speech recognition
(ASR), has been extensively developed [1, 2, 3].
Recent advances in A VSR have been driven by large-scale
datasets [4], architectural improvements: from recurrent [5] to
transformer-based models [6], and multimodal learning tech-
niques such as self-supervised pre-training [7] and cross-modal
distillation [8]. Staged training strategies [9] have further im-
proved performance, and recent integration of large language
models (LLMs) as decoders has enhanced contextual reasoning
significantly [10]. Despite the superior contextual capabilities
afforded by LLM-based A VSR, the requisite high-resolution

tion, leaving unexplored whether compressed tokens retain suf-
ficient cross-task semantic information to support a joint recog-
nition and translation objective.(b)The compressed tokens are
aligned only to the source language, and rarely has previous
work explored mapping them into a multilingual space to sup-
port multilingual translation without expanding the parameters
of the large language model.
To address these issues, We propose MTC-A VSR, a multi-
task framework that integrates compressed multimodal tokens
with a prompt-conditioned LLM. Our contributions are twofold:
(a) we extend token compression to joint audio-visual speech
recognition and translation, demonstrating that a single stream
of 3.5 tokens per second suffices for both tasks without encoder
retraining. (b) we introduce a lightweight language adapter that
recodes source-aligned tokens using a jointly optimized mul-
tilingual lexicon, enabling zero-shot language switching with
the LLM kept frozen, along with a token-contrastive alignment
loss that explicitly maps adapter outputs to their correspond-
ing lexical entries for joint training of both the adapter and the
QLoRA [13] adapters. Through the above two works, we pro-
vide feasible solutions to the two aforementioned problems.
2. Proposed Method
2.1. Overview
The overall framework of our proposed MTC-A VSR model is
depicted in Fig. 1. The pipeline is divided into three concep-
tual stages: multimodal compression, language adaptation, and
task-conditioned decoding. The responsibilities of each stage
are as follows. (I) In the multimodal compression stage, the
model extracts audio and video features separately and per-
forms feature fusion and compression operations. (II) In the lan-
guage adaptation stage, the compressed tokens are re-encoded
via LAM against a multilingual lexicon to incorporate multilin-
gual cues. (III) Finally, in the task-conditioned decoding stage,
a frozen LLM fine-tuned by QLoRA adapter receives each to-
ken sequence prefixed by a task-specific prompt and generates
either source transcripts or target translations. The overall ob-
jective combines two components: a cross-entropy (CE) loss
on the LLM outputs for ASR and each translation target, and
a separate token-contrastive alignment loss (TCAL); they are

2.2. Multimodal Compression
To obtain compact yet linguistically rich representations, our
model processes inputs through modality-specific front-ends
followed by a dynamically query-allocated A V-QFormer. The
visual front-end extracts aligned lip-motion embeddings, while
the audio front-end produces mel-filterbank features resampled
to the visual time scale. These features are fused via multi-
head cross-attention into a unified representationXav, which is
then fed into the A V-QFormer using the query-allocation strat-
egy from MMS-LLaMA. Specifically, we employ a learnable
query bankQ∈R N×D , whereNis the total number of queries
andDis the embedding dimension. The number of queries
allocated to an input is computed asN alloc =⌊f Q·Tv/Fv⌋,
wherefQ = 3.5tokens/s (which is validated by MMS-LLaMa
as achieving the best trade-off between efficiency and perfor-
mance),T v is the frame count, andF v is the frame rate. The
firstN alloc queries formQ alloc∈R Nalloc×D, which along with
the fused featuresX av are processed by the A V-QFormer to
produce multimodal speech tokensM∈R Nalloc×D as follows:
M=Q-Former(Q alloc,X av).(1)
Finally, two linear projection layers mapped the tokens into
the embedding space of the LLM, ready for downstream task
conditioning.
2.3. Language Adaptation
To enable cross-lingual expressivity in the compressed multi-
modal tokens, we introduce a Language Adaptation Module
(LAM) between the A V-QFormer and the LLM decoder. The
LAM consists of a multilingual lexicon and a two-layer gated
cross-attention network. The shared lexiconE∈R |V|×D is
formed by merging the source and four target language sub-
vocabularies, where each entrye∈Eis initialized from the
corresponding LLM embedding and fine-tuned as a learnable
parameter. The adapter uses two gated cross-attention layers:
the queries are the compressed tokensm∈M, while the
keys and values are drawn fromE. Following Flamingo [14],
the output multilingual representationz∈Zis computed
via a gated cross-attention block, which incorporates multi-
head cross-attention (Attn), LayerNorm [15] (LN), and a feed-
forward network (FFW).The calculation process is as follows:

2.4. Task-Conditioned Decoding
Inspired by Whisper’s multitask training format [16], we
prepend explicit task-control tokens to steer the frozen LLM.
The prompt starts with a<sot>token, followed by either
<recognize><En>for ASR or<translate><lang>for trans-
lation, and ends with<eot>. Here,<lang>is selected from
the set{Spanish (Es),French (Fr),Portuguese (Pt),
Italian (It)}. During training, each chunk is randomly prefixed
with a task token to balance both objectives. At inference,
switching between tasks is done by altering the prompt prefix.
2.5. Training Strategy
To ensure stability and to disentangle the responsibilities of
compression, alignment, and cross-lingual adaptation, we adopt
a rigorously staged training regime. Each stage is executed only
once and is terminated by an explicit checkpoint; parameters
frozen at the end of a stage remain frozen thereafter, guaran-
teeing that subsequent stages cannot inadvertently disturb the
representations already acquired.

3. Experiments
3.1. Experimental Setup
To train our models, we use LRS3 [4] and augment the training
set with 1,326h of English videos from V oxCeleb2 [17], whose
transcripts are generated using Whisper Large-v2 [16]. For En-
X translation, we use MuA ViC [18], which provides translations
of LRS3 into four languages. All audio and video are resampled
to 16 kHz and 25 fps, respectively. Mouth regions of size 96 ×
96 are cropped using RetinaFace [19], converted to grayscale,
and horizontally flipped for augmentation. Audio is mixed with
babble noise from MUSAN [20] with 75% probability. We re-
tain target-language translations to support multi-task learning
under consistent input conditions. The decoder uses a frozen
LLaMA3-3B backbone [21] with QLoRA adapters applied to
the query, key, value, and output projections.
All trainable modules use AdamW withβ 1 = 0.9,β 2 =
0.98, weight decay 0.01. A cosine scheduler starts at1e −4,
warms up over 0.5k steps, and decays to1e −5 across 30k total
steps. Stage-2 employs the linear annealing schedule forλfrom
1.0 to 0.3. Gradient clipping is set to 1.0. Inference uses beam
search (beam=5, temperature=0.3).

3.4. Ablation Study
3.4.1. Validation of Component-Wise Efficiency and LLM Size
Impact
To dissect the contribution of each component within MTC-
A VSR, we conducted a step-wise ablation study on the MuA ViC
En-Es track, using Spanish BLEU and English WER as evalu-
ation metrics; all experiments were run on the full 1,759 hour
dataset, and the results are reported in Table 3. Starting with a
frozen LLM using only prompts (no LAM, no TCAL), Spanish
BLEU remains low (27.4) despite strong English transcription
(WER 0.81), revealing a severe cross-lingual alignment gap.
Adding the LAM improves BLEU to 27.8 and reduces WER
to 0.78, confirming that LAM successfully maps tokens into
the multilingual embedding space without harming recognition.
Multilingual Lexicon further refines semantic correspondence,
pushing BLEU to 28.0 and WER to 0.75. Finally, enabling
the TCAL yields the best trade-off (BLEU 28.1, WER 0.74),
demonstrating that token-level contrastive supervision explic-
itly tightens the token-to-word correspondence.
Furthermore, to investigate how the model size of LLMs
affects A VSR performance, we conducted experiments using
three models based on LLaMA with 1B, 3B, and 8B parame-
ters. Under clean-audio conditions, LLaMA 3.2-3B achieves
the best WER of 0.74 and the highest BLEU of 28.1 on the
En-Es translation task, outperforming both 1B and 8B mod-
els. However, under noisy conditions, the LLaMA3.1 8B model
achieved the best WER of 2.02 and consistently outperforms the
1B model. These results suggest that larger model might per-
form better under the challenging environment. As expected,
the larger model incurs higher GPU memory usage and com-
putational costs. Given its comparable performance to the 8B
model, we report results using the LLaMA3.2-3B model for
other experiments due to its significantly lower computational
requirements.

3.4.2. Comparison of different language alignment strategies
To validate the computational efficiency and effectiveness of
the proposed LAM module, we compare five alignment strate-
gies across three dimensions. Here, “GPU Memory Usage” de-
notes the extra memory consumption introduced by each strat-
egy on top of a shared baseline (12.4 GB). As shown in Ta-
ble 4, LoRA-based injection, while highly parameter-efficient,
underperforms due to its static, global, and non-selective fu-
sion mechanism. Our LAM module incurs only a modest in-
crease of approximately 0.3 GB in GPU memory significantly
less than the 0.6 GB overhead of the CMT. Although LAM ex-
hibits slightly higher memory and FLOP costs compared to the
other three strategies, it consistently achieves substantial perfor-
mance gains in noisy (BLEU 25.3) and clean conditions (BLEU
28.1). Notably, despite its larger parameter count and higher
computational demand, CMT treats the multilingual lexicon as
a generic sequence and applies self-attention indiscriminately.
In contrast, LAM leverages cross-attention and Tanh gating to
achieve precise, context-aware alignment while maintaining rel-
atively low resource overhead. This design not only avoids the
inefficiencies of brute-force fusion but also strikes an optimal
balance between model capability and computational economy.
4. Conclution
In this work, we present MTC-A VSR, a multitask framework
that couples ultra-compressed MMS tokens with a prompt-
conditioned LLM to perform simultaneous ASR and En-X
translation. A lightweight language adapter and the proposed
TCAL loss align tokens to a multilingual lexicon without reac-
tivate the encoder. On LRS3, the system achieves WER 0.74%;
on MuA ViC, it sets new SOTA BLEU scores of 28.1 (Es), 26.3
(Fr) and 21.8 (Pt), validating the residual linguistic capacity of
compressed multimodal representations under joint training.
