Abstract
Lyric intelligibility, the ease with which listeners understand
song lyrics, affects listening experience, memorability, and lan-
guage learning. We propose a data-driven system extracting
acoustic features, and Whisper encoder representations. Fea-
ture engineering, including temporal statistics and interactions
among correlated features, enhances prediction. A regression
module maps features to intelligibility scores using neural mod-
els (FCNs, MOE, CNN+GRU, Transformers) and gradient-
boosted trees (CatBoost, LightGBM, XGBoost). Experiments
on a manually annotated dataset show gradient-boosted trees
outperform neural networks, with CatBoost achieving Root
Mean Square Error (RMSE) 27.367 and correlation (Corr)
0.654. Results highlight the effectiveness of structured feature
modeling and Whisper-derived representations.
Index Terms: Lyric Intelligibility, Feature Fusion, Whisper En-
coder, CatBoost, Regression Prediction
1. Introduction
Lyric intelligibility refers to the degree to which listeners can
understand song lyrics, and this metric plays a central role
in shaping the overall listening experience. The formation of
lyric intelligibility is affected by multiple factors, including the
singer’s vocal technique and singing style, as well as the tempo
and lexical complexity of the song, and acoustic conditions such
as accompaniment intensity and mixing effects [1, 2]. Individ-
ual auditory differences also constitute a non-negligible variable
[3]. High intelligibility enables listeners to accurately perceive
the emotional core of a song, whereas low intelligibility may
lead to distorted emotional transmission and even semantic mis-
interpretation [4]. Meanwhile, lyric intelligibility directly influ-
ences the spreadability and memorability of songs [5].
Against this background, predicting lyric intelligibility is of
practical significance. In the field of automatic speech recogni-
tion, speech intelligibility quantifies the extent to which listen-
ers can correctly identify the linguistic content of spoken ut-
terances in a given acoustic environment. This metric focuses
mainly on the clarity of natural spoken speech, and its eval-
uation strongly relies on phonetic distinctness, signal-to-noise
ratio, and speaker articulation [6]. In contrast, lyric intelligi-
bility is specific to sung lyrics in musical contexts; its variation
is influenced not only by vocal articulation but also by musical
elements including melody, rhythm, harmony, and accompani-
ment dynamics [7]. Furthermore, lyric intelligibility is closely

To achieve an objective and quantitative evaluation of lyric
intelligibility, existing research primarily follows two major
paradigms: handcrafted acoustic feature modeling and ASR-
based transcription accuracy estimation.
The first paradigm relies on manually designed acoustic
features to model the perceptual cues of intelligibility. Early
works typically extract low-level descriptors related to vocal
clarity and musical conditions, such as vocal-to-accompaniment
energy ratio, harmonics-to-residual ratio, spectral energy distri-
bution, and syllable rate [11, 12]. While these handcrafted fea-
tures offer good interpretability and computational efficiency,
they are limited to capturing shallow acoustic characteristics
and lack the ability to encode phonetic, lexical, or semantic in-
formation critical for human-like lyric understanding.
The second paradigm estimates lyric intelligibility using
word-level transcription accuracy from automatic speech recog-
nition (ASR) systems. However, a fundamental and well-
documented mismatch exists between machine transcription
correctness and human-perceived intelligibility [13], and this
discrepancy is far more severe in musical contexts than in nor-
mal speech. Unlike speech, singing involves strong pitch vari-
ations, melodic constraints, vowel stretching, consonant reduc-
tion, and heavy interference from accompaniment—all of which
cause sung lyrics to be drastically less intelligible than spo-
ken language [9, 10]. Consequently, ASR word accuracy alone
cannot serve as a direct proxy for human lyric comprehension,
creating a significant representation gap between ASR outputs
and subjective intelligibility scores. Despite these limitations,
pre-trained speech models exemplified by Whisper still provide
unique value: their large-scale self-supervised pre-training en-
ables them to capture robust phonetic and prosodic representa-
tions that benefit intelligibility assessment [14]. Lyric intelli-
gibility prediction is commonly divided into intrusive and non-
intrusive paradigms, depending on whether reference lyrics are
used. In this work, we adopt a Whisper-based intrusive ap-
proach as our baseline system. Our study targets several key
limitations in lyric intelligibility prediction:
• Conventional methods rely heavily on shallow, hand-
crafted acoustic features, which lack high-level semantic
and phonetic representations.
• ASR-transcription-based approaches suffer from a critical
representation gap between machine prediction accuracy
and human-perceived intelligibility, especially in musical
contexts.

2. Methods
This section first introduces the baseline system for lyric in-
telligibility prediction, which relies solely on automatic speech
recognition (ASR) transcription accuracy. We then detail our
improved framework that addresses the inherent limitations of
the baseline—specifically, its failure to account for the dis-
crepancy between machine transcription performance and hu-
man subjective perception of intelligibility. Our enhancement
centers on integrating multi-dimensional feature representations
and leveraging regression models to achieve more robust and
human-aligned predictive performance.

2.3. Feature Extraction
For each music segment, two feature sets are extracted:
•Acoustic Features: Derived from waveform and spectrum
analysis, including energy, RMS, zero-crossing rate, spectral
centroid, bandwidth, rolloff, duration, and SNR from source
separation. These features capture fundamental signal prop-
erties independent of semantic content [15]. These explicit
features reflect the overall characteristics of the audio from
objective indicators.
•Pre-trained Encoder Features: Obtained from the encoder
output of the Whisperbase.enmodel, which provides
high-dimensional temporal embeddings containing phonetic
and semantic cues of the vocal content [13]. The role of tem-
poral features is to complement acoustic features by encoding
high-level phonetic, prosodic, and semantic information that
is difficult to capture with handcrafted descriptors.
2.4. Mapping Module
We evaluate two categories of regression models for mapping
the fused features to intelligibility scores: Neural models, in-
cluding fully-connected networks (FCN), mixture-of-experts
FCN (MoE FCN), CNN+GRU hybrids, and Transformer en-
coders, are capable of modeling complex nonlinear relation-
ships within high-dimensional feature spaces [16, 17, 18];
Gradient-boosted tree models such as CatBoost, LightGBM,
and XGBoost are more effective for structured statistical fea-
tures and can naturally capture feature interactions, particularly
under limited training data [19, 20, 21].
All features are normalized to zero mean and unit variance
before model training to ensure fair comparison.

3. Experiments and Results
3.1. Models and Modules
3.1.1. Proposed Model Family
To address the limitations of the baseline, we evaluate two
categories of predictive models: neural network models and
gradient-boosted tree models. All models share the same multi-
dimensional feature input but differ in feature processing and
mapping mechanisms:
•Neural Network Models: Include Fully-Connected
Network (FCN), Mixture-of-Experts FCN (MoE FCN),
CNN+GRU hybrid network, and Transformer encoder.
These models are designed to capture complex nonlinear
relationships in high-dimensional feature spaces, with the
Transformer leveraging self-attention to model long-range
dependencies between features.
•Gradient-Boosted Tree Models: Cover XGBoost, Light-
GBM, and CatBoost. These models excel at handling struc-
tured features and implicit feature interactions, with Cat-
Boost further optimizing for categorical feature processing
and overfitting prevention—key advantages for our mixed-
type feature set [19].
3.1.2. Feature Processing and Engineering
For the original audio data, we compute representative temporal
statistics including mean, standard deviation, maximum, mini-
mum, skewness, and kurtosis to obtain fixed-dimensional rep-
resentations. To better model nonlinear relationships for tree-
based regression models, we further construct pairwise interac-
tion features among the most influential features using weight-
based computation. Before fusing audio embeddings and acous-
tic features as the input to the subsequent mapping module, we
normalize them to zero mean and unit variance to eliminate
scale differences and ensure stable training.
3.2. Datasets
We conduct all experiments on the manually annotated lyric in-
telligibility dataset provided by the Cadenza 2026 Challenge,
which is widely used in related research for its high-quality hu-
man labels [22]. The dataset contains music segments spanning
multiple genres , with each segment paired with a continuous
intelligibility score labeled by at least three annotators.
To assess model generalization performance, we adopt a
5-fold cross-validation strategy: the dataset is randomly parti-
tioned into 5 equal subsets, with 4 subsets used for training and
1 for validation in each fold. This approach avoids overestima-
tion of performance caused by fixed train-test splits and better
reflects the model’s adaptability to unseen data.

3.5. Experimental Design
Our experiments follow a progressive verification logic to clar-
ify the effectiveness of each component:
1.Baseline Verification: Evaluate the performance of the
Whisper-based ASR transcription baseline to establish a ref-
erence benchmark.
2.Model Category Comparison: Compare the overall perfor-
mance of neural network models and gradient-boosted tree
models to identify the more suitable model type for this task.
3.Ablation Study: For the optimal model type (CatBoost), an-
alyze the contribution of feature interactions by comparing
model performance with and without interaction features.

3.6.1. Model Category Superiority: Gradient-Boosted Trees
Outperform Neural Networks
Both Table 2 and Figure 3 confirm that gradient-boosted tree
models (XGBoost, LightGBM, CatBoost) achieve significantly
better performance than neural network models.
Two factors explain this superiority: First, the task’s train-
ing data scale (thousands of samples) is insufficient to support
neural networks—especially Transformers, which suffer from
severe overfitting. Second, our feature set (mixed continuous
acoustic features and discrete statistical features) is naturally
compatible with gradient-boosted trees, which handle hetero-
geneous data without additional preprocessing, whereas neural
networks require complex feature adaptation [19].
3.6.2. Optimal Model: Whisper + CatBoost with Feature In-
teractions
The synergy between Whisper encoder embeddings and Cat-
Boost mapping model emerges as the optimal solution, as val-
idated by both quantitative and visual results. In Table 2, Cat-
Boost (using Whisper features with interactions) achieves the
lowest RMSE (27.367) and highest Corr (0.654), outperform-
ing the baseline by 6.66% in RMSE reduction and 10.85% in
Corr improvement. Figure 3 further highlights this: the ”Whis-
per + CatBoost” point is the leftmost and second-highest in the
plot, directly confirming its dominance. [13, 24].
3.6.3. Encoder Embedding Comparison: Whisper Outper-
forms Mert/Wav2Vec2
The performance hierarchy—Whisper outperforms Mert, which
in turn outperforms Wav2Vec2—holds for all mapping mod-
els (CatBoost, XGBoost, LightGBM) in Figure 3, indicating
that Whisper’s large-scale weak supervision (680k hours of
speech data) enables it to extract more robust prosodic and lex-
ical features for lyric intelligibility—features that Mert (music-
specialized) and Wav2Vec2 (general speech) fail to capture
[13, 14]. Compared with music-focused and general speech
pre-trained models, Whisper embeddings better model the pro-
nunciation variation.
3.6.4. Baseline Limitation: ASR Transcription Alone Is Insuffi-
cient
Both Table 2 and Figure 3 confirm the baseline’s limitations.
This discrepancy arises because baseline focuses solely on
word-level correctness, while human lyric intelligibility de-
pends on prosody, melody, and semantic coherence—elements
captured by our multi-feature framework. For example, Fig-
ure 3 shows that ”Whisper + CatBoost” (which integrates these
elements) is 1.953 units leftward (lower RMSE) and 0.064 up-
ward (higher Corr) than the baseline, directly validating the
value of our feature fusion approach [9, 25]. This demonstrates
that rigid word matching criteria cannot fully simulate human
auditory perception, and comprehensive feature modeling is es-
sential to narrow the objective–subjective evaluation gap in lyric
intelligibility prediction.
4. Conclusion
We present an automatic lyric intelligibility assessment frame-
work that fuses acoustic features and representations from pre-
trained encoders. Through comprehensive comparison, we ver-
ify the superiority of the Whisper encoder, which benefits from
large-scale unsupervised pre-training, in capturing robust pho-
netic and semantic cues for lyric intelligibility prediction. Tem-
poral statistics and pairwise feature interactions are further em-
ployed to enhance the discriminative power of the fused fea-
ture set. Experimental results show that gradient-boosted tree
models, especially CatBoost, outperform neural networks and
achieve state-of-the-art performance on the Cadenza Challenge
dataset [22]. Future work will explore end-to-end multimodal
models that integrate acoustic, semantic, and linguistic features
for further performance improvement.
