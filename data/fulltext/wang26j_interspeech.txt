Abstract
Although speaker disentanglement can improve within-corpus
depression detection, cross-corpus transfer remains challenging
as multi-source factor shifts are encoded and accumulated across
the network hierarchy, making speaker-only and output-level
constraints insufficient. To address this issue, we propose a
Layer-wise Multi-factor Adaptive Disentanglement framework
(LMAD) that enforces constraints at multiple key encoder lay-
ers to reduce the dependence of intermediate representations on
speaker and corpus factors while preserving dependence on de-
pression information, thereby mitigating the accumulation of nui-
sance information across layers. Furthermore, LMAD employs
adaptive weights to modulate disentanglement strength along
inter-layer and intra-layer dimensions. Experimental results
shown that LMAD consistently outperforms both the baseline
and speaker-only disentanglement models under bidirectional
DAIC-WoZ↔Androids transfer, while maintaining competitive
within-corpus performance.

1. Introduction
Depression is a severe mental disorder affecting approximately
4% of the global population, and individuals with severe symp-
toms may face an elevated risk of suicide [ 1, 2]. Prior studies
have shown that depression-associated neural impairments can
change the speech production process, leading to measurable
abnormalities across multiple acoustic dimensions [3]. This evi-
dence provides a foundation for developing automated speech-
based depression screening systems. In recent years, deep learn-
ing approaches, enabled by strong representation learning ca-
pacity, have become one of the dominant paradigms for speech-
based depression detection [4, 5].
Speech is a complex information carrier in which multiple
variables coexist, including speaker identity, language, recording
conditions, and task protocols [6, 7]. These factors are highly
entangled with depression-related cues [8]. During data-driven
training, deep models may inadvertently encode nuisance fac-
tors that are unrelated to depression but strongly correlated with
the data source into the learned depression representation. This
can create shortcut features and yield high deceptively perfor-
mance on the training corpus. When the model is evaluated
on a different corpus, e.g., with different recording conditions,
speaker or language, these shortcuts often break down, leading
to pronounced performance degradation [9]. Therefore, the key
challenge for real-world deployment is not peak performance on
a single dataset, but stable cross-corpus transfer.

Many prior works focus on speaker identity, a salient and
readily observable nuisance source, and apply disentanglement
objectives to reduce speaker information in the learned repre-
sentations [10, 11, 12]. This reduces speaker-specific bias and
improves generalization to unseen speakers. However, although
such approaches yield consistent improvements in within-corpus
evaluation, speaker-only disentanglement is insufficient to ad-
dress representation shift induced by multi-source discrepancies
in cross-corpus settings. Consequently, these methods often pro-
vide limited and inconsistent gains under cross-corpus transfer.
To mitigate cross-corpus discrepancies, [13] explores multi-
domain feature alignment. Nevertheless, both domain-alignment
and disentanglement methods typically impose disentanglement
only at the encoder output, i.e., on the final-layer representation.
Owing to inter-layer dependencies and the cumulative nature of
deep feature transformations, nuisance factors encoded in earlier
intermediate representations can be propagated and amplified
in deeper layers, ultimately shaping the geometry of the repre-
sentation space and the resulting decision boundary. As a result,
output-level constraints alone are often insufficient to counteract
such layer-wise accumulation of domain bias [14, 15].
From a layer-wise representation perspective, deep encoders
exhibit marked hierarchical non-uniformity [16, 17]. Different
layers encode speaker identity, corpus factors, and depression-
related cues with varying strengths, and even within a sin-
gle layer, channels and dimensions can differ substantially in
their sensitivity to these factors. More importantly, depression-
related cues are not strictly orthogonal to speaker and corpus
factors [12]. Consequently, applying disentanglement regular-
ization with a uniform strength across all layers can lead to
over-disentanglement in some layers, weakening depression-
discriminative information, while leaving residual speaker and
corpus bias in others due to under-disentanglement. Therefore, a
central challenge in cross-corpus depression detection is to sup-
press multi-source nuisance factors while preserving depression-
discriminative information, and to prevent their layer-wise accu-
mulation and propagation throughout the encoder hierarchy.
To address these issues, we propose a Layer-wise Multi-
factor Adaptive Disentanglement framework under an unsuper-
vised domain adaptation (UDA) setting. We use Hilbert-Schmidt
Independence Criterion (HSIC) [18, 19] to quantify the statistical
dependence between layer-wise representations and speaker iden-
tity, corpus factors, and depression-related information. We then
impose disentanglement constraints at multiple key encoder lay-
ers based on these dependencies to suppress speaker- and corpus-
related dependence while preserving depression-discriminative
information. Furthermore, we introduce an adaptive weight-
ing strategy that combines dependence statistics with gradient-
direction agreement to modulate factor-specific disentanglement
strength along both inter-layer and intra-layer dimensions.

3. Experimental Setup
3.1. Corpus Description
We evaluate LMAD on two benchmark corpora, DAIC-WoZ
[20, 21] and Androids [22]. DAIC-WoZ is an English clinical-
interview corpus with 189 subjects, split into train/valid/test sets
with 107/35/47 subjects, respectively. Androids is an Italian cor-
pus with 118 subjects (64 depressed), and provides a predefined
5-fold cross-validation protocol. To better match the interaction
style of DAIC-WoZ, we use only the spontaneous-speech portion
of Androids.
3.2. Speech Pre-processing
Following prior work [ 8, 10, 11, 12, 13, 17, 23, 24, 25], we
segment the long recordings of each subject in each corpus into
3.84-second speech segments. Each segment is converted into
40-dimensional Fbank features (window length: 64 ms; frame
shift: 32 ms), which are used as the model input features.
3.3. Backbone Implementation
We instantiate LMAD with two encoder backbones: DepAu-
dioNet [24], denoted as Db and ECAPA-TDNN [26] denoted
as Eb. For DepAudioNet, we impose layer-wise constraints
on its four layer-wise outputs. For ECAPA-TDNN, we apply
constraints to 7 representations taken after Conv1dReluBn, SE-
Res2Block-1, SE-Res2Block-2, SE-Res2Block-3, Conv1dRelu
(fusion), Attentive Statistics Pooling, FC-BN.

3.4. Training Protocol
We under an unsupervised domain adaptation setting. In each
transfer direction, one corpus is treated as the labeled source
domain Ds and the other as the unlabeled target domain Dt,
and we swap their roles for bidirectional evaluation. During
training, the main depression detection loss is computed only on
labeled source samples, while the layer-wise disentanglement
constraints use speaker IDs and the domain indicator c∈{ s, t}
from both corpora. Each mini-batch is constructed by mixing
source and target segments with a fixed ratio (default 1:1) to
stabilize dependence estimation.
Androids corpus provides a predefined 5-fold split. For each
fold r∈{ 1, . . . ,5}, we train on the DAIC-WoZ training set
jointly with the Androids training subset of fold r, and evaluate
on the DAIC-WoZ test set and the Androids test subset of fold
r. We repeat each fold with five random seeds and report the
fold-level average, final results are averaged across the five folds.
3.5. Optimization and Metrics
We train for 100 epochs using Adam with an initial learning
rate of 0.01, decayed by a factor of 0.9 every 10 epochs, and
a batch size of 512. We report subject-level macro-F1 and per-
class F1 for healthy control (hc) and depressed (dp) subjects;
subject-level depression prediction are obtained by aggregating
segment-level posteriors via majority vote.

4.2. Layer-wise Depression Dependence Proportion
Figure 3 reports the layer-wise depression dependence propor-
tion P (l) for four settings using Eb trained on DAIC-WoZ and
evaluated on the combined DAIC+Androids test set. The back-
bone (Eb) curve is nearly flat (0.33–0.35), and final-layer-only
alignment (MDFA) yields only marginal changes (0.34–0.36),
indicating limited redistribution of dependence across the hierar-
chy. LMADn
Eb without adaptive weighting exhibits an inter-layer
oscillatory pattern, with the depression proportion dropping to
0.31 at the final embedding layer. This suggests that using fixed
disentanglement strength can lead to an imbalanced layer-wise
allocation of task-relevant information, undermining the stabil-
ity of the final representation. In contrast, LMADw
Eb increases
depression proportion steadily toward deeper layers and peaks at
the embedding layer (0.50), the highest among all settings. This
pattern is consistent with the within- or cross-corpus gains, sug-
gesting that gradient-aware adaptive weighting more effectively
shifts the final embedding toward depression-related dependence
while reducing speaker and corpus dependence.

5. Conclusion
We proposed LMAD, a layer-wise multi-factor adaptive disen-
tanglement framework for cross-corpus speech-based depres-
sion detection under an unsupervised domain adaptation setting.
Experiments on bidirectional DAIC-WoZ↔Androids transfer
show that LMAD consistently outperforms both the baseline and
speaker-only disentanglement methods, while maintaining com-
petitive within-corpus performance. A limitation of this work is
that the set of key layers for disentanglement is selected a priori,
which may not yield the optimal disentanglement pattern for a
given backbone. Future work will focus on enabling the model
to adaptively select disentanglement layers during training to
further improve transferability and overall performance.
