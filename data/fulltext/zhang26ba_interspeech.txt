Neural Oscillatory Mechanisms of Speaker Normalization Under Cognitive 
Load: Evidence from Cantonese Tone Perception 
Kaile Zhang, Gang Peng 
The Hong Kong Polytechnic University, Hong Kong SAR, China 
kaile-keller.zhang@polyu.edu.hk, gang.peng@polyu.edu.hk 
 
Abstract 
Speaker normalization is essential for speech perception, 
particularly in tonal languages like Cantonese. Listeners can 
normalize talker variability under cognitive load, yet the neural 
mechanisms remain unclear. We recorded EEG from Cantonese 
speakers during a tone normalization task with and without 
concurrent visual tasks. Although behavioral performance was 
unaffected by visual distraction, neural data revealed distinct 
processing strategies. Without load, alpha -band 
desynchronization emerged, but load conditions triggered 
sustained parieto-occipital alpha activation, reflecting active 
suppression of visual distractors. High load uniquely induced 
prefrontal delta-band inhibition, indicating an attentional shift 
from internal visual processing to external auditory stimuli. 
These results suggest that normalization under load is not 
automatic. Instead, the brain actively reallocates resources to 
shield speech processing from distraction, supporting an active 
control hypothesis. 
Index Terms: speaker normalization, context effect, cognitive 
load, visual interference, brain oscillation 
1. Introduction 
1.1. Speaker normalization in Cantonese tone 
perception 
A pervasive challenge in speech perception is the "lack of 
invariance" [1]. The acoustic realization of phonemes varies 
substantially across talkers due to physiological differences in 
vocal tract length and laryngeal size. To achieve perceptual 
constancy, listeners must engage in speaker normalization, 
dynamically adjusting the perceived speech signals relative to 
the speaker’s vocal characteristics [2]. The preceding context 
provides effective cues about the speakers’ vocal 
characteristics. For example, based on a speaker’s greeting “早
晨”(/zou25 san21/, good morning), a listener can quickly 
establish an acoustic-tone map for that particular speaker, with 
the lowest fundamental frequency (F0) for the low tone (i.e., the 
end point of /san21/) and highest F0 for the high tone (i.e., the 
end point of /zou25/) [3]. With this speaker-specific map, 
listeners can recalibrate the incoming F0s to the proper tonal 
categories. This process is known as extrinsic normalization 
[4].  
Extrinsic normalization is particularly critical for tonal 
languages like Cantonese, where pitch variations convey lexical 
meaning. The Cantonese tonal system includes three level 
tones, high (T55), mid (T33), and low (T22), distinguished 
primarily by F0 height [5]. However, F0 is highly susceptible 
to speaker variability [6]. A frequency produced as a high tone 
by a male speaker may be acoustically identical to a mid or low 
tone produced by a female speaker. Consequently, listeners 
cannot rely on absolute pitch. Instead, they rely heavily on 
extrinsic normalization, utilizing spectral cues in the preceding 
carrier phrase to calibrate their perception of the target tone, 
which significantly improves the perceptual accuracy [7]. The 
extrinsic normalization typically results in a contrastive context 
effect, where a target is perceived as lower in pitch when 
preceded by a high-F0 context, and vice versa [8].  
1.2. Speaker normalization under cognitive load  
While the behavioral necessity of speaker normalization is 
clear, the cognitive architecture supporting it remains debated. 
The active control hypothesis posits that speaker normalization 
is not a purely automatic and bottom-up process, but a resource-
demanding and controlled process dependent on central 
cognitive capacity [9]. Evidence for an active control 
normalization process includes findings that high-variability 
speech is processed more slowly and less accurately than low-
variability speech [8], [9], that familiar-talker speech is easier 
to identify [10], and that superior parietal regions associated 
with attentional networks show increased activity during 
speaker normalization process [11]. However, this view faces 
an empirical paradox. Recent dual-task studies demonstrate that 
listeners can successfully normalize talker variability even 
when cognitive resources are depleted by a concurrent task, 
regardless of whether that task is visual [12] or auditory [13], 
linguistic [14] or nonlinguistic [15]. This resilience suggests 
that speaker normalization is not an “all-or-nothing” process 
that fails under cognitive load. Rather, the brain likely employs 
compensatory neural strategies to maintain speaker-adaptation 
success in adverse conditions. Therefore, the critical question is 
not if listeners can normalize speaker variability u nder 
cognitive load, but how the neural system reconfigures itself to 
support speaker normalization when cognitive resources are 
scarce. 
1.3. The effect of brain oscillations on speech 
perception  
Neural oscillations play a key roles in coordinating information 
flow across brain networks during complex cognitive tasks, 
such as the speech perception in adverse conditions [16], [17]. 
Thus, neural oscillations offer a promising window into  
understanding how listeners adapt to speaker variability under 
cognitive load. The following brain oscillations were expected 
to be particularly relevant to speaker adaptation under load.  
The alpha-band (8–13 Hz) brain activity is central to this 
question. Moving beyond the view of alpha as a mere “idling” 
rhythm, alpha-band activity plays a critical role in the 
suppression of task-irrelevant information [18], [19]. In the 
context of the “cocktail party” problem, alpha oscillation acts 
as an inhibitory gating mechanism [20]. Research consistently 
demonstrates that alpha power increases in brain regions 
processing ignored acoustic streams (e.g., a competing talker) 
and decreases in regions processing the attended target stimuli 
[21]. When speech is presented in noise, the cognitive load 
required to separate the target from the background noise is 
reflected in the decreased alpha power in the temporal regions 
[22]. This localized alpha-band modulation ensures that limited 
cognitive resources are not exhausted by irrelevant distractors, 
thereby protecting the online processing of the target signal.   
Low-frequency oscillations play a critical role in attentional 
allocation. Delta power (1–3 Hz) typically increases during 
tasks requiring internally directed attention, such as mental 
calculations, and decreases when attention is oriented toward 
the external environment [23], [24]. This delta enhancement 
during high-load mental tasks has been interpreted in two ways. 
It may represent a deliberate reallocation of attentional

maintenance of contextual information in working memory

which depends on the retention and use of context cues. 
1.4. The present study 
While the roles of these brain oscillations are well documented 
in general cognitive tasks, it remains unclear which specific 
neural mechanisms support speaker normalization when 
cognitive resources are limited by a competing task. The 
present study investigates the neural dynamics of extrinsic 
normalization in Cantonese under varying levels of cognitive 
load. Native speakers identified Cantonese tones produced by 
multiple speakers with the help of context cues while 
simultaneously performing a visual search task of varying 
complexity. By analyzing behavioral accuracy alongside EEG 
time-frequency responses, the present study aimed to reveal 
how neural oscillations contribute to maintaining perceptual 
constancy in multi-tasking conditions. 
2. Methods  
2.1. Participants 
Thirty-two right-handed native Cantonese speakers (15 
females, 17 males; mean age = 20.5 years) participated in the 
study. All participants reported normal hearing, speech, and 
language abilities, and they all have normal or corrected-to-
normal vision. Prior to the experiment,  written informed 
consent was obtained from all participants . Participants 
received appropriate compensation for their time. The study 
was approved by the Human Subjects Ethics Sub-Committee of 
The Hong Kong Polytechnic University. 
2.2. Stimuli 
The stimuli closely followed those used in previous studies on 
Cantonese tone normalization [3], [29]. Auditory stimuli 
consisted of context-target pairs. Contexts were derived from 
the Cantonese phrase “呢個字係” (/li55 ko33 tsi22 hɐi22/, this 
word is...), recorded by four native speakers with distinct but 
overlapping pitch ranges (2 male, 2 female). To simulate intra-
speaker variability and to elicit a contrastive context effect, F0 
trajectories were shifted by 3 semitones using Praat [30], 
creating high-, mid-, and low-F0 contexts. Targets were the 
Cantonese syllable /ji33/ (“意”, meaning), produced by the 
same four speakers without pitch manipulation. Although the 
mid-level tone T33 is ambiguous in isolation, extrinsic 
normalization typically bias perception toward /ji55/ (“醫”, 
doctor) in low-F0 contexts and /ji22/ (“二”, two) in high-F0 
contexts. By observing the responses in different contexts, we 
shall tell whether listeners engaged in normalization. Target 
durations were normalized to 450 ms to ensure consistent EEG 
time-locking. The stimulus set included 12 unique contexts (4 
speakers × 3 pitch heights) and four targets.  
Visual stimuli for the secondary task were adapted from 
Bosker’s study [12]. Stimuli consisted of object grids 
containing geometric shapes ( e.g., diamonds, triangles, 
squares) on a black background. Cognitive load was 
manipulated via grid complexity: a 4 × 4 grid for the low-load 
(LL) condition and an 8 × 8 grid for the high -load (HL) 
condition (Figure 1). A target white diamond replaced a random 
object in 50% of trials. 
2.3. Experiment procedure 
The experiment employed a dual-task paradigm comprising a 
primary auditory word identification task and a secondary 
visual search task. Participants were seated in a soundproof 
booth with audio delivered via insert earphones. The 
experiment consisted of three counter-balanced blocks: no 
secondary task (NL), secondary task of LL, and secondary task 
of HL. Each block contained 108 test trials (9 repetitions of the 
12 context-target combinations) and 36 fillers, randomized to 
maximize variability. 
 
Figure 1: The trial procedure. No object grid and the 
last frame for the no-visual-task condition. 
The trial procedure is illustrated in Figure 1. In dual-task 
blocks (LL and HL), a visual grid appeared simultaneously with 
the auditory context. Participants scanned the grid for a white 
diamond while attending to the auditory context. Following the 
context, a silent interval (300–500 ms) preceded the target 
word. A response prompt appeared 350–550 ms after the target 
stimulus, instructing participants to identify the word as /ji55/, 
/ji33/, or /ji22/ via keypress (max 1500 ms). Subsequently, a 
second prompt required a presence/absence judgment for the 
visual target. In single-task blocks (NL), a fixation cross 
replaced the visual grid, and the secondary response step was 
omitted. Participants were instructed to assign equal priority to 
both the auditory and visual tasks. 
2.4. EEG data recording and preprocessing  
EEG data were recorded using a SynAmps 2 amplifier 
(NeuroScan, USA) with a 64-channel Ag/AgCl electrode cap 
arranged according to the international 10–20 system. Signals 
were sampled at 1000 Hz and electrode impedances were 
maintained below 5 kΩ. The online reference was positioned 
between Cz and CPz . Horizontal and vertical 
electrooculograms (EOG) were recorded via bipolar channels 
to monitor eye movements.  
Preprocessing was conducted in MATLAB using custom 
scripts with functions from EEGLAB [31] and ERPLAB [32]. 
Continuous data were band-pass filtered (0.01–50 Hz) and re-
referenced offline to the average d mastoids. Target-locked 
epochs spanning -500 ms to 800 ms were extracted and 
baseline-corrected using the pre-stimulus interval. Epochs 
exceeding ±100 μV at any scalp electrode were rejected. Ocular 
artifacts were automatically detected using moving -window 
criteria: blinks via VEOG (peak-to-peak threshold >100 μV, 
200 ms window, 50 ms step) and saccades via HEOG (step -
function threshold >40 μV, 400 ms window, 10 ms step). 
Participants retaining fewer than 70% of trials in any 
experimental condition were excluded, resulting in a final 
sample of 30 participants.

3. Results 
3.1. Behavioral results 
According to the contrastive context effect, if subjects used the 
context cues to guide their tone perception (i.e. the extrinsic 
normalization), they should give a high tone response (i.e., 
/ji55/) in the low-F0 context, a mid tone response (i.e., /ji33/) in 
the mid-F0 context, and a low tone (i.e., /ji22/) in the high-F0 
context condition. Therefore, if subjects gave the expected 
response, we set the expected response accuracy of that trial as 
1, otherwise as 0. Figure 2 illustrated the expected response 
accuracy in each condition. To reveal if the speaker 
normalization process was affected by cognitive load, we fit a 
generalized linear mixed effect model on the expected response 
accuracy with Context F0 (high, mid, low), Cognitive Load 
(NL, LL, HL), and their interaction as the fixed effect, and with 
the by-subject and by-speaker intercepts (i.e., four speakers 
recording the stimuli) as the random effects.  
 
Figure 2: The expected response accuracy. 
The analysis revealed a significant main effect of Context 
F0, χ2 (2) = 19.77, p < 0.001. Pos hoc comparisons revealed that 
the expected response accuracy in the high -F0 context was 
significantly higher than that in the low-F0 (β = 0.31, SE = 0.07, 
z = 4.47, p < 0.001) and mid-F0 (β = 0.45, SE = 0.07, z = 6.54, 
p < 0.001) conditions. This might be caused by the differences 
in the context-target pitch distance. In the present study, high-
F0 and low-F0 contexts were generated by shifting the original 
F0 contour three semitones upward or downward, respectively, 
while the target tone was always T33. Consequently, perception 
of T22, which is closer in pitch to T33, was easier in high-F0 
contexts relative to the perception of T55 in low-F0 contexts. 
The Cognitive Load, χ2 (2) =0.32, p = 0.85, and its interaction 
with Context F0, χ2 (4) = 4.54, p = 0.34 were not significant, 
indicating that the manipulation of cognitive load did not affect 
the speaker normalization process.  
A paired-sample t-test revealed that participants performed 
significantly better on the visual search task in the LL condition 
(M = 0.91, SD = 0.06) than in the HL condition [M = 0.7, SD = 
0.09; t(31) = - 14.67, p < 0.001] 
3.2. Time-frequency results 
Single-trial EEG epochs were decomposed into time-frequency 
representations using the newtimef function in EEGLAB. 
Event-Related Spectral Perturbation (ERSP) was calculated 
using the Fast Fourier Transform with Hanning window 
tapering to measure relative spectral power changes. Epochs 
were zero-padded by a factor of 16 to improve frequency 
resolution. The analysis covered a time range of -500 to 800 ms 
and a frequency range of 1 to 50 Hz (1 Hz steps). A baseline 
window of -300 to -100 ms was selected to minimize overlap 
between pre- and post-stimulus activity [33]. ERSP values were 
baseline-normalized (dB conversion). To increase the signal-to-
noise ratio, data were averaged across the three context F0s, as 
behavioral normalization occurred regardless of context F0 (see 
Figure 2). Significant ERSP deviations from baseline were 
identified via non-parametric permutation testing (1000 
iterations, p < 0.01). Surrogate distributions were generated by 
shuffling spectral estimates from the baseline period.  
 
Figure 3: The ERSP results in each condition. 
The non-parametric permutation test revealed that the NL 
condition exhibited significant alpha-band (8–13 Hz) inhibition 
relative to baseline from approximately 0–415 ms (the upper 
panel in Figure 3). In contrast, contexts with load elicited 
significant alpha-band activation: 246–800 ms for the LL 
condition (the middle panel in Figure 3) and 159–800 ms for 
the HL condition (the lower panel in Figure 3). Additionally, 
target tone perception in HL showed significant delta-band (1–
3 Hz) inhibition from 303–565 ms (the lower panel in Figure 
3). The topography in each condition was plotted by averaging 
the alpha-band (or delta-band) ERSP in the significant time 
window. Topographies (right panels in Figure 3) indicated that 
alpha suppression in NL was most prominent in prefrontal 
(FP1, FP2, FPz, F11, F12) and parieto-occipital (PO7, PO8) 
regions. Conversely, alpha activation in load conditions was 
localized to parieto-occipital areas (PO3, PO4, PO8). Delta 
inhibition in HL was maximal in the prefrontal area. Contrary 
to our expectations, no significant beta-band activity was 
observed. This finding suggests that the maintenance of context 
information in extrinsic normalization does not prominently 
engage beta-band oscillations, at least as measured by scalp-
recorded EEG.

In summary, the time-frequency analysis on the EEG data 
revealed distinct neural signatures for speech normalization 
depending on cognitive load. Normalization without load 
triggered early alpha inhibition (0–415 ms) in prefrontal and 
parieto-occipital regions. However, normalization under load 
triggered sustained parieto-occipital alpha activation (~200–
800 ms). Relative to LL, the HL condition was distinguished by 
increased alpha activation (428–707 ms) and specific prefrontal 
delta suppression (303–565 ms). 
3.3. Behavioral results and ERSP correlation 
We further investigated the relationship between spectral power 
changes and behavioral performance. ERSP values for each 
condition were averaged across the significant time windows 
and electrode clusters identified in Section 3.2. Pearson 
correlation analysis revealed a significant negative correlation 
between the secondary task accuracy and the alpha-band power 
in the LL condition ( r = -0.38, p = 0.036). Specifically, 
participants with higher alpha-band activation exhibited lower 
accuracy on the secondary task in the LL condition. No other 
significant correlations were observed. 
4. Discussion 
The present study investigated how neural oscillations support 
the speaker normalization in Cantonese tone perception under 
varying cognitive load. Behaviorally, the contrastive context 
effects were comparable across all load conditions, indicating 
that extrinsic normalization of Cantonese tones is highly 
resilient to visual distractions. However, the time-frequency 
analysis on the EEG data revealed that this behavioral 
constancy is not achieved through an automatic process. 
Instead, the brain dynamically reco nfigures its oscillatory 
networks, specifically through alpha and delta band 
modulations in parieto-occipital and prefrontal regions, to 
protect speech normalization when cognitive resources are 
limited. 
4.1. Parieto-occipital alpha synchronization inhibits 
visual distractions 
The time-frequency analysis revealed a prominent shift in the 
alpha-band activity depending on the presence of a secondary 
task. In the NL (no load) condition, tone normalization elicited 
early alpha-band event-related desynchronization (ERD) in 
prefrontal and parieto-occipital regions. However, when the 
visual tasks were introduced, this pattern reversed into a 
sustained alpha event-related synchronization (ERS) localized 
to the parieto-occipital area, with HL (high load) eliciting 
significantly stronger alpha ERS than LL (low load) during the 
late perceptual stage (428–707 ms). 
This load-induced change in the alpha-band activation can 
be interpreted by the inhibitory gating hypothesis. Alpha ERD 
typically reflects cortical excitation and active task engagement 
[21]. Therefore, the significant early alpha-band ERD in the NL 
condition suggested that listeners actively engaged into the 
normalization tasks when there is no visual distraction. Alpha 
ERS does not reflect task engagement, but rather the active 
suppression of task-irrelevant or potentially interfering neural 
activities [21]. Because the parieto-occipital region is heavily 
involved in visual and spatial processing, the pronounced alpha 
ERS in the dual-task conditions suggests an active top-down 
inhibition of the visual processing at this region. That is,  to 
maintain the acoustic-phonemic processing required for 
speaker normalization, the brain actively suppresses the 
processing of the competing visual distractors via the alpha-
band synchronization. This interpretation is strongly supported 
by the behavioral-neural correlation results where participants 
with higher alpha-band activity in the LL condition exhibited 
lower accuracy on the visual task. By sacrificing visual 
processing via alpha inhibition, listeners effectively increase 
the signal-to-noise ratio for the auditory task, preserving 
speaker normalization under cognitive load. 
4.2. Prefrontal delta desynchronization modulates 
attentional resource allocation 
The HL condition was uniquely characterized by significant 
delta-band desynchronization over the prefrontal region (303–
565 ms). Previous research has established that delta power is 
sensitive to the direction of attentional focus, increaseing when 
attention is directed inward toward internal cognitive 
processing, such as during mental arithmetic [24], and 
decreasing when attention is oriented toward external sensory 
input [23]. The prefrontal cortex, particularly the frontopolar 
region, is central to this directional regulation, functioning as a 
gateway that dynamically allocates
