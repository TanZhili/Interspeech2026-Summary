Categorical Perception of Mandarin Tones in Jingpo Native Speakers
Binghao Wang1,2, Xinyuan Li1,2, Yao Lu1,2,∗∗
1 Laboratory of Language Sciences, Peking University, People’s Republic of China
2 Department of Chinese Language and Literature, Peking University, People’s Republic of China
wbhykx@stu.pku.edu.cn, xinyuanli@stu.pku.edu.cn, yaolu@pku.edu.cn
Abstract
Against the background of promoting Mandarin around China,
the tonal differences between Jingpo and Mandarin may affect
Jingpo speakers’ perception of Mandarin tones. This study ex-
amined the categorical perception (CP) of Mandarin tones by 27
native Jingpo speakers and 23 native Mandarin speakers using
12 synthesized tone continua based on the 4 Mandarin tones.
Participants were asked to complete identification and discrim-
ination tasks. Results show that while Jingpo participants could
distinguish the phonemic categories of most tone pairs in Man-
darin, they did not exhibit CP between Tone 35 and Tone 214,
probably due to a lack of falling-rising tones in Jingpo. Be-
sides, Jingpo participants’ CP degree for several tone pairs was
significantly lower than that of Mandarin participants. These
findings not only validate the interpretability of the Perceptual
Assimilation Model but also contribute to the understanding of
cross-linguistic influence in tone perception.
Index Terms: Mandarin tones, categorical perception, Jingpo,
Perceptual Assimilation Model
1. Introduction
Categorical perception (CP) means that the categories mastered
by people affect their perception, so that continuous speech
changes are perceived as limited, classifiable categories [1]. In
contrast, continuous perception refers to a smooth transition
from one category to another without a clear distinction [2].
CP and continuous perception have been widely examined
in phonetics. Although not without controversy, it is found that
consonants are perceived categorically [3, 4], whereas vowels
are perceived continuously [5]. Regarding tone perception, pre-
vious studies have found that tones of different contours are
perceived categorically [6, 7], while tones with similar con-
tours tend to be perceived continuously, such as the low-level
and high-level tones in Cantonese [8] and the five level tones
in Xinzhai Hmu [9]. Mandarin comprises four tones: high-level
(T1), high-rising (T2), falling-rising (T3), and high-falling (T4),
with pitch values of 55, 35, 214, and 51, respectively. The ear-
liest study on Mandarin tone perception focused on T1 and T2
[10] and found typical CP. Subsequent studies found that T2-
T3 exhibits continuous perception [11], whereas T2-T4, T3-T4,
T1-T3, and T1-T4 all follow CP patterns [12, 13].
With the increasing promotion and widespread use of Man-
darin, speakers of ethnic minority languages in China have be-
gun to learn Mandarin as a second language (L2). According to
the Perceptual Assimilation Model (PAM) [14], L2 sounds are
perceived relative to native categories, such that when two L2
sounds are mapped into the same L1 category (Single-Category

Assimilation), discrimination is typically poor. Previous re-
search suggests that while tone contour matters, onset and off-
set pitch also influence the perceived similarity of L2 tones. For
example, although Mandarin T3 has a contour similar to Tone
315 in Thai, Mandarin listeners often categorize Tone 315 as
Mandarin T2, likely due to its high offset pitch overriding the
contour mismatch [15].
For many ethnic minority people in China, the tone system
of their L1 may be more complex than Mandarin. The influence
of L1 tone experience on L2 tone perception remains unclear
and contested, whether positive or negative [16, 17]. Recent
studies, such as research on Lhasa Tibetan speakers perceiving
Mandarin tones [18], highlight the role of L1 tone experience in
L2 tone perception. As a tone language, Jingpo may influence
the perception of Mandarin tones by its native speakers, making
it important for further investigation.
This study conducted a perception experiment on Mandarin
tones among native Jingpo speakers and native Mandarin speak-
ers, to explore their perception patterns and the potential impli-
cations for L2 tone perception, to address the following research
questions: (1) What are the perception patterns of Mandarin
tones among native Jingpo speakers? (2) How do native Jingpo
speakers differ from native Mandarin speakers in Mandarin tone
perception? How does native language tone experience influ-
ence their perception of Mandarin tones?
2. Tones in Jingpo
Spoken by a subgroup of Jingpo people, Jingpo belongs to the
Burman branch within the Sino-Tibetan language family. It has
a complex tone system with four phonemes [19, 20]. Previous
studies report that Jingpo has two falling tones with pitch values
of 31 and 51, and two level tones with pitch values of 33 and
55. Jingpo also contrasts modal and pressed vowels [21]. While
most tones retain the same pitch values across these phonation
contexts, Tone 55 is realized as a rising tone with a pitch value
of 35 in modal contexts, and remains unchanged with pressed
vowels [22, pp. 8-10]. Although not a distinct phoneme, this
rising variant is treated separately in this study.
Based on previous descriptions of the Jingpo tone system,
this study recruited a 37-year-old male native Jingpo speaker
and broadcaster for recording. For each tone, 5 lexical items
were selected. Fundamental frequency (F0) was extracted using
Praat [23], and durations were normalized. F0 contour plots
were generated in R 4.5.2, as shown in Figure 1. The results
largely align with previous findings, except that the onset of
Tone 35 was slightly lower, noted as 25 here. The pitch values,
contours, and example words for the Jingpo tones are shown in

3. Methods
3.1. Stimuli
The stimuli were synthesized from four characters in Mandarin,
namely “搭” [ta 55], “达” [ta 35], “打” [ta 214], and “大” [ta 51].
These syllables share the same consonant and vowel, differ-
ing only in tone. Specifically, [ta 55] carries T1, [ta 35] carries
T2, [ta214] carries T3, and [ta 51] carries T4. The samples were
recorded by a 35-year-old female native speaker at 44.1 kHz
with 16-bit depth. Each sample was nearly 500 ms in duration.
The continua were generated using the PSOLA (Pitch Syn-
chronous Overlap and Add) method [24]. For each tone con-
trast, one endpoint tone served as the source syllable, and its F0
contour was incrementally modified 10 times toward the oppo-
site endpoint to form one continuum. The procedure was then
repeated in reverse, yielding a second continuum with identi-
cal F0 contours but different phonation types. Six tone con-
trasts (T1-T2, T1-T3, T1-T4, T2-T3, T2-T4, T3-T4) yielded 12
continua. Each continuum contained 11 stimuli, for a total of
132 stimuli. The audio files for the T1-T2 continuum are pub-
licly accessible at https://github.com/wbh-XC/Interspeech2026
for reference. The F0 curves of the six sets of continua are
shown in Figure 2; the vertical axis represents F0, and each plot
contains 11 curves corresponding to the 11 stimuli.

3.2. Participants
27 native Jingpo subjects (12 male, 15 female) took part in the
experiment, aged 16-47 years (M = 26, SD = 8.9). All were born
and raised in Dehong Dai and Jingpo Autonomous Prefecture,
Yunnan Province. They primarily used Jingpo in daily com-
munication. Their Mandarin proficiency was assessed using a
standardized test prior to the experiment. All scored Level 2-
B, which suffices for daily communication but carries a promi-
nent accent. Besides, 23 native Mandarin subjects (10 male,
13 female) were recruited in the experiment, aged 18-30 years
(M = 23, SD = 3.9). They were born and raised in northern
China. They primarily used Mandarin in daily communica-
tion. None reported language-related disorders. All participants
could communicate in Mandarin and recognize the four charac-
ters mentioned in Section 3.1.
3.3. Experiment
The experiment consisted of identification and discrimination
tasks. In the identification task, the 12 continua were presented
sequentially. Each stimulus was played twice in random order,
yielding 264 responses. While a sound was played, two Chi-
nese characters representing the tone contrast appeared on the
screen (e.g., “搭” on the left and “达” on the right for the T1-
T2 continuum). Following the convention of previous studies
[25], 5 seconds were given for the participants to choose the
heard character after sound playback by pressing the “←” or
“→” key.
The discrimination task used an AX paradigm. Stimuli
were presented in pairs with a 500-ms interstimulus interval.
The participants had 5 seconds to judge whether the two sounds
carried the same tone. The continua were presented sequen-
tially, each comprising 54 randomly ordered pairs. For each
continuum, the 11 stimuli were labeled 1-11. The pairs included
9 identical pairs (2-2, 3-3, ..., 10-10) and 18 two-step different
pairs in both forward (1-3, 2-4, ..., 9-11) and backward orders
(3-1, 4-2, ..., 11-9), each played twice, totaling 648 judgments
per subject. The experiment was conducted using E-Prime 2.0.
The overall missing trial rate was only 2%. These trials were
excluded from the statistical analysis..

3.4. Data Analysis
Data processing for the experiments followed the method de-
scribed in [26]. For the identification task, the proportion of re-
sponses selecting each character was calculated for every stim-
ulus position, generating two identification curves per tone con-
trast. The x-coordinate of their intersection was defined as the
categorical boundary (xcb). Boundary width (Wcb) was defined
as the horizontal distance between the 25% and 75% identifi-
cation points.W cb andx cb were calculated by a binary logistic
regression model based on the binomial distribution of the iden-
tification scores, i.e., the percentage of the responses choosing
each character as the answer.
For the discrimination task, the peakedness of the discrim-
ination curve (P pk) was calculated following the procedure in
[26], as the difference between the between-category discrimi-
nation rate and the within-category discrimination rate.
Linear mixed-effects models were fitted for each of the 12
continua, with participant as a random effect, stimulus position
as a fixed effect, and discrimination rate as the dependent vari-
able, followed by likelihood Ratio Tests. Tukey HSD post-hoc
tests were then used to determine the locations of discrimination
peaks.
CP was identified using three criteria [26]: (1) presence of a
clearx cb, (2) a peak in the discrimination curve, and (3) align-
ment of the peak with thex cb. A smallerW cb and a larger
Ppk indicate a higher CP degree. Moreover,W cb andP pk
were calculated for each participant in each tone continuum.
Independent-samples T tests were conducted for the 12 con-
tinua to examine whether ethnicity influencedW cb andP pk.
All statistical analyses were conducted in R 4.5.2.
4. Results
The visualized perceptual results for each tone continuum are
presented in Figure 3, with Jingpo and Mandarin participants’
responses plotted together for direct comparison. The light blue
curves represent the results of Mandarin participants, and the
brown curves represent the results of Jingpo participants. Dots
and squares show the identification scores, while triangles indi-
cate discrimination rates. Thex cb,P pk, andW cb for all con-
tinua are listed in Table 2.
For the T1-T2 tone continuum (Figure 3a), Jingpo par-
ticipants exhibited a sharp discrimination peak regardless of
whether T1 or T2 served as the source syllable, with significant
differences in discrimination rates between groups (p < .001).
There were no significant differences inW cb andP pk between
Jingpo participants and Mandarin participants (p > .05).
For the T1-T3 continuum (Figure 3b), Jingpo participants
showed a relatively steep discrimination peak regardless of
whether T1 or T3 served as the source syllable, with significant
differences in discrimination rates between groups (p < .001).
Compared with Mandarin participants, no significant differ-
ences in theirW cb andP pk were found (p > .05), except that
when T1 served as the source syllable, theW cb of Jingpo par-
ticipants had significantly larger values (p=.012).
For the T1-T4 continuum (Figure 3c), when T1 served
as the source syllable, Jingpo participants showed a relatively
steep peak with discrimination rates varying significantly across
stimulus positions (p < .001). TheW cb of the Jingpo subjects
and that of the Mandarin participants didn’t significantly differ
(p > .05), whileP pk was significantly smaller for Jingpo par-
ticipants (p=.011). When T4 served as the source syllable,
the experimental results of Jingpo participants showed a broad
peak, and discrimination rates differed significantly across stim-
ulus positions (p < .001). Again,W cb showed no group differ-
ence (p > .05), andP pk was significantly smaller for Jingpo
participants (p=.007).
For the T2-T3 continuum (Figure 3d), when the source syl-
lable was T2, the discrimination rates of Jingpo participants var-
ied significantly across positions (p < .001), but post-hoc tests
yielded two largely overlapping subsets, indicating no clear
peak.P pk did not significantly differ between Jingpo and Man-
darin participants (p > .05), whileW cb was significantly larger
for Jingpo participants (p=.032). When the source syllable
was T3, there was no significant difference in the discrimina-
tion rate among Jingpo participants (p=.14), and no peak
emerged either. Compared with Mandarin participants, Jingpo
participants showed a significantly largerW cb (p=.006) and a
significantly smallerP pk (p=.011).
For the T2-T4 continuum (Figure 3e), when the source
syllable was T2, there was a significant difference in the dis-
crimination rate across stimulus positions among the Jingpo
participants (p < .001), with a broad discrimination peak.
Jingpo participants showed a largerW cb (p=.038) and a
smallerP pk (p=.006) than Mandarin participants. When the
source syllable was T4, the discrimination rate of Jingpo partic-
ipants showed a significant difference across stimulus positions
(p < .001), with a gentle peak. Jingpo participants exhibited

5. Discussion
5.1. Perceptual Patterns of Mandarin Tones by Native
Jingpo Speakers
Overall, the identification curves of the Jingpo participants were
relatively steep, indicating a reliable phonemic-level distinction
of Mandarin tone pairs. As shown in Table 2, the T2-T3 con-
tinuum had a notably largeW cb. In the discrimination task, all
continuum groups except T2-T3 displayed clear discrimination
peaks that aligned fully or partially withx cb. The results indi-
cate that native Jingpo speakers establish continuous perception
for the T2-T3 tone pair and CP for all other tone pairs.
Compared with native Mandarin speakers, it is found that
the performance of native Jingpo speakers on the T1-T2 and T1-
T3 continua is already close to that of native Mandarin speakers.
However, on the T1-T4, T2-T3, T2-T4, and T3-T4 continua,
they still show largerW cb, smallerP pk, or both, indicating a
relatively weak degree of categorization.
5.2. Difficulty in Perceiving Mandarin T2 and T3
The lack of CP for the T2–T3 contrast among Jingpo speakers
can be attributed to properties of both the Mandarin and Jingpo
tone systems.
In Mandarin, T2 is a high-rising tone, and T3 is a falling-
rising tone. Though their pitch contours should differ, T2 shows
an initial dip in natural speech, making its contour resemble
that of T3. This similarity weakens the categorical bound-
ary between T2 and T3. Notably, tones with similar contours
have been found to show continuous perception even for native
speakers [27]. T2 and T3 are also well-documented challenges
in Mandarin tone perception [28], further limiting the degree
of categorization achieved by Jingpo speakers. Experiments in
this study also demonstrated the difficulty of differentiating T2
and T3 for native Mandarin speakers.
The Jingpo tone system also constrains T2-T3 perception.
As shown in Table 1, Jingpo contains level, rising, and falling
tones, but no falling-rising tone. For Jingpo listeners, both T2
and T3 share some similarity with the Jingpo tone 25 in terms
of pitch height, albeit to different degrees, known as “Category-
Goodness Difference” in PAM theory [29], reducing discrim-
inability. Combined with the contour similarity between Man-
darin T2 and T3, this prevents the emergence of CP.
6. Conclusion
This study examined the categorical perception (CP) of the four
Mandarin tones among native Jingpo speakers. Except for the
T2-T3 continuum, Jingpo speakers established CP for all tone
pairs. For the T1-T4, T2-T4, and T3-T4 continua, Jingpo speak-
ers showed significantly weaker performances in differentiating
the tones than native Mandarin speakers did.
The experiment demonstrates that the absence of falling-
rising tones in Jingpo has notable effects on the perception of
Mandarin tones, thereby providing empirical evidence for the
applicability of the Perceptual Assimilation Model. The find-
ings may deepen our understanding of this under-researched
language and shed light on the complex influence of the L1 tone
system on L2 tone perception.
