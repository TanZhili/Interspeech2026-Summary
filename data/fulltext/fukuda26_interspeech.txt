What Makes Us Hate Our Own Voice? Large-scale experiments on
Playback–Imagery Gaps and Individual–Speech Feature Effects

Abstract
This study examines factors associated with the playback–
imagery gap underlying voice confrontation. Listening to one’s
recorded voice (playback) can be uncomfortable and has been
attributed to a mismatch between expected self-voice (imagery)
and the recording, yet the factors associated with this gap re-
main unclear. With Japanese participants (N= 459), we com-
pared impression ratings after playback of one’s recorded utter-
ances versus imagery of producing the same utterances using
counterbalanced block order. Playback produced more negative
affective evaluations than imagery, especially higher discom-
fort, whereas self-likeness did not show the same pattern. Ex-
ploratory trait–acoustic interactions suggested listener-specific
weighting of acoustic cues in self-voice evaluation. The gap
also varied with order and block structure, suggesting contex-
tual modulation. These results provide evidence relevant to self-
voice aversion and motivate listener-adaptive models and more
controlled follow-up studies for self-voice applications.
Index Terms: self-voice, auditory imagery, voice confronta-
tion, individual differences, speech features
1. Introduction
Hearing a recording of one’s own voice is often aversive, rais-
ing the question of how self-voice is represented and evaluated
affectively. Classicvoice confrontationwork reported nega-
tive reactions once listeners learned that the playback was their
own [1, 2]. Discomfort is often attributed to a conduction
mismatch between speaking (air+body) and recordings (air),
which can alter perceived timbre [3, 4]. However, because own-
voice perception integrates multiple cues (e.g., air- and bone-
conducted information) [5], a mismatch in bone-conducted
transmission alone is unlikely to account for the large individ-
ual differences we observe. Moreover, self-voice can be gen-
erated internally via auditory imagery [6]; thus,imagined and
played-backself-voice may be evaluated differently, and short-
term carryover between the two may modulate their gap [7].
In this paper, we conducted an online within-participant
study to quantify how listeners evaluate their own voice during
playback versus imagery, and to examine acoustic and listener
factors potentially associated with this playback–imagery eval-
uation gap.

2.4. Confirmatory analyses (Pred1–Pred3)
All hypothesis tests were two-sided, and we report 95% confi-
dence intervals.
Pred1 (playback vs. imagery).Using all analyzable trials,
we fit a trial-level mixed-effects model [8] with a fixed effect
ofcondition(playback vs. imagery), a fixed indicator for
crossoverparticipants (who experienced both conditions) vs.
repetition baselineparticipants (who experienced the same con-
dition twice), and a participant random intercept. The primary
test is the fixed effect ofcondition. For the remaining out-
comes (e.g. desirability), we control BH-FDR [14] across the
six endpoints.
Pred2 (order effects).Restricting to the counterbalanced
crossover participants, we compute a participant-level gap∆ y
for each outcomeyas mean(playback)−mean(imagery)
(means over the two sentence trials per condition).
We test thesequenceeffect (playback→imagery
vs. imagery→playback) by ordinary least squares with
heteroscedasticity-consistent 3 robust standard errors (HC3;
i.e., uncertainty estimates that remain valid even when the
residual variance differs across individuals) [15, 16].
The six non-primary trial-level outcomes are BH-FDR-
corrected (desirability, valence, self-likeness, familiarity, eeri-
ness, and strangeness; see Sec. 2.3).
Pred3 (trait predictors of the gap).Using the same∆ y, we
regress∆ y on each pre-task listener measurez(recording ex-
posure frequency, general self-voice liking / voice preference,
Rosenberg self-esteem, SPS-6 social anxiety, age, and gender;
see Sec. 2.3), while adjusting forsequence.
Continuous traits enter linearly; recording self-exposure is
additionally tested as binary (anyvs.none). For secondary out-
comes, we apply BH-FDR across all (trait×outcome) tests.
2.5. Robustness checks: block drift and imagery quality
Because crossover order effects can be confounded with generic
block-to-block drift [9], we (a) quantify within-condition
drift in the repetition-baseline participants as block2−block1
within each condition (playback–playback and imagery–
imagery), and (b) report a drift-adjusted sequence contrast
(imagery→playback minus playback→imagery) using a sim-
ple correction based on the repetition-baseline drift (See Fig. 2
for a schematic of the repetition-baseline drift estimate and the
drift-adjusted sequence contrast.). We also assess whether post-
block imagery vividness/difficulty covaries with∆as a condi-
tion check on the imagery manipulation; these associations are
interpreted descriptively (not as causal effects).

3.1. Confirmatory analyses
3.1.1. Pred1: Playback evokes more negative affective evalua-
tions than imagery
As shown in Fig. 3, playback produced more negative evalu-
ations than imagery on the main affective outcomes, whereas
effects on secondary outcomes were more mixed.
Discomfort.For the primary endpoint, playback produced re-
liably higher discomfort than imagery (β= 0.490, 95% CI
[0.385,0.595],p= 6.69×10 −20).
Across BH-FDR-corrected non-primary outcomes, play-
back was evaluated as less desirable (β=−0.518), more
eerie (β= 0.506), less familiar (β=−0.179), stranger
(β= 0.439), and more negative in valence (β=−0.320).
Self-likeness did not differ reliably between conditions.
Interpretation.Overall, the clearest playback–imagery differ-
ence was observed for affective evaluations, whereas identity-
related self-likeness showed a weaker or less consistent pattern.

∆in eeriness (β=−0.157,q= 0.044).

3.2. Robustness checks (descriptive)
3.2.1. Robustness to block-to-block drift
Overall drift was small (mostly within±0.33points). The
largest shifts were observed for discomfort (playback+0.125;
imagery−0.330) and eeriness (playback−0.300; imagery
−0.213), consistent with partial habituation.
3.2.2. Drift-adjusted order effects (simple correction)
As a robustness check, we computed a drift-adjusted gap for
playback–imagery / imagery–playback by correcting each par-
ticipant’s∆using the corresponding drift estimates. This atten-
uation made several order effects non-significant (e.g., discom-
fort: adjustedβ=−0.161,p= 0.250; valence:β= 0.007,
p= 0.957; familiarity:β= 0.058,p= 0.668; eeriness:
β= 0.198,p= 0.153).
After adjustment, desirability (adjustedβ= 0.338,p=
0.0088) and strangeness (adjustedβ=−0.513,p= 0.0012)
remained significant, suggesting that drift alone cannot explain
these effects5.

3.3. Exploratory: acoustic correlates and moderation
The following analyses are exploratory and are intended to sum-
marize candidate acoustic-feature patterns rather than to pro-
vide definitive mechanistic explanations.
3.3.1. RQ1: Acoustic correlates of the playback–imagery gap
In validation,2features replicated under Holm correction with
consistent effect direction. Higher19-th MFCC predicted a
larger discomfort gap (β= 0.288,p= 0.016), and24-th
wav2vec2 feat. predicted a larger eeriness gap (β= 0.248,
p= 0.029).
Interpretation.Despite large-scale screening, replicable main
effects of individual low-level descriptors were sparse, suggest-
ing limited explanatory power unless modeled via higher-level
structure or interactions.
3.3.2. RQ2: Trait-dependent modulation of acoustic effects
In validation,12interactions replicated under Holm correction
with consistent interaction direction, suggesting candidate trait-
dependent modulation.
Illustrative cases highlight the range of moderation effects.
V oice preference moderated RMS-energy effects on desirabil-
ity6 (e.g., RMS p90:β val = 0.278,p= 0.032). Social
anxiety (SPS) interacted with multiple embedding dimensions
for self-likeness (emb671 mean:β=−0.490,p <0.001;
emb184 mean:β= 0.534,p= 0.006). Self-exposure

Interpretation.Although only a small fraction of screened ef-
fects replicated (2/11,977for main effects;12/59,885for in-
teractions), the replicated interactions suggest that acoustic cor-
relates of self-voice impressions are not purely stimulus-driven
at the group level; stable listener traits may shape the weighting
of acoustic cues in both affective evaluation and identity-related
judgments.
4. Discussion
Summary.Across outcomes, playback incurs an affec-
tive/perceptual penalty relative to imagery, with context effects
partly attributable to drift but remaining robust for desirability
and strangeness, and with exploratory evidence compatible with
person-specific modulation via trait×acoustic interactions.
4.1. Playback evokes more negative affect than imagery.
Key finding.Playback evokes more negative affect than
imagery—most consistently for discomfort—whereas self-
likeness does not differ reliably (see Fig. 3).
Evidence and interpretation.Playback appears costly primar-
ily in affective/perceptual terms rather than in self-recognition:
ratings shift toward aversion while identity-related self-likeness
remains comparable. This dissociation is unlikely to reflect a
ceiling artifact, because self-likeness ratings were not concen-
trated at the scale maximum in the pooled data (overall mean
= 4.69on a 1–7 scale;8.8%at the maximum).
One possible account is increased prediction error relative
to everyday expectations shaped by multimodal self-voice expe-
rience (e.g., air- and body-conducted feedback) versus predomi-
nantly air-conducted playback. In exploratory models that addi-
tionally account for post-block imagery vividness/difficulty, the
playback–imagery discomfort difference remained qualitatively
similar.

4.2. Order-related differences in imagery vs. playback
Key finding.Crossover groups indicate order dependence in
the playback–imagery gap, but repetition baselines show that
modest block drift explains part of the apparent sequence ef-
fect; after drift adjustment, the most robust context dependence
remains for desirability and strangeness (see Sec. 3.1.2).
Evidence and interpretation.Imagery-first ordering reduced
several negative-affect discrepancies (Fig. 4), yet blocked ad-
ministration conflates sequential carryover with time-dependent
drift. Repetition baselines reveal modest within-condition drift,
and a simple drift adjustment attenuates order effects for dis-
comfort and related ratings while preserving effects for desir-
ability and strangeness (see Sec. 3.2.2).
4.3. Listener-dependent acoustic weighting in self-voice
evaluation
Key finding.Self-voice evaluation may be characterized as a
dynamic, person-specific inference process shaped by state (re-
cent context and imagery strength) and trait (stable sensitivi-
ties): playback adds an affective/perceptual penalty (especially
discomfort) without proportionally degrading self-likeness, and
sequential effects reflect both drift and context dependence that
remains most robust for desirability and strangeness.
Modeling implications.A possible computational account is
that listeners combine a listener-specific prior over expected
self-voice with sensory evidence from playback, such that play-
back may increase affective prediction error when evidence
deviates from this prior. Short-term exposure can shift ex-
pectations (drift/habituation), whereas stable traits may mod-
ulate prior precision and cue weights, consistent with trait
main effects on the discrepancy (see Sec. 3.1.3) and replicated
trait×acoustic interactions (see Sec. 3.3.2).
4.4. Limitations and future work
This online study could not directly verify whether participants
performed the imagery task as intended, although post-block
vividness/difficulty ratings provided partial checks. Generaliz-
ability is limited by the Japanese-speaking, self-selected online
sample and by the limited demographic information reported
beyond age and gender. Future work should use more direct ma-
nipulation checks, fully controlled sentence materials, and alter-
native baselines such as neutral synthetic voices or preference-
matched voices.
5. Conclusion
We quantified the playback–imagery evaluation gap in a large
within-participant study. Playback yielded more negative af-
fective evaluations than imagery, especially higher discomfort,
while self-likeness remained comparable. Order effects were
partly explained by block drift, and exploratory trait×acoustic
interactions suggested possible listener-specific cue weighting.
These findings motivate more controlled studies of self-voice
aversion and listener-adaptive self-voice applications.
