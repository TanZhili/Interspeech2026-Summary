Abstract
Evaluation of speech systems for Indian languages remains
severely fragmented. Models are benchmarked on disjoint
datasets with inconsistent preprocessing and metrics, making
reproducibility and fair comparison nearly impossible. We ad-
dress this with Spashta Audio-Bench, a modular, plug-and-
play framework analogous to HuggingFace Evaluate, where
any model checkpoint or dataset registers through a standard-
ized interface without modifying the core pipeline. Evaluat-
ing ten open-source ASR and TTS models across seven corpora
spanning 22 Indian languages and Indian-accented English, it
reveals what single-dataset, single-metric benchmarking con-
ceals: parameter scaling does not guarantee cross-language ro-
bustness, naturalness and intelligibility diverge in TTS, and do-
main sensitivity persists across all ASR architectures. Cru-
cially, it introduces TTS-ASR degradation as an objective, re-
producible intelligibility measure, jointly evaluating naturalness
and intelligibility where prior benchmarks assess at most one.
We release it as reusable infrastructure to promote standardized,
multi-metric speech evaluation for Indian languages.
Index Terms: speech recognition, multilingual, ASR, TTS, In-
dian languages
1. Introduction
We presentSpashta Audio-Bench 1, a modular, plug-and-
play evaluation framework for Automatic Speech Recognition
(ASR) and Text-to-Speech (TTS) systems across Indian lan-
guages. Like HuggingFace Evaluate [1], any model check-
point and dataset can be integrated through a standardized inter-
face without modifying the core pipeline, replacing fragmented,
single-use evaluation scripts with a reusable infrastructure that
enforces uniform preprocessing, inference, and multi-metric
scoring across 23 languages (22 + Indian-accented English).
India presents a uniquely challenging environment for
speech technology [2]. Its languages span Indo-Aryan and Dra-
vidian families with substantial variation in phonology, mor-
phology, and script [3, 4]. Dravidian languages such as Malay-
alam and Tamil are heavily agglutinative; non-phonemic or-
thographic mappings in Bengali and consonant conjuncts in
Marathi pose additional difficulties for acoustic and language
modeling. While large-scale pretrained systems, such as Whis-
per [5], MMS [6], IndicConformer, have advanced low-resource
speech recognition, their proliferation has not been matched by
rigorous evaluation standardization. Models are assessed across
disjoint datasets with inconsistent preprocessing and metrics,
hampering reproducibility and fair comparison.

1.1. Related Work
Indian language ASR evaluation has progressively scaled from
single-language corpora to multilingual benchmarks. Indic-
SUPERB [7] established performance standards across 12 lan-
guages using the 1,684-hour Kathbath corpus; Vistaar [2] ex-
tended this with diverse training regimes; and LAHAJA [8] ad-
dressed multi-accent robustness for Hindi; IndicFake [9] estab-
lished for Indic deepfake detection. Globally, SUPERB [10]
and ESB [11] have demonstrated the value of standardized,
multi-task evaluation infrastructure. However, all of these
are fixed, single-use benchmarks rather than extensible frame-
works. In the generative domain, TTS evaluation for Indian
languages [12, 13] has relied predominantly on isolated sub-
jective scoring under controlled conditions, an approach that is
difficult to reproduce and does not capture intelligibility failures
that naturalness metrics obscure.
Spashta Audio-Bench bridges these gaps with three key ad-
vances over prior work: joint ASR and TTS evaluation, ob-
jective TTS intelligibility measurement via TTS–ASR degrada-
tion, and a modular architecture that lets any model or dataset
be plugged in without rebuilding evaluation infrastructure. Prior
benchmarks address at most one of these; Spashta Audio-Bench
addresses all three within a single, reproducible framework.

1.2. Research Contributions
•A modular, plug-and-play evaluation framework.Any
ASR or TTS model checkpoint and any speech dataset can
be registered and evaluated in a single pass via a standard-
ized interface, with all code publicly available.
•A unified pipeline for Indian languages.Spashta Audio-
Bench aggregates diverse public datasets (read, conver-
sational, and crowd-sourced) with uniform preprocessing
(audio resampling, transcript normalization), ensuring per-
formance differences reflect model capability rather than
pipeline artifacts.
•Comprehensive benchmarking of open-source ASR and
TTS models.We provide an extensive comparative evalua-
tion across Indian languages, surfacing performance dispar-
ities across language families, domains, and resource levels
that fragmented benchmarking conceals.
•A joint intelligibility–naturalness evaluation paradigm
for TTS.TTS–ASR degradation is introduced as a repro-
ducible, objective intelligibility measure alongside predicted
MOS, FAD, and P.808, exposing trade-offs that single-metric
reporting systematically conceals.

All datasets and model checkpoints used in Spashta Audio-
Bench are open-source and employed strictly for evaluation.

2.3. Evaluation Metrics
ASR Metrics.Performance is measured using Word Error Rate
(WER) and Character Error Rate (CER), both computed as edit
distance(S+D+I)/Nover words and characters respectively.
Prior to evaluation, text is lowercased and punctuation is re-
moved. CER is particularly informative for morphologically
rich and agglutinative Indian languages, where a single erro-
neous character can inflate word-level penalties.
TTS Metrics.TTS evaluation combines objective intelligibility
and predicted perceptual quality:
•TTS–ASR Degradation:WER and CER, computed on syn-
thesized audio. IndicConformer is employed as an oracle;
errors are computed with respect to the target text. Indic-
Conformer is chosen because it attains the lowest average
WER across the evaluation benchmarks, providing the most
reliable estimate of intelligibility and content preservation.
•Fr ´echet Audio Distance (FAD) [20]:Distributional similar-
ity between real and synthetic audio embeddings (VGGish
encoder). Lower FAD indicates higher acoustic realism.
•Predicted MOS [21, 22]:Naturalness estimated on a 1–5
scale via neural predictors (DNSMOS and P.808 3), which
decompose quality into signal clarity, background noise sup-
pression, and overall impression. These are model-predicted
estimates, not human judgments, and interpreted accordingly.
3. Results
Results span all evaluated datasets, languages, and models, re-
flecting performance variability across architectures, resource
levels, and acoustic domains.
3.1. Automatic Speech Recognition Results
ASR performance varies substantially across datasets and lan-
guages (Table 3a). IndicConformer generally achieves com-
petitive WER and CER on high-resource languages such as
Hindi, Marathi, and Urdu, with gains most pronounced on cu-
rated, studio-quality data where its hybrid CTC and RNNT ob-
jective provides stable decoding. Notably, however, AudioX-S
outperforms IndicConformer on IndicTTS (26.74% vs. 31.58%
WER), and AudioX-N is strongest on RASA (20.90% WER),
illustrating that no single architecture dominates uniformly
across all conditions.
Wav2Vec 2.0-based models perform well on datasets
closely aligned with their fine-tuning domain, but degrade
sharply under domain shift: on crowd-sourced and hetero-
geneous corpora, Vak-W2V WER exceeds 63% despite its
dedicated Wav2Vec2 pretraining. AudioX (North and South)
demonstrates competitive robustness on conversational and
spontaneous speech, particularly for English and selected re-
gional languages, but does not consistently surpass smaller
Conformer architectures on read-speech benchmarks or mor-
phologically complex languages. Across all models, low-
resource languages like Assamese, Dogri, Maithili, and Santali

3.2. Cross-Dataset and Cross-Language Variability
Cross-dataset variability is pronounced: for the same language,
a model’s WER can vary by more than 30 percentage points
depending on recording conditions, speaker diversity, and tran-
scription conventions. For example, IndicConformer’s over-
all WER spans from below 20% on studio read speech (SLR)
to above 35% on spontaneous recordings (RASA), highlight-
ing the inadequacy of single-dataset evaluation. CER trends
largely mirror WER but reveal additional nuance for aggluti-
native scripts, where a single character error inflates word-level
penalties disproportionately, making CER an essential comple-
mentary metric for Indian language evaluation.
3.3. Text-to-Speech Results
TTS evaluation reveals a clear divergence between predicted
perceptual quality and objective intelligibility (Table 3b). Mod-
els with the highest predicted MOS (Parler: 4.13–4.15) do not
achieve the lowest TTS→ASR WER; conversely, MMS-TTS,
which achieves the best intelligibility (WER: 36.14%, CER:
9.60%), produces comparatively lower pMOS and OVR scores.
This divergence is attributable in part to the limited calibration
of DNSMOS and P.808 neural estimators for Indian language
phonology: a model may score well on signal-level quality pre-
dictors while failing to articulate complex phonotactics.
Veena achieves the lowest FAD on IndicTTS (3.52), indi-
cating the closest acoustic similarity to natural speech, yet its
TTS→ASR WER is the highest (53.79%). Kokoro and Bark
perform adequately on English (SV) but degrade substantially
on Indian language sets Kokoro’s FAD rises to 23.86 on IV-
R and its TTS→ASR WER reaches 50.05% reflecting severely
limited phonetic generalisation beyond their training distribu-
tion. These results collectively demonstrate why single-metric
TTS evaluation is insufficient: no model simultaneously domi-
nates across FAD, pMOS, and intelligibility.
3.4. Low-Resource Language Behavior
Low-resource and morphologically complex languages exhibit
the highest performance variance across both tasks (Table 4).
Low-resource Indo-Aryan languages such as Maithili (58.29%
WER) and Dogri (56.71% WER) show error rates more than
double those of Hindi (23.76% WER), despite belonging to the
same language family, a gap attributable primarily to training
data volume rather than structural complexity. An outlier is
Urdu, which achieves the lowest WER across all 23 languages
(9.38%). This is likely due to substantial lexical and phono-
logical overlap with Hindi, for which training data is abun-
dant; models effectively transfer across the two languages when
Perso-Arabic script is normalised consistently.
Among Dravidian languages, Malayalam (51.47% WER),
Kannada (47.70% WER), and Tamil (45.65% WER) all exhibit
high error rates consistent with their heavily agglutinative mor-
phology, which generates a large number of compound surface
forms and inflates word-level penalties. The Dravidian fam-
ily as a whole remains among the most challenging for current
models, with no language falling below 45% WER. By con-
trast, Sindhi (20.69% WER) achieves a markedly lower error
rate relative to other low-resource languages, likely due to lex-
ical overlap with neighbouring Indo-Aryan varieties and more

3.5. Discussion
Our multi-metric evaluation yields four key insights that mo-
tivate the continued adoption of standardised, multi-metric
frameworks like Spashta Audio-Bench.
•The illusion of parameter scaling.Increasing model size
does not guarantee proportional gains across all languages.
Performance is bottlenecked by pretraining data distribution:
AudioX (∼2B parameters) does not consistently outperform
the substantially smaller IndicConformer (600M) across lan-
guages and domains, and Vakyansh-C (95–120M) outper-
forms AudioX on certain read-speech benchmarks where its
fine-tuning domain is well matched. Long tail languages re-
main vulnerable regardless of parameter count, motivating
phonetically balanced data curation over sheer scale.
•Domain and acoustic fragility.ASR models exhibit acute
domain sensitivity: systems achieving strong results on
studio-quality read speech (SLR) degrade significantly on
spontaneous or crowd-sourced recordings (RASA, NIR).
This variability, quantified by Audio-Bench’s cross-dataset
evaluation, is invisible in single-dataset benchmarking.
•The intelligibility–naturalness trade-off.TTS systems
optimised for perceptual and acoustic objectives such as
Kokoro (FAD: 21.19 on I-TTS) and Veena (TTS→ASR
WER: 53.79%) frequently sacrifice linguistic fidelity in In-
dian languages. This trade-off is systematically concealed
by single-metric evaluation, motivating Audio-Bench’s dual-
axis (intelligibility + naturalness) TTS paradigm.
•Architectural inductive biases matter.Hybrid CTC and
RNNT objectives, as in IndicConformer, provide better de-
coding stability for morphologically rich and agglutina-
tive languages than unconstrained encoder–decoder atten-
tion. This suggests that architectural choices tailored to In-
dian language structure yield returns that data scaling alone
cannot replicate.
4. Conclusion
We introduced Spashta Audio-Bench, a modular, plug-and-play
framework for benchmarking any ASR or TTS checkpoint and
any dataset through a standardized interface across 22 Indian
languages and Indian accented English. Its multi-metric de-
sign surfaces what single-metric evaluation hides. The gains
promised by scale and the qualities promised by perceptual met-
rics both fail to reach underrepresented languages and uncon-
strained acoustic conditions. A 2B-parameter model need not
beat a 600M one. These are structural properties of the current
model landscape, not artifacts of any one benchmark, and only
unified evaluation makes them visible. Because the framework
is extensible, every new checkpoint or corpus a team registers
extends this picture instead of starting an incomparable one. We
release it so that rigorous, multi-metric evaluation becomes the
default for low-resource speech, not the exception.
