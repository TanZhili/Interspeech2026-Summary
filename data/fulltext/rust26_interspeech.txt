Abstract
The distinctive properties of child-directed (CDS) and contin-
gent caregiver speech, compared to child-surrounding speech,
are well documented, yet their underlying mechanisms re-
main unclear. We investigate how prosodic patterns in con-
tingent CDS emerge from caregiver–child interaction, focus-
ing on pitch-contour similarity. In longitudinal, naturalistic
language acquisition corpora of English, Japanese, and Rus-
sian, we quantify acoustic similarity via Dynamic Time Warp-
ing and model its relation to temporal proximity between turns
and child age. Bayesian mixed-effects analyses reveal greater
pitch-contour similarity for temporally adjacent utterances and
decreasing similarity with increasing child age, consistent with
social accommodation effects. Our results suggest that key
acoustic properties of CDS may partly arise from general in-
teractional processes between caregivers and children such as
accommodation but can be modulated by culture and language
specific norms and grammar.

1. Introduction
Adult speakers adapt their communicative behavior in response
to their interlocutors, a phenomenon described in terms such as
alignment [1], accommodation [2], and mimicry [3]. In spoken
interaction, such adaptive processes have been shown to affect
both segmental and suprasegmental aspects of speech [4], in-
cluding pausing behavior and speech rate [5], fundamental fre-
quency [6, 7], vowel formants [8], as well as lexical choice
and syntactic structure [9, 10, 11, 1, 12, 13]. These linguistic
adaptations are part of a broader pattern of behavioral coupling
observed beyond speech, encompassing bodily movement and
posture [14], facial gestures [15], and gaze [16]. The mecha-
nisms that give rise to this could be, on the one hand, priming
[1, 17] or accommodation [2, 18]. Priming accounts assume
that recently processed linguistic material directly shapes sub-
sequent production [1, 17]. Accommodation accounts, on the
other hand, emphasize socially motivated convergence or di-
vergence to regulate social distance, signal affiliation, enhance
comprehensibility, or reduce interpersonal asymmetries [2, 18].
Comparable forms of adaptive behavior have also been docu-
mented in animal communication, where vocal accommodation
is linked to social bonding, group cohesion, and coordination
[19, 20, 21].
A specific form of adaptation is child-directed speech,
which is well documented to facilitate language acquisition
compared to adult-directed speech. It exhibits less complex

syntax [22, 23, 24], greater repetition [25, 26], clearer articu-
lation [27, 28], and higher and more variable pitch [29, 30], a
robust and perceptually salient feature linked to attentional en-

Recent research has shown that especially contingent CDS,
i.e., adult utterances that immediately follow a child utter-
ance, exhibit systematic adaptation, resulting in facilitated input
structures [31, 32, 33, 34, 35] with fewer unique word types,
shorter mean length of utterance, and a higher proportion of
one-word utterances [31, 36, 37]. Contingent interaction has
further been linked to improved phoneme production and dis-
crimination [38, 39], syntactic learning [40], and vocabulary
growth [34, 41, 42, 43]. The amount of contingent interaction is
also a stronger predictor of language outcomes than the quantity
of overheard speech alone [44, 45, 46].
However, the mechanisms underlying this phenomenon re-
main poorly understood. Here, we propose that some defin-
ing properties of child-directed speech and specifically contin-
gent child-directed speech arise from underlying general inter-
actional principles of alignment. We call this theChild-directed
Speech as Adaptation Hypothesis. To test whether we find evi-
dence for alignment we study the similarity ofF0 pitch contours
in caregiver-child utterances within conversations.
We assume that the observed alignment arises from either
priming or accommodation. To disentangle these accounts, we
distinguish alignment operating on short between turn versus
developmental timescales. Priming predicts rapid, local conver-
gence between adjacent turns, such that pitch-contour similar-
ity within a conversation should increase as temporal proximity
between child and caregiver utterances decreases (smaller∆t
in Figure 1) [1, 17]. Under a priming account, however, pitch-
contour similarity should not vary systematically with child age.
Accommodation, in contrast, predicts the same local conver-
gence between adjacent turns but additionally expects system-
atic modulation across development. Specifically, accommo-
dation predicts decreasing pitch-contour similarity in contin-
gent caregiver–child utterances as children grow older [2, 18].
In short, we contrast two opposing hypotheses: (1) short-term
alignmentwithoutchange of similarity over the age of the
child, consistent withpriming, and (2) short-term alignment
withchange of similarity over the age of the child, consistent
withaccommodation.
We quantify pitch contour similarity using dynamic time
warping (DTW), a length-robust method for measuring similar-
ity between time series (here,F 0 measures over time indicat-
ing pitch contours) [47]. To statistically test our hypotheses,F 0
contour similarity is evaluated as a function of temporal proxim-
ity between utterances and child age, i.e., we predictF0 contour
similarity by temporal proximity (∆tin Figure 1) and child age
in language-wise Bayesian multilevel linear regression models.

Here, we analyze longitudinal, naturalistic caregiver-child

languages: English, Japanese, and Russian all drawn from the
ACQDIV database [48]. This database is designed to maximize
typological diversity across languages.
By adopting a naturalistic cross-linguistic and interaction-

derstanding of children’s every-day language learning experi-
ences. Our findings have implications for theories of language
acquisition that emphasize interaction and adaptation.
2. Data & Methods
2.1. Choice of Languages & Corpora
We analyze English, Japanese and Russian from the ACQDIV
database [48], a database of longitudinal language acquisition
corpora representing typologically diverse languages recorded
in highly naturalistic settings. We selected corpora in which in-
teractions occur almost exclusively between the target child and
their primary caregiver (all female), thereby ensuring that adult
speech in the dataset is predominantly child-directed. This se-
lection criterion was adopted to minimize potential confounding
factors inherent in naturalistic recording environments, such as
the presence of additional children or adults. Such factors can
obscure the intended addressee of the utterances and thereby
complicate the interpretation of alignment effects. An overview
of the corpora’s properties is provided in Table 1.

2.2. Pitch Contour Similarities
To investigate interactional alignment effects using pitch con-
tour similarity between caregivers and children, we extracted
caregiver and child utterances from the annotated corpora.
These corpora include transcribed utterances that are diarized
(i.e., annotated for speaker identity), and time-aligned to the
video recordings with explicit onset and offset timestamps for
each utterance. They are further enriched with metadata, in-
cluding the child’s age and gender. This structure enables us
to identify child-produced utterances and determine the tempo-
rally closest caregiver utterances within a 2s window. We con-
sider this a conservative estimate, given that the average inter-
turn gap between utterances is approximately 250ms [49]. By
tracking these interactions across the developmental span rep-
resented in each corpus, we assess how caregiver speech sys-
tematically adapts in response to the child’s linguistic behavior
over time. An overview of the procedure is provided in Figure
1.
To study short-term alignment, we extracted non- overlap-
ping caregiver utterances (to avoid overlapping pitch estima-
tion) that followed each child utterance. To extractfollowing
caregiver utterances, a 2-second time window was defined start-
ing at the offset of the child utterance. All adult utterances be-
ginning within this window were considered following utter-
ances and included in the analysis (Figure 1).
To quantify the temporal relation between adult and child
utterances, we calculated the time difference∆tin millisec-
onds.∆twas defined as the onset time of the caregiver utter-
ance minus the offset time of the child utterance.
We quantify pitch contour similarity based on the shape of
the fundamental frequency (F 0) trajectory. To compare pitch
contours, we use Dynamic Time Warping (DTW) [50], an al-
gorithm designed to align time-series vectors of potentially un-
equal length. In our case, these vectors represent sequences
of fundamental frequency (F0) values extracted from child and
adult utterances with Python’sparselmouthpackage [51].
Because utterances vary in duration, DTW is particularly well-
suited for this task: it identifies the optimal alignment between
two contours by non-linearly stretching or compressing the time
axis. This yields a robust, time-invariant measure of pitch con-
tour similarity [47].
To obtain aF 0 pitch-contour similarity score for each
adult–child utterance pair (see Figure 1), we follow standard
recommendations of pitch contour extraction [52]. First, we
extract fundamental frequencyF 0 values by autocorrelation us-
ingPraatvia Python’sparselmouthpackage [51]. A pitch
floor and ceiling was set to help extractF0 frequencies (for chil-
dren: floor at 250Hz, ceiling at 500Hz, for adults (all female):
floor at 150Hz, ceiling at 500Hz). Each utterance is represented
as a time series ofF 0 values. Outliers (octave jumps) were ex-
cluded and voiceless utterance parts were linearly extrapolated.
TheF 0 estimates were then smoothed by Savitzky-Golay filter-
ing [53]. To isolate contour shape from speaker-specific pitch
height, each utterance was then mean-centered, so that similar-
ity reflects relative pitch movements rather than absoluteF0 val-
ues. We then compute pairwise pitch contour similarity using
DTW via thedtw-python[1.3.1]library [54] to calculate
the normalized distance (with respect to path length) between
utterances.

4. Discussion
We examined the hypothesis that pitch-related properties of
CDS partly emerge from interactional dynamics between care-
givers and children. To this end, we quantified pitch-contour

similarity between caregiver–child utterances using DTW-
based distance measures as a function of temporal proximity
and child age. We asked whether the underlying process is
priming or rather social accommodation. We find systematic
evidence for short- and long-term caregiver-child pitch align-
ment in English and Russian, alongside variation in Japanese,
supporting the view that pitch-related properties can emerge
from caregiver–child interactional dynamics and more specif-
ically accommodation.
We tested two competing hypotheses. Evidence for short-
term alignment in the absence of age-related modulation would
support a priming account, whereby recently perceived speech
shapes immediately subsequent production [1, 11]. In contrast,
evidence for both short-term alignment and systematic change
in similarity across child age would support an accommodation
account, with caregivers adapting their speech more strongly

to younger than to older children, consistent with socially and
developmentally motivated alignment [2, 57, 58].
The observed effects of∆tonly aligns with our prediction
in Russian: caregiver and child utterances tend to exhibit greater
pitch-contour similarity when they occur closer in time. In other
words, the shorter the delay between the child’s utterance and
the caregiver’s response, the more closely the caregiver’s pitch
contour resembles that of the preceding child utterance.
Interestingly, we do not observe a short-term alignment ef-
fect in English and find an inverse pattern in Japanese. One
possible explanation is that the temporal proximity effect is
modulated by culturally shaped interactional norms in care-
giver–child communication. Under this account, English care-
givers may show little tendency to imitate pitch contours, while
Japanese caregivers may even diverge from child-produced con-
tours as response latency decreases. This interpretation is par-
tially consistent with cross-linguistic evidence showing sub-
stantial variability in the prevalence and acoustic characteristics
of child-directed speech [59, 60]. Future work should systemat-
ically compare languages while explicitly incorporating cultural
variables concerning/ caregiver–child interaction.
A complementary explanation is methodological: child
pitch contours may be locally accommodated but embedded
within longer caregiver utterances. In such cases, a child-
produced contour could be partially reproduced and integrated
into a larger prosodic structure, which would dilute contour-
level similarity when assessed over the full utterance. Our
DTW-based measure, operating on utterance contours, may un-
derestimate localized alignment or imitation effects. Future
analyses targeting sub-utterance units or alignment at finer tem-
poral scales may help disentangle these possibilities.
Most importantly, we find credible effects for the increased
dissimilarity of pitch contours across child age in all three lan-
guages. While effects on short-term alignment are inconsis-
tent and possibly depend on cultural contexts, we still inter-
pret this as caregiver accommodation [2]. This is due to the
fact that priming accounts cannot explain the effect for child
age, whereby caregivers adjust their speech more for younger
children. Rather than reflecting sensitivity to children’s cogni-
tive–linguistic limitations (as argued for structural simplifica-
tion in contingent caregiver speech [31, 32, 33, 34, 35]), ad-
justment to pitch-contour similarity across child-age is more
consistent with socially motivated accommodation: with very
young children, greater prosodic convergence may serve affilia-
tive and affective functions, such as signaling emotional close-
ness, reducing perceived communicative distance, and making
the caregiver’s speech more approachable and less intimidat-
ing [18, 61]. As children mature the need for such strong con-
vergence may diminish, yielding the observed decline in pitch-
contour similarity over time.
Combined, these findings suggest that characteristic
prosodic properties of CDS - particularly in contingent care-
giver speech - may partly emerge from general interactional
alignment mechanisms. However, the combination of incon-
sistent short-term pitch similarity across cultures and consistent
age-related decline in alignment points to accommodation of
caregiver behavior towards the child that is possibly mitigated
by cultural attitudes to child rearing. Together, this pattern sup-
ports the view that defining features of CDS can arise due to
accommodation but but are subject to cultural variation.
A limitation of our study is that we focus exclusively on
pitch contours and on a limited set of languages and cultural
contexts. Other acoustic (e.g., VOT, speech rate) and struc-
tural variables (e.g., utterance length, lexical repetition) may
also play an important role in interactional speech adjustments.
In addition, culture-specific pragmatic norms and grammar may
modulate or override such effects and warrant more fine-grained
investigation.
5. Conclusion
We provide evidence that characteristic prosodic properties of
CDS, particularly in contingent caregiver speech, can emerge
from general interactional alignment mechanisms. Caregivers’
pitch-contour similarity decreases with child age across lan-
guages, supporting an accommodation account in which care-
givers adapt their prosodic behavior more strongly for younger
children. Cross-linguistic variation might indicate that such in-
teractional processes are modulated by culture-specific attitudes
towards child rearing. Together, these findings suggest that
defining features of CDS may arise dynamically caregiver–child
interaction operating over developmental timescales.
