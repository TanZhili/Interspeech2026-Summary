Abstract
Ultrasound tongue imaging is a promising modality
for Silent Speech Interfaces, yet a core assumption in
ultrasound-to-acoustic mapping remains untested, namely
that the tongue is the primary source of acoustic information.
We test this hypothesis by proposing an automatic tongue
region extraction method for controlled input manipulation on
a 2D-CNN baseline, and a dual encoder with cross-attention
gating for mapping ultrasound frames to 80-dimensional
mel-spectrograms. Based on our results, surprisingly, removing
the tongue region produced no statistically significant MSE
degradation, suggesting the 2D-CNN may exploit broader
image cues rather than tongue-specific features. The dual
encoder similarly yields no significant gain over the baseline
across MSE, MCD, PESQ, and MOSnet. However, Grad-CAM
maps reveal that the dual encoder focuses activations on the
tongue region, unlike the baseline, representing a meaningful
step towards more interpretable ultrasound-to-acoustic
mapping.

1. Introduction
Speech is a fundamental mode of human communication,
produced through the coordinated movement of several
articulators like the tongue, the lips and the jaw. In certain
scenarios, however, producing audible speech is not possible
(due to speech disorders or laryngectomy) or desired (e.g.
in noise-sensitive environments). Silent Speech Interfaces
(SSI) have been proposed as an alternative communication
method, aiming to recognize or reconstruct speech directly
from articulatory movement signals, without requiring the
production of audible speech [1, 2]. Various sensing modalities
have been explored for capturing these movements, including
surface electromyography (sEMG, [3, 4]), magnetic resonance
imaging (MRI, [5, 6]), lip video [7, 8] and ultrasound tongue
imaging (UTI, [9, 10]). Among these, UTI stands out as a
particularly promising modality for SSI as it offers to capture
a high-resolution, real-time tongue shape and movement while
remaining non-invasive, portable, and relatively cost-effective.
UTI-based articulatory-to-acoustic mapping has been
widely studied in recent years. Early work by Hueber et al. [9]
showed that it is feasible to convert silent articulatory data
into acoustic features using statistical models. Later studies
moved towards deep learning, applying convolutional and
residual neural networks to improve mapping performance [11,
12, 10]. Questions of input representation have also been

Despite these advances, the quality of synthesized speech from
UTI-based systems still noticeably falls below natural speech
quality, and architectural improvements have yielded only
incremental gains over CNN baselines [14, 15]. Data scarcity
and speaker dependency have been identified as contributing
factors, yet these alone do not fully account for the persistent
performance ceiling observed across the literature.
This raises the question of whether the problem lies
not only in the architecture or in the amount of data, but
also in how the model interacts with the input itself. An
ultrasound frame contains not only the tongue outline, but also
surrounding tissue, acoustic shadows, and imaging artefacts, all
of which are fed into the model without distinction. It remains
unclear whether current architectures genuinely exploit tongue
dynamics during mapping, or whether they rely on broader and
less interpretable frame pixel statistics.
Accordingly, in this paper we investigate the spatial
contribution of the tongue region to ultrasound-to-acoustic
mapping. In our experiments, speaker-specific networks are
trained to map individual ultrasound frames to 80-dimensional
mel-spectrograms, and we test the importance of the tongue
region by two techniques. First, to test the reconstruction
performance without the tongue shape, we use controlled
input manipulation. Then, inspired by attention-based spatial
guidance applied in ultrasound tongue image segmentation
research [16, 17], we use a purpose-built dual encoder
architecture with cross-attention gating mechanism to explicitly
incorporate tongue region information during the mapping
process. Reconstruction quality is first evaluated using
Mean Squared Error (MSE) of the mel-spectrogram. For
a more complete perceptual assessment, the predicted
mel-spectrograms are converted to speech using the HiFi-GAN
neural vocoder [18], and the synthesized outputs are evaluated
in terms of Mel Cepstral Distortion (MCD), Perceptual
Evaluation of Speech Quality (PESQ), and MOSnet scores.
Additionally, Gradient-weighted Class Activation Mapping
(Grad-CAM) [19] visualisations are computed for both the
baseline and the proposed architectures to provide a qualitative
analysis of which spatial regions of the ultrasound frame the
networks attend to during the mapping process.

3. Methodology
3.1. Experimental setup
All experiments were conducted on a server equipped with
an AMD Ryzen 7 3700X CPU (2.80 GHz, 8 cores, 16
threads), an NVIDIA A100-PCIE GPU (40 GB VRAM),
and 125 GB of RAM. The system ran Ubuntu 20.04.4 LTS
(kernel 5.11.0-41-generic). Deep learning computations were
performed using TensorFlow 2.20.0 with CUDA 12.0 and with
NVIDIA driver 525.89.02.
We used the scanline data of the ultrasound, after being
resized to 64× 128 pixels using bicubic interpolation from its
original size of 64× 842. Prior to training, ultrasound frame
pixel values were normalized to the range [−1, 1]. To ensure
consistent evaluation throughout the experiments, 10 out of 24
shared read sentences (the recordings between 004 xaud and
013 xaud) were reserved for testing across all speakers. The
remaining dataset was partitioned into training and development
sets for each speaker in a 9:1 ratio. All models in this
work were trained for a maximum of 50 epochs with a batch
size of 128, optimized using stochastic gradient descent with
an initial learning rate of 0.1. A reduce-on-plateau learning
rate schedule and early stopping were both applied with a
patience of 3 epochs based on development set MSE. Statistical
significance of performance differences was assessed using the
Mann-Whitney U test [21] at a 95% confidence level.
3.2. Tongue region extraction
To analyze the spatial contribution of the tongue region to
UTI-based acoustic mapping, we first developed an automatic
tongue region extraction procedure with temporal stabilization.

A region-of-interest mask was first applied to suppress
anatomically irrelevant areas, specifically the uppermost region
of the frame (where airflow artifacts are present) and the
lower region (where the chin tissue is located). Adaptive
Gaussian thresholding was then used to enhance high-contrast
structures corresponding to the tongue surface. Connected
component analysis was performed on the binarized image,
and the largest non-background component was selected as the
tongue candidate region. Morphological closing and opening
operations with a 3× 3 structuring element were applied to
reduce speckle-induced artefacts and small discontinuities.
Due to the noisy structure of the frames, the extracted
tongue region can fluctuate across consecutive frames. To
handle this issue, temporal stabilization was introduced using
two complementary criteria. First, the Intersection-over-Union
(IoU, [22]) value was computed between the masks of the
consecutive frames. If the IoU fell below a predefined threshold
(0.3), the current mask was considered unstable. Second, we
computed the centroid of each mask and measured its Euclidean
displacement relative to the previous frame. If the centroid
shift exceeded a distance threshold (20 pixels), the mask was
similarly treated as unreliable. When either condition was
violated, the previous mask was retained without modification,
ensuring temporal continuity. All numerical parameters used in
the development of this method were determined empirically
based on visual inspection of the extracted masks across
speakers.
3.3. Baseline architecture
The baseline architecture for ultrasound-to-acoustic mapping
follows the standard 2D-CNN proposed in [14], which
maps individual ultrasound frames to the corresponding
80-dimensional mel-spectrogram vectors in a frame-by-frame
manner. The network consists of four convolutional layers with
progressively increasing numbers of filters of 30, 60, 90, and
120, each employing a 13× 13 kernel, followed by a dense
layer of 1000 neurons and a fully connected output layer.
3.4. Input manipulation experiment
Having obtained the tongue region for each ultrasound frame
across all utterances per speaker, we designed an input
manipulation experiment to determine its spatial contribution to
ultrasound-to-mel mapping. The baseline architecture was used
to compare mapping performance under two input conditions:
the original ultrasound frames and the corresponding no-tongue
frames. In the no-tongue condition, pixels belonging to the
detected tongue region were replaced with values randomly
sampled from the background pixel distribution of the
same frame, preserving surrounding texture while effectively
eliminating the tongue surface echo. Background sampling
was employed rather than simple zero-substitution, as the
latter would preserve the silhouette of the tongue region,

3.5. Proposed architecture
The input manipulation experiment demonstrated that a
standard 2D-CNN does not inherently leverage the tongue
region when performing ultrasound-to-acoustic mapping. This
raises the question of whether explicitly directing the network’s
attention towards the tongue region can improve the mapping
quality. To this end, we propose a dual encoder network with
cross-attention gating, designed to incorporate the extracted
tongue mask as an explicit spatial prior during feature learning.
The proposed model, shown in Figure 2, accepts the
full ultrasound frame ( 64 × 128) and the corresponding
binary tongue mask extracted using the automatic segmentation
method described in Section 3.2 as inputs. The architecture
consists of four progressive encoding stages, each containing a
full-image encoder block and a lightweight mask encoder block
processing the two streams in parallel. In each stage, the full
encoder block applies a 13× 13 convolutional kernel to capture
broad spatial context from the ultrasound frame, while the mask
encoder block applies a smaller 3× 3 kernel to the tongue mask
stream. The number of filters increases progressively across
stages as 30, 60, 90, and 120, following a standard feature
hierarchy (and mirroring the baseline 2D-CNN architecture).
At stages 2 and 4, spatial downsampling is applied via 2× 2
max pooling to both streams.
At each stage, a cross-attention gate combines the
full-image features and the mask features to produce a gated
representation. The gate is implemented as a two-layer 1× 1
convolutional network applied to the mask features, producing
a spatial attention map via sigmoid activation. This attention
map is applied to the full-image features through element-wise
multiplication, and the result is combined with the original
full-image features via a residual connection, ensuring that
while the gate directs the network’s attention towards the
tongue region, the surrounding contextual information remains
accessible.

Rather than flattening the final feature map, skip
connections are introduced by applying Global Average Pooling
(GAP) to the gated output at each stage. This way multi-scale
spatial information is preserved from all encoding stages,
yielding compact stage-level descriptors of dimensions 30, 60,
90, and 120 respectively. These are concatenated into a single
300-dimensional vector, which is passed to a fully connected
layer of 1000 units with Swish activation [23],L1 regularisation
with λ = 5 × 10−6, and a dropout rate of 0.2. The final
output layer is a linear dense layer producing an 80-dimensional
mel-spectrogram prediction, which is consistent with the
baseline architecture.
3.6. Speech synthesis
To provide a complete assessment of the proposed architecture,
speech was synthesized from the predicted mel-spectrograms
using the HiFi-GAN neural vocoder [18]. Specifically, we
used the first variant of the publicly available pre-trained model
trained on the multi-speaker VCTK dataset (VCTK V1)2,
selected for its higher synthesis quality among the available
pre-trained variants.

only exception was the MOSnet score for speaker 02fe ( p =

The consistently large gap between both models and the
vocoded original across all metrics and speakers confirms that
the overall mapping quality remains far from natural speech,
which mirrors the observations reported in the literature [14,
15]. These results indicate that explicitly directing the
network’s spatial attention towards the tongue region does not
translate into measurable improvements in synthesized speech
quality under the current experimental conditions.
Alongside the comparable objective performance, the
Grad-CAM visualisations in Figure 3 reveal a qualitatively
meaningful difference between the two architectures.
Activations were computed with respect to the last
convolutional layer of each model, providing a spatial map of
the regions most influential to the final prediction. The baseline
model distributes activation broadly across anatomically
ambiguous regions including background tissue and acoustic
shadows, making it difficult to attribute predictions to any
meaningful structure. In contrast, the proposed dual encoder
consistently concentrates activation within the tongue region,
demonstrating that the cross-attention mechanism successfully
steers spatial focus towards the articulatorily relevant area
of the frame. This concentration pattern was observed
consistently across speakers and randomly sampled utterances.
Despite yielding comparable objective scores, the proposed
model produces predictions through a more transparent
and anatomically grounded feature representation. This
further reinforces the conclusion from the input manipulation
experiment that comparable reconstruction quality can be
achieved regardless of where the network spatially attends,
while also representing a meaningful step towards more
interpretable ultrasound-to-acoustic mapping systems.

5. Conclusion and Future Work
In this work, we investigated the spatial contribution of
the tongue region to ultrasound-to-mel-spectrogram mapping
through controlled input manipulation and a purpose-built dual
encoder architecture with cross-attention gating. An input
manipulation experiment showed that removing tongue region
pixels did not result in statistically significant changes in
mapping performance for any of the four speakers studied.
Motivated by this, we proposed a dual encoder architecture
incorporating the extracted tongue mask as a spatial prior
through cross-attention gating, to examine whether directing
the network’s attention to the tongue region could yield
measurable improvements. The proposed architecture showed
no statistically significant improvements over the baseline
across any of the evaluated metrics. However, Grad-CAM
visualisations confirmed that the cross-attention mechanism
successfully concentrated spatial focus on the tongue region,
producing more anatomically interpretable activation maps.
These findings suggest that, under frame-by-frame 2D-CNN
mapping and the present training conditions, predictive
information is not critically dependent on explicit tongue-region
pixels. This does not preclude the importance of tongue
dynamics in temporally aware models, but indicates that
single-frame CNN architectures may rely on broader image
statistics.
As part of our future work, we plan to examine whether
tongue region information becomes more relevant in temporally
aware mapping frameworks. Since the experiments in this
work operated in a frame-by-frame manner, tongue dynamics
across consecutive frames were not modelled. Since the
trajectory and deformation of the tongue surface over time
encode articulatory gestures that single frames cannot capture,
which makes temporal modelling a promising next step.
