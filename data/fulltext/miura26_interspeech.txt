Abstract
Optimal speech rates are crucial for screen reader usability, yet
existing research relies on accuracy measures that conflate item
difficulty and individual ability. This study applies Bayesian
item response theory (IRT) to quantify both constructs across
speech rates (150–500 WPM) among Japanese visually im-
paired users (N=11). Using phonemically balanced ITA corpus
sentences, we estimated item difficulties and person abilities via
Bayesian cumulative probit and beta regression models. Despite
the small sample, weakly informative priors yielded stable esti-
mates (maximum ˆR = 1.004). Difficulty rose with faster rates,
alongside substantial individual differences (ability SD = 0.91–
1.10). Totally blind participants showed higher comprehension
than low-vision peers, and both visual impairment status and lis-
tening speed experience independently predicted it. This proof-
of-concept establishes the first IRT-based framework for TTS
evaluation, demonstrating large-scale feasibility.
Index Terms: speech rate, text-to-speech, screen reader, visual
impairment, item response theory, accessibility
1. Introduction
Over 285 million people worldwide have visual impairments,
including 39 million who are blind [1]. In Japan, roughly
300,000 individuals have severe visual impairment [2, 3].
Screen readers—assistive technologies that convert on-screen
text into synthetic speech—are essential for digital access [4,
5, 6]. However, the optimal speech rate configuration remains
poorly understood, particularly for non-English languages such
as Japanese, which differs fundamentally in phonological struc-
ture (mora- vs. syllable-based) and character systems.
Experienced users can comprehend remarkably fast speech:
Asakawa et al. [7] demonstrated comprehension at 1,400–1,500
morae/minute (400–500 WPM), far exceeding conversational
rates (∼150 WPM). Neuroimaging reveals that ultra-fast com-
prehension in early-blind individuals recruits visual cortical ar-
eas [8], suggesting cross-modal plasticity. However, substan-
tial individual variation exists [9, 10], necessitating personal-
ized configuration. Current screen readers offer only manual
adjustment without evidence-based guidance.
Existing evaluation methods have critical limitations. Sim-
ple accuracy measures conflate item difficulty and individual
ability: an 80% score could reflect high ability on hard items or
moderate ability on easy items—fundamentally different sce-
narios for personalization. Moreover, most research focuses on

Item Response Theory (IRT) addresses these limitations by
separately modeling item difficulty and person ability on a com-
mon latent scale [11, 12]. Although IRT has demonstrated ef-
fectiveness in speech assessment [13, 14], it has not yet been
applied to speech rate comprehension or TTS design. Criti-
cally, traditional IRT requires large samples (N ≥ 200), pos-
ing challenges for specialized populations. Bayesian IRT over-
comes this through regularizing priors that yield stable esti-
mates and explicit uncertainty quantification even with small
samples [15, 16].
This study applied Bayesian IRT to quantify speech rate
comprehension abilities among Japanese visually impaired
screen reader users (N=11). We address two research questions:
(1) How do item difficulties vary across speech rates (150–500
WPM), and how large are individual differences in comprehen-
sion abilities? (2) Do totally blind and low vision users differ
systematically in speech rate comprehension abilities?
2. Related Work
2.1. Screen Readers and Speech Rate Perception
Screen reader development has evolved substantially since the
1990s [17]. Contemporary systems, such as JAWS, NVDA, and
V oiceOver, offer structural navigation, customizable verbosity,
and TTS integration [5]; however, optimal speech rate configu-
ration remains challenging [3].
Research on speech-rate perception among individuals with
visual impairments has consistently demonstrated enhanced au-
ditory processing. Asakawa et al. [7] reported maximum listen-
ing speeds of 1,400–1,500 morae/minute (approximately 400–
500 WPM) among experienced Japanese screen-reader users—
substantially exceeding typical conversational speech (approx-
imately 150 WPM). Dietrich et al. [8] used fMRI to show that
ultra-fast speech comprehension in early-blind individuals re-
cruits visual cortical areas, suggesting neuroplastic reorganiza-
tion underlying enhanced auditory temporal processing.
Guerreiro and Gonc ¸alves [9] investigated the concurrent
presentation of two-voice speech to enhance information scan-
ning and found optimal comprehension at approximately 278
WPM (1.75× default rate) for visually impaired participants.
Bragg et al. [10] conducted a large-scale study (N = 990 ) ex-
amining listening rates across diverse populations, confirming
that individuals with visual impairments exhibit significantly
higher comfortable listening speeds than sighted individuals, al-
though substantial individual variations exist.

2.2. IRT and Bayesian IRT for Speech Assessment
IRT models item responses as functions of both item parameters
(difficulty and discrimination) and person abilities, enabling the
separation of these constructs on a common latent scale [11, 12].
Contrary to classical test theory, which treats total scores as
ability measures contaminated by error, IRT provides item-
level calibration and person-level ability estimation, facilitating
adaptive testing and cross-study comparability. IRT has been
successfully applied to speech assessments. Ng et al. [13] devel-
oped Cantonese speech recognition tests using the Rasch model,
demonstrating that psychometric item selection improved reli-
ability. Brackenbury et al. [14] applied two-parameter logistic
IRT to articulation screening, identifying phonemes with opti-
mal difficulty and discrimination. However, IRT has not been
applied to speech rate comprehension or TTS evaluation.
Traditional IRT requires large samples (N ≥ 200) for sta-
ble maximum likelihood estimation [11]. Bayesian IRT ad-
dresses this limitation through prior distributions that regularize
parameter estimates, providing stability even with small sam-
ple sizes [15]. Weakly informative priors (e.g., normal(0, 1) for
standard deviations) prevent overfitting while allowing the data
to dominate the inference when informative [16]. Crucially,
Bayesian methods quantify uncertainty via posterior distribu-
tions rather than point estimates, explicitly acknowledging the
limited precision in small-sample settings. Recent applications
have demonstrated the effectiveness of Bayesian IRT for spe-
cialized populations in which large samples are infeasible [15].
For non-specialists unfamiliar with IRT, we briefly explain
the core concept. IRT assumes that each person has a latent
abilityθ (e.g., comprehension ability), and each item has a dif-
ficulty parameterβ (reflecting how difficult the item is to com-
prehend), and a discrimination parameterα (reflecting how well
the item differentiates between high- and low-ability persons).
The probability of a correct response is modeled as
P (Xij = 1|θi,β j,α j) = F (αj(θi−βj)) (1)
where F represents a cumulative distribution function (CDF).
By separating person ability from item difficulty on a common
latent scale, IRT enables fair comparison across items and per-
sons, which is impossible with raw accuracy measures. For TTS
evaluation, this indicates that we can distinguish whether poor
performance arises because the speech is too fast or because in-
dividuals have lower comprehension ability—a distinction that
is critical for personalized design.
2.3. Japanese Speech Corpora for TTS Evaluation
We utilized the ITA corpus [18], which provides phonemi-
cally balanced Japanese sentences designed for TTS evaluation
while controlling for sentence length and lexical familiarity—
conditions that are ideal for IRT-based analysis requiring inter-
pretable item characteristics [19, 20].
3. Method
3.1. Participants
Eleven Japanese individuals with visual impairments partici-
pated in this study (seven males, four females; mean age 38.2
years, SD = 12.4, range 22–58). Six participants had total
blindness, and five had low vision. Three participants had con-
genital visual impairment, while eight acquired visual impair-
ment later in life (mean onset age 24.1 years for acquired cases).
All held disability certificates issued in Japan and reported reg-
ular screen reader use (mean experience 8.6 years, SD = 6.2).
Participant background characteristics were assessed using
a questionnaire. Mobility training experience ranged from 0 to
15 years (mean 6.3 years, SD = 5.1). Braille proficiency was
rated on a 5-point scale (1 = cannot read, 5 = highly proficient;
mean 2.8, SD = 1.6). Audio game experience ranged from 0
to 20 years (mean 4.2 years, SD = 6.1). PC usage frequency
varied from daily use (n = 6) to several times per week (n =
5). Participant self-reported screen reader speech rates ranged
from 200 to 600 characters/minute (mean 387 chars/min, SD
= 134), corresponding to approximately 150–500 WPM when
converted using standard Japanese character density estimates.
Ethical approval was obtained from the Institutional Review
Board at Tsukuba University of Technology. All participants
gave informed consent and received compensation.
3.2. Speech Stimuli
Stimuli comprised 42 phonemically balanced Japanese sen-
tences selected from the ITA corpus, a standardized speech cor-
pus designed for TTS evaluation. The ITA corpus provides
comprehensive phonological coverage while controlling syn-
tactic complexity and lexical frequency [18]. Sentences were
systematically sampled to ensure balanced distribution across
three length categories: short (8–18 characters, n = 12 ),
medium (21–30 characters, n = 18 ), and long (45–66 char-
acters,n = 12 ), based on the RECITATION324 subset, which
contains phonetically balanced reading-style sentences.
Each sentence was synthesized at five speech rates using the
macOS say command with the Kyoko voice (Japanese female
speaker): 150, 225, 300, 400, and 500 WPM. The synthesis
parameters were standardized (W A V format, 32-bit float, 44.1
kHz sampling rate). The resulting 210 audio files (42 sentences
× 5 rates) were randomized for presentation order.
3.3. Evaluation Measures
Participants provided three dependent measures for each audio
presentation:
1. Comprehension accuracy (0–100%): They repeated each
sentence immediately after hearing it. Responses were scored
at the mora level by calculating the percentage of correctly
reproduced morae. This objective measure assesses phono-
logical perception accuracy without subjective judgment.
2. Comprehensibility (1–7 Likert scale): Subjective rating of
how easy the sentence was to understand (1 = very difficult
to understand, 7 = very easy to understand).
3. Listenability (1–7 Likert scale): Subjective rating of how
easy the sentence was to hear/perceive auditorily (1 = very
difficult to hear, 7 = very easy to hear).

3.4. Procedure
The experiment was conducted individually using a web-based
interface accessible through the participants’ screen readers.
Audio files were presented through Google Forms with embed-
ded playback controls. Participants were allowed to play each
audio file once, with an optional second playback. The number
of playbacks was recorded for further analysis.
Each participant completed three repetitions of each
sentence-rate combination, yielding 630 trials per participant
(42 sentences× 5 rates× 3 repetitions) for a total dataset of
6,930 responses (11 participants × 630 trials). However, due
to incomplete data collection, the effective dataset comprised
2,310 responses. Sessions were self-paced, with participants
encouraged to take breaks as needed. The total participation
time averaged 3–4 h across multiple sessions.
3.5. Analysis
3.5.1. Comprehension Scoring Method
Mora-level accuracy was determined by comparing each re-
peated sentence to the original word-by-word. Errors were iden-
tified at the mora level, which is the basic phonological unit in
Japanese. A mora was considered correct if it matched exactly;
partial credit was not given. The percentage of correctly re-
produced morae was calculated for each sentence, with scores
ranging from 0–100%. This method has been validated in prior
Japanese speech perception research [19].
3.5.2. Bayesian Item Response Theory Models
Given the exploratory nature and small sample size (N = 11), we
employed Bayesian IRT methods, which provide stable param-
eter estimates through regularizing priors [15]. Three Bayesian
models were estimated corresponding to the three measures:
Bayesian Cumulative Probit Models for comprehensibil-
ity and listenability (1–7 scales): These models are Bayesian
analogues of the Graded Response Model [21], modeling or-
dered categorical responses as arising from underlying con-
tinuous latent abilities. The model included random effects
for person abilities and item difficulties, and fixed effects for
speech rate conditions. We specified weakly informative priors:
normal(0, 2) for threshold parameters, normal(0, 1) for random
effect standard deviations, and normal(0, 0.5) for fixed effects.
Bayesian Beta Regression for comprehension accuracy
(0–100%): Continuous percentage scores were transformed to
(0, 1) and modeled using beta regression, which naturally han-
dles bounded continuous data. The model structure paralleled
the ordinal models with random effects for persons and items.
We specified a gamma(2, 2) prior for the precision parameter.
All models were estimated using Hamiltonian Monte Carlo
sampling via the brms package [22] in R, with four chains of
4,000 iterations each (2,000 warmup). Convergence was as-
sessed using ˆR statistics (target: ˆR <1.01) and effective sam-
ple sizes. Person abilities (θ) were extracted as posterior means
from random effects, with 95% credible intervals quantifying
uncertainty. Posterior predictive checks assessed model fit by
comparing the observed data with the posterior predictive dis-

3.5.3. Statistical Comparisons and Covariate Analyses
To investigate ability differences by visual impairment status
(total blindness vs. low vision), we computed the posterior
mean abilities for each group and examined whether credible
intervals overlapped. We also examined the correlations among
the three ability measures (comprehensibility, listenability, and
comprehension accuracy) using posterior means.
We conducted Bayesian regression analyses to determine
if listening speed experience and visual impairment indepen-
dently predict comprehension. Participants’ self-reported lis-
tening speeds were standardized relative to their screen reader’s
default settings: PC-Talker at 5 and V oiceOver at 50. Four par-
ticipants who used screen readers secondarily retained default
settings (1.0×); seven primary users customized their speeds
(1.4×–3.0×, M=2.2×, SD=0.6×).
4. Results
4.1. Model Convergence and Overview
All Bayesian models converged successfully (maximum ˆR =
1.004, effective sample sizes > 1,000), and posterior predic-
tive checks indicated adequate fit [16]. We estimated difficulty
parameters for 70 items (14 sentences × 5 speeds) and person
abilities for 11 participants across three measures: comprehen-
sibility (1–7), listenability (1–7), and comprehension accuracy
(0–100%).
4.2. Speed Effects, Sentence Length, and Individual Differ-
ences
Person abilities varied substantially across all measures (com-
prehensibility: θ =−0.68 to 2.45, SD = 0.91; listenability:
θ =−0.89 to 2.81, SD = 1.10; comprehension: θ =−0.60
to 0.53, SD = 0.38). The mean credible interval width was
1.29, reflecting the parameter uncertainty inherent in small-
sample Bayesian estimation. The three measures showed high
intercorrelations (comprehensibility-listenability: r = 0 .91;
comprehensibility-comprehension: r = 0 .76; listenability-
comprehension:r = 0.69).
Item-level difficulty parameters showed a weak correlation
with speech rate (r = 0.16; Table 2). However, the Bayesian
fixed effects revealed distinct patterns (Figure 1). For speed
conditions measured relative to the 150 WPM baseline, com-
prehensibility showed a systematic decline at faster rates: 225
WPM showed no significant change ( β = −0.17, 95% CI:
[−0.49, 0.16]), while 300, 400, and 500 WPM showed substan-
tial declines (β =−1.23 to−2.00, with all credible intervals
excluding zero). Despite these systematic speed effects, person
ability variance (SD = 0.91–1.10) exceeded the speed-induced
variation, indicating that individual differences remained the
dominant factor.

4.3. Visual Impairment Status and Listening Speed Expe-
rience
Individuals with total blindness (TB) demonstrated consistently
higher abilities than those with low vision (LV) across all three
measures (Table 3). The participant with the highest ability es-
timates (comprehensibility θ = 2.45, listenability θ = 2.81)
was totally blind and used screen reader speech at the maximum
available speed, with high-speed check mode enabled.
To examine whether listening speed experience and vi-
sual impairment status independently predict comprehension,
we conducted a Bayesian regression analysis controlling for
participants’ relative listening speeds. Participants with total
blindness used substantially faster listening rates (M=2.33 ×,
SD=0.58) than those with low vision (M=1.42×, SD=0.50). Ta-

This finding reveals a novel dissociation: totally blind
participants showed both higher comprehension accuracy and
higher listenability ratings despite using faster rates, suggesting

5. Discussion
5.1. Implications and Practical Applications
This proof-of-concept study demonstrates that Bayesian IRT,
the first such application to TTS speech rate evaluation, can ef-
fectively quantify speech rate comprehension abilities even with
N = 11: weakly informative priors regularized estimates and
prevented overfitting, while posterior credible intervals explic-
itly acknowledged the limited precision—a transparency crucial
for small-sample exploratory studies [16].

threshold pattern with minimal decline at 225 WPM and sharp
drops above 300 WPM. This suggests a comprehension limit of
around 250–300 WPM for this experienced group. Second, in-
dividual differences are significant, indicating that the optimal
reading rate varies widely among users. The highest-ability par-
ticipant (totally blind, θ = 2.45) likely tolerated faster speeds
than most. Third, totally blind users may benefit from higher
default rates; their average abilities are 0.45–0.71θ units above
low-vision users. This advantage persists after accounting for
listening speed experience, implying that visual impairment af-
fects auditory processing beyond habituation. Additionally, ex-
tended exposure or training might further improve abilities.
These independent effects of visual impairment status and
listening speed experience have important implications for
screen reader design. Current one-size-fits-all defaults (typi-
cally 150–180 WPM) underutilize the capabilities of visually
impaired users, particularly those with total blindness. Person-
alized recommendations should account for both user charac-
teristics (visual impairment status) and experience level (self-
reported comfortable rates), moving beyond one-size-fits-all de-
faults toward evidence-based, individualized assistive technol-
ogy configuration.
