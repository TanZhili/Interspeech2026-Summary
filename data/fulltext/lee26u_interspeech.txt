LLM-as-Joiner: Decoupling Alignment from Language Modeling
in Label-synchronous ASR
Jaeyoung Lee1,2, Masato Mimura 1, Ryo Magoshi 2, Tatsuya Kawahara2
1 NTT, Inc., Japan
2 Kyoto University, Japan
jae.lee@ntt.com, masato.mimura@ntt.com
Abstract
We present LLM-as-Joiner, an LLM-based ASR architecture
that decouples speech-text alignment from language modeling.
A speech encoder first converts an input ofTacoustic frames
intoUtoken-level speech states, one per output token, follow-
ing the Aligner-Encoder framework. A pretrained LLM then
predicts the transcript from theseUstates, with speech injected
at a chosen LLM layer; only upper LLM layers are adapted
via LoRA. Compared to conventional decoder-only speech-as-
prefix decoding, the LLM operates on a shorter context (U
rather thanT+U), reducing memory and compute. On Lib-
riSpeech and multilingual Common V oice, the proposed LLM-
as-Joiner outperforms size-matched baselines, and joint train-
ing with a lightweight recognizer yields additional gains in
the lightweight head, suggesting implicit transfer of linguistic
knowledge from the LLM to the speech encoder.
Index Terms: Speech recognition, Large language models
(LLMs), Aligner-Encoders
1. Introduction
Modern end-to-end automatic speech recognition (ASR) is
commonly approached with RNN-Transducer (RNN-T) mod-
els [1] or attention-based sequence-to-sequence frameworks
[2, 3]. Progress in both lines has been driven by stronger
speech encoders: the Conformer architecture [4] captures both
local and long-range structure in speech, and large-scale self-
supervised pretraining [5, 6, 7] further improves acoustic repre-
sentations and also supports monotonic alignment from speech
to text. Sequence-to-sequence approaches have further been
popularized by Whisper-style systems [8], especially relevant
in the LLM era as their decoders are architecturally close to
language-modeling Transformers.
Decoder-only speech-as-prefix integration has become a
common way to leverage strong linguistic priors from LLMs
into ASR [9, 10, 11, 12]. While pretrained LLMs provide
powerful text modeling, they are not pretrained with speech-text
temporal alignment; consequently, decoder-only ASR must ac-
quire this alignment during speech fine-tuning, often with addi-
tional alignment aids (e.g., compressors [10], boundary tokens
[13], or forced alignment [12]). In ASR, alignment is mono-
tonic and has conventionally been well addressed by speech en-
coders; asking a full LLM to infer alignment over long acoustic
contexts could be inefficient. Moreover, speech-prefix decoding
expands the LLM context and can dominate memory cost.
Recent label-synchronous ASR explores obtaining token-
level structure without full-sequence decoder-side attention
over encoder frames. Continuous Integrate-and-Fire (CIF) [14]
offers an alternative monotonic alignment mechanism by pre-
dicting token boundaries and aggregating theT-frame sequence
intoUtoken-level embeddings via integrate-and-fire, after
which a decoder operates on theseUstates [14, 15]. Aligner-
Encoders [16] push this further by simply applying frame-wise
cross-entropy to encoder states at fixed label positions1,...,U
and decoding inO(U)time, moving alignment into the encoder.
Our method builds on Aligner-Encoders and leverages thisU-
length interface, particularly effective for LLM integration be-
cause it shortens the LLM context and removes the need for the
LLM to learn speech-text alignment from scratch.
We proposeLLM-as-Joiner: the speech encoder performs
monotonic alignment and producesUlabel-synchronous states,
while a pretrained LLM provides joiner-side language modeling
and modality fusion. The LLM is integrated as-is, with no blank
token and no added input/output embeddings, and its layers are
partitioned into lowerpredictorand upperjoinerroles, with
speech conditioning injected at the partition; only the joiner-
side layers are LoRA-tuned. Because the LLM runs onUposi-
tions rather thanT+Uspeech-prefix contexts, memory cost is
reduced.
This work makes three main contributions: (1) We in-
troduce a novel LLM-based ASR architecture that decou-
ples monotonic alignment (learned by an Aligner-Encoder)
from language modeling (provided by a pretrained LLM used
as the joiner), where the LLM operates onUaligned la-
bel positions rather than a long speech-prefixed sequence.
(2) We present a practical integration recipe that reuses the
LLM with tokenizer/embeddings/LM head unchanged, injects
speech at a chosen layer, and adapts only joiner-side layers
via LoRA. (3) We show consistent WER improvements over
size-matched baselines on LibriSpeech [17] and multilingual
Common V oice [18] under a controlled setting without large
pretrained speech encoders, and find that end-to-end training
through the LLM joiner yields implicit knowledge transfer to
the speech encoder.
2. Related Work
2.1. Label-synchronous ASR and Aligner-encoders
AED ASR (e.g., LAS and Transformer variants) is label-
synchronous in that decoding advances one step per output to-
ken [2, 3], where alignment is typically realized via decoder-
side attention over the length-Tencoder frame sequence. An al-
ternative line of work constructs token-level acoustic represen-
tations with explicit monotonic structure. Continuous Integrate-
and-Fire (CIF) predicts monotonic token boundaries and aggre-
gates a frame sequence intoUtoken-level embeddings [14].
Aligner-Encoders [16], illustrated in Figure 2, further sim-
plify training of alignment by supervising a fixed set of encoder
positions directly with a token-level cross-entropy objective.

2.2. LLM-based ASR integration
Recent LLM-based ASR systems typically connect a speech en-
coder to a pretrained text LLM byconditioning a decoder-only
model on speech embeddings(“speech-as-prefix”). As illus-
trated in Figure 1, the encoder produces a sequence of acous-
tic embeddings that are prepended to the text stream, and the
decoder-only LLM autoregressively predicts text while attend-
ing over this speech prefix. Speech-LLaMA studies decoder-
only speech-to-text and LLM integration, while CJST intro-
duces a CTC-based compressor to shorten the speech prefix and
trains speech/text jointly [10, 11]. A related line keeps the LLM
largely unchanged and learns a lightweight adaptor between the
encoder and LLM; SLAM-ASR shows that such simple compo-
sitions can be strong baselines [19]. In contrast, in our approach
the LLM serves as the joiner and operates onUaligned label
positions rather than a long speech-prefixed context.
2.3. LLMs within transducer-style factorisations
Several works aim to preserve or improve modularity of trans-
ducer models by isolating language-model-like components.
HAT [20] characterizes and leverages an internal language
model within a transducer formulation, and the Factorized Neu-
ral Transducer [21] separates blank and vocabulary predic-
tion to facilitate language-model adaptation. More recently,
Transducer-Llama integrates LLMs into streamable transducer-
based ASR [22]. Our method differs in mechanism and ob-
jective: by adopting an Aligner-Encoder backbone, we avoid
transducer lattice computation and blank-token integration, and
instead apply an LLM as a joiner over aU-length interface.
3. Methodology
Figure 3 summarizes the proposed LLM-as-Joiner architecture,
which decouples monotonic speech-text alignment from lan-
guage modeling in a label-synchronous ASR framework. An
Aligner-Encoder backbone learns the monotonic alignment and
produces a sequence ofUlabel-synchronous speech states con-
sumed by downstream modules. We instantiate the predic-
tor/joiner computation with a pretrained LLM by injecting the
speech states at a split layer of the LLM via gated fusion; only
the upper (joiner-side) layers are LoRA-tuned [23]. The LLM
is reused as-is, without introducing a blank token or modifying
its tokenizer, input embeddings, or LM head.

3.2. LLM-as-Joiner

with a pretrained decoder-only LLM by splitting it at layerℓ:
lower blocks act as the predictor and upper blocks as the joiner,
with speech injected at the split. This split-and-inject design
is motivated by analyses showing modality fusion emerges in

beddings and LM head; using the LLM as the predictor would
instead require an additional joiner-side output head. We treat
the split layerℓas a hyperparameter; empirically, early to mid
injection yields similar accuracy, while late injection degrades
(Section 4.2). Given the token history, the embedding layer

3.4. Lightweight standalone recognizer
To obtain a deployable model without the LLM at inference,
we attach a lightweight non-LLM head to the same Aligner-
Encoder and train it jointly with the LLM-as-Joiner head. The
lightweight head consumes the same label-synchronous speech
statesSand defines its own predictor and joiner: the predictor
is a 1-layer LSTM [26] over the token history and the joiner
is a small feed-forward network. The lightweight head pre-
dicts over a corpus-matched subset of LLM-tokenizer tokens
to keep tokenization consistent on both training and test sets.
At inference we can drop the LLM and decode using only the
lightweight head, while joint training encourages the shared en-
coder to learn linguistic structure induced by the LLM pathway
without an explicit distillation loss.
3.5. Training objective
We train a single shared speech encoder with two autoregressive
heads: an LLM-as-Joiner head and a lightweight head. Both
heads use the token-level negative log-likelihood in Eq. 6; we
denote these losses asL llm andL lite when Eq. 6 is evaluated
under the LLM and lightweight distributions, respectively. We
additionally apply an inter-CTC lossL ctc [27] to encoder out-
puts at an intermediate layer. The overall objective is a weighted

where we setλ llm = 1.0,λ lite = 0.5, andλ ctc = 0.1.
4. Experimental evaluations
We evaluate on LibriSpeech and Common V oice 16.1 using a
five-language subset (cv-5langs: de/en/es/fr/it) [17, 18]. Forcv-
5langs, we train a single multilingual model on the union of all
five languages and report per-language WER on the test splits;
for LibriSpeech we report WER on test-clean and test-other.
All Aligner-based systems use the same Conformer-L
speech encoder (17 layers, 8 heads, hidden dim2048) on
80-dim log-mel filterbank features, with inter-CTC loss at
12th layer, trained from scratch on the target dataset (except
Whisper-small); thus our results are not directly comparable to
LLM-ASR work using externally pretrained speech encoders.
For all LLM-based settings we use Llama-3.2-3B
(base) [28] and inject speech at split layerℓ∈{0,7,14}(of 28
layers; defaultℓ= 7), whereℓ= 0injects speech directly with
the token embeddings (no lower LLM blocks). LoRA (rank 16)
is applied only to attention modules above the injection point
(Section 3).
For the lightweight head, we use a subset vocabulary (4,359
for LibriSpeech and 6,351 forcv-5langs) formed from the sub-
set of tokens produced by the LLM tokenizer on the training
transcripts. We use the Transformer learning rate schedule with
the peak at10 −3, 20k warmup steps, and subsequent decay,
with an effective batch size of 70 minutes of audio per update.
All systems are decoded with beam search (beam size 6) with-
out external LM, rescoring, or test-time adaptation.

We include two baselines in Table 1.LLM Decoder-onlyis a

CTC objective for 50 epochs. (ii) We then attach the frozen en-
coder to a decoder-only LLM in a speech-as-prefix ASR setup

the LLM for 10 epochs, reporting parameter-averaged results
over the best five checkpoints. We use two-stage training be-

5. Conclusion
We presentedLLM-as-Joiner, a label-synchronous ASR archi-
tecture that separates monotonic alignment from language mod-
eling by letting an Aligner-Encoder produce aU-length token-
level speech interface and applying a pretrained decoder-only
LLM as the joiner. Across LibriSpeech and multilingual Com-
mon V oice, LLM-as-Joiner improves WER over size-matched
baselines, and the jointly trained lightweight head benefits from
implicit knowledge transfer from the LLM pathway, yielding
strong multilingual gains. LLM-as-Joiner also improves decod-
ing efficiency by avoiding a long speech-prefixed LLM context.
