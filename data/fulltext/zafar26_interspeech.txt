Abstract
The ADReSS and ADReSSo challenge datasets have be-
come the de-facto standards for research on dementia detec-
tion through speech, with over half of recent ICASSP and
Interspeech studies on the topic relying on them. Despite
their widespread adoption, both datasets exhibit properties that
may undermine the validity of reported results. In this pa-
per, the acoustic variability in the test sets of ADReSS and
ADReSSo is systematically examined. Results show that (i)
near state-of-the-art classification performance on the test sets
can be achieved using only two low-level acoustic features, (ii)
classifiers trained on randomly permuted dementia labels can
nonetheless reach competitive test performance, and (iii) feature
discriminability does not generalize across Monte Carlo resam-
pling, with no stable features emerging from low-level acous-
tic feature sets. These findings indicate that strong results on
these datasets can stem from spurious correlations rather than
pathology-relevant cues. The analysis calls for more cautious
interpretation of benchmark performance, as well as renewed
attention to small dataset evaluation strategies in dementia de-
tection.

1. Introduction
Dementia is a progressive neurodegenerative condition affect-
ing millions worldwide, with incidence projected to triple by
2050 [1]. Early diagnosis is critical, as both pharmacological
and lifestyle interventions have shown promise in slowing dis-
ease progression [2]. Cognitive decline associated with demen-
tia disrupts multiple processes, including memory, language,
and executive function, many of which manifest in spontaneous
speech [3]. This has motivated growing interest in speech-
based approaches to dementia detection, leading to the release
of multiple benchmark datasets through community challenges
[4, 5, 6, 7, 8]. Among these, the ADReSS [4] and ADReSSo
[5] challenges stand out as the most widely adopted resources,
featuring American English speech samples of dementia and
healthy control participants.
Since 2020, more than 100 papers in the ICASSP and Inter-
speech proceedings alone have addressed dementia or cognitive
decline detection using speech, with over half reporting classi-
fication results using ADReSS or ADReSSo datasets. These
datasets have thus become the de facto standard for speech-
based dementia research. However, despite their widespread
use, both datasets present challenges that remain underexam-
ined, notably substantial acoustic variability and optimistic test

The ADReSS and ADReSSo datasets are derived from the
Pitt Cookie Theft Corpus [9], which has since been shown
to permit trivially easy discrimination between dementia and
healthy speech using only silent segments [10]. To mitigate
such confounds, the ADReSS and ADReSSo challenge organiz-
ers applied preprocessing steps intended to normalize recording
conditions across the datasets. Nevertheless, residual imbal-
ances remain, raising concerns about whether models trained
on these datasets capture pathology-relevant cues or instead
exploit incidental acoustic artifacts. Subsequent studies have
highlighted related issues, including the influence of the inter-
viewer’s speech in distinguishing dementia from healthy speech
[11] and achieving above-chance discriminability using silent
segments in the ADReSSo dataset [12].
While these studies have established that particular seg-
ments of the recordings can produce inflated performance, the
question of whether the entire recording is susceptible to brittle
classification on low-level acoustic features remains underex-
plored. To address this question, we re-examine the ADReSS
and ADReSSo datasets and present three key observations:
• Near state-of-the-art classification performance can be ob-
tained with just two low-level acoustic features on the chal-
lenge test sets. Closer inspection reveals that these features
may encode little beyond channel-related variability.
• Even when dementia labels are randomly permuted (some
control recordings re-labelled as dementia and vice versa),
classifiers may achieve competitive test set performance with
just two low-level acoustic features.
• The test set performance does not generalize across Monte
Carlo resampling splits of the datasets. While subsets of
acoustic features discriminate individual splits, no feature
generalizes reliably in either feature sets investigated.
These findings suggest that strong classification perfor-
mance on ADReSS and ADReSSo may arise from spurious
correlations specific to the challenge split, calling into ques-
tion the robustness and generalizability of systems evaluated
on these benchmarks. The results highlight the need for more
cautious interpretation of benchmark performance and for im-
proved evaluation practices when working with small dementia
speech datasets.

ADReSSo dementia detection datasets is evaluated using an in-
terpretable classification pipeline. Acoustic descriptors from
the openSMILE toolkit [13] are extracted and assessed in pair-

2.1. Datasets
Two benchmark datasets are studied: the ADReSS dataset re-
leased in 2020 and the ADReSSo dataset released in 2021, both
of which provide balanced subsets of speech recordings from
dementia patients and healthy controls [4, 5]. The speech con-
sists of participants performing the Cookie Theft picture de-
scription task [14], in which individuals describe a complex
kitchen scene illustrated in a static drawing. Both ADReSS and
ADReSSo are derived from the Pitt Corpus, but with additional
preprocessing steps. Crucially, both datasets are pre-balanced
by age, gender, and diagnosis to control for potential confounds.

The pyannote [15] V oice Activity Detection (V AD) library
is used to segment each audio recording into speech and non-
speech regions, from which two derived dataset variants are
constructed. The first is a silence-only variant, where each
recording consists of a concatenation of all silence segments
from the original audio. The second is a speech-only variant,
where each recording is formed by concatenating only the de-
tected speech segments.
2.2. Feature Sets
Low-level acoustic descriptors (LLDs) have been used as base-
line models in all five dementia detection challenges to date.
These descriptors are well-curated in the openSMILE toolkit
[13], with two of the most prominent sets being the extended
Geneva Minimalistic Acoustic Parameter Set (eGeMAPS) [16]
and the Computational Paralinguistics Challenge feature set
(ComParE) [17]. The eGeMAPS feature set (eGeMAPSv02)
comprises 88 features and provides a compact, theory-driven
summary of prosodic, spectral, and voice-quality characteris-
tics. By contrast, the ComParE feature set (ComParE 2016)
is far more exhaustive, comprising 6,373 features that capture a
wide spectrum of prosodic, spectral, energy-based, and cepstral
descriptors, along with extensive statistical functionals.
2.3. Feature Selection
Given the large number of descriptors in both eGeMAPS (88
features) and ComParE (6,373 features), a stepwise feature se-
lection strategy is adopted. First, classifiers to discriminate con-
trol from dementia recordings are trained on each individual
feature in isolation, allowing us to quantify its discriminative
power on both the fixed test set and on resampled splits gener-
ated during the Monte Carlo validation (see Section Validation).
From this analysis, the top-performing individual features are
identified and in a second stage, pairwise combinations of these
top features are used to train classifiers. This allows for the
investigation of whether complementary information improves
classifier performance. The minimal feature pairing is a delib-
erate attempt to examine how far classifier performance can be
pushed by exploiting a small subset of features.
2.4. Validation
Logistic regression classifiers are used in all experiments, as
they provide an interpretable linear decision boundary in the
2D case. By design, if logistic regression achieves meaning-
ful separation between dementia and healthy speech, then more
complex models are unnecessary.
The implementation follows a standardscikit-learn
pipeline. Missing values are imputed with the mean, and all fea-
tures are z-score normalized. Theliblinearsolver is used
due to its stability in high-dimensional, small-sample settings.
All classifier performance is reported using the macro-F1
score, which provides an equal-weighted balance of precision
and recall across both classes. This ensures that classification
quality is not biased toward the majority or easier-to-classify
class.
Label Permutation:To evaluate the robustness of the clas-
sification, label permutation experiments are conducted as a
negative control. For each of 100 iterations, the class labels
in the training split -dementiaandcontrol- are randomly per-
muted and the classifier is retrained and evaluated. The resulting
performance represents the chance-level baseline of the pipeline
under label-shuffling. This procedure ensures that any observed
discriminative power in the real experiments is not an artefact
of noise or spurious correlation.
Monte Carlo Resampling:To evaluate the generalizabil-
ity of the features, Monte Carlo resampling validation is con-
ducted. Specifically, we repeatedly sample random train/test
splits in a 70/30 ratio from the full dataset while ensuring that
pathology labels remain balanced across splits. This ratio mir-
rors the composition of original datasets. Each experiment is
repeated for 100 iterations, allowing us to average over differ-
ent distributions of age, gender, and MMSE.

From an acoustic perspective, the selected features
merit closer examination. For ADReSS,audSpec Rfilt
sma[22] quartile1represents the first quartile of a band-
limited auditory spectrum filter, effectively a summary statistic
of energy distribution in one subband. This measure is suscep-
tible to recording channel characteristics and background noise.
By contrast,pcm fftMag spectralCentroid sma de
quartile2captures the median value of the derivative of the
spectral centroid, i.e., the distribution of energy across frequen-
cies over time. While spectral centroid dynamics may plausibly
relate to articulatory or phonatory changes, the feature remains
sensitive to artefacts such as microphone response and silence
handling.
For ADReSSo,mfcc sma[6] centroidis the cen-
troid statistic of the sixth Mel-frequency cepstral coefficient.
This coefficient primarily reflects spectral envelope shape and
may react to duration and voice activity detection boundaries.
As such, it is prone to channel confounds. The second fea-
ture,mfcc sma[11] flatness, measures the flatness of
the eleventh MFCC trajectory, which can correspond to reduced
articulatory variability. Nevertheless, it too remains vulnerable
to stationary noise and recording-chain effects.

3.2. Convincing Random Labels
Even when dementia labels are randomly reassigned within the
training set, classifiers trained on just two low-level acoustic
features achieve test set scores that overlap with the best test set
performance.
Specifically, across 100 repeat label permutations of the
train set labels, the two-feature models produced a wide spread
of test performance. For ADReSS, macro F1 scores ranged
from 0.123 to 0.875, and for ADReSSo from 0.153 to 0.831.
These distributions are shown in Figure 2, where each point rep-
resents the outcome of a single permutation run.

As Figure 2 illustrates, there exist non-negligible cases of
random labeling where the classifier attains scores comparable
to those reported with actual labels. That is, chance configura-
tions of spurious correlations between features and labels can
occasionally produce results that are deceptively strong.

4. Discussion
Results suggest that strong scores on the fixed ADReSS ans
ADReSSo test splits can arise without stable, pathology-linked
signal. Two-feature models reached high macro F1 on the
benchmark tests (0.875 and 0.831; Table 2), yet their mean
performance under Monte Carlo resampling dropped markedly
(0.622 for ADReSS; 0.667 for ADReSSo; Fig. 3). Moreover,
label-permutation controls occasionally overlapped the true-
label test scores (Fig. 2), and no feature combination general-
izes well across Monte Carlo splits. These observations indicate
that optimistic test results are possible when small splits align
with incidental structure in low-level descriptors, rather than re-
flecting robust dementia-related cues. The drop in performance
when looking at the silence-only and speech-only metrics sug-
gests that these features are quite brittle.
These findings are read as a caution about evaluation, not
as a blanket indictment of prior work. First, the acoustic feature
families we explored exhibit high within-set variability: many
descriptors are sensitive to channel and background conditions,
and pairwise analyses showed little recurrence of “winning”
features across resamples (Fig. 4). Second, the dataset sizes are
modest, so a few influential points can shift linear boundaries
on a single split. This might explain why test-set gains did not
translate into generalization.
Practically, this argues for more robust validation proto-
cols. Beyond reporting a single test score, studies should (i) run
repeated, stratified resampling or nested cross-validation and
report dispersion (e.g., Monte Carlo mean and variance); (ii)
include negative controls (e.g., label permutation) to calibrate
chance alignment; and (iii) treat results on a prescribed split as
one estimate among many.
Methodologically, the findings underscore the value of
models whose behaviour is explainable and anchored in domain
knowledge. Low-level acoustic descriptors are useful probes,
but robust systems should connect decisions to clinical mark-
ers in dementia and make those links inspectable. In short,
careful validation and explainable modeling are both needed to
turn promising test-set performance into trustworthy evidence
for dementia detection.
