Cross-Lingual Compositional Learning for Code-Switched Lip Reading
Jeonghyeon Joo, Jiyoung Lee∗∗
Division of Artificial Intelligence & Software, Ewha Womans University
joojeonghyeon@ewha.ac.kr, lee.jiyoung@ewha.ac.kr
Abstract
Code-switching, the alternation of multiple languages in an ut-
terance, is pervasive in multilingual communication but un-
derexplored in visual speech recognition (VSR). Especially,
the burden of collecting large-scale and realistic videos has
limited the advances in code-switched VSR. We propose Co-
CoVSR, a cross-lingual compositional learning framework that
exploits monolingual corpora. Specifically, without additional
data collection or generative synthesis, CoCoVSR adapts a
pretrained multilingual VSR model to code-switched scenar-
ios, while preserving shared articulatory features. Experimen-
tal results show that CoCoVSR achieves SOTA on Chinese-
English CSLR. Importantly, CoCoVSR achieves competitive
performance on both seen (MultiVSR Chinese) and unseen
(LRS2 English) multilingual datasets, highlighting its capabil-
ity to preserve multilingual performance with negligible degra-
dation. The code is available athttps://github.com/
ewha-mmai/CoCoVSR.
Index Terms: visual speech recognition, code-switching,
cross-lingual compositional learning
1. Introduction
Visual speech recognition (VSR), commonly known as lip read-
ing, is the process of inferring linguistic content by analyzing
the spatiotemporal dynamics of a speaker’s mouth movements.
Conventionally, related works have evolved from simple iso-
lated word recognition [1, 2] to complex continuous speech sys-
tems [3–5], largely driven by deep neural networks that map
visemes to phonetic sequences. Recent advancements [6–8]
have even extended these capabilities into multilingual settings,
where models are trained to recognize multiple languages with
shared phonetic structures across different linguistic families.
These developments have established a foundation for more in-
clusive communication technologies [7, 9].
Despite such progress, contemporary VSR research re-
mains limited in addressing the inherent fluidity of human con-
versation, particularly in the code-switching phenomenon [10].
While code-switching is a pervasive element of natural mul-
tilingual discourse [11–14], it remains a neglected frontier in
the visual domain. The primary obstacle to advancing code-
switched VSR is the extreme scarcity of annotated multilingual
visual data, which creates a significant bottleneck for training
robust deep networks. For example, the open-source dataset,
CSLR [15], currently provides 5K short English-Chinese utter-
ances (75 frames at 25 fps) from 60 speakers. Moreover, the
prevalence of repeated sentence patterns constrains lexical vari-
ability and switching diversity. Consequently, it remains un-

mixture-of-experts (MoE) architectures [13, 16, 17] or

explicit language routing, increasing memory consumption and
system complexity. In contrast, visual speech signals ex-

2. Related Work
2.1. Visual speech recognition (VSR)
VSR aims to decipher lip movements to real-world applications,
including biometric matching [21], keyword spotting [22], and
human-computer interaction [23]. and incorporating powerful
context modeling in LLMs [24–27], recent works [6, 8, 28] have
extended VSR to multilingual corpora by training unified mod-
els across languages. However, the scarcity of labeled multilin-
gual visual speech data remains a fundamental bottleneck.
To solve this problem, Kimet al.[29] have employed
feature disentanglement to separate general speech knowledge
from language-specific patterns using an independent memory-
based decoder, while mA V-HuBERT [6] has exploited dis-
cretized audio speech units in a curriculum learning manner to
stabilize visual unit discovery. In contrast, MultiVSR [7] has
expanded multilingual training data by automatically generat-
ing pseudo-labels using an ASR system (e.g., Whisper [30]).
Despite these advances, multilingual VSR methods have been
limited by the assumption of a monolingual utterance-level (i.e.,
single language decoding in a sentence with a predetermined
language token). Therefore, code-switching in VSR is underex-
plored.
2.2. Code-switching speech recognition
Code-switching, the dynamic alternation of two or more lan-
guages within a single conversational context, is a prevalent
phenomenon in multilingual communication [14, 31]. It poses
significant challenges for ASR, as recognition errors are often
occur near language-switch boundaries due to language confu-
sion and phonetic overlap [32].
To address these challenges, prior work has explored
language-aware modeling strategies. For example, Liuet
al.[33] and Wanget al.[34] have incorporated explicit lan-
guage identification (LID) signals to bias the VSR model with
token-level language posteriors. However, such approaches are
prone to error propagation, as incorrect LID predictions di-
rectly affect transcription quality. In contrast, modularized ap-
proaches [32, 35, 36] have compounded language-specific rep-
resentations for intra-sentential prediction. Recently, mixture-
of-experts (MoE) [13] is further specialized to multilingual
ASR systems by LID-guided routing [34] and cross-attention-
based fusion method [11]. While these methods have shown
impressive performance improvements in ASR, they have intro-
duced substantial parameter overhead and relied on stable rout-
ing decisions when local language evidence is weak or noisy.
While [15] have established the first CSLR benchmark , the
evaluated code-switching VSR methods, such as BiGRU [37],
MS-TCN [38], and Conformer [39] variants, were primarily de-
veloped for monolingual sequence modeling. Moreover, mod-
els designed for code-switching are typically evaluated primar-
ily on mixed-language benchmarks, while their ability to re-
tain multilingual recognition performance on purely monolin-
gual utterances is often underexamined. In contrast, we investi-
gate how a pretrained multilingual VSR model can be compo-
sitionally adapted to support code-swtiching, while maintaining
its original multilingual competence.

In this work, our goal is to expand the pretrained multilingual
VSR system [19] to describe code-switched context. To ad-
dress the scarcity of labeled code-switched visual speech data,
we propose CoCoVSR, cross-lingual compositional learning
framework that constructs training samples from existing mono-
lingual corpora, as illustrated in Fig. 2. CoCoVSR adopts a
shared adapter-based PEFT strategy, which endows the model
with code-switching capability while preserving original mul-
tilingual representations. The overall architecture is illustrated
in Fig. 3.

3.2. Cross-lingual compositional video augmentation
Code-switched visual speech data remain limited in publicly
available corpora, as collecting such data requires substan-
tial annotation effort and substantial recording cost. In con-
trast, monolingual (or multilingual) VSR data [19, 20, 40] are
comparatively easier to obtain across different languages. To
solve this limitation, we construct pseudo code-switched train-
ing samples, termed CoCo-MultiVSR, by compositionally com-
bining monolingual utterance clips from different languages.
For each pseudo-video sample, we randomly select one
Mandarin clip and one English clip and concatenate them in
both possible orders. Formally, we sample monolingual video
clipsV zh∼D zh andV en∼D en from Chinese and English cor-

Dataset.To train the model, we use CSLR [15], which con-
tains approximately 5K English-Chinese scenarios with a fixed
3-second clip. Although the raw videos are sampled at 30 fps,
we resample the frame rate to 25 fps to align with the input re-
quirements of our backbone model. The videos are provided
in grayscale and preprocessed using a S3FD face detector [43]
to crop the mouth region at a resolution of96×96. Addition-
ally, we use a subset of MultiVSR [19], particularly English
and Chinese, to facilitate comparison with the CSLR bench-
mark. Although the full MultiVSR training set contains 843K
English and 41K Chinese YouTube video clips, only 540K En-

cols, where both English and Chinese linguistic units are jointly
evaluated within code-switched utterances. Lower WER, CER,
and MER indicate better recognition performance.
4.2. Implementation details
All videos are uniformly sampled at 25 fps. We use S3FD [43]
to crop the faces in raw videos in MultiVSR [19]. The cropped
face region is resized to224×224. While if the video clip is
already provided as96×96size, it is used directly; otherwise,
frames are resized to160×160resolution and center-cropped
to96×96, followed Prajwalet al.[5]. We use LoRA [42] as an
adapter in both the transformer encoder and decoder, with a 32
rank. We train all models on 50 epochs with 8 batch size. The
training starts with a learning rate of1e-4, using linear schedul-
ing and a warm-up stage for the first 10% of total training iter-
ations. At inference, we adopt beam search with a beam width
of 20. For all experiments, we use 4 NVIDIA RTX 6000 GPUs.

4.4. Analysis
Table 2 presents the effectiveness of training data composition.
Training only on CSLR achieves the best CSLR accuracy but
substantially degrades performance on other datasets. By incor-
porating CoCo-MultiVSR, CoCoVSR achieves a better balance
between code-switching accuracy and multilingual recognition.
In addition, we analyze the effectiveness of adapter archi-
tectures in Table 3. The shared adapter consistently outper-
forms language-specific multiple adapters on CSLR, achieving
lower CER, WER, and MER. Interestingly, while adapters are
effective in preserving the pretrained multilingual representa-
tions, language-specific design, which is widely used in ASR,
degrades code-switching performance on the VSR system.
Finally, we visualize the qualitative results in Fig. 4. The
results show that CoCoVSR demonstrates strong performance,
despite the inherent difficulty of distinguishing phonetic units
from visual cues.
5. Conclusion
We introduce CoCoVSR, the cross-lingual composition learn-
ing framework for code-switched lip reading. By randomly aug-
menting code-switched clips from monolingual visual speech
clips, our approach generates diverse pseudo code-switched
samples, exposing the model to a wide range of cross-lingual
switching patterns. With parameter-efficient finetuning on the
multilingual backbone, we efficiently achieve the state-of-the-
art performance on CSLR, while minimizing the performance
degradation on LRS2 and MultiVSR.
