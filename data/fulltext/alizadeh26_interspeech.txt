Abstract
We investigate the impact of informal Persian speech on auto-
matic speech recognition and speech translation in low-resource
settings. Persian exhibits substantial variation between formal
and informal registers, yet most publicly available datasets fo-
cus on formal speech, leaving informal variants underrepre-
sented. To address this gap, we introduce the Toorintan-Persian
Informal Dataset, a carefully transcribed and validated collec-
tion of informal speech from diverse speakers and contexts. We
apply targeted normalization techniques to improve data con-
sistency and fine-tune state-of-the-art models on this dataset.
Experiments demonstrate that models trained on informal data
generalize effectively across both formal and informal registers
and outperform existing baselines in WER and BLEU scores.
To facilitate further research, we also release a Persian text nor-
malizer. Both the dataset and associated tools are publicly avail-
able on Hugging Face.1,

1. Introduction
The rapid progress of automatic speech recognition (ASR), ma-
chine translation (MT), and speech translation (ST) has been
fueled by the availability of large-scale, high-quality corpora.
However, for many languages, including Persian (Farsi), most
existing resources are limited to formal domains such as broad-
cast news, scripted speech, or read text. This mismatch with
everyday informal speech poses challenges for real-world ap-
plications, where spontaneous, conversational, and colloquial
expressions are far more common. In Persian, a clear distinction
between formal and informal speech presents unique challenges
for ASR systems. For example, the formal phrase/mi: xO:hæm/
contrasts with its informal, colloquial counterpart/mi:xOm/,
which is far more common in everyday usage. When train-
ing data contains transcripts in the formal register but the audio
reflects informal variants, this mismatch introduces noise that
impairs acoustic–text alignment. The result is elevated error
rates, degraded model performance, and unstable convergence.
Ensuring consistency between the spoken register and its tran-
scription is therefore essential, especially in languages like Per-
sian, where formal and informal usage diverge significantly.
Several Persian corpora have been introduced in recent
years; . For example, the FARSDAT corpus [1] offers phonet-
ically balanced read sentences but is relatively small and old
data. Similarly, the DeepMine database [2] provides large-scale
recordings in both Persian and English, yet its primary focus lies

on speaker verification and ASR tasks within formal contexts
rather than spontaneous, conversational speech. The Arman-A V
dataset [3] is a large-scale audio-visual corpus for Persian, con-
taining approximately 220 hours of videos from 1,760 speak-
ers. It is the first large-scale Persian dataset supporting lip read-
ing and facilitates tasks such as ASR and audio-visual speech
recognition. However, most of the recordings originate from
interview-style settings, while content from series and movies
constitutes less than 10%of usable data due to challenges such
as varying camera angles, multi-speaker interactions, and back-
ground noise. Consequently, the dataset does not adequately
capture more diverse, in-the-wild speech conditions. Similar to
other formal Persian corpora such as DeepMine and FARSDAT,
these structured recordings fail to reflect the full variability of
real-world speech.
Other multilingual datasets also include Persian data, al-
beit with limitations. For instance, FLEURS [5] is a multilin-
gual speech dataset designed to evaluate cross-lingual transfer
in speech technologies. It covers 102 languages, and its audio
consists of read speech recorded by native speakers in natural
environments, which may be quiet or noisy. Common V oice
[4] is a multilingual read-speech dataset collected via crowd-
sourcing. Text prompts are sourced from public domain texts
and volunteer contributions, with speakers recording them un-
der various acoustic conditions and using different devices. This
dataset captures diverse accents and speaking styles. CoV oST-2
[6] is a large-scale multilingual speech-to-text translation cor-
pus built upon the Common V oice dataset. It uses validated
speech clips from Common V oice, and the corresponding trans-
lations are provided by professional translators.
Despite these efforts, a significant gap remains in avail-
able resources that adequately capture the informal and collo-
quial aspects of Persian speech. Informal Persian speech ex-
hibits characteristics such as dialectal variation, reduced pro-
nunciations, and non-standard vocabulary, which are rarely rep-
resented in current datasets. Consequently, ASR and translation
systems trained on formal or read-text corpora often suffer sig-
nificant performance degradation when applied to spontaneous,
conversational speech. Addressing this gap requires resources
that capture naturally spoken Persian in informal contexts, ac-
companied by high-quality transcriptions and translations.
In this work, we introduce the Toorintan-Persian Informal
Dataset (T-PID), a new parallel Persian–English corpus of in-
formal speech designed to advance research in ASR and ST. To
the best of our knowledge, T-PID is the first publicly described
dataset featuring Persian informal speech paired with aligned
English translations, providing a crucial foundation for devel-
oping and evaluating systems that go beyond formal or scripted
language.

2. Dataset Description
2.1. Collection Method
The data collection process focused on selected speakers with
extensive publicly available audio resources across multiple me-
dia platforms. The collected recordings were chosen to repre-
sent diverse informal speaking styles and conversational con-
texts. This process was carried out in two main stages:
Stage 1:
A team of 10 annotators was tasked with identifying in-
dividual speakers and their appearances in Iranian films and
television programs. For each selected speaker, relevant video
sources were identified, and segments in which the speaker ap-
peared alone, without overlapping speech, were manually anno-
tated. The exact start and end times of each isolated speech seg-
ment were carefully recorded. These segments may still contain
background noise, reverberation, music, or other acoustic arti-
facts.
Stage 2:
Using the timestamps and video sources identified in the
first stage, the corresponding audio segments were extracted
from the original films and saved as individual clips. These
clips were then randomly assigned to a team of 15 annotators,
all of whom had strong proficiency in spoken and written Per-
sian. Each annotator was trained on a standardized set of tran-
scription guidelines prior to beginning the task. They listened to
their assigned clips and transcribed the spoken content in Per-
sian, applying appropriate punctuation to reflect the natural flow
and tone of informal speech. This makes the written text exactly
match what was said by the speaker.
To ensure consistency and high-quality output, a quality
control team of three members closely monitored the transcrip-
tion process throughout.
This meticulous pipeline—combining careful speaker and
video selection, isolated speech segment extraction, experi-
enced annotators, and rigorous quality control—ensures that
the dataset is highly accurate and representative of informal,
conversational Persian. Compared to existing Persian speech
datasets, this resource offers significant improvements in both
quality and practical usability.
To generate English translations, we first evaluated several
state-of-the-art machine translation systems on a representative
subset of our dataset. Based on qualitative assessments of flu-
ency, adequacy, and contextual accuracy particularly for infor-
mal and conversational Persian, ChatGPT-4o mini [7] demon-
strated the most consistent and contextually appropriate perfor-
mance. Consequently, it was selected to translate the full Per-
sian transcription set into English.
Following the automated translation stage, we implemented
a data filtering process to remove clips with very short durations
or transcripts that lacked sufficient semantic content for mean-
ingful translation. Additionally, for segments containing id-
iomatic expressions, cultural references, or proverbs—elements
that typically pose greater challenges for machine translation
systems—we conducted targeted random manual checks and
applied corrections where necessary.
Instead of performing a full manual review of all 22,443
translated segments, we adopted a structured quality assurance
approach based on systematic random sampling. A total of
400 segments were randomly selected for manual evaluation.
For the segments that required corrections, post-editing was ap-
plied, and the overall Word Error Rate (WER) of the modifi-
cations was 4 % This low error rate indicates that only mi-
nor adjustments were necessary, and therefore a complete re-
annotation of the English portion was not considered necessary.
This hybrid validation framework ensured translation qual-
ity in linguistically complex cases while maintaining efficiency
and scalability in the dataset construction process. Using
this pipeline, we initially collected approximately 58 hours of
speech data. After systematic pruning and quality control, the
final curated dataset comprises 36.77 hours of high-quality, us-
able audio segments.
The final dataset is provided in a straightforward format:
each W A V audio file is paired with its corresponding Persian
transcription and English translation. This structure allows the
dataset to be directly usable for ASR, MT and ST.
2.2. Data Analysis
The dataset consists of 22,443 utterances, divided into training,
validation, and test sets with 18,972, 626, and 2,845 samples
respectively. Each utterance contains a Persian transcript, its
English translation, and an associated audio file. This dataset
provides sufficient samples for model training, validation, and
evaluation. The dataset includes an equal number of male and
female speakers, with 11,244 utterances from male speakers
(50.1%) and 11,199 from female speakers (49.9%), ensuring
balanced gender representation. In terms of total speech du-
ration, the dataset contains approximately 19.06 hours of male
speech and 17.72 hours of female speech.
Audio durations were analyzed to understand the temporal
characteristics of the recordings. The average audio duration
is 5.90 seconds. Most utterances are relatively short, although
some longer recordings exist. The distribution of audio dura-
tions is illustrated in Figure 1, demonstrating that the dataset
primarily consists of concise audio suitable for efficient pro-
cessing and model training.

characters and words. Across the dataset, Persian utterances
contain 68.05 characters on average and 14.24 words per utter-

3. Experiments and Results
We divided the T-PID dataset into training, validation, and test
sets. The training set contains 29.52 hours of speech, compris-
ing 9,507 male and 9,465 female utterances from 1012 different
speakers. The validation set includes 2.66 hours of speech with
311 male and 315 female utterances from 334 speakers. Fi-
nally, the test set consists of 4.59 hours of speech, containing
1,426 male and 1,419 female utterances from 831 speakers.
3.1. Speech To Text
To evaluate the effectiveness of our data validation process,
we fine-tuned Whisper-small [8], Whisper-medium [8] and
Wav2Vec2-BERT [9] on our dataset and compared their per-
formance using WER, assessing results both on our data and
against the WER values reported in their original papers.
The Whisper paper (Appendix C) [8] recommends applying
text standardization techniques—such as removing phrases en-
closed in parentheses—but this guideline does not hold for read-
speech datasets like Common V oice [4], FLEURS [5], and CoV-

oST [6], where speakers read the text verbatim, including con-
tent within parentheses. Therefore, this standardization cannot
be universally applied. During our data processing, we encoun-
tered several dataset-specific challenges. For example, the tran-
scribed text sometimes did not align with the spoken audio, such
as when speakers used informal language but the transcript was
written formally. We also found inconsistencies in affix usage
for instance, affixes that are conventionally written separately
were occasionally attached to preceding words; we did not nor-
malize these cases. Furthermore, some transcriptions contained
text in languages other than Persian, which negatively affects
ASR performance given that the models are tailored for Persian
only. To address these issues, we applied targeted corrections:
if speakers read the content inside parentheses, we retained the
text but removed the parentheses; if not, we removed both the
parentheses and their content. Non-Persian words were manu-
ally replaced with their Persian equivalents, while affix attach-
ment was left unchanged. Since these issues impact WER, these
corrections were incorporated into our experiments, with final
results presented in Table 3. To further improve consistency
and address common formatting challenges, we developed and
publicly released a general Persian text normalizer, available on
Hugging Face.

high WERs on the T-PID test set, which can largely be at-
tributed to hallucination effects. In many instances, the mod-
els generate unrelated or spurious text when processing sponta-
neous and acoustically diverse speech. This issue is particularly

dataset exhibits a substantially higher perplexity compared to
Common V oice 9 and FLEURS, indicating greater linguistic di-

puted using a GPT-2 [10] language model trained on Persian
text, ensuring a language-specific and reliable measure of tex-
tual complexity. The higher perplexity of T-PID is consistent
with its spontaneous, informal, and audio-first nature, and helps
explain the difficulty baseline models face when evaluated with-
out adaptation. In contrast, FLEURS shows the lowest perplex-
ity, reflecting its more controlled and text-driven design.
From an acoustic perspective, the speech-to-reverberation
modulation energy ratio (SRMR) [11] values of T-PID and
Common V oice 9 are relatively close, while FLEURS exhibits
a slightly lower SRMR, suggesting differences in recording
conditions and reverberation characteristics. Since T-PID was
constructed in an audio-first manner—where speech was col-
lected prior to transcription and transcripts were subsequently
extracted with high precision—the resulting speech–text align-
ment is tighter and more faithful to the acoustic signal. This
property is particularly advantageous for CTC-based architec-
tures such as Wav2Vec2-BERT, which explicitly model frame-
level alignment between acoustic features and token sequences.
The strong alignment and reduced transcription mismatch likely
contribute to the relatively better performance of the CTC-based
model on T-PID, despite its higher linguistic complexity.
It is also important to note that a publicly available Persian
base checkpoint for Wav2Vec2-BERT was not available. Ac-
cording to the official training guidelines, the model must first
be adapted to the target language before evaluation. As a result,
only the fine-tuned results are reported.
Overall, the results suggest that although T-PID is linguis-
tically more complex and less predictable than the comparison
datasets, fine-tuning on informal and naturally occurring speech
not only improves in-domain performance but also yields strong
cross-domain generalization to more formal speech datasets.

3.2. Speech To Text Translation
In this section, we evaluate translation performance using a cas-
caded ST pipeline. The pipeline consists of two main compo-
nents: an ASR module and a MT module. The ASR compo-
nent generates transcriptions from the input audio, while the
MT component translates these transcriptions into the target
language. For the ASR stage, we employed Whisper-small,
Whisper-medium, and Wav2Vec2-BERT, fine-tuned versions.
The models were fine-tuned on the T-PID dataset for Persian
transcription. For the MT stage, we used NLLB-200 [12],
which was additionally fine-tuned on the T-PID training text
data. To better understand the impact of fine-tuning, we report
results using combinations of base and fine-tuned ASR and MT
models. The results are presented in Table 2.
Following [8], we evaluate our pipeline using the Bilin-
gual Evaluation Understudy (BLEU) metric [13]. Consistent
with the text standardization guidelines provided in Appendix
C of [8], we removed only the parentheses in FLEURS, and
both the parentheses and their content in CoV oST2, to ensure
fair comparison. As shown in Table 2, fine-tuning NLLB-
200 substantially improves performance of ASR models out-
put across all datasets. For FLEURS, BLEU scores increase
from 5.62 to 13.78 for Whisper-small, from 5.61 to 14.42 for
Whisper-medium, and from 8.60 to 21.41 for Wav2Vec2-BERT.
A similar trend is observed on CoV oST2, where fine-tuning
improves BLEU from 14.02 to 16.54 (Whisper-small), from
14.10 to 16.77 (Whisper-medium), and from 16.63 to 20.69
(Wav2Vec2-BERT). The largest improvements are obtained on
the T-PID test set, where Wav2Vec2-BERT achieves the highest
BLEU score of 25.30 after fine-tuning, compared to 11.96 in the
base setting. According to [8], the end-to-end Whisper-medium
model reports BLEU scores of 15.0 on FLEURS and 15.5 on
CoV oST2, while Whisper-small reports 5.8 and 4.9 on the same
datasets, respectively.
Overall, the results demonstrate that fine-tuning on the T-
PID dataset significantly enhances cascade ST performance for
both ASR and MT components. Among the evaluated models,
Wav2Vec2-BERT consistently achieves the best performance
after fine-tuning, highlighting the effectiveness of domain adap-
tation in low-resource speech translation scenarios.
4. CONCLUSIONS AND FUTURE WORK
This study showed that properly processed informal Persian
speech can significantly improve low-resource ASR and ST
systems. Fine-tuning models like Whisper-medium, Wav2Vec-
BERT, and NLLB-200 on the T-PID dataset led to better WER
and BLEU scores, even outperforming baselines on formal
speech. The results highlight the value of informal speech as
a resource for low-resource languages. The T-PID dataset and
a Persian text normalizer have been released to support fur-
ther research. Future work includes expanding the dataset with
more diverse speech and developing Persian-specific ASR and
ST models.
