Word Iconicity and Phonological Surprisal as Predictors of Age of Acquisition
Alexander Kilpatrick1,∗∗, Rikke Bundgaard-Nielsen2
1 University of Aizu, Japan
2 University of Melbourne, Australia
alex@u-aizu.ac.jp, rikkieb@unimelb.edu.au
Abstract
Iconic words are acquired earlier than arbitrary words cross-
linguistically and controlled experiments show that children
find phonotactically typical word forms easier to learn than un-
usual ones. This contrasts with adult processing, where un-
usual word forms—including iconic words—are better recalled
and more deeply encoded. Using machine learning and regres-
sion analyses, we examine the relationship between iconicity,
information-theoretic measures of phonological surprisal and
entropy, and age of acquisition (AoA). Iconicity robustly pre-
dicts earlier AoA across the lexicon, while surprisal and entropy
show weak and inconsistent effects on acquisition but substan-
tially predict adult processing outcomes. These findings reveal
a developmental dissociation: form-meaning transparency scaf-
folds early word learning, while phonological unpredictability
supports adult processing efficiency.
Index Terms: iconicity, age of acquisition, phonological sur-
prisal, word learning, information theory
1. Introduction
Cross-linguistically, iconic words like ‘crash’ and ‘miaow’,
whose sounds resemble their meanings, tend to be acquired ear-
lier than arbitrary words, even after controlling for factors like
frequency, length, and concreteness [1, 2, 3] . This early advan-
tage is robust at both the level of individual word meanings and
whole vocabularies, with children’s first receptive and produc-
tive vocabularies containing a disproportionately high fraction
of iconic items [1, 4]. Iconic words are thought to have this
prominent position in child vocabularies because clear form-
meaning mapping facilitates word-learning (e.g., bootstrapping
hypothesis; [5]). One of the consequences of strong form-
meaning mapping in iconic words is that they often contain un-
usual phonemes and unusual phoneme sequences (in any given
language) [6]. This means that iconic words, beyond providing
a likely bootstrapping advantage due to the transparent form-
meaning mapping, also present listeners with unusual linguis-
tic information: iconic words are generally high in surprisal, a
measures that quantifies how expected one phoneme is is after
another [6].
The unusual sounds in iconic words might lead to the as-
sumption that the unusual phonological shapes of iconic words
also facilitates their learning. This is not the case: controlled
child word learning experiments show that unusual word forms
are harder to learn for children than words that conform to lan-
guage specific phonotactics and typicality measures [7]. The
fact that unusual word-forms do not facilitate word learning
in children presents a contrast to adult word processing behav-

forms are advantageous: words with unusual forms—including
iconic words—are better recalled and processed more deeply
than words that have more language-typical forms [6, 8].
We might plausibly argue that the behavioural changes re-
flect language experience (what is unusual can only be deter-
mined on the basis of rich experience of what is usual), but we
do not know when that change happens. At what age might
children have sufficient language-specific knowledge to detect
small but systematic differences in the information-theoretic
structures of words? And, when might we see evidence of the
differential processing of unusual word forms that we see in
adult language users? We address these questions here.
Iconicity refers to perceived similarity between a given lin-
guistic form, typically a word, and its referent, such that the
sound or shape of a word reflects something about what it
means [9]. Large scale studies have demonstrated that iconic
words are consistently overrepresented in the early vocabularies
of infants and toddlers, with the effect being strongest in very
early language acquisition between the ages 10 and 30 months
[1, 3, 4, 10]. This pattern is robust across spoken and signed
languages, including English, Spanish, and Israeli Sign Lan-
guage [2, 11, 12]. It is often assumed that early iconic word
learning may bootstrap children’s acquisition of form-meaning
mappings [9, 13, 14].
Iconic words are, however, not confined to initial word
learning. Iconic words are used more frequently by young chil-
dren in their speech, and adults use more iconic words when
speaking to children than to other adults, suggesting that iconic-
ity may scaffold both production and comprehension across the
early developmental period [3, 4, 15]. Iconicity is argued to be
particularly beneficial for learning adjectives and verbs, cate-
gories that are often more abstract and harder for young learn-
ers to acquire [2], and for words referring to absent or unfamil-
iar referents [13, 16]. As children’s vocabularies expand and
linguistic skills mature, the influence of the proportion of iconic
words shrinks and other factors become more prominent [1, 11].
Despite the high proportion of iconic words in early
vocabularies—and the purported advantage of a clear form-
meaning mapping of iconic words—research on word learning
and word segmentation with infants and toddlers in laboratory
settings show that early phonotactic knowledge is helpful in
the word learning process. In highly controlled environments
where form-meaning mappings are minimal (or systematically
avoided), infants have been shown to prefer to listen to wordlists
containing words that are phonotactically legal and ‘regular’ in
the language they are acquiring [6, 17], over lists of words that
break language specific expectations. In other words, familiar
or typical phoneme sequences attract attention. Other research
has demonstrated that infants and preschoolers learn phonotac-

This facilitatory effect of phonotactic legality and typical-

including iconic words—have advantages in processing [6].
The high proportion of iconic words found in child vocabu-
laries is reduced to a much smaller fraction in adult vocabularies
where abstract word forms dominate [19]. Despite this relative
reduction in the proportion of iconic words, adult language con-
tinues to contain a large number of words that are unusual or
atypical in their phonological structure—just like iconic words
are. And these surprising-sounding words are special in terms
of their processing.
Recent work [6] has demonstrated that words with unusual
word forms are processed more slowly, acquired at an older age,
and yet are more recognisable in a memory experiment, sug-
gesting deeper long-term encoding. Other work [8] has shown
that unusual word forms often refer to highly salient concepts,
suggesting that salient concepts are assigned surprising labels,
or that labels given to highly salient concepts are particularly
resistant to pressures to regularise unusual forms. Either way,
salient concepts receive extra attentional resources and deeper
processing. The contrast in AoA between abstract unusual word
forms and iconic word forms is consistent with bootstrapping
hypotheses [5] emphasising the importance of transparent form-
meaning mapping in the learning of iconic child vocabulary, and
likewise follows naturally from the laboratory-based research
findings reviewed above, demonstrating that infant and child
word-learning is easier for typical word forms.
The discrepancy in what is reported for child language
development—strong preference for iconic words and clear
learning advantages for phonotactically legal and typical word
forms—and adult language processing—memory and encoding
advantages for highly surprising (abstract) word-forms [6]—
raises the question of when the pattern of learning and pro-
cessing behavior changes. Logically, the most plausible (if un-
surprising) drivers of change is increased language experience
coupled with increased cognitive capacity (including increased
attentional resources). This would predict that information-
theoretical variability should be more strongly associated with
adult processing outcomes than child processing or word learn-
ing, as it is not the surprising information-theoretical profiles of
iconic words that make them special and frequent in child lan-
guage acquisition, but their affordance of easier form-meaning
mapping, facilitating associative learning. The present study
asks three questions:
1. Does the relative importance of information-theoretical mea-
sures differ across developmental and adult processing out-
comes (recognition memory, semantic decision, and lexical
decision)? We predict that information-theoretical measures
will contribute more strongly to processing than to AoA.
2. Does iconicity predict AoA across childhood? Here, we pre-
dict a negative iconicity effect that may be strongest for early-
acquired words.
3. Does average phonemic surprisal interact with AoA and
iconicity? Here, we predict a weaker and less consistent sur-
prisal effect on AoA compared to iconicity.
2. Method
2.1. Data Preparation
In order to analyze the effect of unusual word–shapes, including
iconic words, on word processing and language acquisition, we
rely on information-theoretic measures of phonemic surprisal
and entropy [20]. Phonemic surprisal captures how unexpected
each phoneme is given the preceding phoneme in the word. This
is a concept clearly linked to language-specific phonotactics and
the notion of typicality: words in a given language must obey
phonotactics, but some phonemes and phonotactically legal se-
quences of phonemes may still occur more often than others.
We also explore phonemic entropy, the uncertainty of the distri-
bution of possible upcoming phonemes.
Iconicity ratings were taken from [21], who provide crowd-
sourced iconicity scores for over 14,000 English words. AoA
norms were obtained from [22] (30,000 English words, adult
retrospective ratings) and [23] (44,000 English word meanings,
test-based norms). In addition, child-based age-of-acquisition
estimates were derived from the MacArthur–Bates Commu-
nicative Development Inventories (CDI) [24], which provide
parent-reported vocabulary norms for early lexical develop-
ment. We report combined results despite methodological dif-
ferences across the AoA sources (8–30 months for [24]; 5–13
years for [22, 23]), as results were consistent across both.
Recognition memory data were drawn from [25], [26], and
[27]. Semantic decision accuracy and reaction time were taken
from the Calgary Semantic Decision Project [28], and auditory
lexical decision accuracy and reaction time from the Massive
Auditory Lexical Decision database [29]. Word frequency [30],
concreteness [31], phonemic length, and morphemic length [32]
were included as lexical predictors.
Eight phoneme-level information-theoretical measures
were computed: surprisal and entropy at the first position, last
position, maximum across positions, and averaged across all
positions within each word. Information-theoretical measures
were calculated using the approximately 50 million word cor-
pus SUBTLEX-US [30], which was then cross-referenced with
the Carnegie Mellon University Pronouncing Dictionary [33] to
obtain phonemic transcriptions and frequencies based on stan-
dard American English pronunciation.
2.2. Analytical Approach
In this study, we explore whether iconicity and information ex-
pression, operationalised using various surprisal and entropy
measures, play a role in AoA. The first stage was to imple-
ment a series of extreme gradient-boosted machine learning
models (XGBoost; [34]). Dependent variables included AoA
[22], recognition memory [25, 26, 27], semantic decision accu-
racy and reaction time [28], and auditory lexical decision ac-
curacy and reaction time [29]. Predictors comprised iconicity,
frequency, concreteness, valence, phonemic length, morphemic
length, and the eight information-theoretical measures. We used
XGBoost as it is well-suited to handle collinearity among cor-
related predictors (e.g., the multiple surprisal and entropy mea-
sures). Each model was trained with 500 boosting rounds, a
learning rate of 0.05, and a maximum tree depth of 4, with sub-
sampling and column sampling set to 0.8 to reduce overfitting.
Feature importance was assessed using absolute SHAP values,
the proportion of model improvement attributable to splits on
each feature.

acquisition, we ran a series of sliding window regression mod-

3. Results
The feature importance XGBoost model scores are presented
in Figure 1. They show a clear picture of how information-
theoretical measures (surprisal and entropy) and lexical predic-
tors (frequency, concreteness, iconicity, valence, and length)
relate to AoA, recognition memory accuracy, semantic deci-
sion and lexical decision accuracy and response times. For
AoA, frequency, concreteness, and phonemic length dominated
feature importance, consistent with the established literature.
Information-theoretical measures, including average surprisal
and entropy variants, contributed relatively little impact on the
AoA model. By contrast, the models for recognition memory,
semantic decision, and auditory lexical decision showed sub-
stantially higher feature importance for information-theoretical
measures, particularly for average entropy and average sur-
prisal. This pattern is consistent with the interpretation that
phonological information structure is more important in adult
processing efficiency than AoA.

4. Discussion
The present paper explored whether iconicity and information
expression, operationalised using various surprisal and entropy
measures, play a role in language acquisition, through a se-
ries of analyses of the relationship between lexical predictors,
information-theoretical measures and AoA. Note that these re-
sults are correlational; longitudinal or experimental work is
needed to establish causality.
The results of the XGBoost feature-importance analyses,
sliding window regression, and the quartile regression model in-
dicate that information-theoretical and phonemic measures con-
tribute substantially to adult psycholinguistic processing out-
comes, including recognition memory, semantic decision, and
auditory lexical decision, but contribute very little to AoA.

Iconicity, by contrast, is a robust predictor of AoA and re-
tains explanatory power in adult processing models, suggesting
a dissociation between phonological unpredictability and form-
meaning mapping across the lifespan. The quartile analyses
further revealed that the iconicity effect is not uniform across
the acquisition continuum. The association between iconicity
and earlier AoA is significant in Q1 and weakens markedly in
Q4, where the interaction term is large and negative. This pat-
tern is consistent with the distributional structure of the English
lexicon: highly iconic words tend to be concentrated in early-
acquired vocabulary, so that the latest-acquired words are, on
average, substantially less iconic, and later-acquired vocabu-
lary is disproportionately arbitrary in form-meaning relation-
ship. This interpretation aligns with the bootstrapping account
proposed by [9] and [13]: early iconic word learning may lay
the groundwork for the acquisition of arbitrary form-meaning
pairings that characterise the later lexicon. The stability of the
iconicity effect across Q1 to Q3 suggests it is a relatively uni-
form predictor within early-to-middle-acquired vocabulary, and
the non-linear sharpening at Q4 is therefore a structural property
of the late lexicon rather than evidence for a developmental re-
versal. Average phonemic surprisal did not significantly predict
AoA as a main effect in the quartile interaction model, and the
significant Q4 interaction for surprisal (b= 0.062,p=.008) is
small in magnitude and in clear contrast to the iconicity interac-
tion in the same quartile: phonologically surprising sequences
are associated with later AoA, but this effect does not approach
the strength of the iconicity interaction.
Taken together, the results suggest that iconic words pro-
vide two distinct advantages that operate at different points in
development. Early in acquisition, iconicity facilitates word
learning by grounding new form-meaning pairings in embod-
ied, perceptual experience, allowing children to leverage sen-
sorimotor similarity between sound and referent [1, 3]. Later,
the phonological variability that co-occurs with iconicity may
support fine-grained phonological representations by exposing
learners to a wider range of phonological contrasts. The present
study thus demonstrates that iconicity robustly predicts AoA
and strengthens for late-acquired vocabulary, while phonolog-
ical surprisal and entropy contribute minimally to AoA but sub-
stantially to adult processing outcomes, pointing to dual advan-
tages of iconic words across development.
