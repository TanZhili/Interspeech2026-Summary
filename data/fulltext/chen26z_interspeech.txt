Abstract
Dialect speech embodies rich cultural and linguistic diver-
sity, yet building a unified text-to-speech (TTS) system for di-
alects remains challenging due to scarce data, inconsistent pho-
netic representations, and dialect interference in joint modeling.
We explore a unified dialect TTS pipeline based on a Diffusion
Transformer (DiT) architecture. A unified International Pho-
netic Alphabet (IPA) front-end is adopted to reduce pronuncia-
tion ambiguity across dialects. Built upon the F5-TTS frame-
work, we introduce a dialect-aware Mixture-of-Experts (MoE)
text encoder to mitigate style averaging in multidialect train-
ing. For low-resource dialect expansion, we employ parameter-
efficient fine-tuning (PEFT) to enable adaptation with only a
few hours of speech data. We further investigate incorporat-
ing high-quality dialect data into the unified model. Experi-
ments show that GRPO-based reinforcement learning reduces
the word error rate (WER) for both the target dialect and sev-
eral related dialects. 1
Index Terms: Text-to-speech synthesis, low-resource dialects,
Mixture-of-Experts, parameter-efficient fine-tuning, reinforce-

1. Introduction
Zero-shot text-to-speech (TTS) models have advanced rapidly
in recent years [1, 2, 3, 4, 5], demonstrating remarkable capabil-
ities in generating natural and expressive speech. Dialect TTS,
a vital extension of speech synthesis, is culturally and linguis-
tically significant across China’s diverse dialects and many re-
gional languages in Europe. However, building a unified TTS
system capable of modeling multiple dialects remains challeng-
ing. Dialect speech resources are typically scarce, heteroge-
neous, and often collected for automatic speech recognition
(ASR) rather than TTS. In addition, dialects exhibit substantial
phonetic variation and inconsistent orthographic conventions,
making pronunciation modeling and cross-dialect generaliza-
tion particularly difficult.
A major difficulty lies in the front-end representation.
Many existing dialect TTS systems rely on character or pinyin
inputs, which may lead to phonetic ambiguity when the same
character corresponds to different pronunciations across di-
alects. The International Phonetic Alphabet (IPA) provides a
linguistically grounded representation that can explicitly cap-
ture phonetic distinctions across languages and dialects [6]. A
unified IPA-based representation therefore offers a promising

Another challenge arises from joint modeling of multiple
dialects in a single acoustic model. When dialect data are
trained together without explicit structural guidance, the model
may suffer from style averaging or phonological interference,
weakening dialect-specific characteristics. Moreover, many di-
alects have extremely limited speech data, often only a few
hours, making it difficult to extend the system to new dialects
without overfitting or catastrophic forgetting.
To address these challenges, we explore a unified dialect
TTS pipeline built upon the F5-TTS architecture. We first
construct a unified phonetic front-end using the IPA, enabling
consistent pronunciation modeling across dialects. On top of
this representation, we introduce a dialect-aware Mixture-of-
Experts (MoE) module in the text encoder to capture dialect-
specific phonological variations while mitigating style interfer-
ence during multidialect training. To support the expansion
to low-resource dialects, we further adopt a parameter-efficient
fine-tuning (PEFT) [7] strategy that enables adaptation to new
dialects with only a few hours of speech data.
Beyond adapting to new dialects, another practical scenario
arises when high-quality speech data become available for an
existing dialect. Simply retraining the model with such data
may not always lead to consistent improvements across di-
alects. To investigate this issue, we further explore reinforce-
ment learning optimization based on Group Relative Policy Op-
timization (GRPO) [8], which encourages phonetic consistency
through reward-driven training. Experimental results show that
this approach can reduce the word error rate (WER) not only
for the target dialect but also for several related dialects.
Building upon the F5-TTS architecture, our contributions
are summarized as follows:
• Addressing pronunciation ambiguity: A unified IPA-based
front-end enabling consistent pronunciation modeling across
Chinese dialects;
• Mitigating style averaging in joint dialect training: A dialect-
aware Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) module in the text embedding
layer to mitigate cross-dialect style interference;
• Balancing low-resource expansion and high-quality data inte-
gration: A scalable adaptation strategy combining parameter-
efficient fine-tuning (PEFT) and GRPO-based reinforcement
learning for low-resource dialect expansion and high-quality
data integration.
We will open-source the dataset construction pipeline, mul-
tidialect dataset, and full training and inference scripts.

2. Related Work
2.1. IPA: A Standardized Phonetic Representation
IPA is a standardized phonetic system, enabling precise and un-
ambiguous representation of human speech. In early TTS sys-
tems, IPA plays a crucial role [9], offering accurate phonetic
guidance and reducing mispronunciations. Its consistency also
makes it essential for building unified multilingual TTS models
[10, 11]. As shown in the figure 1, we have built a unified dialect
front-end system based on IPA. Different types of dialect share
a set of identifiers, providing a prerequisite for the expansion of
low-resource dialect TTS.
2.2. F5-TTS: A Lightweight and Efficient High-Quality
TTS Model
F5-TTS [12] is a fully non-autoregressive TTS model built upon
Optimal Transport Conditional Flow Matching(OT-CFM)
[13] and aDiffusion Transformer(DiT) backbone [14]. It syn-
thesizes speech in the reference speaker’s voice given a refer-
ence audio, its transcript, and target text. The model encodes
text using ConvNeXt V2 blocks [15], conditions on masked
Mel-spectrograms and flow-matching latents, and employs DiT
layers to progressively denoise latent spectrograms, effectively
capturing long-range prosody and speech structure.
3. Approach
3.1. Overview
We propose a multi-stage training strategy that progressively
extends the model from Mandarin to multidialect speech and
finally to low-resource novel dialects. The proposed framework
is trained in four stages.
Stage 0: Initialization with F5-TTS checkpoint
The training starts from a pre-trained F5-TTS checkpoint,
trained on large-scale Mandarin and English data. This provides
a strong prior for high-quality speech generation and serves as
the foundation for downstream adaptation.
Stage 1 & 2: Joint Training on Multidialect IPA Data
In both Stage 1 and Stage 2, the model is trained on a uni-
fied dataset of IPA-aligned text-speech pairs covering Standard
Mandarin and multiple Chinese dialects. It is designed to align

Stage 3: Adaptation to Low-Resource Dialects
Building upon the multidialect-capable model, Stage 3 per-

a parameter-efficient fine-tuning (PEFT) approach [19] in which
only LoRA and Conditioning Adapters are trained, while the
backbone model remains frozen. Furthermore, data augmen-
tation methods are also introduced to expand the small training
set. This enables speech synthesis with only a few hours of data,
preserving multilingual knowledge and mitigating catastrophic
forgetting.
This staged training paradigm effectively balances model
capacity, data efficiency, and generalization, enabling rapid ex-
pansion to new dialects in zero-shot or few-shot speech syn-
thesis. To further investigate how high-quality dialect data can
be incorporated into the unified model, we additionally explore
reward-driven optimization based on reinforcement learning.

3.3. Low-resource Dialect Adaptation Method
Due to the scarcity of high-quality speech data for many di-
alects—often limited to less than ten hours—we further inves-
tigate the generalization of our framework to extremely low-
resource dialects. Inspired by [19], We incorporate a Condition-
ing Adapter into the text embedding module and apply LoRA
to the query and value projections within the attention layers of
the input embedding and DiT blocks. The MoE structure is kept
frozen during fine-tuning to preserve its learned routing behav-
ior and avoid overfitting under limited data.
We also use acoustic data augmentation techniques to ex-
pand the limited training data of the new dialect without altering
its dialectal style. Specifically, for each original utterance, we
apply pitch and time-scale modifications with factors of 0.85,
0.9, 0.95, 1.05, 1.1, and 1.15. By combining with this simple
and effective augmentation method, the zero-shot speech syn-
thesis capability of this model can be extended to a new dialect
without affecting the original dialect synthesis ability.

4. Experiments
4.1. Data
We utilize the Common V oice Cantonese dataset [25], the
Emilia Mandarin dataset [26], dialectal data from the KeSpeech
corpus [27] and an open-source Sourthern Min dataset [28].
In addition, we incorporate commercially acquired speech data
in the Shanghai and Tianjin dialects to broaden the coverage
of dialect. For low-resource fine-tuning, we employ approxi-
mately 3 hours of professionally recorded Peking Opera recita-
tion, covering both Jingbai and Yunbai styles, along with an
equal amount of Nanjing dialect data for comparison. Com-
pared to commercial systems trained on hundreds of thousands
of hours (e.g., CosyV oice2∼150k hrs, Qwen-TTS∼3,000k
hrs), our unified model is trained on approximately 0.7k hours
of Mandarin and 0.4k hours of dialect speech, demonstrating
strong data efficiency.
4.2. Implementation Details
We use the AdamW optimizer for the model in all experiments.
In stages 1 and 2, a peak learning rate of 7.5e-5 is employed,
with a warm-up of 2k steps and a linearly decayed learning rate
setting over 200K total training steps. The batch size is set to
28k frames per GPU. In Stage 3, only the LoRA (rank=16,α=1)
and Conditioning Adapters are updated, while the main model
is frozen. We trained the model for 100K steps with a learning
rate of1×10 −5.
For Flow-GRPO, we use group size 8, effective group num-
ber 12, KL weight 0.1, noise levelα=0.4, an SDE window of
steps 2–6 (window size 2), 10 diffusion sampling steps, and
learning rate1×10−7.
4.3. Metrics
We adopt a combined objective and subjective evaluation
framework. For objective evaluation, we report WER using
Qwen3-ASR [29] as the evaluation ASR model; and UTMOSv2
[30], a learned metric for predicting speech naturalness. For
subjective evaluation, we conduct human listening tests using
mean opinion score (MOS). Five native speakers are recruited
per dialect as raters. For the two Peking Opera varieties (Jingbai
and Yunbai), evaluation is conducted by a panel of linguistics
experts and Peking Opera enthusiasts in place of native dialect
speakers, given the specialized nature of this register.

4.4.1. Comparison with Existing Methods
This evaluation covers both objective metrics and subjective
listening tests, comparing our system against several state-
of-the-art commercial TTS models including Edge TTS [22],
CosyV oice2 [23], and Qwen-TTS [24].
Tables 1 shows our method’s performance across all di-
alects, including low-resource varieties like Peking Opera (Jing-
bai and Yunbai) and Nanjing. Our approach effectively learns
from limited data via continual learning, whereas direct fine-
tuning leads to significant forgetting of prior knowledge.
While our system shows higher WER than commercial
baselines on shared dialects, this gap is largely attributable to
training data scale — commercial systems use up to 3,000k
hours versus our 1.1k hours — and to our zero-shot voice
cloning evaluation condition. The elevated WER for Southern
Min (74.39%) and Yunbai (61.30%) reflects their phonological
distance from Mandarin and stylized prosodic nature respec-
tively; notably, both varieties are entirely absent from all com-
pared commercial systems.
4.4.2. Ablation Study
To demonstrate the superiority of our method, we conduct two
ablation studies on four dialects. Table 2 presents two ablation
studies on four dialects. Removing MoE consistently degrades
both MOS and WER, while replacing IPA with pinyin causes
catastrophic performance collapse (WER>90%), confirming
the necessity of both components. We compare IPA against
pinyin because the original F5-TTS uses pinyin.
4.4.3. Optimization with High-Quality Dialect Data
We further investigate how additional high-quality dialect data
can be used to improve the unified multidialect model. The first
approach directly incorporates the high-quality data through
continued training of the base model. The second approach
applies the Flow-GRPO optimization described in Section 3.4,
where the model is optimized using ASR-based rewards while
keeping the front-end encoders frozen and updating only the
DiT backbone.
The quantitative results of these two approaches are pre-
sented in Table 3. As shown in the table, reinforcement learn-
ing based optimization provides more consistent improvements
compared with direct continued training, indicating that reward-
driven learning can better leverage high-quality dialect speech
data.
5. Conclusion
We introduce DiaMoE-TTS, a unified multidialect TTS system
based on F5-TTS. It uses a shared IPA front-end for consistent
phonetics, a dialect-aware MoE to reduce style interference, and
PEFT for few-hour adaptation. Experiments show Flow-GRPO
outperforms continued training in leveraging high-quality di-
alect data, lowering WER for both target and related dialects.
