Abstract
The proliferation of audio-visual deepfake technology poses a
threat to social trust. Approaches relying on globally pooled
multimodal features can dilute transient forgery traces (e.g.,
lip-sync misalignments). We propose MS-GNN, a multi-scale
graph neural network for audio-visual deepfake detection. MS-
GNN partitions the audio-visual stream into windows to capture
local traces. We use a multi-scale graph: a bottom-up path-
way aggregates window features into segment-level semantics,
while a top-down pathway propagates global context to refine
local evidence. We obtain a global video representation by ag-
gregating window features, and train with window-level (auxil-
iary) and video-level (main) supervision. Experiments on LA V-
DF and FakeA VCeleb show that MS-GNN improves deepfake
detection, particularly for videos with localized forgery traces.

1. Introduction
Advanced generative models, including GANs [1], V AEs [2],
diffusion models [3], and flow matching [4], have substantially
reduced the cost of producing realistic synthetic media, accel-
erating the spread of audio-visual deepfakes and undermining
information integrity and social trust [5]. In practice, manipu-
lations are often confined to short temporal windows. As Fig. 1
illustrates, replacing only a brief spoken phrase can reverse the
conveyed message while leaving most audio-visual content au-
thentic. Such local forgery traces are easily diluted by global
representations, motivating detectors that preserve fine-grained
evidence while leveraging global context.
Early deepfake detectors relied on uni-modal cues, such as
visual artifacts [6, 7] or audio anomalies [8], but often general-
ized poorly due to the lack of cross-modal corroboration. This
limitation spurred multi-modal detectors that explicitly model
audio-visual consistency. For example, Chugh et al. [9] mea-
sure cross-modal discrepancies, while others adopt contrastive
alignment [10] or direct feature fusion [11, 12]. While effec-
tive, these methods typically make global decisions after pool-

Recent work has explored alternative detection paradigms.
For instance, DiMoDif [14] exploits high-level semantic dis-
crepancies between audio and visual discourse, while Ref-
eree [15] uses external reference clips to detect identity incon-
sistencies. However, these baselines rely on semantic alignment
or auxiliary reference data, and may miss subtle, non-semantic
artifacts in reference-free settings. Moreover, regularization-
based methods (e.g., Zou et al. [16]) often handle temporal scale
implicitly via global pooling, without an explicit hierarchy for
fine-grained temporal modeling. This motivates a structure-
aware design that can detect localized inconsistencies without
external references.
To this end, we propose MS-GNN (Multi-Scale Graph
Neural Network), designed to detect localized tampering in
audio-visual streams. MS-GNN partitions the input into non-
overlapping windows and performs bi-directional multi-scale
aggregation and context-guided refinement: bottom-up aggre-
gation builds segment-level context, while top-down propaga-
tion injects this context back to refine window representations.

main video-level objective, improving sensitivity to localized

2. Method
Given an audio-visual stream, MS-GNN detects local forgeries
by modeling dependencies across multiple temporal scales. It
consists of three steps: (i) constructing graph-based representa-
tions for windows, (ii) refining them through stacked MS-GNN
layers, and (iii) aggregating the final window features for video-
level prediction under multi-task supervision.

We first partition the input video into non-overlapping windows.
For each windowi, we extract visual (v i ∈R Dv ) and audio
(ai∈R Da) features and concatenate them with a temporal en-
coding (TE) derived from the window’s timestamp. These aug-
mented features are processed by modality-specific encoders to

3. Experiments
3.1. Datasets
We evaluate our model on two datasets. To assess sensitiv-
ity to local inconsistencies, we useLA V-DF[13], which con-
tains numerous partially forged samples. To assess behav-
ior on globally manipulated videos, we use the large-scale
FakeA VCeleb[17], adopting the five-fold cross-validation
setup from Zou et al. [16].
3.2. Implementation and Metrics
Our model is trained for 30 epochs using the AdamW optimizer
with a learning rate of1×10−4, a batch size of 256, CosineAn-
nealingLR scheduling, and early stopping. We extract 768-
dimensional features from TimeSformer [18] and WavLM [19]
using 400 ms non-overlapping windows. The MS-GNN uses
3 layers (hidden sizes [512, 256, 64]), a segment size of 8
with 50% overlap, and loss weights ofλvid=0.9andλ win=0.1
(Eq. 5).
3.3. Results
3.3.1. Performance on LAV-DF
On LA V-DF, which is rich in local forgeries, MS-GNN im-
proves over the compared baselines. As shown in Table 1, our
method achieves 98.12% ACC and 99.81% AUC, outperform-
ing prior methods. This result indicates that our multi-scale
architecture captures local inconsistencies that are missed by
global pooling strategies.

3.4. Ablation Study
To validate our key components, we conducted an ablation
study on LA V-DF (Table 3). The baseline achieves 91.80%
ACC (Row 1). Window interaction and hierarchical fusion
independently improve performance (Rows 2–4), with CLS-
based aggregation yielding better performance than mean pool-
ing. Combining both modules yields a +4.76% ACC gain
(Row 5). The full model incorporating all components achieves
the highest performance, with a +6.32% ACC improvement
over the baseline (Row 10), demonstrating each module’s con-
tribution. Rows 9 and 10 show that MS-GNN does not
strictly require window-level supervision for detection: the
multi-scale architecture alone captures local inconsistencies,
achieving 98.01% ACC. Incorporating window-level supervi-
sion nonetheless yields a marginal further improvement and
provides window-level evidence for interpreting suspicious seg-
ments.

3.6. Hyperparameter Analysis: Window Duration and Seg-
ment Size
Fig. 4(a) illustrates how different segment sizes induce differ-
ent aggregation hierarchies, while Fig. 4(b) summarizes their
impact on performance. Our parameter choices are directly
grounded in the physical and statistical properties of localized
manipulations.
First, the400ms window aligns with the natural duration
of a spoken word, matching the word-level tampering typical
in LA V-DF [13]. Defining windows by absolute time rather
than frame counts also makes feature extraction less sensitive
to varying video resolutions and frame rates.
Second, as shown in Fig. 4(b), performance peaks at
Ssize=8, corresponding to a3.2s receptive field. Since LA V-
DF fake segments average0.65s and max out at1.6s [13], this
3.2s segment provides sufficient context to cover local anoma-
lies alongside authentic content for reliable audio-visual con-
trast. Smaller segments lack sufficient reference context, while
larger segments withS size≥12may dilute subtle forgery fea-
tures into the dominant authentic background. The degradation

4. Conclusions
We introduced MS-GNN, a multi-scale graph neural network
for detecting localized audio-visual forgeries by linking fine-
grained local evidence with global context. Experiments on
LA V-DF and FakeA VCeleb demonstrate improved video-level
detection, especially for localized manipulations, while future
work will explore adaptive segmentation and more efficient fea-
ture extraction.
