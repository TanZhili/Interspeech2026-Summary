Mixture-of-Accent-Adapters for Robust ASR: Injecting Accent Cues into
Pretrained Whisper

Abstract
Accented speech remains a key challenge for inclusive ASR, yet
adapting large pretrained models often relies on costly full fine-
tuning. Moreover, previous methods lack integration of accent-
specific adapters, controlled encoder-level accent-cue injection,
and multitask supervision for specialization regulation and gen-
der information leakage mitigation. We propose Mixture-of-
Accent-Adapters (MoAA), a novel adaptation framework for
Whisper that enables on-demand specialization while default-
ing to the frozen backbone when unnecessary. A pooled-state
control bottleneck estimates accentedness, guiding accent-cue
injection via weighted retrieval from a learnable soft accent
codebook and routing lightweight accent-specialist adapters,
while calibrating a gated blend with the backbone. An adversar-
ial gender head reduces leakage, and reference-free hallucina-
tion suppression mitigates rare decoding artifacts. On AESRC
benchmark, MoAA achieves 7.49% WER and 3.81% CER.
Index Terms: Accent-Robust ASR, Adapter Routing, Soft
Codebook Conditioning, Hallucination Suppression
1. Introduction
Accented automatic speech recognition (ASR) poses a barrier
to speech technologies. Although modern end-to-end systems
can achieve strong accuracy on well-represented varieties of En-
glish, their error rates often increase markedly when pronunci-
ation patterns shift due to regional or non-native accents. As
ASR systems are increasingly built on large pretrained encoder-
decoder or self-supervised backbones, an enduring challenge is
adapting model capacity to preserve accent-robust recognition
without maintaining a separate full model per accent.
A substantial body of work has investigated special-
ization and routing mechanisms as strategies for mitigat-
ing accent-induced variability. Early studies on multi-dialect
training demonstrated that carefully structured learning ob-
jectives can disentangle pronunciation-related representations
from grapheme prediction [1]. Subsequent mixture-based meth-
ods introduced explicit accent- or attribute-conditioned trans-
formations, using auxiliary predictors to generate gating signals
that combine experts specialized for accent variability [2]. More
broadly, dynamic routing and mixture-of-experts architectures
have been scaled to large acoustic models, with particular at-
tention to routing stability and effective expert utilization [3–5].
Taken together, these findings suggest that accent variability is
not well captured by a single uniform representation, whereas
conditional computation yields improved robustness.
In parallel, existing research suggests that parameter-
efficient adaptation is a viable alternative to full fine-tuning.

is adapted through compact trainable modules, which can be

Another approach employs multi-task learning to integrate
accent features into ASR objectives. Prior work indicates that
accent embeddings and auxiliary accent-recognition objectives
can enhance recognition in accented settings [2, 13]. Moreover,
joint modeling approaches may exploit accent identification to
drive specialization [14]. More recent multi-task formulations
emphasize both opportunities and pitfalls, showing that auxil-
iary accent recognition can strengthen accent modeling yet in-
duce negative transfer to ASR when the objectives are not care-
fully balanced [15], thereby motivating more structured designs
that decouple and subsequently re-couple accent and speech
modeling to improve recognition [16]. In contrast, adversarial
domain-invariant learning suppresses accent cues in intermedi-
ate representations via gradient reversal, often with relabeling
strategies to better handle unseen accents [17–20]. Further-
more, memory-oriented approaches incorporate accent-specific
prototypes or codebooks that the model can attend to during en-
coding, such as search-based selection [21, 22] or frame-level
accent cue derivation to adjust accent bias [23].
Despite substantial progress, several gaps remain. First,
accent awareness is often treated as always-on conditioning
[6, 23, 24] or always-off invariance [17, 19], whereas real-world
speech requires selective use of accent-specialized capacity.
Second, routing and multi-task supervision are frequently stud-
ied in isolation, as auxiliary heads can provide supervision yet
may induce negative transfer [15], while routing can be unsta-
ble without a gating control [3, 21]. Third, robustness is rarely

routing. Multitask heads provide structured utterance-level sig-

soft codebook retrieval transforms these predictions into con-

routing uses the resulting conditioned representation to allocate
specialized capacity only when needed.
To address these issues, we propose Mixture-of-Accent-
Adapters (MoAA), a unified framework that combines soft ac-
cent cue injection, accentedness-gated adapter routing, and ad-
versarial multitask supervision in a frozen Whisper-small back-
bone (Figure 1). MoAA uses a pooled-state control bottleneck
to support auxiliary prediction, soft codebook retrieval, and sta-
ble routing. The predicted accentedness score jointly governs
accent-cue injection and interpolation between the frozen back-
bone and routed adapter outputs, enabling on-demand special-
ization without requiring ground-truth accent labels at inference
time. In addition, we apply gradient reversal layer (GRL)-based
supervision to reduce gender leakage in the shared control rep-
resentation, and we complement the model with a lightweight
deterministic module to suppress rare hallucination errors.
The key contributions of this work are as follows:
• We introduce a router-controlled bank of bottleneck adapters
and a learnable soft accent codebook with posterior-weighted
retrieval, thereby injecting accent cues into Whisper without
requiring any ground-truth accent labels at inference.
• We integrate multi-task supervision with GRL-based mitiga-
tion of gender information leakage while supporting accent-
robust recognition. Moreover, accentedness-gated routing
enables on-demand accent specialization.
• We validate the effectiveness of our method by comparing it
against full fine-tuning, LoRA, and single-adapter baselines.
We further mitigate rare insertion-burst decoding failures us-
ing a lightweight reference-free post-decoding module, de-
terministic hallucination filtering (DHF).
2. Method
2.1. Overview and backbone
Our goal is to allocate adaptation capacity only when accent-
induced variability is likely to harm recognition. MoAA
achieves this by integrating (i) a compact shared representation
with multitask supervision to stabilize conditional computation,
(ii) accent cue injection through a learnable soft codebook with-
out requiring ground-truth accent labels at test time, and (iii)
accentedness-gated routing over lightweight adapters, while re-
taining the pretrained backbone as a reliable default. Moreover,
we incorporate adversarial supervision to reduce gender infor-
mation leakage in the shared representation.

model pretrained on large-scale multilingual speech. Given log-

2.5. Deterministic hallucination filtering (DHF)
DHF first normalizes and tokenizes each hypothesis into alpha-
betic, numeric, and ordinal units. For hypotheses flagged as
suspicious, it generates a small set of deterministic candidate
cleanups by removing noise tokens, compressing consecutive
repetitions, compressing repeated multi-token blocks, truncat-
ing at the second occurrence of repeated longern-grams, and
cutting number-dominated tails. Extremely apostrophe-heavy
garbage strings are mapped to an empty output. A reference-
free quality score then ranks the original and cleaned candi-
dates by penalizing repetition, low lexical diversity, spam-like
tokens, excessive numeric content, and numeric-only outputs,
and replacement is made only when the cleaned candidate is
clearly better under a score-based criterion. This deterministic
post-decoding step targets pathological repetition and numeric
hallucination artifacts while preserving normal ASR behavior.
3. Experimental setup
3.1. Datasets
We train on a mixture of AESRC [27] training data and Lib-
riSpeech train-clean-100 [28]. AESRC provides≈200 hours of
English speech spanning 10 L1/L2 accents, while LibriSpeech
is used as a proxy for unaccented speech. Evaluation is per-
formed on the AESRC test set, which contains≈20 hours of
speech drawn from the same accent pool as the training data.
3.2. Baselines
We consider the following four baselines:
(a)Zero-Shot, using Whisper-small without any adaptation.
(b)Full Fine-Tuning, updating all parameters during training.
(c)LoRA, a parameter-efficient baseline that trains low-rank
updates while keeping the backbone largely fixed [29].
(d)Single Adapter, a non-routed variant of MoAA with a sin-
gle shared bottleneck adapter.
3.3. Training details and evaluation
We use Whisper-small for all experiments and baselines to en-
sure a controlled and practically relevant evaluation setting. We
train a pooled-state projection, multitask heads with GRL-based
gender nuisance control (λ=1.0), accent prototypes, a condi-
tioning projection, and a router overNbottleneck adapters
(N=10,r=192), with loss weights(w asr,w ad,w ac,w ge) =
(1,1,2,1). Training runs for 10 epochs with batch size 16,
learning rate5×10 −4, warmup ratio 0.05, weight decay 0.01,
and FP16. We evaluate ASR performance using WER and CER.
Our code is publicly available at GitHub repository.

4.4. Analysis
Performance is strong, reaching 7.49 WER and 3.82 CER.
Residual errors are dominated by substitutions (77.6%), with
smaller insertion (12.4%) and deletion (10.0%) contributions,
indicating that the system generally preserves utterance struc-
ture and fails mainly through localized lexical mismatches.
The per-utterance WER distribution is highly skewed (median
0.000, mean 0.083), showing that most test utterances are tran-
scribed perfectly. This pattern is consistent with the core con-
tribution of MoAA under accent variability. Rather than rely-
ing on generic adaptation, MoAA performs accent-conditioned
routing over lightweight adapters within frozen adaptation, en-
abling selective specialization within a frozen Whisper back-
bone. Its improvement over a single shared adapter suggests
that the gain comes not merely from
