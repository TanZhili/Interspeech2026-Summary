Abstract
Backchannel timing prediction is challenging because re-
sponses occur at varying latencies relative to Backchannel Op-
portunity Points (BOPs). Dual-Process Theory motivates test-
ing whether backchannel fast/slow functional categories exhibit
distinct BOP-relative latency distributions; where BOP-relative
latency is the signed interval between a BOP and an annotated
backchannel onset. Using K-MIND, a 115-hour Korean dyadic
corpus, Cox Proportional Hazards analysis confirms significant
differences in BOP-relative latency distributions across four
functional categories (allp < .001), supporting a DPT-based
fast group/slow group distinction. Based on this result, the
Dual-Path Backchannel Timing Prediction Model (DP-BCT)
decouples the fast/slow groups into the Fast Path and Slow Path
for frame-level onset and functional category prediction. DP-
BCT improves backchannel timing prediction accuracy over
SP-BCT, a single-path baseline, raising Macro-F1 from 0.4862
to 0.6254.
Index Terms: backchanneling, dual-process theory, turn-
taking, latency variation, voice activity projection, BOP, multi-
task learning
1. Introduction
Backchannel timing prediction requires estimating when lis-
tener responses occur relative to conversational opportunities.
Backchannels are listener feedback signals such as continuers
and acknowledgments, and their timing is central to interac-
tional synchrony in spoken dialogue systems [1, 2, 3]. In con-
versation analysis, moments at which backchannels become rel-
evant are referred to as Backchannel Relevance Places (BRPs)
[4, 5, 6]. For computational measurement, this study opera-
tionalizes backchannel-relevant moments as Backchannel Op-
portunity Points (BOPs), which serve as observable tempo-
ral anchors for backchannel timing. We defineBOP-relative
latencyas the signed interval between a BOP and an an-
notated backchannel onset.Functional categories, defined
as communicative-function labels assigned to backchannel in-
stances, are used to analyze BOP-relative latency. Using BOP-
relative latency and functional categories, this study analyzes
backchannel timing and develops a prediction model that incor-
porates category-specific timing patterns.
Dual-Process Theory (DPT) provides an explanatory lens
for relating functional categories of backchannels to possi-
ble differences in BOP-relative latency [7, 8]. DPT distin-
guishes Type 1 processing, associated with fast and automatic
responses, from Type 2 processing, associated with slower and
more integrative responses. In this study, the Type 1/Type 2
distinction motivates a testable DPT-based grouping of func-
tional categories: categories corresponding to simple continuers
are assigned to afast group, whereas categories corresponding
to understanding-, assessment-, and affect-related feedback are
assigned to aslow group. If functional categories exhibit dis-
tinct latency profiles, the resulting patterns can be interpreted
through DPT as behavioral timing correlates of processing de-
mand, not as direct measurements of cognitive processing time.
The DPT-based grouping suggests a potential limitation of
existing backchannel timing models. Prior work often opti-
mizes multiple functional categories within a single shared path
[9]. A single-path formulation may be sufficient when func-
tional categories share similar latency profiles, but may be less
suitable if functional categories exhibit distinct BOP-relative la-
tency profiles. When latency profiles differ across categories,
learning thefast groupandslow groupthrough the same path
can introduce optimization interference, analogous to interfer-
ence observed in multi-task learning [10]. We denote the single-
path baseline as the Single-Path Backchannel Timing Prediction
Model (SP-BCT).
Motivated by the shared-path limitation, we propose the
Dual-Path Backchannel Timing Prediction Model (DP-BCT),
which structurally separates thefast groupandslow group. The
model assigns categories in thefast groupto aFast Pathand
categories in theslow groupto aSlow Path. The dual-path de-
sign provides an inductive bias for modeling category-specific
BOP-relative latency profiles while preserving a unified predic-
tion objective.
The study addresses two research questions:RQ1(Empiri-
cal Analysis): Do functional categories exhibit statistically dis-
tinct BOP-relative latency distributions?RQ2(Experimental
Study): Does structurally decoupling thefast groupandslow
groupimprove backchannel timing prediction accuracy?
The main contributions are two-fold. First, we identify
statistically distinct BOP-relative latency distributions across
functional categories in 115 hours of Korean dyadic dialogue.
Cox PH analysis shows lower hazards for theslow-groupcat-
egories than thefast-groupcategory (HRs=0.71–0.85; all (p <
.001)), supporting the DPT-basedfast group/slow groupdistinc-
tion. Second, we introduce the Dual-Path Backchannel Tim-
ing Prediction Model (DP-BCT), which assigns the two groups
to theFast PathandSlow Path, and show that decoupling
the fast/slow groups improves frame-level onset and category
prediction over SP-BCT, increasing Macro-F1 from 0.4862 to
0.6254 (∆=+0.1392, +28.6% relative).

3. Proposed Model: DP-BCT
Based on the empirical findings in Section 2, DP-BCT performs
frame-level prediction of (i) backchannel timing and (ii) func-
tional categories by assigning the DPT-basedfast groupand
slow groupto two paths within theDual-Path Module: the
Fast Pathand theSlow Path. Following V AP (V oice Activ-
ity Projection)-style work that adapts speech representations to
voice-activity and turn-taking structure before downstream tim-
ing prediction [17, 9], DP-BCT uses a two-stage training design
with a V AD-tuned HuBERT encoder. As illustrated in Fig. 2,
the architecture consists of a shared HuBERT encoder, V AD
and BOP heads, a dual-path module, and a frame-level BC pre-
dictor.
3.1. Model Architecture
HuBERT Encoder.DP-BCT uses a single-channel (mono)
audio stream, unlike the original V AP [17] formulation
with separate streams for each speaker. Given a 20-
s mono audio segment, a pretrained Korean HuBERT
model (team-lucid/hubert-base-korean) 2 extracts
768-dimensional frame-level acoustic representations at 50 Hz
(20 ms per frame), yielding approximately 1000 frames [18].
For downstream prediction, DP-BCT selects the last 100 Hu-
BERT frames, corresponding to a 2-s output window, and uses
them as context features for the downstream modules. Thus,
the downstream input has shapeB×100×768, whereBis
the batch size. Shorter segments are padded and masked so that
padding does not affect transformer computation.
V AD Head.The V AD Head is attached to the HuBERT encoder
only for V AD-oriented encoder adaptation. It produces frame-
level V AD logits from HuBERT features and is supervised by
speaker activity annotations. These annotations are used only
during encoder fine-tuning and are not provided as oracle inputs
2https://huggingface.co/team-lucid/hubert-base-korean
during downstream backchannel timing prediction.
BOP Head.TheBOP Headoperates on the shared HuBERT
context features and predicts frame-level BOP logits using a
two-layer MLP trained with binary cross-entropy. Unlike the
silence-based BOP anchor used for empirical analysis (Sec-
tion 2.2), these logits are learned timing-related signals rather
than hard gates; they provide additional supervision highlight-
ing regions where backchannels are likely to occur.
Dual-Path Module.TheDual-Path Moduleprocesses Hu-
BERT context features through two parallel transformer paths:
theFast Pathand theSlow Path. TheFast Pathtargets
backchannels in thefast group, such asCP. It consists of a lin-
ear projection followed by a 4-layerTransformerEncoder
with eight attention heads and an auxiliary frame-level binary
classifier detecting backchannels in thefast group. TheSlow
Pathmodels backchannels in theslow group, namelyCPU,A,
andE. It mirrors theFast Patharchitecture but uses a separate
auxiliary objective for backchannels in theslow group, enabling
the path to learn broader temporal dependencies.
Frame-level BC Predictor.Fast-path features, slow-path fea-
tures, and BOP logits are fused and passed to the frame-level
BC predictor. The predictor operates over the 100-frame out-
put window and produces BC logits over five classes:Neg(no
backchannel),CP,CPU,A, andE. Thus, the final BC logits
have shape (B×100×5), where B is the batch size. The BOP
Head produces frame-level BOP logits over the same 100-frame
window. During inference, the predicted onset corresponds to
the first frame classified as a non-Neglabel within the evalua-
tion window associated with each BOP.
Training Procedure.Training proceeds in two stages. In
Stage I, the HuBERT encoder is fine-tuned through the V AD
Head using speaker activity supervision. In Stage II, the
V AD-tuned HuBERT encoder is frozen, and the downstream
modules—theBOP Head,Dual-Path Module, and frame-level
BC predictor—are optimized for backchannel timing and func-
tional category prediction. Training combines the main multi-
class objective with auxiliary binary losses from the paths and
BOP Head, encouraging path specialization while maintaining
a unified decision boundary.
4. Experimental Study: Dual-Path
Modeling
This section addresses RQ2 by evaluating whether structurally
decoupling thefast groupandslow groupimproves backchannel
timing prediction.
4.1. Experimental Setup and Training
Experimental Setup.All models are trained with AdamW
(initial learning rate2×10 −5, weight decay10 −4) for up to
100 epochs on NVIDIA A100 GPUs using PyTorch Lightning.
Model selection uses validation Macro-F1 with ReduceLROn-
Plateau (factor = 0.5, patience = 5) and early stopping (patience
8, minimum improvement5×10 −4). All experiments are re-
peated over five seeds (40–44), and results are reported as mean
±standard deviation.
Training Objective and Optimization.The objective is a
weighted sum of the main classification loss and three auxil-
iary losses (Fast Path,Slow Path, andBOP Head). To address
sparse backchannel events, the main loss uses Focal Loss [19]
withγ= 2.0andα= 0.25, prioritizing hard-to-classify pos-
itive samples. Auxiliary tasks use binary cross-entropy with
logits, with each loss weighted by hyperparameters (λ).

Evaluation Metrics.Performance is evaluated using Macro-

gory prediction. Macro-F1 and Accuracy measure category pre-
diction performance over the frame-level labels, while Hit@k
quantifies onset timing precision by counting a predicted on-
set as correct if it occurs within±kmilliseconds around the
ground-truth onset. We evaluate 50 ms, 100 ms, and 200 ms
windows to capture onset timing robustness at different granu-
larities and analyze path behavior.
Model Configurations.Three variants are compared: (1)SP-
BCT, an 8-layer transformer predicting all functional categories
in one path; (2)DP-BCT (Unified), a two-branch model where
both paths learn all categories; and (3)DP-BCT (Proposed),
where theFast Pathmodels CP and theSlow Pathhandles CPU,
A, and E. All variants share the same Stage I V AD-oriented
encoder training and Stage II BOP supervision; only the path
structure and category-to-path assignment differ. This isolates
the effect of dual-path specialization.

5. Discussion and Conclusion
Together, the results answer both research questions. For RQ1,
functional categories exhibit distinct BOP-relative latency dis-
tributions. For RQ2, DP-BCT outperforms the single-path
SP-BCT baseline, with the proposed category-to-path mapping
achieving the strongest performance. Consistent with the view
that timing is central to turn-taking [1], these findings sug-
gest that backchannel timing is better modeled as a category-
sensitive prediction problem. By separating thefast groupand
slow group, DP-BCT provides an inductive bias for frame-level
backchannel prediction, particularly for onset timing precision
and category prediction performance.
An additional observation emerges for Assessment (A)
backchannels. Although assigned to theslow group, their em-
pirical latency (295 ms) is shorter than other categories in the
slow group, such as CPU (376 ms) and E (347 ms). Rather than
constituting a strict anomaly, this pattern may reflect sociolin-
guistic conventions in which rapid assessment or agreement-
like signals function as pragmatic markers of conversational
alignment. In this sense, response timing may be shaped not
only by processing complexity but also by social interaction
norms [20, 21]. Such effects highlight that latency patterns
should be interpreted as behavioral regularities rather than di-
rect cognitive measurements.
Several limitations remain. The experiments focus on a Ko-
rean dataset, and backchannel timing can vary across languages
and cultures; therefore, cross-lingual generalization remains an
important direction for future validation. The present model
also relies on acoustic cues alone, whereas real conversational
feedback often involves multimodal signals such as gaze and
gesture. In addition, the current work relies on fixed tempo-
ral thresholds for BOP extraction and timing evaluation, which
may not fully capture the variability of conversational contexts.
Future work will therefore explore multimodal integration and
adaptive timing mechanisms that incorporate visual and contex-
tual information.
