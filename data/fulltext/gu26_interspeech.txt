Mutual Cancellation between Masking Effects Benefits Speech Intelligibility
Yixin Gu1, Yan Tang1,2
1Department of Linguistics, University of Illinois Urbana-Champaign, USA
2Beckman Institute for Advanced Science and Technology, Urbana, USA
{yixingu2, yty}@illinois.edu
Abstract
Informational masking (IM) induced by competing speech
impairs listeners’ ability to understand target speech. This in-
terference is strongest when the target and masker share sim-
ilar acoustic and linguistic features. Intelligibility also suffers
from modulation masking (MM), where the masker’s modula-
tion disrupts that of the target in multiple domains during au-
ditory processing. In this study, energetic masking (EM) was
constrained using an intelligibility model by equalizing high-
energy glimpses. English sentences were masked by competing
speech in English, Mandarin, or modulation-controlled noise.
Word recognition results showed that listener performance was
a consequence of IM release benefited from language dissimi-
larity that was counterbalanced by IM due to the reduced level
cue between the target and masker. Reducing masker modula-
tion in spectro-temporal and temporal domains improved listen-
ing performance. The release from MM could even cancel EM
for improved intelligibility.
Index Terms: speech intelligibility, modulation masking, infor-
mational masking, energetic masking, glimpsing, speech per-
ception
1. Introduction
In speech perception, an interfering sound causes low-level
energetic masking (EM) on the target speech at the spectro-
temporal regions where the interferer overlaps with the target in
energy [1, 2, 3]. Competing speech, comprehensible or not, also
impedes listener’s perception of target speech by causing in-
formational masking (IM) that may increase the listener’s cog-
nitive load [4, 5]. This effect is particularly prominent when
the target and competing sources share similar acoustic and
linguistic traits [6, 7]. Prior studies, e.g. [8, 9], have shown
that IM is closely related to masker intelligibility, with release
from IM observed when the masker is less intelligible, for ex-
ample through noise vocoding [10], segment reversal or inter-
ruption [11, 12, 13], or when the masker is in an unfamiliar or
low-proficiency language to the listener [7, 12].
More recent evidence has shown that the competing
source’s spectro-temporal modulation can also disrupt that of
the target as modulation masking (MM), resulting in impaired
intelligibility even when IM is absent [14, 15, 16]. Compared
to stationary noise, although modulated noise usually has less
strong EM, overlapping and interfering with the modulation
spectra of the target can offset the benefits from release of EM,
and even degrade intelligibility further [16, 17]. When listen-
ing in noise, speech intelligibility is correlated to the listener’s
ability to “glimpse” and utilize those time-frequency regions on
the target with favorable speech-to-noise ratios (SNR) to decode
speech. Glimpses with high energy contribute most to speech
intelligibility; other glimpses’ benefits largely depend on the
existence and quantity of high-energy glimpses [18]. The avail-
ability and usability of those glimpses are greatly affected by the
temporal, spectro-temporal and even spectro-temporal modula-
tion properties of the noise masker [17, 19].
Previous studies have attempted to isolate IM from EM in
same-talker speech-in-speech perception to understand the con-
tribution of masker’s periodicity and intelligibility to IM. Huo
et al. [20] quantified IM by adjusting the voice-to-noise ratio
on the voiced segments of competing speech. They further ex-
tended the same idea along with time reversal of voiced seg-
ments to minimize the masker intelligibility. It was found that
masker intelligibility caused substantial IM on the target. Fur-
ther release from MM was observed, but it was confounded by
the possible release from IM due to reduced periodicity of the
masker. While it was examined in a post hot analysis using an
intelligibility model, EM of the maskers was not purposefully
controlled by design for comparison between masker; it had in-
teracted with other masking effects.
This study aimed to investigate the impact of IM and MM
on speech intelligibility in a cross-language setup when noise
maskers’ EM was constrained. English sentences were pre-
sented to listeners with competing speech in English and Man-
darin Chinese; the latter was expected to induce less IM than
the former. Instead of manipulating EM by adjusting global
SNR, EM was controlled at three levels estimated by a glimpse-
based intelligibility model [21]. To remove potential IM caused
by periodicity, noise maskers were synthesized to only preserve
the modulation of competing speech in the spectro-temporal do-
main to different extents. It was hypothesized that listeners
would achieve a better intelligibility in the presence of Man-
darin competing speech than in its English counterpart, and that
release from MM could be observed in maskers that were simi-
lar in their EM effect but differed in modulation.
2. Methods
2.1. Stimuli
Target speech materials were drawn from Harvard sen-
tences [22], uttered by a male native speaker of American En-
glish. Sentences from the same corpus were also used as com-
peting speech (CS) to maximize IM. To provide listeners with
a cue to discern the target speech from the CS, only sentences
starting with the word, “the”, were used as targets. Sentences
were also drawn from a TIMIT-like Mandarin corpus [23] as
CS to examine the IM effect due to linguistic similarity. A 30-
minute base CS signal was made by randomly concatenating
sentences from the corpus in each of the languages. Given a tar-
get utterance, its competing counterpart in English or Mandarin
was a random excerpt from the corresponding base signal, pro-

Given a target-CS pair, four noise maskers were derived to
preserve the CS’s modulation to different extents. As opposed
to doing this on a different CS excerpt for the same target, this
warranted that the same region of the target always aligned and
interacted with the same region on the original CS for a better
comparison of masking effects across maskers. In the spectro-
temporal domain, the spectral envelopes of the CS were esti-
mated using linear predictive coding (LPC) every 20 ms. The
frame on a white noise signal, aligning with the CS in time, was
filtered by the linear coefficients; the intensity of the frame was
then adjusted to match that of the CS frame. The waveform
of the desired noise was reconstructed by concatenating the fil-
tered frames with a hamming window and an overlap of 50%
between frames being applied. While the resultant noise’s mod-
ulation was expected to resemble that of the original CS, it car-
ried no periodicity, which were found to introduce large IM to
listeners [9]. The extent to which the modulation of CS was pre-
served depended on LPC order: the higher the order, the finer
spectral envelope of the CS was captured. Early pilot experi-
ment suggested that when the LPC order was greater than 50,
the similarity between the original CS and the desired masker
in the spectro-temporal modulation domain, measured as the
average cross-correlation coefficient over 32 2D Gabor-filtered
spectrograms [24], achieved a mean of 0.89 from 500 test CS
signals. Hence, the LPC orders of 50, 12, and 5 were chosen
to make three noise maskers: STM-50, STM-12 and STM-5,
varying in the degrees of preserving the original CS’s modu-
lation. In the temporal domain, the TS, whose length matched
that of the CS, was modulated by applying the smoothed Hilbert
envelope of the CS, leading to the temporally-modulated (TM)
noise. These procedures were performed for both English and
Mandarin conditions, except that the TM for Mandarin was gen-
erated by applying the temporal envelope of Mandarin CS to
theEnglishTS – this allowed examining and comparing the im-
pact of temporal modulation across languages. Fig. 1 shows
the spectro-temporal excitation patterns (STEP, [25]) of an En-
glish CS signal and the maskers that share its modulation in
different domains; maskers derived from Mandarin CS signals
exhibited the similar pattern: dynamic range reflecting modula-
tion depth and modulation similarity between the CS and other
maskers measured from their STEPs1 decreased from CS, STM-
50, STM-12, STM-5, TM to TS.

der the same SNR, the magnitude of EM is associated with the
acoustic properties of the noise; stationary maskers tend to have
stronger EM than modulated maskers [26]. Hence, using a con-

2.3. Listening Experiment
Twenty-five native American English speakers (10 males, 15
females) between the ages of 18 and 34 years (mean=21.0,
s.d.=3.8) were recruited for this study. They all had normal
hearing confirmed by prior-experiment hearing screening.
The 36 conditions were sorted by the average SNR (as in
Fig. 2) of stimuli, and then grouped into 12 blocks of 3 condi-
tions to reduce the effect of auditory adaptation when the overall
intensity changed overly from stimulus to stimulus. The presen-
tation level of the target speech was calibrated to approximately
67 dBA; the intensity of the masker was adjusted to achieve the
desired SNR for this pair as computed in Sec. 2.2. Each par-
ticipant listened to a total of 180 unique sentences once with
5 sentences in each of the conditions. A balanced design was
adopted: no two participants heard the same set of five sen-
tences under the same condition. The order of presenting the 12
blocks of stimuli and the sequence of 15 stimuli in a block were
randomized. A practice session with 10 sentences was intro-
duced prior to the main test. The experiments were conducted
in a sound-treated audio booth. Participants were instructed to
type down the words they heard from the target sentences start-
ing with the prompt word “the”.
3. Results
3.1. Listener Intelligibility
Fig. 3 shows the listener intelligibility, measured as mean
word recognition rate (WRR), in each condition at the three
EM levels. Overall, WRR increased as expected as EM re-
duced (i.e. as HEGP increased from 0.3 to 0.5) for both lan-
guages. Over maskers, while the WRRs of 17.4%(±0.1%) and
33.8%(±0.2%) at 0.3 and 0.4 HEGP respectively were barely
different when CS was in English from in Mandarin, at 0.5
HEGP the maskers derived from the latter language indeed led
to a WRR of 56.9% – 5.6 percentage points (ppts) higher than
that in the maskers derived from the English CS. Within each
EM level, the WRRs in CS were consistently the lowest for both
languages, likely due to high IM and MM; a progressive im-
provement in WRR was observed when the extent to which the
original modulation in CS was preserved decreases from STM-
50 to TM. Removal of temporal modulation as in TS resulted
in a moderate reduction in WRR relative to TM: an average of
13.3 ppts for Mandarin and 7.5 ppts in English over 0.3 and 0.4
HEGP. The drop in WRR relieved when EM eased: WRR triv-
ially ascended for 0.7 and 1.7 ppts in English and Mandarin at
0.5 HEGP, respectively.

Post-hoc Tukey’s pairwise comparisons with Bonferroni
correction were also performed. At the high (0.3 HEGP) and
mid (0.4 HEGP) EM levels, WRR improvement over the CS
condition due to release from MM and IM became only signif-
icant when the modulation of CS was preserved using a 5th-
order LPC as in STM-5 [p<.01], despite the trend observed in
Fig. 3 that WRR improves when the extent to which the mod-
ulation of CS is preserved decreases. In TM with diminished
S-T modulation, significant improvement in WRR over STM-5
was only observed with the Mandarin CS’s temporal envelope
[ps < .001], but not with that of English CS [ps≥.178].
Although the visual impression in Fig. 3 suggested that WRR
was lower in TS than in TM, the different between the two
maskers was not statistically significant [ps≥.677], except
for Mandarin at the high EM level [p < .001]. Comparing
across languages at the two EM levels, listeners performed sim-
ilarly when listening in all maskers [ps > .05]. At the low
EM level (0.5 HEGP), intelligibility gains over CS were sig-
nificant for both languages [ps < .05] when periodicity was
absent in STM-50; similar WRRs in TM and TS was further
manifested [ps≥.832]. Nevertheless, unlike at the other two
higher EM levels, where the languages did not appear to affect
listeners’ performance, WRRs were indeed significantly higher
in the Mandarin CS [p < .01] and its STM-50 [p < .05] than
in their English counterparts, but not in other maskers.

4. Discussion
Linguistic similarity proves to introduce non-negligible IM to
target speech, especially when both the target and CS are in
the same language [27]. Hence, when the target speech was in
English, listeners were expected to perform better in the Man-
darin CS than in the English CS. However, such phenomenon
due to IM only manifested at 0.5 HEGP when the EM was low.
This could be explained by the release from IM when the lis-
tener benefits from the intensity difference between the target
and CS; it is particularly so when the target and CS are in the
same language, as IM in same-talker speech-in-speech situa-
tion is strongest when target and masker have the same inten-
sity (0 dB SNR) [5, 9]. It has also been observed in previous
studies (e.g. [9]) that the improved intelligibility when SNR de-
creased from 0 dB was the result of the release from IM due to
the level cue canceling EM; this improvement could last until
the benefit of the level cue reached ceiling, at which point EM
became dominant. Here, when EM was strong (i.e. SNR was
low), the listeners took advantage of the level cue to overcome
the IM effect due to linguistic similarity in English CS. In Man-
darin CS, the benefit of the level cue might not be as great as in
English, the release from masking, however, might come from
the language of the masker being distant from that of the tar-
get - a phenomenon where release from IM due to one factor
counterbalances IM due to other factor(s). Consequently, lin-
guistic similarity ostensibly had no effect on the degree of IM
at the two high EM levels. In contrast, when EM was low at
high SNR, the level cue was less robust in English CS. Their
benefit to the listener seemed to be no longer great enough to
counter the IM due to linguistic similarity. Hence, the differ-
ence between Mandarin and English could lead to an improve-
ment of at least 11 ppts in WRR in this study. At 0.5 HEGP,
the difference in cross-language performance diminished when
the maskers were less modulated, and almost died out for TM
and TS. This finding is in line with [9] that IM due to linguistic
similarity declines when the masker becomes less intelligible,
and is potentially non-existent under TM and TS where masker
carries minimal to none linguistic content.
While there was little difference in EM between STM-50,
STM-12 and STM-5 as estimated by the glimpse proportion
in Fig. 4, the incremental release from non-EM masking was
evident when the masker’s modulation degraded. Despite EM
being controlled, there were still masking effects due to other
factors in play. For example, the LPC-filtered maskers could
remain intelligible, particularly with sufficient envelope infor-
mation preserved [10]. It is, therefore, inconclusive on whether
that release was from IM, MM or them combined. A specula-
tion is tempted to made on the combined release of both IM and
MM. Many studies have suggested that modulation is crucial
to speech intelligibility (e.g. [14, 19, 26]), it is reasonable to
expect the three masker’s intelligibility to decrease along with
their reduced modulation from STM-50 to STM-5, hence alle-
viated IM. This could be confirmed by a listening test on the
masker’s intelligibility. As it may be conceptually and techni-
cally challenging to fully isolate IM and MM from each other
because of their nature, it is still worth debating whether MM
is a type of IM, or IM (due to some factors at least, such as
masker’s intelligibility) is a consequence of MM.
