Abstract
Subjective listening tests are the standard for evaluating syn-
thetic speech quality, but they are costly, time-consuming, and
difficult to scale. Automatic speech synthetic quality predic-
tors reduce reliance on human ratings, but still depend on large
amounts of human-annotated data and generalize poorly across
languages, speakers, and synthesis paradigms. This paper pro-
poses a framework without human annotation for learning syn-
thetic speech quality predictors by exploiting training dynam-
ics of text-to-speech systems as pseudo annotation. Synthetic
speech is collected from multiple TTS models at different train-
ing checkpoints and paired with training-derived metadata such
as iteration index and loss. A quality predictor is trained from
audio without human perceptual labels. Experiments on bench-
mark datasets covering multiple languages show that iteration
annotation correlates with human annotation across datasets and
languages.
Index Terms: speech synthesis, quality predictor, automatic
evaluation
1. Introduction
The perceptual quality of text-to-speech (TTS) systems has im-
proved in recent years, driven by advances in neural architec-
tures and large-scale training data. To evaluate the TTS qual-
ity, through a subjective evaluation, a human listening test, re-
mains the gold standard for assessing synthetic speech quality
[1]. However, human annotation is costly, time-consuming, and
prone to bias [2, 3]. To address this issue, prior work has fo-
cused on automatic quality prediction models, which aim to es-
timate human perceptual judgments from audio signals [4, 5].
Nevertheless, supervised quality predictors still require costly
annotations and degrade under domain shift [6]. Moreover,
they are constrained by human perceptual resolution, making
it difficult to capture differences in synthetic speech quality,
especially when variations are below the threshold of percep-
tual discriminability. As a result, quality differences that are
relevant for system development or model comparison may re-
main unobserved in subjective evaluations. These limitations
motivate the search for alternative learning strategies or evalua-
tion signals that reduce or eliminate the dependence on listener
annotation. Recent works have sought to avoid the need for
human annotations by leveraging model uncertainty [7], deep
fake information [8] or exploiting information intrinsic to the
model’s internal structure [9]. These works suggest that percep-
tual quality can be approached through alternative proxy indi-
cators and training paradigms that do not rely on explicit human
ratings. Automatic quality estimation also plays a crucial role
in the development of TTS systems [10]. TTS training involves
exploring multiple architectures and selecting optimal check-
points under limited computational budgets. In such settings,
reliable automatic quality indicators can support model selec-
tion and training monitoring, enabling more efficient system
development without repeated subjective evaluations. The cen-
tral problem addressed in this work is how to design a learning
framework for speech quality prediction that does not rely on
human annotation, generalizes across diverse generation con-
ditions, and provides a reliable signal to support TTS system
development, including checkpoint selection and training stop-
ping criteria.
During TTS training, successive model checkpoints natu-
rally exhibit a progressive improvement in synthetic quality,
from low-quality early outputs to higher-quality synthesized
speech before reaching a saturation state. We hypothesize that
if we can generate synthetic speech with different quality, we
can use it to train a speech synthesis quality predictor without
relying on human annotations.
Based on this hypothesis, we propose a framework for
speech synthesis quality prediction named TDScore which does
not require listener annotation. The proposed framework trains
a proxy for synthetic quality predictor by profiting from TTS
training metadata. In other words, we developed an itera-
tion/loss predictor as the synthetic quality predictor. To our
knowledge, no prior work has explicitly leveraged TTS training
dynamics as annotation for learning a speech quality predictor
without human annotation.
2. Related Work
We summarize relevant work on speech quality prediction and
related uses of training dynamics in TTS.
2.1. Supervised and Proxy-Based Speech Quality Predictor
Supervised Mean Opinion Score (MOS) predictors range from
MOSNet [11], MBNet [12], LDNet [13] to SSL-based models
[4] trained on large MOS datasets. Supervised quality predic-
tors remain limited by the availability and cost of human anno-
tations and often exhibit degraded performance under domain
shifts, such as unseen languages or synthesis paradigms [4, 14].
To mitigate this issue, proxy-based approaches have been
proposed, leveraging signals extracted from pretrained mod-
els without relying on human annotation. For instance, [7]
shows that uncertainty-related statistics derived from out-of-
the-box self-supervised speech models correlate with MOS, en-
abling without MOS supervision quality estimation. In a com-
plementary direction, [9] shows that distributional distances
between synthesized and natural speech representations from
early SSL layers correlate with perceived naturalness for neu-
ral TTS. More recently, [8] explores the use of deepfake detec-
tion models as an alternative proxy for speech synthesis qual-

2.2. Training Dynamics as Quality
The idea that training dynamics encode meaningful information
has been explored in the past. In TTS, intermediate checkpoints
often produce perceptually distinct outputs [15, 16], with au-
dible degradations that progressively diminish as training ad-
vances, suggesting that the optimization process implicitly re-
duces salient perceptual defects.
Consistent with this observation, prior work has shown that
intermediate training stages exhibit systematic acoustic and ar-
ticulatory changes such as shifts in vowel formant structure over
the course of training [17]. These results highlight that train-
ing trajectories can be interpreted not only via aggregated loss
values, but also via linguistically meaningful acoustic organi-
zation. In speech processing, however, training dynamics have
mainly been exploited for optimization, convergence analysis,
or curriculum design rather than perceptual modeling.

4. Experiments
This section describes the experimental setup, including the
TTS models and training procedures, the construction of syn-
thetic datasets from intermediate checkpoints, and the evalua-
tion protocol used to assess the proposed quality predictors.
4.1. TTS Models Considered
We consider three neural TTS architectures introduced in
different years and relying on distinct modeling paradigms:
FastSpeech 2 (FS), a non-autoregressive duration-based
model (2020) [18]; FastPitch (FP), a pitch-conditioned non-
autoregressive variant with explicit F0 modeling (2021) [19];
and F5-TTS (F5), a recent state-of-the-art system based on the
Flow/Consistent-Diffusion paradigm (2025) [20]. The training
objectives differ across architectures and constitute one of the
training-derived proxy indicator considered in this work.FP
is trained using a multi-component regression loss combining
mel-spectrogram reconstruction with duration and pitch predic-
tion, whileFSextends this formulation by additionally model-
ing energy [18, 19]. In contrast,F5relies on a diffusion-based
objective formulated within a flow-matching framework [21].
4.2. TTS Training Procedure
All TTS models are trained on the French audiobook corpus re-
leased for the Blizzard Challenge 2023 (Hub task NEB) [22].
The corpus consists of approximately 51 hours of speech from
a single female speaker, recorded under controlled conditions.
Audio signals are provided at a 22.05 kHz sampling rate, with
normalized and manually corrected text transcriptions aligned
at the utterance level. The data is segmented at the utterance
level, resulting in 63,943 training utterances. Each TTS system
is trained from scratch using standard configurations. Check-
points are saved every10 4 training iterations.

4.4. Quality Predictor: TDScore Implementation Details
Unlike conventional MOS predictors, our quality predictor does
not rely on human perceptual annotations; we denote it as
TDScore(Training-DynamicsScore). Instead, it learns to
infer quality-related information from training-derived labels,
namely the training iteration index and the training loss of the
TTS system that generated the audio. We adopt the SSL-MOS
architecture [23] and follow the implementation described in
[24]. For eachTTSsystem (FS,FP,F5), we train two variants:
(i)TDScore–TTS–Ite, predicting the normalized training iter-
ation index, and (ii)TDScore–TTS–Loss, predicting the stan-

5.1. Validation of the TDScore for the loss/ite prediction
On theD loss,D ite (test), we evaluate whether TDScore can
predict the checkpoint iteration and the training loss of the
TTS system that generated each utterance. For iteration pre-
diction, all models achieve strong correlations, confirming that
the training progression is consistently reflected in the synthe-
sized audio.TDScore–F5–Iteachieves the highest utterance-
level correlation (0.90), outperformingTDScore–FP–Ite(0.84)
andTDScore–FS–Ite(0.79). In contrast, training loss pre-
diction is less consistent across architectures. While the pro-
posed models are still able to recover loss-related information
to some extent, correlations are generally lower than for itera-
tion. The best performance is obtained byTDScore–FS–Loss,
which reaches an utterance-level correlation of0.79, indicating
that loss prediction remains feasible but constitutes a less stable
annotation signal than iteration.
5.2. Evaluation In-Domain of the TDScore
On BC, among supervised approaches, SSL-MOS achieves a
system-level SRCC of 0.65, while our supervisedTDScore–
MOSreaches 0.66/0.40 (Sys/Utt). This confirms that the
TDScore architecture is effective with explicit MOS supervi-
sion. Without MOS supervision, TDScore still achieves strong
performance.TDScore–F5–Itereaches0.74/0.54 (Sys/Utt),
outperforming supervised and proxy without MOS methods
reported in the literature, e.g., DeepFake (0.60/0.50) and
layer-wise SSL (0.70 system-level). Other iteration-based
variants,TDScore–FP–Ite(0.54/0.37) andTDScore–FS–Ite
(0.46/0.29), are better than Uncertainty-based proxy (zero shot).
Among loss-based variants,TDScore–FP–Lossachieves the
best performance within the TDScore-loss setting, but remains
below its iteration-based counterpart and only outperforms the
Uncertainty-based proxy in its zero-shot configuration.
5.3. Out-of-Domain Evaluation of the TDScore
On BVCC, supervised methods achieve the highest perfor-
mance, with SSL-MOS reaching 0.94/0.86 andTDScore–MOS
achieving 0.88/0.83. Without MOS supervision,TDScore–
F5–Iteachieves0.73/0.66, outperforming the DeepFake proxy
(+0.39 at the system level).TDScore–FS–Ite(0.53/0.38) and
TDScore–FP–Ite(0.41/0.37) also maintain correlations. Loss-
based variants show also correlations (e.g., -0.64/-0.63 for
TDScore–F5–Loss), outperforming the DeepFake proxy.
On SOMOS, SSL-MOS achieves 0.80/0.23, while
TDScore–MOSreaches 0.73/0.44. Without MOS, baselines
are not reported for this benchmark. Among our non-supervised
variants,TDScore–F5–Iteachieves 0.38/0.22, outperforming
its loss-based counterpart (-0.34/-0.14).
6. Conclusion
This study introduced a training methodology for learning
speech quality predictors without human perceptual annotation,
by exploiting information observed during TTS training.
We first validated that TDScore can predict both training it-
eration and training loss. Iteration prediction achieved strong
correlations, confirming that training progression is reflected
in the synthesized audio. Loss prediction also showed mean-
ingful correlations, although generally lower. In the in-domain
evaluation,TDScore–F5–Iteachieved the strongest correla-
tions, demonstrating that training iteration can act as an effec-
tive proxy for synthetic speech quality prediction. In the out-of-
domain evaluation the performance of the supervised models
shows the benefit of human intervention. However,TDScore–
Iteoutperformed proxy baseline and reduced the gap with su-
pervised approaches, despite not relying on MOS labels. This
highlights the robustness and cross-lingual generalization capa-
bility of iteration-based supervision.
Across configurations,TDScore–Iteoutperformed
TDScore–Loss. This difference can be explained by the
nature of the training dynamics. In our data, the losses are
oscillatory. In contrast, for the iteration, F5 shows smoother
and progressive perceptual improvements across iterations. We
hypothesize the loss is input dependent and can vary across
different samples while iteration represents a learning state of
model which can be more robust.
We observed a higher performance for dataset generated by
F5 than other TTS for iteration predictor. This can be explained
by observed variation of the synthetic quality during training
of TTS. For example while F5 follows a progressive improve-
ment of synthetic quality during training, this improvement is
not clear for the others.
A limitation of our study is that TDScore is constrained by
the properties of current TTS training dynamics. Although F5 is
currently state-of-the-art, future architectures may exhibit train-
ing dynamics that are better suited for constructing datasets. As
a perspective, it would be valuable to train a unified TDScore
model jointly on multiple TTS architectures to improve robust-
ness and generalization. Future work could explore dataset con-
struction strategies, including reduced linguistic diversity and
shorter utterances.
