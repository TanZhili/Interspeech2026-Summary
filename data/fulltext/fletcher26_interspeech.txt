Oral Stop Realisation in Three French Polynesian Languages 
Janet Fletcher, Adele Gregory 
University of Melbourne 
j.fletcher@unimelb.edu.au, a.gregory@unimelb.edu.au 
 
 
Abstract 
Seven distinct indigenous languages are spoken in French 
Polynesia in the Eastern Pacific region. This study explores 
voice onset time (VOT) and voiceless stop realisation in three 
languages: Tahitian, Marquesan (Northern and Southern), and 
Rurutu. All languages are considered to have relatively low 
levels of inter-generational transmission. The results show 
stops in all three languages are voiceless and short lag with 
mean VOT values of around 20 ms. Reflecting a well-observed 
crosslinguistic pattern, velars in Marquesan have longer VOT 
than bilabials and dentals in either of the three languages. 
Following vowel has the strongest influence on VOT duration 
with longer values for /t/ preceding close front vowels, and for 
/p/ preceding close back rounded vowels across the corpus. 
Closure duration is influenced by prosodic factors in Tahitian 
only, with longer durations in accented versus unaccented 
stops. Voicing proportion levels are low with slightly longer 
values in medial contexts but overall, there is no strong 
evidence of voicing or manner lenition in the current corpus.   
Index Terms: voice onset time, stops, French Polynesian 
languages  
1. Introduction 
1.1. French Polynesian languages 
There are seven distinct indigenous languages spoken in French 
Polynesia in the Eastern Pacific region [1].  The languages form 
the Eastern Polynesian branch of the Oceanic language group. 
Tahitian (reo tahiti) is the major language spoken in the Society 
Islands, with Northern Marquesan (‘eo ‘enana) and Southern 
Marquesan (‘eo ‘enata) spoken in the Marquesas.  Mangarevan 
(reo magareva) is spoken in the Gambier Islands, the Pa’umotu 
dialect group (reo pa’umotu) is spoken in the Tuamotu 
archipelago and Rapa (rapa iti) is spoken on Rapa. Tahitian has 
the most speakers, but it is widely accepted that there are low 
levels of inter-generational language transmission [2], and [3] 
among others, claims that many of the other languages spoken 
in French Polynesia are considered endangered due to 
prolonged contact with French and a process of 
‘Tahitianisation’. The languages are uniformly described as 
having simple phonological systems with relatively few 
consonant contrasts (8 to 10), with 5 principal vowels plus a 
length contrast [1, 4]. Syllable structure is primarily CV and the 
languages are stress accent inferring from earlier 
impressionistic descriptions [5, 6, 7]. Stress is penultimate in 
words consisting of light syllables. Final syllables can also 
attract stress if vowels are long [4,5]. North Marquesan main 
stress also appears to be largely penultimate although with some 
variability apparent in connected speech [8,9]. 
1.2. Stop realisation 
This study explores variation in oral stop production in three of 
the French Polynesian languages: Tahitian,  Northern and 
Southern Marquesan, and Rurutu (Austral). For the purposes of 
this paper North and South Marquesan , due to their 
phonological similarity, are described henceforth as Marquesan 
after [1]. As with other  languages of the Polynesian triangle 
including Te reo Māori spoken in Aotearoa/New Zealand [10], 
or Cook Islands Māori [11], the languages under investigation 
in this paper have small oral stop inventories with a single 
voiceless stop series and relatively few place of articulation 
contrasts as shown in Table 1 below  [1]. Most French 
Polynesian languages have experienced debuccalisation with 
Rurutu and Tahitian phonological words described as having 
undergone “erosion” due to the loss of consonant contrasts such 
as /k/ and /ŋ/ which are realised synchronically as /ʔ/[1].  
       Tahitian and Rurutu have two contrastive stops (voiceless 
bilabial and voiceless dental) whereas the two varieties of 
Marquesan examined here have three places of articulation 
(voiceless bilabial, voiceless dental, voiceless velar) in the oral 
stop series. There is however variation between different 
Marquesan varieties with regards to the distribution of /k/ which 
can alternate with /ʔ/ in a number of forms.  To date there has 
been no major quantitative study of voice onset time (VOT) or 
stop closure duration in either Rurutu and Marquesan, and only 
a preliminary investigation of  VOT in Tahitian stops [12]. 
         Impressionistic descriptions of Tahitian, for example, do 
not always agree about the realisation of voiceless stops. All 
concur that they are voiceless, and they have mostly been 
described as unaspirated [e.g. 5], or unaspirated, aspirated, or 
glottalised by [6] who suggests the latter two variants are due 
to coarticulation or complete coproduction with following /h/ 
or /ʔ/. Marquesan stops /p,t,k/ have also been described as 
unaspirated [9] but [8] suggests in the Ùa Pou variety of North 
Marquesan, they show variable periods of aspiration with /t/ 
showing minimal variation, and /k/ having longer periods of 
aspiration which aligns with cross-linguistic patterns of VOT 
(e.g. [13, 14]).

        Experimental investigations of Hawaiian report that the 
two contrastive voiceless stops of Hawaiian, /p, k / have  short 
lag, with mean positive VOT values of 24 ms for bilabials and 
39 ms for velars [15].  Studies of VOT in Te reo Māori also 
show that /p, t, k/ have shorter periods of aspiration compared 
to New Zealand English which like many other English 
varieties has long lag voiceless stops [16, 17], but there has been 
generational shift with younger Māori speakers producing 
longer periods of aspiration than older speakers. Interestingly, 
this was not observed in [15]’s study of Hawaiian but this may 
have been due to the differing nature and time depth of the 
respective corpora.

Another phenomenon that has been attested in earlier studies of 
Eastern Polynesian languages is stop lenition. Prosodic word 
medial velars are often lenited to voiceless fricatives  in 
Hawaiian [15] with 39% of /k/ phonemes realised as fricatives.  
In Tongan, /k/ optionally lenites to either [x] or [ɣ] in word-
medial contexts [18].   With regard to the French Poynesian 
languages examined in this paper, no evidence of this level of 
lenition or spirantisation has been reported in the cited sources 
consulted thus far. 
      The principal aim of this preliminary study was to confirm 
whether or not the single voiceless stop series of Rurutu, 
Marquesan, and Tahitian are indeed short lag and to see whether 
there are any additional factors, including prosodic word 
position  and post-lexical accentuation that influence VOT 
variation.  At this stage, there have been no quantitative 
investigations of acoustic features of these stops in any of the 
languages investigated in this paper aside from [12].  To this 
end,  closure duration and voicing patterns were also explored 
to see if there was evidence of the kind of manner or voicing 
variation observed for other Eastern Polynesian languages.  
 
2. Methodology  
2.1. Speakers and materials 
       Speech data were obtained from five female speakers of 
Tahitian, four speakers of  North Marquesan (two males and 
two females), one speaker of South Marquesan (female) and 
two speakers of Rurutu (two female speakers). All  participants 
were aged between 18 and 70 at the time of recording.  All were 
born in French Polynesia and were bilingual in their resepective 
indigenous language and Tahitian French or the local French 
vernacular spoken in their communities. The recording 
materials consisted of two elicited reading tasks. For Tahitian, 
the set of materials consisted of a sentence frame task where 
speakers produced ninety words designed to illustrate all  
contrastive stops and vowels of Tahitian.  The disyllabic tokens 
were placed in phrase medial position in a carrier phrase. Only 
tokens containing the oral stops /p, t/  were selected for analysis.  
For the Marquesan and Rurutu datasets, a modified Swadesh 
word list [19] of one hundred and sixty words was developed to 
capture typical concepts that were familiar to speakers of the 
languages. The words contained a number of voiceless stops 
and were read in isolation. Whilst the speaking task varied 
between Tahitian and the other two languages  (and was 
therefore not ideal), speakers produced careful readings of all 
materials so the citation forms and sentence frame tokens were 
deemed comparable in terms of speaking style in that all were 
produced under narrow focus. Furthermore, only disyllabic or 
trisyllabic tokens were analysed across the corpus. In future 
work, it will be necessary to collect more balanced datasets 
across the three languages from equivalent numbers of speakers 
and so forth. 
        The 12 speakers were recorded in Papeete, French 
Polynesia,  in a quiet room at the Université de la Polynésie 
Française in November 2018 using a Zoom H6 recorder through 
a Countryman ISOMAX head -mounted microphone. 
Recordings had a sampling rate of 44.1kHz and 16 bit 
quantisation.  The Tahitian speakers produced three or four 
repetitions of each token in the sentence frame task, and the 
Rurutu and Marquesan speakers produced one to two 
repetitions of each token in the Swadesh list.  
       Table 2  summarises the distribution of voiceless stops in 
two different word contexts. All stops were in syllable onset 
position given all three languages have CV syllable structure. 
The South Marquesan speaker produced mostly the same 
tokens as the North Marquesan speakers so for convenience 
these have been grouped together. The total number of stop 
tokens analysed in this study was 3, 839. The dataset was 
heavily weighted towards Tahitian given the differences in data 
acquistion protocole compared to the other two languages. The 
distribution in terms of place of articulation is also unbalanced 
given the original Swadesh list recordings for Marquesan and 
Rurutu were designed to produce materials that are ecologically 
valid and comparable to similar datasets acquired by general 
linguists for a broad range of Oceanic languages. The wordlist 
was not designed to highlight phoneme contrasts in either 
Rurutu or Marquesan unlike the Tahitian materials for which 
there were a number of existing phonological descriptions and 
resources at the time of recording.

2.2. Annotation Procedure and Data Processing 
The audiofiles were force aligned using a version of General 
WebMaus [20] adapted for French Polynesian  phoneme 
inventories. Vowel and stop boundaries were manually checked 
and corrected after examining the waveform and spectrogram 
for each token using Praat [21]. Word boundaries containing 
stops were also checked and adjusted if necessary.  An 
additional VOT tier was added and the post-release phase was 
annotated where there was clear onset of aperiodic energy after 
a complete occlusion and the onset of regular formant activity 
associated with a following vowel visible in the acoustic 
waveform and spectrogram. The labelled data were then 
converted to an emu-sdms database using the emuR package in 
the R environment [22]. Main word stress was also identified. 
All languages examined have lexical stress  and given the nature 
of the corpus, it was a relatively straightforward annotation 
process because all tokens were realised under narrow focus 
and the main stress syllable for each word was always 
associated with a post-lexical intonational pitch accent.  It was 
not possible to investigate lexical stress effects in the absence 
of intonational prominence given the nature of the dataset. The 
prominence contrast explored in this paper was therefore 
accented (post-lexical) versus unstressed.  
           Acoustic duration values were extracted for medial stop 
closure, and for the post-release phase in word-initial and 
medial positions.  An additional measure of voicing proportion 
was derived using an R script that mimics aspects of the voice 
report function in Praat. This analysis was undertaken to see 
whether there was evidence of any voicing allophony, 
particularly in prosodically weaker contexts (i.e. medial, 
unstressed syllable onsets). For purely comparative purposes 
regarding this measure, the original MAUS time stamp for 
word-initial stop onset was retained  to enable a putative 
calculation of voicing proportion for word-initial position even 
though there was often a pause so it was not possible to 
accurately locate the onset of stop closure in this position. The 
release phase was included in the calculation in both word -
initial and medial contexts. Furthermore, it was also observed, 
particularly for Marquesan and Rurutu, that speakers 
occasionally produced aperiodic creaky voice during the release 
phase of some stops if there was a glottal stop in a neighbouring 
syllable. Voicing proportion has proven previously to be 
particularly useful in previous analyses of Tahitian glottal stop 
variation [23].  
2.3. Statistical analysis 
The duration data (stop closure phase and VOT interval) were 
submitted to two  linear mixed effects models using the 
lmerTest function in R [24, 25]. For the VOT model, fixed 
1689
effects were phoneme, language, prominence (post-lexical 
accented  vs unstressed which was also unaccented in the 
dataset), following vowel(/i, e, a, o, u/, and word position 
(initial versus medial).  For the closure duration model, the 
fixed effects were phoneme, language, following vowel, and 
prominence as only medial stops were included in this analysis. 
In both models, speaker and word were included as random 
effects. Voicing fraction was also analysed statistically using 
the same model structure. Interactions among the fixed factors 
were also included and the emmeans package in R was used to 
explore any that were significant [26].  
3. Results 
3.1. VOT  duration 
Figure 1 plots  VOT duration across the three languages. For 
Marquesan, which has the three way stop contrast, /k/ has the 
longest VOT values, with a mean  duration of 41 ms (s.d. = 
16.34, N=305) which reflects a pattern noted across many 
languages of the world [e.g. 30].  /p/ has a mean VOT value of  
23.5 ms (s.d. = 14.82, N=153) with similar values observed for 
/t/ at 21.8 ms (s.d.= 10.6, N=364). For Rurutu, /p/ has a mean 
VOT value of 19.79 ms (s.d. = 10.87, N = 158), and /t/ has a 
mean VOT value of 21.89  (s.d. = 13.16 and N = 380).  For 
Tahitian, /p/ has a mean VOT value of 16.98 ms (s.d. = 11.67, 
N = 792), /t/ has a mean VOT value of 22.57 (s.d. = 16.2, N = 
1687). The output of the step and get-model functions of 
lmerTest are summarised in Table 3. All factors were sum 
coded.  There were no main effects of prominence or language. 
There was a significant effect of word position and a significant 
interaction between phoneme and following vowel which is 
evident in Table 3.

3.2. Closure Duration 
Figure 2 plots closure duration across the three languages for 
word-medial position. For sake of space, only post-hoc effects 
are reported here.  Medial closure values were quite comparable 
across the languages for /p/ and /t/  with no significant 
differences. For Marquesan, /t/ has significantly longer closure 
durations than /k/ (β = 31.69, SE=6.05, t=4.66, p<0.0001), but 
the difference between /p/ and /k/ was not significant.There 
were no significant effects of vowel context but there was a 
significant prominence and language interaction for Tahitian  
with medial stops having longer closures in accented versus 
unaccented syllable onsets (β=39.97, SE=8.68, t=4.61). No 
significant differences were observed in the other languages, 
which was likely because the corpus was larger and more 
controlled for Tahitian.

3.3. Voicing Proportion 
Figure 3 plots voicing proportion in word initial versus word 
medial contexts for the three languages.  All stops showed some 
variation in voicing proportion. Statistical analyses showed no 
significant main effects of prominence or of following vowel 
but there was a significant interaction between and phoneme 
and language (F=5.2, p<0.01) and phoneme and position 
(F=8.36, p<0.01). There were higher voicing proportion values 
in word medial position overall (as evident in Figure 3) which 
was due to a period of VTT ‘voice termination time’ (i.e. the 
1690
length of time to cease vocal fold activity) that was clear at the 
beginning of the measured consonant interval in speech 
waveforms. Rurutu medial /p/ had the highest mean voicing 
fraction value of 0.21 with high levels of variability (s.d.=0.24) 
followed by Tahitian medial /p/ at 0.19 (s.d. = 0.23). 
Subsequent emmeans analyses confirmed that for /p/ ,  
Marquesan  has lower voicing fraction values than Tahitian (β=-
012, z=-4.57, p<0.001), and Tahitian /p/ has higher voicing 
fraction values than Marquesan /t/ ( β=0.117, z=-5.04, 
p<0.0001) and /k/ (β=0.13, z=5.41, p<0.0001),  and Tahitian /t/ 
(β=0.056, z=6.64, p<0.0001) and Rurutu /t/ (β=0.11, z=3.34, 
p<0.05). In terms of prosodic position, all stops have higher 
voicing fraction values in medial versus initial position (/p/ 
β=0.09, z=6.177 p<0.0001), /t/ β=0.032, z=3.601, p<0.01, and 
for Marquesan, /k/ β=0.058, z=2.794, p<0.05).  
       There were no intervocalic fricative realisations for any of 
the languages, with the exception of one token produced by a 
female speaker of South Marquesan in a medial velar 
environment with surrounding close front vowels. Apart from 
this, all stop closures showed periods of complete occlusion and 
were highly stop-like, with no evidence of significant lenition 
i.e. voicing aside from the VTT interval, or frication during the 
entire occlusion phase with respect to this parameter.

4. Discussion 
The results presented in this preliminary study confirm that 
stops produced by speakers of three French Polynesian 
languages, Marquesan, Rurutu, and Tahitian in our corpus, are 
realised largely as short lag voiceless stops, similar to Hawaiian 
[15], and to a certain extent Māori [although see 16]. Most stops  
had short VOT values of around 20 ms with the exception of /k/ 
in Marquesan which had a mean VOT of around 40ms 
reflecting well-documented crosslinguistic trends [e.g. 30]. The  
languages explored in this corpus clearly fit the typical profile 
proposed for Proto-Polynesian [27] which assumes a single 
series unaspirated voiceless series,  and the languages are also 
typical of other single stop series languages, e.g. Plains Cree 
[28], and to a certain extent Warlpiri [29].  Stops also show the 
same range of positive VOT values as ‘unaspirated’ stops for 
languages with a 2-way stop contrast [30].  Prosodic effects 
were less evident in this study than reported for other 
Polynesian languages like Hawaiian [15] presumably due to the 
different nature of the corpus. This French Polynesian corpus 
did not consist of spontaneous speech or narratives, and it was 
not possible to analyse lexical stress versus post -lexical 
prominence, or unstressed versus stressed (unaccented) syllable 
onsets for example. Whilst the results are clear and compelling 
with regard to VOT patterning, there is a need to record a more 
comparable corpus across the languages to confirm these 
findings, and work is currently underway on a corpus of 
narratives across the three languages that will also facilitate a 
more nuanced analysis of lexical stress and post-lexical 
prominence effects. 
       Differences in VOT due to position in word were minimal. 
Stop closures were longer in Tahitian in medial accented versus 
unaccented (unstressed) syllables but no effect of post-lexical 
prominence was observed in the other two languages.  Once 
again, further work is required to see whether more variation 
would be evident in a more prosodically varied corpus like the 
one examined by [15]. We were also unable to measure stop 
closure in word initial position in this study so a domain-initial 
lengthening effect may still be present but other instrumental 
phonetic techniques would be required to measure this with any 
degree of accuracy. 
       Nevertheless, VOT patterns showed a degree of contextual 
variation in our data. The most important source of variation 
was following vowel quality.  For example, /p/ followed by /u/ 
had longer VOT values in words like /ˈpuːhaː/ thigh in 
Marquesan and /ˈpuːai/ strong in Tahitian due to the 
coarticulatory effects of the following close back rounded 
vowel. Values were around 33-34 ms which still falls into the 
voiceless ‘unaspirated’ stop category according to [13, 30]. 
Dentals followed by close front vowels also had significantly 
longer positive VOT values compared to other vowel 
environments in all three languages compared to other vowel 
environments.  These values approach the ‘aspirated’ category, 
particularly in the case of Rurutu with a mean VOT value of 55 
ms when followed by /i/ [30]. The longer aspiration phase in /ti/
