Abstract
Children’s speech recognition remains challenging due to
acoustic variability and limited labeled data. This study ana-
lyzes the behavior of self-supervised learning (SSL) represen-
tations for children’s speech recognition under a strict zero-
shot adult-to-child transfer setting. Layer-wise representations
from Wav2Vec2, HuBERT, and Data2Vec are evaluated using
hybrid ASR systems trained only on adult speech. Although
several layers achieve comparable best-case word error rates,
substantial variability exists across representations. Error analy-
sis reveals dataset-dependent error structures, while LLM-based
post-recognition correction provides little improvement. Oracle
analysis further shows that different layers capture complemen-
tary acoustic information, particularly under stronger domain
mismatch. These findings indicate that recognition errors are
primarily driven by acoustic representation limitations rather
than recoverable linguistic inconsistencies.
Index Terms: children’s speech recognition, zero-shot ASR,
error analysis, representation variability, LLM
1. Introduction
Automatic speech recognition (ASR) for children remains chal-
lenging due to substantial acoustic and linguistic differences
between adult and child speech [1–3]. Higher pitch, evolv-
ing articulation, pronunciation variability, and developmental
speech patterns often lead to systematic degradation when mod-
els trained on adult data are applied to children [4, 5]. In many
realistic scenarios, labeled children’s speech data is limited,
making zero-shot or cross-domain evaluation particularly rel-
evant [6–8]. Understanding how adult-trained representations
behave under this domain shift is therefore both practically and
scientifically important.
Self-supervised learning (SSL) models trained on large-
scale adult speech corpora have become powerful acoustic rep-
resentation learners [9–12]. Intermediate layers of these mod-
els encode progressively abstract information and are widely
reused for downstream ASR tasks [7]. Prior studies have shown
that different SSL layers can yield different recognition perfor-
mance when used as acoustic features [13, 14]. In practice, rep-
resentations are often compared using word error rate (WER),
and a single globally best-performing layer is selected. While
effective for performance benchmarking, this strategy implicitly
assumes that global recognition accuracy sufficiently reflects
representation quality.
However, WER is an aggregate metric that combines sub-
stitutions, deletions, and insertions into a single score and there-
fore does not reveal how recognition errors are structured. Two
systems with similar WER may exhibit substantially different
error patterns, which can influence robustness under domain
shift, stability across utterances, and the effectiveness of post-
recognition correction. In the zero-shot adult-to-child setting, it
remains unclear whether representations with comparable WER
exhibit similar error behavior or whether important variability
exists that is not captured by global metrics.
This observation motivates a deeper examination of repre-
sentation diversity beyond WER. Specifically, we investigate
the following questions:(1)Do SSL representations with simi-
lar WER produce similar error structures in zero-shot children’s
ASR?(2)Is the globally best-performing representation also
consistently optimal across individual utterances?(3)Are er-
rors produced by different representations equally recoverable
through post-recognition correction?
To answer these questions, we construct multiple zero-shot
ASR systems using intermediate SSL representations and eval-
uate them on the PFSTAR [15] and CMU Kids [16] datasets.
We analyze substitution, deletion, and insertion patterns to char-
acterize error diversity. We further perform oracle selection
across representations to quantify per-utterance variability and
estimate the upper bound achievable through ideal representa-
tion selection. Finally, we apply deterministic post-recognition
language model correction to assess error recoverability and ex-
amine whether lower WER implies higher correction potential.
Our findings reveal three consistent trends with important
implications for zero-shot ASR design.First, representations
with comparable WER often exhibit distinct error structures,
indicating that aggregate recognition accuracy alone is insuf-
ficient to characterize representation quality.Second, oracle
analysis demonstrates that no single representation is consis-
tently optimal across utterances, motivating adaptive represen-
tation selection strategies.Third, the representation with the
lowest WER is not always the most recoverable after correction,
suggesting that recoverability and recognition accuracy are not
equivalent properties.
The main contributions of this work are:
• A detailed analysis of error diversity across SSL representa-
tions in zero-shot children’s ASR.
• An oracle-based evaluation that quantifies per-utterance rep-
resentation variability.
• A recoverability study demonstrating that lowest WER does
not guarantee highest correction potential.
• Empirical evidence that WER alone is insufficient for select-
ing representations under domain shift.
By moving beyond aggregate recognition accuracy and
jointly analyzing error structure, oracle gains, and recoverabil-
ity, this work provides a more comprehensive understanding of
SSL representation behavior for children’s speech recognition.

2. Related Work
SSL models such as Wav2Vec2, HuBERT, and Data2Vec have
become central to modern speech recognition by providing
reusable acoustic representations pretrained on large-scale unla-
beled corpora [17–20]. Beyond full fine-tuning, several studies
have investigated the use of frozen SSL embeddings as input
features for conventional acoustic models [13, 14, 21, 22]. It
has been observed that intermediate transformer layers encode
different levels of acoustic and contextual information, leading
to performance variation when different internal representations
are used for ASR. Most prior analyses treat representation se-
lection as a performance optimization problem, where layers are
compared using global WER and the representation achieving
the lowest WER is selected. While effective for benchmarking,
such evaluations typically focus on aggregate accuracy and pro-
vide limited insight into how different representations behave at
the error level.
Children’s speech recognition remains particularly chal-
lenging due to acoustic variability, pronunciation differences,
and developmental factors [23, 24]. Prior work has explored
acoustic model adaptation, data augmentation, and transfer
learning strategies to address these challenges [12, 19, 20, 25].
With the emergence of SSL models pretrained on adult speech,
zero-shot or cross-domain application to children’s speech has
become increasingly feasible. However, evaluation in such set-
tings is still largely centered on overall WER comparisons.
Since WER aggregates substitutions, deletions, and insertions
into a single score, it may obscure structural differences be-
tween systems. Post-recognition correction methods, includ-
ing neural language models and large language models (LLMs),
have also been explored to improve recognition outputs [24,26,
27]. In contrast to prior work that focuses on selecting a sin-
gle best-performing representation based solely on WER, this
study analyzes representation diversity through error structure,
per-utterance variability, and correction behavior in zero-shot
children’s ASR.
3. Experimental Setup
3.1. Speech Datasets
3.1.1. Children’s Speech Datasets
We evaluate our approach on two publicly available children’s
speech corpora: PFSTAR [15] and CMU Kids [16].
The PFSTAR corpus [15] contains British English record-
ings from children aged 4 to 14 years. The training portion in-
cludes approximately 8.3 hours of read speech from 122 speak-
ers. For evaluation, we use the designated test subset compris-
ing 1.1 hours of read speech from 60 speakers (28 female, 32
male), aged between 4 and 13 years. The dataset contains rela-
tively longer read sentences and substantial speaker variability,
which influences representation-dependent behavior under do-
main shift.
The CMU Kids corpus [16] consists of American English
speech from children aged 6 to 11 years. The dataset includes
5,180 utterances totaling approximately 9 hours from 78 speak-
ers (24 male, 52 female). The evaluation subset used in this
study comprises 2.83 hours of speech. Compared to PFSTAR,
CMU Kids contains shorter utterances and different recording
characteristics, leading to distinct error patterns in zero-shot
recognition.
3.1.2. Adult Training Datasets
To simulate a strict zero-shot adult-to-child setting, acoustic
models are trained exclusively on adult speech data.
For British English experiments, we use 15.5 hours of
speech from 92 speakers in the WSJCAM0 corpus [28]. For
American English experiments, Mini LibriSpeech dataset is
used to maintain accent consistency with CMU Kids.
No children’s speech data is used during acoustic model
training, and no fine-tuning or adaptation is performed on the
evaluation datasets.
3.2. SSL Models
We evaluate three pretrained SSL models: Wav2Vec2, Hu-
BERT, and Data2Vec. Each model follows a similar architec-
ture consisting of a convolutional feature encoder followed by
a 24-layer Transformer network. All models are used in their
large configurations with publicly available checkpoints.
Frame-level representations are extracted from each Trans-
former layer independently, resulting in 1024-dimensional fea-
tures computed at a 20 ms frame rate. In all experiments, SSL
models are kept frozen to ensure strict zero-shot evaluation and
consistent representation comparison.
Wav2Vec2 [29] is pretrained using contrastive learning to
predict masked speech units from a set of distractors. Hu-
BERT [30] employs masked prediction using iteratively refined
pseudo-labels. Data2Vec [31] learns representations through
self-distillation by matching latent targets from a teacher net-
work.
3.3. Zero-Shot ASR
ASR experiments are conducted using the Kaldi toolkit [32]. A
hybrid DNN-HMM acoustic model is trained on adult speech
using conventional decoding pipelines. The network consists of
five hidden layers with 1024 units each and is trained using a
decaying learning rate schedule. Decoding is performed with a
bigram language model.
For SSL-based systems, conventional acoustic features are
replaced with frozen SSL representations. Each Transformer
layer is evaluated independently under identical acoustic mod-
eling and decoding conditions. No fine-tuning, adaptation, or
additional supervision is applied on children’s speech, ensuring
a strict zero-shot setting.
3.4. Error Analysis and Evaluation Protocol
Recognition performance is measured using WER, decomposed
into substitutions (S), deletions (D), and insertions (I). To char-
acterize structural differences across representations, we com-
pute the relative contribution of each error type as:

4.3. LLM-Based Error Correction
To investigate whether recognition errors can be corrected using
linguistic context, we apply a LLM as a post-recognition cor-
rection mechanism. Specifically, we use the instruction-tuned
Mistral-7B-Instruct model to correct the 1-best ASR hypothe-
ses generated from the best-performing SSL representations.
In the zero-shot configuration, the ASR hypothesis is pro-
vided to the LLM with a prompt instructing the model to min-
imally correct transcription errors. The corrected sentence pro-
duced by the model is then evaluated against the reference tran-
scription.
In practice, zero-shot LLM correction produces nerepre-
sentation diversity becomes increasingly importantarly identi-
cal WER across both datasets. In all cases, the model preserves
the original hypothesis rather than modifying it. Several fac-
tors may explain this behavior. First, children’s speech often
contains pronunciation variability and acoustic ambiguities that
cannot be resolved using textual context alone. Second, the
LLM does not have access to the acoustic evidence that pro-
duced the recognition error. Finally, the acoustic decoder may
already select the most linguistically plausible hypothesis, leav-
ing little room for purely language-based correction.
We further investigate whether adapting the LLM using
available training transcripts improves correction ability. The
model is fine-tuned using parameter-efficient LoRA training
on the training text. However, this adaptation degrades WER.
Training on clean textual data encourages additional substitu-
tions or insertions rather than correcting ASR-specific acoustic
errors, occasionally introducing hallucinated corrections.
These observations suggest that many recognition errors in
zero-shot children’s speech recognition originate from acous-
tic ambiguities rather than linguistic inconsistencies. Conse-
quently, improvements are more likely to arise from stronger
acoustic representations rather than post-hoc linguistic correc-
tion.
4.4. Representation Stability Across Datasets
Given the limited effectiveness of text-based correction, we next
investigate whether recognition improvements can instead be
obtained through better representation selection.
To quantify representation complementarity, we perform
oracle evaluation by selecting, for each utterance, the hypoth-
esis with the lowest WER across all layer-specific systems. The
resulting oracle WER represents the upper bound achievable if
the optimal representation could be chosen dynamically.

To further quantify representation complementarity, we re-

ers. Layers are ranked based on global WER, and oracle selec-

to the top 5, 4, 3, and 2 layers. As shown in Table 4, oracle

portant under stronger domain mismatch conditions.
Although all three models exhibit increased instability on
CMU Kids, their behavior differs slightly. Data2Vec shows
closer alignment between globally best-performing layers and
oracle selection frequency, whereas HuBERT exhibits higher
per-utterance variability. Despite these architectural differ-
ences, the overall trend remains consistent across models.
The restricted-layer oracle analysis further supports this
observation. As shown in Table 4, oracle WER increases
progressively as the number of available layers is reduced.
While this degradation remains limited on WSJCAM0 and PF-
STAR, it becomes substantially larger on CMU Kids, indicat-
ing stronger representation complementarity under greater do-
main mismatch. Together, these results suggest that SSL layers
capture partially non-overlapping acoustic information, and that
representation diversity plays an increasingly important role in
zero-shot children’s ASR.
