From Academic Tool to Community Infrastructure: A Call for Indigenous
Partnership in Speech Data Governance

Abstract
We present ELSI (ExELang Legacy Support Interface), a gov-
ernance platform for managing sensitive child-centered audio
datasets that includes recordings from 10 indigenous language
communities across multiple continents. While ELSI was de-
signed to balance open science with participant privacy, we
identify a fundamental gap: custodianship concentrates control
in the hands of European academic researchers, with no formal
plan for indigenous communities to govern their own data. We
describe the platform, map its current model against CARE and
OCAP principles, and issue a concrete call for indigenous com-
munity partners to co-design a framework for identifying legit-
imate community custodians and implementing an enforceable
access and control layer. We commit to the architectural and
governance changes needed to make this real, and describe what
meaningful co-design would look like in practice.
Index Terms: data sovereignty, indigenous languages, speech
data governance, CARE principles, community co-design,
long-form recordings
1. Introduction
Speech technology has suffered from a data availability gap
that disadvantages low-resource and indigenous languages [1].
However, the limited amount of usable data is only part of the
problem. Equally important and less often acknowledged is that
when indigenous language dataiscollected and processed, the
governance of that data typically reflects the priorities of the re-
searchers who collected it, not the communities who produced
it.
Over four years, our team has assembled a collection of
more than 40 child-centered audio datasets spanning 18+ lan-
guages, including 10 recordings from indigenous communities
in Papua New Guinea [2, 3], Mexico [4], Bolivia [5, 6], Peru
[7], Timor-Leste [8], the Solomon Islands [9] and Vanuatu
[10]. Alongside this collection, we developed ELSI (ExELang
Legacy Support Interface), a software platform designed to al-
low researchers to manage, analyze, and responsibly share these
datasets within a tiered access framework.
ELSI is functional, holds real data, and serves a growing
research community. Currently, it is designed as a tool for re-
searchers. Control over all indigenous community recordings
in our collection lies with three European academic custodians.
Communities from where the audio comes from have no formal
role in deciding who can use that data, for what purposes, or
under what conditions.
This paper is our public acknowledgment of that gap and
our commitment to close it. We describe what ELSI currently

This tiered architecture successfully separates research
workflows from raw audio access and has enabled reproducible
benchmarking across the collection. Existing platforms for
sharing sensitive speech data, notably HomeBank [13] and
Databrary [14], have established important precedents for
tiered access control. However, both platforms are designed
aroundwhocan access the raw data. ELSI shares this concern
but goes further, separating the research pipeline into distinct
roles with different permissions (e.g. raw audio, model training,
and derived outputs are governed independently). More impor-
tantly, neither HomeBank nor Databrary provide mechanisms
for source communities to participate in governance at all. They
offer access control for researchers, not authority to communi-
ties. A community whose recordings are in HomeBank has no
formal way to be notified when their data is accessed, to veto
a particular use case, or to define what kinds of research are
permissible. This is the gap ELSI is positioned to address —
not by replacing those platforms, but by building a governance
layer that treats community authority as a central design princi-
ple rather than a secondary concern.
3. The Governance Gap
3.1. Who the custodians are
The three current ELSI Custodians are academic researchers
in France and Finland. None are members of the indige-
nous communities whose recordings the system holds. This is
not incidental, but rather reflects how the collection was built,
through researcher-led collaborations where governance came
from institutional ethics protocols rather than community con-
sent frameworks.
This is not a criticism of the individuals involved or of the
individual corpus collectors, who in many cases have deep,
long-standing relationships with the communities they work
with. But it means that formal control over recordings from
communities in Papua New Guinea, Bolivia, and elsewhere sits
entirely with people who are geographically, culturally, and in-
stitutionally distant from those communities.
3.2. Mapping ELSI against indigenous data management
best practices
A project-specific Indigenous data governance logic must op-
erate under multiple constraints. Legal and regulatory regimes
impose non-negotiable conditions that delimit what is permis-
sible within a given jurisdiction. Institutional mandates and
funding requirements further restrict operational space. Nor-
mative stewardship models such as FAIR [15] introduce aspi-
rational criteria oriented toward openness and reuse, while In-
digenous data governance frameworks such as CARE [16] or
OCAP [17] re-center authority, benefit, and ownership on com-
munities. These constraints are partially conflicting. Gover-
nance, in practice, therefore consists in constructing an accept-
2859
able configuration within a field of competing logics.
Within this layered system, ELSI begins by complying
with all applicable statutory requirements. With data stored
in France, ELSI is subject to French law, including the ”Loi
Informatique et Libert ´es” [18], research obligations under the
”Code de la recherche” [19], and European regulations such as
the GDPR [20]. As a cross-border project, governance must
also satisfy collection laws in the source jurisdictions, ensur-
ing that data were lawfully collected and lawfully transferred
out of their community and country of origin. In practice, this
entails applying the most restrictive applicable requirements at
each stage of the data lifecycle or excluding datasets whose le-
gal conditions cannot be reliably satisfied.
Once the legal architecture defines the remaining degrees of
freedom, Indigenous data governance frameworks clarify what
ELSI does not yet provide. For example, the CARE Principles
for Indigenous Data Governance emphasizeCollective Bene-
fit, ensuring that data use supports community goals;Author-
ity to Control, recognizing Indigenous peoples’ rights to gov-
ern access, interpretation, and reuse of their data;Responsibil-
ity, requiring researchers and technologists to act in ways that
strengthen communities and avoid harm; andEthics, ground-
ing data practices in Indigenous values and worldviews. The
OCAP® principles frame Indigenous data rights in terms of
Ownership,Control,Access, andPossession, where posses-
sion refers to physical custody of the data.
Operationalizing these principles is non-trivial. Communi-
ties differ in their governance structures, decision-making pro-
cesses, definitions of harm, and expectations regarding individ-
ual versus collective authority. In addition, physical custody of
data may raise data security, infrastructure, and liability con-
cerns.
Table 2 maps the current ELSI model against each CARE
principle. The tiered access model provides meaningful protec-
tion, and the non-public status of most indigenous recordings
prevents wide distribution and usage. But on the dimensions
of authority, control, and community benefit, the current ELSI
model falls short by lack of comprehensive, coherent opera-
tional guidance. Communities cannot currently veto a use case,
receive notification when their data is accessed, or define what
kinds of model training are permissible. There is no mechanism
for collective benefit to flow back to source communities.

We want to be explicit: the communities whose recordings
are held in ELSI did not design the governance structure govern-
ing their data. They consented to research conducted by local
teams, who then shared data with us under inter-institutional
agreements. This chain may be ethically adequate by stan-
dard IRB criteria, but it is not the same as meaningful data
sovereignty for the communities involved.
4. What Community Custodianship Could
Look Like
We do not consider ourselves in a position to define data gover-
nance principles on behalf of communities or to prescribe what
their priorities should be. Communities must be custodians of
their own corpora. A central challenge, however, lies in defining
what constitutes the relevant “community” for a given corpus
and how custodianship should be formally designated.
We sketch an architectural and governance proposal—not
as a completed solution, but as a structured starting point for
co-design.
4.1. A community custodian tier
The most direct change would be to create aCommunity Cus-
todianrole alongside the existing Custodian role. A com-
munity organization, whether an indigenous language board,
a community-designated data steward, or a formal indigenous
data governance body, would hold a Custodian-level credential
scoped specifically to their community’s recordings.
This would give them:
•Veto powerover any proposed use of their recordings, in-
cluding model training, publication of derived metrics, or re-
searcher access grants
•Access logsshowing who has accessed their community’s
data and for what stated purpose
•Permitted use definitions: the ability to specify which types
of analysis are acceptable (e.g., language documentation vs.
commercial speech technology development)
•Overall rights: the ability to withdraw a dataset from the col-
lection, or to require deletion of derived outputs to a reason-
able extent, should the community’s position on data sharing
change
This is technically implementable within ELSI’s existing
infrastructure, since data is already organized by corpus. The
changes required are primarily in the permissions layer and in
building the tooling (dashboards, notification systems, use-case
declaration forms) that would make these rights practical.
Indigenous data governance frameworks frequently invoke
thecommunityas the locus of authority. In practice, however,
authority over recordings may involve identifiable individuals,
family groups, local leaders, informal power structures, and
sometimes competing governance bodies. However, a language
board may not represent all speakers, a village council may not
represent all families, and individuals may not recognize the au-
thority of collective bodies.
It is, however, neither within our mandate nor within our
epistemic competence, particularly where custodial authority
is unclear or contested, to determine who/what should be rec-
ognized as the legitimate indigenous custodian(s) of a corpus.
The designation of Community Custodian(s) is therefore, in our
opinion, the most consequential and most difficult governance
decision in this model and must be part of the co-design effort.

4.2. What this is not
We want to be realistic about the limits of what ELSI can offer.
ELSI is not a mechanism for returning raw audio to communi-
ties (though we are open to discussing how that could work). It
is not a substitute for direct relationships between researchers
and communities. And a governance layer designed by Euro-
pean academics — even one that genuinely attempts to imple-
ment CARE principles — cannot be assumed to reflect what
specific communities actually want. This is why we are issuing
a call for co-design rather than announcing a solution.
5. Call to Action
5.1. What we are asking for
ELSI became, in 2025, part of Heliceo, an interdisciplinary re-
search initiative dedicated to documenting cultures, languages,
and multilingual child acquisition in the Pacific region [21].
This integration connected us directly with a network of ex-
perts experienced in collecting audio recordings in indigenous
communities, all of whom have implemented informed consent
procedures at the individual level.
However, beyond individual consent, governance practices
vary widely and are largely ad hoc. There is rarely an ex-
plicit framework guiding the process of identifying, then ob-
taining authorization from community governing bodies, of se-
curing export permissions from local authorities, or defining
who should hold ongoing access and control rights over the
data. These dimensions are typically negotiated informally,
without shared heuristics, formal documentation, or method-
ological standards.
In practice, most researchers rely on personal judgment and
on relationships built with the community. While these relation-
ships are often strong and ethically grounded, the absence of
structured governance protocols creates inconsistency and po-
tential vulnerability for both researchers and communities.
We are therefore seeking partnerships with indigenous
community organizations, language boards, data governance
bodies, and research institutions willing to collaborate on the
design of a community custodianship identification framework.
This framework would define legitimate custodial authority and
implement an access and control layer as outlined above.
We have two objectives: (1) to make the data accessible to
community members to the greatest extent they deem appropri-
ate; (2) to ensure that control over data availability, usage condi-
tions, and downstream applications rests with recognized com-
munity authorities. This includes developing the technical and
organizational tools necessary to ensure that data use produces
tangible benefits for the communities from which it originates.
We are especially interested in connecting with organiza-
tions that have existing relationships with the data collectors
whose work is represented in Table 1. We are also interested
in hearing from and collaborating with researchers who have
already navigated community co-design of data governance for
speech or language data.
5.2. What we are committing to
We commit to the following:
• Allocating engineering effort to implement whatever gover-
nance architecture results from co-design
• Granting any community partner organization a Custodian-
level review of the design before deployment affecting their
community’s data
• Publishing the co-design process and its outcomes openly, so
others can build on it
5.3. Next steps
We will convene an open working group following this ses-
sion. Community organizations, researchers, and individuals
who wish to participate can contact the corresponding author.
We will share a technical brief describing the proposed commu-
nity custodian architecture and welcome feedback before any
implementation begins.
6. Discussion
This paper makes a relatively unusual move: presenting a work-
ing system and arguing that it needs to change before it should
be expanded. We do this because we believe the speech tech-
nology community benefits from papers that model this self-
assessment, and because we think the Indigenous V oices special
session is precisely the right venue for it.
The value of a diverse and cross-linguistic collection like
ELSI is apparent. Models trained only on CHILDES, or on pub-
lic recordings from industrialized communities, exhibit system-
atic gaps in performance on indigenous and small-scale com-
munity speech [1]. Addressing those gaps requires the kind of
data we have. But it also requires that the communities who
produced that data have meaningful agency over how it is used.
We are at an early stage of the UN International Decade of
Indigenous Languages. The infrastructure choices being made
now (e.g. who controls data and which governance frameworks
get built into platforms) will shape what speech technology
looks like for these communities for a long time. Tools built
without communities are tools that serve researchers, not peo-
ple. ELSI is ready to change and we are looking for the right
partners to change it with.
