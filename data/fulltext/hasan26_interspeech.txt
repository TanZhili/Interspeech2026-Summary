Abstract
Speech emotion recognition (SER) is challenging due to the
complex interplay of spectral and prosodic features. We pro-
pose a dual-stream DNN-KAN architecture for Bangla speech,
processing MFCCs through efficient DNN blocks and statisti-
cally optimized prosodic features via lightweight Kolmogorov-
Arnold Networks (KANs) with learnable B-spline activations.
With only 1.1M parameters, our model achieves92.12%
on SUBESCO and82.79%on BanglaSER underspeaker-
independent(SI) evaluation. Most prior Bangla SER work
omits SI evaluation, so published figures likely reflect speaker
memorization rather than genuine generalization. Re-running
our feature-selection pipeline on EmoDB yields 93.92%, show-
ing that the selection methodology transfers across languages.
At 1.1M parameters, the model surpasses emotion2vec (25M)
by 2.70% and wav2vec2-xlsr (326M) by 1.07% on SUBESCO
SI, showing that statistically grounded feature engineering can
match large pre-trained models at a fraction of the cost.

1. Introduction
Speech emotion recognition enables more empathetic human-
computer interaction systems, from mental health monitoring
to customer service automation. While significant progress has
been made in SER for major languages [1, 2, 3], Bangla, spo-
ken by over 300 million people, remains relatively underex-
plored in terms of its language-specific emotional expression.
SER systems trained on English corpora perform poorly when
deployed for Bangla speakers, yet building competitive Bangla
systems by simply scaling up generic models is costly and in-
accessible to most research groups in the region. Bangla ex-
hibits phonetic characteristics including aspirated consonants
and retroflex sounds, alongside prosodic patterns whose distinct
role in emotional expression has not been thoroughly studied
despite advances in modern SER models.
Current approaches to SER follow two main paradigms.
Large pre-trained models like wav2vec2 [4, 5, 6] achieve im-
pressive accuracy through hundreds of millions of parameters
but are computationally expensive and lack interpretability for
practical deployment. Traditional CNN-LSTM [7, 8] and trans-
former [9] architectures offer better efficiency but typically pro-
cess only standard features like MFCCs, Mel spectrograms,
chroma, and zero-crossing rate (a time-domain noisiness de-
scriptor), missing language-specific prosodic patterns crucial
for emotional expression in tonal and semi-tonal languages.
Kolmogorov-Arnold Networks (KANs) [10] represent a
promising architectural innovation, employing learnable activa-
tion functions on network edges rather than fixed node activa-
tions. This design enables KANs to model complex non-linear
relationships with significantly fewer parameters than MLPs.
Prosodic features such as F0 contours are continuous, smooth,
and emotionally structured in ways that fixed activations like
ReLU approximate poorly; learnable splines are better suited to
capture these gradual shape variations, which is why we route
prosodic features through KAN modules rather than standard
DNN blocks.
We propose a dual-stream architecture (Figure 1) that pro-
cesses spectral and prosodic information through specialized
pathways. MFCCs flow through DNN blocks optimized for
spectral patterns, while statistically optimized prosodic fea-
tures, selected via discriminative analysis on Bangla speech,
pass through lightweight KAN modules for non-linear acoustic
modeling. Through this analysis we identified 15 highly dis-
criminative features (Section 3.1) from 80 initial candidates,
forming 51 prosodic descriptors capturing pitch, energy, and
temporal dynamics. The same selection pipeline applied to
other languages recovers strong performance, so the contribu-
tion is the methodology rather than features unique to Bangla.
2. Related Work
2.1. Speech Emotion Datasets and Architectures
SER research spans multiple languages through established
benchmark datasets including RA VDESS for English [11],
EMOVO for Italian [12], and EmoDB for German [13]. Bangla
emotion corpora have emerged with SUBESCO providing con-
trolled recordings [14], BanglaSER offering varied speakers
and emotional expressions [15], KBES focusing on audio inten-
sity [16], and BanSPEMO contributing spontaneous emotional
speech [17].
Recent architectures combine convolutional and recurrent
networks to capture both local patterns and temporal depen-
dencies. One approach employs 1D CNN with LSTM to pro-
cess temporal convolutions of speech signals [18], while an-
other fuses CNN with BiLSTM using both MFCC and Mel
spectrograms [19]. Vision Transformers have emerged as al-
ternatives by treating spectrograms as images and processing
them through patch-based attention mechanisms [20]. Self-
supervised models like wav2vec2 and emotion2vec [21] learn
universal speech representations; while emotion2vec is substan-
tially more compact than wav2vec2-xlsr (25M vs. 326M param-
eters), both remain 23–300×larger than our model and offer no
language-specific inductive bias.
Bangla-specific research has primarily focused on adapt-
ing standard architectures without considering the language’s
unique acoustic properties [22, 23]. Critically, the majority of
existing Bangla SER work reports speaker-dependent results,

2.2. Kolmogorov-Arnold Networks and Fusion Methods
KANs restructure neural architecture by placing learnable ac-
tivation functions on edges rather than nodes, enabling supe-
rior function approximation with significantly fewer parameters
than traditional MLPs [10]. While early applications demon-
strate promise in multimodal emotion recognition [24] and
speech deepfake detection [25], KAN remains relatively un-
explored for SER despite its natural alignment with modeling
continuous prosodic variations. Previous SER fusion strate-
gies typically employ static concatenation or weighted aver-
aging, with recent work exploring cross-attention mechanisms
that process spectral and prosodic streams through shared trans-
former blocks [26].
Our work addresses Bangla emotion recognition through
language-adapted design. Rather than applying standard fea-
tures, we conduct systematic statistical analysis (Figure 2) to
identify discriminative markers in Bangla emotional speech.
We assign distinct processors to each feature type, DNNs
for spectral and KANs for prosodic, and introduce emotion-
adaptive gating that weights streams dynamically based on
emotional state.

3.3. Cross-Attention and Emotion-Adaptive Gating
Cross-modal attention [29] captures acoustic coordination
through bidirectional exchange. Early attention (128D) en-
ables low-level coordination with spectral queries attending to
prosodic patterns, allowing the model to locate where in the
MFCC stream pitch events are occurring. Late attention (256D)
reverses roles for high-level integration, letting prosodic context
refine spectral class boundaries after both streams have been in-
dependently processed. The 0.5 weighting preserves original
features while incorporating complementary information.
Emotion-adaptive gating addresses arousal-dependent
acoustic emphasis. High-arousal emotions manifest through
prosodic changes while subtle emotions require spectral detail.

3.4. Training Configuration and Evaluation Protocol
Implementation uses PyTorch with reproducible training
(seed=42) on NVIDIA P100. Hyperparameters were selected
via grid search: learning rate5×10 −4, weight decay 0.015,
dropout 0.3, batch size 16, maximum 200 epochs with patience
50. Data augmentation was deliberately avoided: many pub-
lished works do not clarify whether augmentation is applied
before or after splitting, which can allow augmented variants
of test utterances into training and inflate results. By excluding
augmentation entirely, our comparisons are conservative and re-
producible.
3.4.0.1. Speaker-independent evaluation.
Our primary metric isspeaker-independent(SI) accuracy,
which ensures no speaker appears in both training and test sets.
For SUBESCO we use a 16/4-speaker split; for BanglaSER a
25/9-speaker split. We additionally perform speaker-wise 5-
fold cross-validation on SUBESCO to confirm stability, yield-
ing92.07%±0.91%. Pairwise McNemar’s tests [30] confirm
that accuracy differences between our model and all baselines
are statistically significant (p<0.01) on both Bangla datasets.
3.4.0.2. Speaker-dependent figures.
For completeness we also report speaker-dependent (SD) re-
sults in Table 2, where samples from the same speaker may ap-
pear in both splits. The majority of prior Bangla SER work re-
ports SD results only; these figures are inherently optimistic due
to speaker memorization and should not be treated as equivalent
to SI performance.

4.2. Cross-Dataset Evaluation
Lower performance on RA VDESS, EMOVO, and EmoDB does
not by itself establish linguistic specificity, as domain mismatch
is an equally valid explanation. To test this, we applied the same
feature-selection pipeline to EmoDB training data; accuracy
rose from 87.85% to 93.92% (Table 3), showing the method-
ology transfers across languages and that the cross-language
drop reflects domain shift rather than an architectural limitation.
The prosodic-only configuration drops most sharply on Western
datasets (52.3% on RA VDESS), while the MFCC-only config-
uration degrades far less, further supporting that the prosodic
features are tuned to the distributional properties of the Bangla
training data rather than to any universal acoustic pattern.
The features in our selected set (F0 statistics, HNR, energy)
are broadly useful across languages [28]. The term “statistically
optimized for Bangla” refers to the specific subset, thresholds
(η2 >0.94), and multi-scale encoding identified from Bangla
data, which differ from the optimal configuration for other lan-
guages, as the EmoDB experiment confirms.

4.3. Ablation Study
Table 4 analyzes individual component contributions through
systematic ablation on SUBESCO (SI). Each architectural ele-
ment provides measurable gains, with cross-attention and emo-
tion gating contributing 1.64% and 0.81% respectively.
Statistical feature selection is the single most critical com-
ponent. Removing it from the full model (feeding all 80 can-
didates) costs 3.58% SI, while adding the selected prosodic
features to the baseline gains +3.31% (Table 4). Replacing
KAN with DNN for prosodic processing costs 1.27% in the
full model; its incremental contribution in Table 4 is +1.22%.
Overall the full model gains +12.29% SI over the DNN-only
baseline.
4.4. Discussion
Prior Bangla SER work has largely applied generic architec-
tures without language adaptation and evaluated in speaker-
dependent settings, which inflates reported accuracy. Un-
der speaker-independent evaluation, our 1.1M-parameter model
still matches SSL models of 25–326M parameters, showing that
for a known target language with limited compute, statistically
grounded feature selection can substitute for large-scale pre-
training. It is also more interpretable, since the selected features
and their discriminative weights can be inspected directly. We
note three open limitations. Feature-discriminability analysis
was conducted only on SUBESCO, and the 9.33-point SI gap to
BanglaSER suggests corpus-adaptive selection would be bene-
ficial. The KAN component yields a modest standalone gain,
and a comparison with alternative non-linearities would more
rigorously justify the architectural choice. Finally, the domain-
shift interpretation for cross-dataset results is supported by the
EmoDB retraining experiment but lacks a formal distributional
analysis, which we leave for future work.
5. Conclusion
This work shows that statistically grounded feature selection
enables competitive SER without large-scale pre-training. By
introducing KAN to speech emotion recognition and develop-
ing a transferable prosodic feature pipeline, we achieve strong
speaker-independent accuracy with only 1.1M parameters. The
evaluation framework adopted here, with SI as the primary met-
ric and explicit SD/SI separation sets a more rigorous standard
for future Bangla SER research and we encourage the commu-
nity to adopt it. The pipeline and architecture are directly appli-
cable to other low-resource languages facing similar challenges.
