Abstract
Dementia is neurological disorder that causes loss of cognitive
functions primarily in older adults. Early dementia detection is
essential to ensure professional treatment and increase the qual-
ity of life of affected people. In this paper, we propose a mul-
tilingual approach to the diagnosis of dementia with the use of
speech foundational models. We investigate the effectiveness of
using acoustic multilingual features for cognitive impairments
detection. The work evaluates several popular speech founda-
tional models such as Whisper, WavLM, Hubert, Wav2Vec2
with custom downstream heads. This pipeline is trained in sin-
gle or multilingual settings and additionally evaluated on un-
seen languages to test their zero shot capabilities of predict-
ing dementia. This pipeline achieves results of 0.854 F1 score
average on combined English datasets and 0.761 F1 score in
broader, more challenging multilingual setting.

1. Introduction
Dementia is a cognitive impairment occurring frequently among
older adults, that in general, results from the degeneration of
neurons or disturbances in other body systems that affect how
neurons function. These changes cause the manifestation of
symptoms such as memory loss, communication and language
impairments, agnosia, apraxia and impaired executive function
[1]. According to a report [2] currently more than 55 million
people worldwide suffer from dementia, and there are nearly 10
million new cases every year. The given statistics indicate it is
becoming an increasingly growing problem.
In this work, we cast the detection of cognitive impair-
ment as a binary classification problem based on biomarkers de-
rived from speech recordings. Early detection and intervention
are crucial to mitigate further deterioration and facilitate timely
clinical support. According to [3], speech deficits such as lexi-
cal, sentence and discourse impairments are strongly associated
with the occurrence of dementia. Such distinctive changes in
the voice indicate a great possibility of using them for classifica-
tion. Speech signals have the benefit of being gathered naturally
and continuously throughout the day, enabling the collection of
substantial data without burdening the patients [4].
Despite the rapid development of multimodal approaches,
many recent studies rely heavily on speech-based models, as
they have become an industry standard for audio representa-
tion learning. Foundational speech encoders such as Wav2Vec
2.0, HuBERT, and WavLM are pre-trained on large-scale cor-
pora and have demonstrated strong performance across a va-
riety of downstream tasks. However, their effectiveness typi-
cally depends on access to vast amounts of labeled or unlabeled
data, which makes them challenging to utilize in low-resource
or specialized clinical scenarios such as dementia detection. To
date, there has also been no comprehensive comparison of these
leading speech encoders across a broad range of datasets and
languages within the context of cognitive impairment detection.
Key contribution of this study is addressing this gap by system-
atically evaluating the most popular speech encoders in both
monolingual and multilingual settings, and by further analyz-
ing their transferability on unseen languages in a zero-shot sce-
nario, where models are trained on a selected set of languages
and tested on others.
Contribution:We present an evaluation of popular speech
foundational models such as: Wav2Vec 2.0, HuBERT, Whisper
and WavLM on combined dementia corpora. We show that in-
cluding other language even when building a monolingual sys-
tem, can greatly increase performance. Our system achieved
0.831 ROC AUC and 0.761 F1 score on combined multilingual
corpora. We also present an insight into zero shot prediction
of dementia on unseen language, as was done for English with
0.786 ROC AUC. To the best of our knowledge this is the first
work presenting multilingual dementia detection on more than
two languages.
2. Related work
Given the limited availability of effective disease-modifying
therapies for dementia, early detection of cognitive decline is
essential for planning, risk-factor management, and timely sup-
port. Mild cognitive impairments (MCI) is often viewed as
a prodromal stage on the dementia/Alzheimer’s disease (AD)
continuum, and guidelines recommend systematic assessment
and longitudinal monitoring [5, 6].
Speech and language change offer a scalable, non-invasive
digital biomarker: temporal/acoustic cues can separate AD
from healthy aging [7], and fully automatic speech analy-
sis can distinguish healthy controls, MCI, and early AD [8].
Benchmark shared tasks have improved comparability, no-
tably ADReSS (Cookie Theft description; AD classification
and MMSE regression) [9] and ADReSSo (fully automatic
pipelines, decline prediction) [10]. Recent systems leverage
pretrained representations (e.g., wav2vec 2.0 + BERT), achiev-
ing strong ADReSSo results [11, 12, 13], with LLM-based aux-
iliary evaluation further improving performance [14].

3. Dataset
Most of the data used in this work were obtained courtesy of De-
mentiaBank, a part of the TalkBank [23] project. The selection
criteria for datasets were: the quality, amount of data present
(N≥40) and task similarity, with focus on describing images.
The only major exception from this rule was the Ivanova [24]
dataset, with recordings from a reading task. Exact scenar-
ios and sample counts are visible at Table 1. The choice of
similar tasks was intended to limit the ability of any model to
adapt to a given dataset based on the distinguishing features of
the task. The final datasets obtained via DementiaBank were:
ADReSS, ADReSSo, ADReSSM, TAUKADIAL, Dem@Care
and Ivanova. Table 1 shows detailed information about each
dataset.
To further expand our multilingual evaluation, we included
additional Polish dataset DiagNeuro. In [25], this dataset was
originally collected in a clinical setting using a mixed reality
(MR) environment to capture multimodal biomarkers. In our re-
search, we focused exclusively on high-quality acoustic record-
ings from image description tasks, which provide a solid refer-
ence point and align well with DementiaBank datasets.
Although some datasets include disease-specific labels
(e.g., AD or PD), in our combined corpus all non-healthy partic-
ipants correspond to dementia (i.e., an dementia stage is present
irrespective of the underlying etiology). Therefore, we treat the
prediction target as dementia vs. healthy control and harmonize
labeling by mapping all non-healthy participants to a single de-
mentia class, even when the recorded diagnosis indicates AD,
PD, or another condition. This formulation reflects our focus
on speech markers of cognitive decline shared across clinical
etiologies.
4. Methods
We adopt a standard pipeline inspired by SUPERB [26] and re-
lated works [27, 11, 28]. Speech segments are processed by
a selected speech encoder composed of multiple transformer
layers. Layer representations are aggregated using a learned
weighted mean. The resulting segment-level representation is
passed to a downstream classifier that predicts the probability
of dementia. During inference, segment-level probabilities are
averaged to obtain a participant-level score. Final classification
is based on a validation-selected threshold. The overall archi-
tecture is shown in Figure 1. The simplicity of this pipeline is
one of the key assumptions of this work, as we believe that in
this form it, it is aligned with the building blocks of the largest
number of the monomodal or multimodal architectures gener-
ally researched. This furthers improve the transferability of our
findings into other works.
4.1. Data preprocessing and diarization
Many recordings contain both participant and investiga-
tor speech, requiring diarization. When transcripts with
timestamps were available (e.g., DementiaBank), they were
used to obtain near-perfect speaker separation. Otherwise,
pyannote.audio[29, 30] was applied. Recordings were seg-
mented into fragments containing at least 6 seconds of partici-
pant speech. We acknowledge that diarization errors may influ-
ence segment-level aggregation.
If the dataset had an official, train/val/split it was retained.
Otherwise or in the case of TAUKADIAL (see section 6), a dis-
ease stratified subject split was performed, which assigned 70%
of subjects to the training split, and 10% and 20% were kept for
validation and testing.
4.2. Foundational speech encoders
We evaluate multiple pretrained speech encoders commonly
used in prior benchmarks [26, 28, 27]. The selected models
(see Table 2) were chosen to represent diverse large-scale pre-
trained architectures. For all encoders, transformer layer out-
puts are combined using learned weights and passed as a two-
dimensional tensor to the downstream classifier. The embed-
ding processing pipeline is identical across models.
4.3. Downstream model
We evaluate three downstream classifiers. Following [27], we
primarily use a lightweight ECAPA-TDNN with variable hid-
den sizes of 16, 32, or 64. Larger configurations led to overfit-
ting. Additionally, we test two feed-forward architectures: one
with self-attention pooling to reduce the time dimension, and
one requiring temporal aggregation before classification. Hid-
den sizes of 256, 128, and 64 were evaluated, with the second
layer set to half of the first. Dropout of 0.3 was applied for reg-
ularization. Reported results correspond to the best-performing
downstream model.
4.4. Training
We trained this pipeline with the learning rate and weight decay
set to 0.0001, using AdamW optimizer with exponential decay
set to reduce learning rate by 5% after each epoch. Batch size
was set to 32, and 30s fragments of the recordings were used.
The segments were randomly sampled from the recording ev-
ery time, meaning that if the duration of the recording was one
minute. It was counted as two samples in the dataset, and re-
sulted in two random segments being sampled. Each segment
could start at any point within the recording, as long as the re-
maining part was at least 30s. If a recording was shorter than
30 seconds, padding was applied. The NVIDIA hardware was
used for training in two environments, first with x8 A100 GPUs
with CUDA 12.9 and second x4 RTX PRO 6000 with CUDA
13.1.
5. Results
We compared monolingual and multilingual training strategies
to evaluate cross-lingual generalization. In the monolingual
setup, models were trained and tested on the same target lan-
guage. In the multilingual setup, models were pretrained on a
set of other languages and evaluated directly on the target lan-
guage (cross-lingual transfer, Tables 4,5). Additionally, we ex-
amined a fine-tuned variant, where the multilingual model was
further adapted to the target language.

5.1. Speech models evaluation
We benchmarked four widely used self-supervised speech en-
coders: Wav2Vec 2.0, HuBERT, WavLM, and Whisper. These
models were selected due to their architectural diversity and
strong performance across speech-related downstream tasks. To
ensure a fair comparison, each encoder was frozen and inte-
grated with the same set of downstream classification heads. All
experiments were conducted with identical hyperparameters,
isolating the impact of the encoder itself on dementia detection
performance. This controlled setup allowed us to directly com-
pare representation quality across models Table 2. The WavLM
family achieved the best overall performance, reaching a 0.761
F1 score in the multilingual setting. It also led in the English
and Greek subsets and ranked second in all other languages.

At the beginning of the study found some files duplicated
between various datasets creating possibility for data leakage.
This imposed a necessity of cautious data inspection, which
resulted in several datasets being discarded and some being
shrunk e.g. all training files for ADReSSM challenge are also
present in previous ADReSS challenges. The duplicates were
detected based on filename or other metadata similarity. In
cases where we couldn’t find clear algorithm, the entire prob-
lematic dataset had to be removed, this is the main reason be-
hind not including the popular Pitt and Delaware datasets.
We believe that the results obtained with our solution
demonstrates that it is possible to build a multilingual cogni-
tive impairment detector that is more robust to language vari-
ation within the evaluated corpora, with multilingual training
improving monolingual inference. In fact, our multilingual ex-
periments show a strong synergistic effect: including multiple
languages during the training phase has a positive effect, im-
proving the model’s predictive ability for individual languages.
This suggests foundational models learn universal pathological
markers from a diverse corpus, enhancing accuracy when ap-
plied to specific target languages.
We also observed that input audio length is a crucial hyper-
parameter, because durations vary across corpora, tailoring the
sample training length to the dataset’s average optimizes model
performance. We suspect that, especially in the case of patients
with mild cognitive impairment, a substantial part of the record-
ing may be non-informative for classification; this should be
addressed in future research with segment-level XAI (e.g., attri-
bution over segment embeddings) and validated under potential
diarization noise.
