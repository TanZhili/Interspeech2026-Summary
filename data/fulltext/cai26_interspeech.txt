Relative Importance of Formants to the Intelligibility of Vocoded Speech in 
Cochlear Implant Simulation 
Ying Cai, Yuting Ding, Xuefei Wang, Fei Chen 
Department of Electrical and Electronic Engineering, Southern University of Science and 
Technology, Shenzhen, China 
fchen@sustech.edu.cn 
 
Abstract 
This study examined the relative significance of formants for 
the intelligibility of vocoded speech, which simulates the 
speech processing of cochlear implant (CI) speech processors. 
Mandarin sentences were edited to generate sine -wave speech 
that retained only the trajectories of the first three formants. 
Three experimental conditions were designed: each removed 
one of the first three formant trajectories, leaving two formant 
trajectories intact. Results indicated that the second formant 
trajectory contributed most to intelligibility, while the third had 
the least impact, aligning with findings from wideband (non -
vocoded) speech. However, vocoder model parameters (e.g., 
envelope cut -off frequency and number of frequency bands) 
regulated this relative importance. These findings provide 
insights for optimizing CI speech processor design to deliver 
critical spectral information for improving CI users ’ speech 
understanding. 
 
Index Terms : cochlear implants, vocoder simulation, sine-
wave speech 
1. Introduction 
Cochlear implants (CIs) have been successfully utilized to 
restore partial hearing in individuals with profound -to-severe 
hearing loss, whereas traditional hearing interventions (e.g., 
hearing aids) fail to facilitate meaningful hearing restoration for 
this population [e.g., 1-3]. The speech processor plays a pivotal 
role in delivering key acoustic cues that underpin sound 
perception, speech comprehension, and music appreciation in 
CI users [e.g., 4-5]. In this context, two critical considerations 
emerge: elucidating the individual perceptual contributions of 
specific acoustic cues to CI -mediated speech perception, and 
investigating how CI speech processing strategies modulate the 
perception of these essential acoustic cues. 
Currently, most CI speech processors extract multiple 
temporal envelopes and use them to modulate the amplitude of 
electric pulse trains, which in turn stimulate residual auditory 
neurons. The temporal envelope has been shown to convey 
limited yet perceptually sufficient information, at least in quiet 
listening conditions, as documented in numerous simulation 
and clinical investigations [e.g., 1, 6-7]. Although most CI users 
achieve reasonable speech understanding in quiet, several 
persistent challenges rem ain with current CI technologies, 
particularly for speech comprehension in adverse listening 
environments such as noise and reverberation  [e.g., 8]. Under 
these difficult conditions, the limited envelope cues conveyed 
by conventional CI processing are often inadequate to support 
robust speech understanding. One major factor contributing to 
this degraded performance is the restricted spectral information 
extracted and transmitted by contemporary CI speech 
processors. First, due to constraints in electrode design, current 
clinical devices typically employ no more than 20 + electrodes 
distributed along the cochlea, resulting in substantially coarser 
spectral resolution compared with the fine frequency selectivity 
available to normal -hearing (NH) listeners [e.g., 9] . Second, 
standard CI strategies extract only the temporal envelope 
waveform, while discarding temporal fine structure and phase 
information. A large body of research has highlighted the 
importance of temporal fine structure and phase cues for speech 
intelligibility, especially in noisy backgrounds [10]. Because of 
these inherent limitations, CI users often experience difficulties 
in tasks demanding fine spectral discrimination, including pitch 
perception and the recognition of tonal languages. 
Speech signals comprise a rich set of acoustic cues 
supporting speech perception, many of which convey 
overlapping or complementary information. The high -
dimensional nature of speech is partly reflected in the fact that 
speech intelligibility can remain re latively intact even when 
certain acoustic cues are degraded or removed. Accordingly, 
numerous studies have explored the quantity and type of 
acoustic information necessary for speech recognition, 
especially under quiet listening conditions.  Sine-wave speech 
(SWS) is constructed to provide a sparse representation of the 
speech acoustic structure, using frequency - and amplitude -
modulated sine waves that track vocal tract formants  [11-12]. 
This approach offers a valuable experimental framework for 
investigating the perceptual contribution of formants to speech 
recognition, while eliminating other acoustically important 
features such as harmonic structure and fundamental frequency 
contour. Feng et al. evaluated Mandarin SWS recognition and 
reported high sentenc e identification accuracy among native 
Mandarin-listening participants [13]. Xu reported that the SWS 
represented both spectral and temporal structures of the speech 
and that the temporal envelopes imbedded in SWS carr ied 
important information for speech recognition  [14]. Han and 
Chen examined the relative perceptual weights of the first three 
formants (F1, F2, F3) in NH listeners. Their results indicated 
that the second formant trajectory was the most influential for 
speech intelligibility, whereas F3 show ed the least perceptual 
impact among the three formants  [15]. Consistent with these 
observations, prior research has also highlighted the functional 
significance of F2 for speech intelligibility. For example, 
Woodall and Liu observed that combining spectral 
enhancement of F2 (achieved by boosting the F2 peak by 3, 6, 
and 9 dB ) with elevated overall speech level improved vowel 
formant discrimination performance in listeners with hearing 
impairment [16]. 
Building on earlier findings regarding the perceptual role of 
formants, the first objective of the present study was to further 
investigate their relative contributions in the context of speech 
understanding with cochlear implants. This work offers novelty

2. Methods  
2.1. Subjects and materials 
Ten native NH Mandarin listeners with NH  (six males, aged 
19–25 years and mean = 22 years) were recruited via 
convenience sampling from the Southern University of Science 
and Technology. All participants received financial 
compensation for their involvement.  Stimuli consisted of 
sentence materials taken from the Mandarin version of the 
Hearing in Noise Test (MHINT) [ 17]. The MHINT database 
includes 24 lists, each comprising 10 ten -syllable Mandarin 
sentences. All sentences were spoken by a male talker with 
fundamental frequency ranging from 75 to 180 Hz . The 
experimental protocol involving human subjects was reviewed 
and approved by the Ethics Review Committee of Southern 
University of Science and Technology. 
2.2. Signal processing 
When synthesizing sine -wave sentences, formant trajectories 
carrying frequency-varying and amplitude-varying information 
were first extracted from the original speech signal via linear 
predictive coding analysis, using a 16 -ms Hanning window 
with 50% overlap between adjacent frames. The MATLAB 
code for implementing the aforementioned SWS processing is 
accessible at https://www.haskinslaboratories.org/sws [Last 
viewed on 5 February 2026]. Four types of sine-wave sentences 
with varying amounts of formant cues were generated. The first 
condition included all three formant trajectories (i.e., Condition 
F1F2F3), while the other three conditions excluded one formant 
trajectory each to examine it s impact on the intelligibility of 
sine-wave sentences: Condition F1F2 (excluding Formant F3), 
Condition F1F3 (excluding Formant F2), and Condition F2F3 
(excluding Formant F1). Spectrograms of an example 
unprocessed sentence and its corresponding processed  sine-
wave sentence (Condition F1F2F3) are presented in Fig . 1 (a) 
and (b). 
The sine-wave sentences underwent additional processing 
using a noise vocoder [ e.g., 6]. For the vocoder -based speech 
processing, the input signal was first pre -amplified with a pre-
emphasis filter (a first-order high-pass filter with a 1200 Hz cut-
off frequency) and then divided into N frequency bands (N = 4 
and 8 in the present study) via fourth -order Butterworth band-
pass filters covering the range of 80 to 7600 Hz [ 18]. The cut-
off frequencies for the N=4 band -pass filters were [80, 416, 
1214, 3108, 7600 Hz], while those for the N=8 band-pass filters 
were [80, 212, 416, 730, 1214, 1960, 3108, 4876, 7600 Hz]. The 
output of each band -pass filter was subjected to full -wave 
rectification and low-pass filtering (a fourth-order Butterworth 
filter with a cut-off frequency fcut-off of 100 or 200 Hz) to extract 
the temporal envelope. This envelope was then multiplied by 
white noise, and the resulting signal was filtered using the same 
band-pass filter as the corresponding frequency band. Finally, 
the amplitude-modulated noise from all bands were summed to 
generate the noise -vocoded stimulus, whose amplitude was 
scaled to match the root -mean-square energy of the original 
input signal.  Figure 1 (c) exemplifies the s pectrogram of a 
noise-vocoded sine-wave sentence (Condition F1F2F3).

circumaural headphone at a comfortable listening level. Prior to 
the formal testing session, listeners completed practice with 40 
non-experimental sentences (under the vocoded  [N=8, f cut-
off=200 Hz] F1F2F3 condition) accompanied by feedback. Each 
listener participated in 16 experimental conditions, derived

4. Discussion and Conclusions 
This study extend ed prior research investigating the relative 
contributions of the first three formants to speech perception 
within the framework of CI speech processing. The outcomes 
of this work deepen our understanding of the perceptual 
significance of formants for speech  recognition. First, 
consistent with earlier findings from normal -hearing listeners 
[e.g., 15] , the present study found that among the first three 
formants, F3 had the smallest influence on speech intelligibility. 
Removing F3 from forman t-based synthesis did not 
significantly alter speech understanding scores under both 
wideband (non-vocoded) and vocoded conditions. In contrast, 
both F1 and F2 exerted substantial effects on speech 
intelligibility: removing either F1 or F2 during sine -wave 
speech synthesis led to considerable reductions in sine -wave 
speech recognition. Regarding the relative importance of F1 
and F2, results indicated that F2 was relatively more influential 
than F1, as illustrated in Fig. 2 (b) and (c), where removing F2 
resulted in the lowest performance scores. This pattern of 
relative importance for F2 aligns with previous observations 
[15]. 
Notably, this study also found that parameters of CI speech 
processing can modify the relative perceptual contributions of 
F1 and F2. Figure 2  (c) demonstrates that when the envelope 
cut-off frequency was lowered to 100 Hz, the difference in 
recognition scores between the F1F2 and F2F3 conditions 
became non -significant (marked ‘ns’). Figure 2  (d) further 
shows that when the number of frequency bands was reduced 
to N=4, F1 (in the F2F3 condition) became relatively more 
important than F2 (in the F1F3 condition). These findings 
highlight that the perceptual influence or relative weighting of 
formant cues can vary across different listening conditions. CI 
speech processing imposes distinct temporal and spectral 
constraints on speech perception. Reduced spectral resolution , 
simulated here using a small number of frequency bands (N=4), 
may differentially degrade the perceptual weighting of F1 and 
F2. Using  a sine -wave synthesis paradigm with selective 
formant preservation, the present study observed that F1 carried 
a greater perceptual weight than F2 under CI simulations with 
limited spectral resolution. Lowering the envelope cut -off 
frequency also affected the relative contributions of F1 and F2, 
although results in Fig. 2 (c) suggest this effect was modest. 
These findings carry meaningful implications for the 
development of CI speech processing strategies. When spectral 
cues (e.g., formant trajectories) are transmitted using a 
restricted number of frequency bands, careful consideration 
must be given to the se lection of the number of bands. On one 
hand, it is important to preserve the natural perceptual hierarchy 
of formants that supports human speech communication. On the 
other hand, optimized design choices  (such as frequency band 
allocation) can be employed to maintain or even strengthen the 
perceptual salience of target formants. 
Future research may expand upon this work in several 
directions. The present study evaluated formant contributions 
under quiet listening conditions; however, noise masking is 
likely to modulate these effects and overall speech recognition 
performance. How noise influences the relative perceptual roles 
of formants thus warrants further investigation. Additionally, 
this study was conducted using Mandarin Chinese, a tonal 
language in which pitch perception strongly supports speech 
intelligibility. It will be c ritical to assess whether the present 
findings generalize to non -tonal languages such as English. 
Furthermore, the current sine -wave synthesis preserved both 
formant frequency and amplitude information. Previous work 
has examined the separate contributions  of formant frequency 
and intensity, reporting that in some listening conditions, 
removing amplitude cues did not significantly affect sine-wave 
speech intelligibility [ e.g., 20 ]. The relative importance of 
formant frequency versus intensity cues in CI spe ech 
understanding could therefore be explored in greater detail. 
In conclusion, this study investigated the perceptual 
contributions of formants to CI speech recognition using a sine-
wave speech synthesis and perception paradigm. Results 
revealed that F3 had the least impact on intelligibility, with 
speech understanding relying primarily on F1 and F2. Overall, 
F2 was relatively more important for sine -wave speech 
recognition than F1. However, this advantage was modulated 
by key parameters of CI speech processing. In particular, the 
number of frequency bands used in CI si mulations was found 
to substantially alter the relative perceptual contributions of F1 
and F2.
