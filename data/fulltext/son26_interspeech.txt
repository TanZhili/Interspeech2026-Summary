Teacher-Agnostic Temporal Knowledge Distillation for
Resource-Efficient Sound Event Detection

Abstract
Sound event detection (SED) needs frame-level detection, yet
recent work has focused on high-capacity models, leaving
resource-efficient SED relatively underexplored. We propose
teacher-agnostic temporal knowledge distillation (TAT-KD), a
knowledge distillation (KD) framework for SED that distills
from any teacher architecture by using teacher logits as a com-
mon distillation space. To transfer temporal context, we intro-
duce a conformer-based temporal context projector that mod-
els temporal dependencies during projection and a teacher-
confidence-aware distillation loss that downweights ambigu-
ous outputs via normalized confidence weights. On the do-
mestic environment sound event detection benchmark, TAT-
KD outperformed training from scratch and logit-/feature-based
KD, achieving polyphonic sound detection score of 0.574 with
4.548M parameters and 3.668G multiply–accumulate opera-
tions, showing competitive performance with far fewer param-
eters and computations than state-of-the-art models.
Index Terms: sound event detection, knowledge distillation, ef-
ficient deep learning
1. Introduction
Sound event detection (SED) aims to recognize which acous-
tic events occur and to localize their temporal boundaries via
frame-level activity predictions [1]. As a core task in the detec-
tion and classification of acoustic scenes and events (DCASE)
challenge [2–5], SED has been widely studied and adopted in
applications such as smart homes, robotics, and smart cities
[6–11]. Building on the convolutional recurrent neural net-
work (CRNN) baseline [12], subsequent studies have enhanced
SED models with improved convolutional representations while
maintaining relatively lightweight architectures [13–19]. Nev-
ertheless, under constrained parameter and compute budgets,
compact models often fall short of state-of-the-art (SOTA)
performance. Recent studies report SOTA performance with
large-capacity models, including transformer-based architec-
tures [20–24]. However, these accuracy gains often come with
substantially larger model size and higher computational cost,
making them less attractive under computational and memory
constraints. As a result, bridging the accuracy–capacity gap re-
mains a central challenge in resource-efficient SED.
Knowledge distillation (KD) [25] is a framework for train-
ing a compact student by transferring knowledge from a pow-
erful teacher. In SED, however, teacher–student KD has been
relatively underexplored, which is different from the mean
teacher framework [26] widely adopted for semi-supervised
learning. To the best of our knowledge, there is limited prior

work on KD for SED, and the reported performance gains re-
main insufficient [27]. Distillation for resource-efficient SED
is challenging: effective KD typically relies on strong teach-
ers whose architectures often differ substantially from those
used in resource-constrained students, making heterogeneous
teacher–student pairs inevitable. In principle, most logit- and
feature-based KD methods can be applied in such heteroge-
neous teacher–student settings. However, heterogeneous distil-
lation is often unstable, as teacher and student representations
can differ substantially and contain architecture-specific redun-
dancy, making standard KD approaches less effective [28, 29].
These challenges call for a teacher-architecture-agnostic distil-
lation interface that can reliably distill knowledge from teachers
with arbitrary architectures into a student model, while account-
ing for temporally structured, frame-level predictions in SED.
In this work, we propose teacher-agnostic temporal knowl-
edge distillation (TAT-KD), a distillation framework tailored to
frame-level SED and robust across heterogeneous teachers. In-
spired by one-for-all KD (OFA-KD) [28], TAT-KD uses teacher
logits as a common distillation space and attaches stage-wise
projectors to map student features into the logit space dur-
ing training, which are removed afterward to preserve infer-
ence efficiency. However, OFA-KD is formulated in image-
classification settings, and directly adopting it for SED is non-
trivial. SED requires frame-level transfer of temporally contex-
tualized teacher knowledge to guide time-varying event activity
and boundary localization. As a key component for frame-level
temporal knowledge transfer, we introduce a temporal context
projector (TCP) that aligns feature dimensions with the teacher-
logit space and models temporal dependencies using a Con-
former block [30]. We further propose a teacher-confidence-
aware distillation (TCAD) loss that emphasizes reliable teacher
predictions via normalized confidence weighting.
Experiments on the domestic environment sound event de-
tection (DESED) benchmark [2] show that TAT-KD delivers
large gains over a student trained from scratch and consistently
outperforms standard logit-based KD [25] and feature-based
KD [31] for every teacher–student pair. In particular, a com-
pact SE-CRNN student [19] achieves polyphonic sound detec-
tion score (PSDS) [4] of 0.574 with only 4.548M parameters
and 3.668G multiply–accumulate operations (MACs), showing
competitive detection performance with substantially fewer pa-
rameters and computations than SOTA models.

2.1. Temporal Context Projector
The TCP is a conformer-based projector for KD in SED, de-
signed to transfer temporal context from teacher outputs to stu-
dent. In SED, temporal dependencies across frames are reflected
in the temporal patterns of teacher logits, and capturing these
dependencies is important for effective knowledge transfer.
The TCP consists of three components. A single convolu-
tional layer followed by a pooling layer first aligns the tem-
poral resolution of the student hidden features with that of the
teacher logits. A Conformer block then models temporal de-
pendencies by jointly capturing short-range temporal patterns
through convolution and long-range temporal relationships via
self-attention. By incorporating both local and global tempo-
ral context, the TCP facilitates effective frame-level knowledge
transfer. Finally, a multi-layer perceptron (MLP) serves as a
projection head that maps the temporally enriched features to
the class dimension of the teacher logits, ensuring dimensional
compatibility for distillation. Figure 1 depicts the detailed struc-
ture of the TCP module.

3. Experimental Setup
3.1. Dataset
All experiments were conducted on the DESED benchmark,
which serves as the official dataset for DCASE 2023 Chal-
lenge Task 4 [2] and targets SED in domestic environments. The
training set consisted of 10-second audio clips, including 1,578
weakly labeled clips, 3,470 strongly labeled clips, 10,000 syn-
thetic strongly labeled clips, and 14,412 unlabeled in-domain
clips. We evaluated on the real validation set, which contains
1,168 strongly labeled clips.
3.2. Evaluation Metrics
We evaluated the SED performance using PSDS. PSDS is
commonly reported under two settings, PSDS 1 and PSDS 2,
which emphasize different aspects of detection. PSDS 1 places
a stronger emphasis on temporal localization, while PSDS 2 is
more tolerant to cross-class confusions and less strict about
event onset and offset boundaries. Following the DCASE chal-
lenge evaluation protocol [32], we report results under the
PSDS1 setting, which emphasizes temporal localization. For
brevity, we refer to PSDS 1 as PSDS throughout the paper. In
addition, we also reported model resource efficiency in terms of
the number of parameters and MACs.

3.3. Implementation Details
3.3.1. Student Models
We adopted SE-CRNN [19] as a lightweight student for
resource-efficient SED, offering competitive detection perfor-
mance with a small footprint. To assess robustness across stu-
dent model sizes, we built smaller SE-CRNN variants by re-
ducing encoder and context-network channel widths while pre-
serving the architecture. We refer to these variants asSCk with
k= 32,16,8,and4, wherekdenotes the number of output
channels in the first convolutional layer. Table 1 provides details
of the student models, including parameter counts and MACs.
All student models distilled with KD methods were trained
for 400 epochs with a learning rate of5×10 −4. Each training
batch was formed by combining real strongly labeled, synthetic
strongly labeled, real weakly labeled, and real unlabeled data,
with batch sizes set to 3, 3, 6, and 12, respectively.
3.3.2. Teacher Models
To demonstrate that TAT-KD enables effective distillation re-
gardless of teacher architecture, we considered three teach-
ers with distinct architectures, namely ATST-SED [22], JiT-
TER [23], and MDFD-SED [18], as summarized in Table 2.
ATST-SED leverages a Transformer as an extra encoder, while
JiTTER builds on MAT-SED [24] as its baseline and improves
the training strategy. CRNN-based models such as MDFD-SED
often underperform large-capacity models on DESED, so we
additionally included MDFD-TAT as a stronger CRNN-based
teacher. MDFD-TAT was obtained by distilling JiTTER into
MDFD-SED using our TAT-KD, and thus shares the same archi-
tecture as MDFD-SED. Notably, MDFD-TAT achieved a com-
petitive PSDS of 0.577 with minimal parameter count and com-
putation among the teachers considered in this work, making it
a strong CRNN-based teacher for our KD experiments.

3.3.3. KD Baselines
We compared TAT-KD with two standard KD baselines, as
KD methods specifically designed for SED remain largely un-
explored. Logit-based KD [25] uses the BCE loss between
the teacher and student logits along with the supervised loss.
Feature-based KD [31] uses the mean squared error loss be-
tween the teacher and student hidden features and the BCE loss
between the teacher and student logits, both along with the su-
pervised loss. For feature-based KD, the CRNN-based teachers
in Table 2 and SE-CRNN have the same number of encoder
stages, which allows stage-wise feature matching. In contrast,
such stage-wise matching was not feasible for ATST-SED and
JiTTER due to architectural differences, so we applied the fea-
ture loss only to the final hidden representation.
4. Results and Analysis
4.1. Comparison with Other KD Methods
Table 1 compares TAT-KD with the KD baselines across the
four teacher models in Table 2. TAT-KD achieved the best re-
sults for all teacher–student pairs, consistently outperforming
the KD baselines and surpassing models trained from scratch.
The gains were smaller when MDFD-SED was used as the
teacher, likely due to its limited detection performance, but
TAT-KD still improved over the KD baselines. Notably,SC 32
distilled from MDFD-TAT reached PSDS of 0.574 with only
4.548M parameters and 3.668G MACs, achieving competi-
tive performance with substantially fewer parameters and lower
computational cost than SOTA teachers in Table 2.
Among the teachers, MDFD-TAT yielded the strongest
overall distillation results, which we attributed to its architec-
tural similarity to the student. In contrast, ATST-SED achieved
the best PSDS among the teachers in Table 2 but yielded smaller
distillation gains, suggesting that its post-processing-dependent
outputs may be less suitable as soft targets. We further analyzed
this effect in the ablation study.
4.2. Effectiveness of TCP
Table 3 summarizes the effect of different projectors in the TAT-
KD framework. We fixed the teacher to MDFD-TAT, which
yielded the strongest KD performance in Table 1, and replaced
only the projector while keeping the rest of the framework
unchanged. Across all student variants, the TCP consistently
yielded the best distillation performance, outperforming MLP-
and convolutional neural network (CNN)-based projectors that
mainly focus on dimensional alignment. This result suggested
that temporal dependency modeling in the projector is impor-
tant for frame-level SED distillation, and that the TCP benefits

We summarized the impact of the proposed TCAD loss in Ta-
ble 4. To isolate its contribution, we conducted two compar-
isons. First, we replaced the BCE loss in logit-based KD with
the TCAD loss. Second, within the full TAT-KD framework,
we substituted the TCAD loss with the BCE loss. In both
cases, the TCAD loss yielded better performance, suggesting
that confidence-aware weighting based on teacher predictions
benefits knowledge transfer.

4.4. Ablation Study
As shown in Table 2, ATST-SED is the best-performing SED
model among the considered teachers, but it was less effective
for distillation than JiTTER and MDFD-TAT. Table 5 indicates
that ATST-SED relies more heavily on median filtering, a post-
processing technique commonly used in SED, with its PSDS
dropping from 0.583 to 0.495, the largest decrease among the
teachers. This suggested that ATST-SED produced less stable
frame-level outputs, which can make KD more challenging.
5. Conclusion
We proposed TAT-KD for resource-efficient SED, enabling
teacher-agnostic distillation via teacher logits as a common dis-
tillation space. With the TCP and the TCAD loss, TAT-KD
achieved the best performance across all evaluated teacher–
student settings on DESED, outperforming training from
scratch and standard KD baselines while preserving inference
efficiency.
