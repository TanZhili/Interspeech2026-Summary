Predict-Then-Adapt: Inferring Coordinates from Speech for Continuous
Geo-Conditioned Dialectal ASR
Pouya Mehralian∗∗, Hugo Van hamme
Department of Electrical Engineering (ESAT-PSI), KU Leuven, Belgium
pouya.mehralian@kuleuven.be, hugo.vanhamme@kuleuven.be
Abstract
Geo-conditioned adaptation can improve dialectal ASR, but
most methods assume speaker coordinates are available at in-
ference. We remove this dependency by inferring location
from speech at test time and conditioning on the predicted
coordinates. We attach a lightweight Coordinate Regression
Head (CRH) to a frozen encoder and train it to regress lati-
tude/longitude from utterance-level representations. Inference
uses two passes: (i) predict coordinates with geo-conditioning
disabled, then (ii) decode with geo-conditioning enabled con-
ditioned on the prediction. CRH adds<0.1%parameters
and increases inference latency by<3%. On Dutch dialects,
CRH achieves 15–25 km mean great-circle error using 10–
30 s of speech, and the GLoRIA method is robust to errors
in this range. As a result, CRH+GLoRIA outperforms rank-
matched LoRA and approaches oracle-coordinate adaptation
performance, while preserving GLoRIA interpretability via an-
alyzable coordinate-gated low-rank components.

1. Introduction
ASR performance on dialectal speech remains limited despite
large pre-trained models, due to systematic phonetic/lexical
variation and scarce annotated dialectal data. Geographic in-
formation is a particularly effective and interpretable signal in
dialect-rich settings, since regional varieties often exhibit strong
spatial structure [1, 2]. Recent work showed that condition-
ing on continuous coordinates can improve generalization com-
pared to discrete dialect labels [3].
GLoRIA [4] is a parameter-efficient, interpretable geo-
conditioned adaptation method that extends LoRA [5] by modu-
lating low-rank update directions with coordinate-driven gates.
While effective, such coordinate-conditioned approaches as-
sume access to speaker coordinates at inference, which is often
unavailable, noisy, or undesirable to use in practice.
This paper removes that dependency by inferring loca-
tion from speech at test time and applying geo-conditioning
with the inferred coordinates. We attach a lightweight Co-
ordinate Regression Head (CRH) on top of a frozen encoder
to regress latitude/longitude from utterance-level representa-
tions, and perform two-pass inference: first predict coordi-
nates with geo-conditioning disabled, then decode with geo-
conditioning enabled conditioned on the predicted location. Ex-
periments on Dutch dialects show that CRH attains tens-of-
kilometers localization accuracy from short segments, that geo-

2. Related Work
2.1. Dialectal ASR
Dialect-heavy ASR is commonly addressed via (i)dialect-
specificmodels, which can be effective but require maintaining
multiple systems and substantial compute [6, 7], and (ii)uni-
fiedmodels that incorporate dialect information during train-
ing or decoding. Unified approaches typically condition on
categorical dialect labels or embeddings injected into the en-
coder/decoder [8–10], use mixture-of-experts or hierarchical
routing to specialize subnetworks [6, 11], or apply multi-task
learning with auxiliary dialect classification objectives [10, 12].
While effective provided enough data is available per dialect
class, this is an approximation of the dialectal variation contin-
uum, with strongest errors in the transition regions.
2.2. Continuous geo-conditioning for ASR
To better reflect gradual geographic variation, recent work
leveragescontinuouscoordinates rather than discrete dialect
IDs, enabling smooth interpolation across regions and improved
robustness to unseen varieties [3]. GLoRIA follows this line
by performing parameter-efficient, interpretable adaptation with
coordinate-driven gates that modulate low-rank update direc-
tions. A practical limitation shared by geo-conditioned methods
is the assumption that coordinates are available (and reliable) at
inference time.
2.3. Speech-based coordinate inference
Predicting location from speech has been studied in related
tasks such as geolocating speakers and geolocation-aware lan-
guage identification. Foley et al. [13] introduce a large-scale
speech geolocation setup and show that geolocation models can
serve as useful proxy tasks for LID. Wang et al. [14] propose
geolocation-aware spoken LID by predicting geolocation as an
auxiliary signal and conditioning intermediate representations
on the predicted vectors to improve robustness to accents and
dialectal variation. For dialectology, [15] studies audio-based
Austrian dialect classification and geographic regression.
In contrast to these works, the goal here is not geolocation
per se, but enabling geo-conditioned ASR when metadata is
missing: a lightweight coordinate regression head predicts co-
ordinates from the utterance, which are then used to activate
the same coordinate-gated adaptation mechanism, preserving
the interpretability of the underlying geo-conditioning.

3. Dataset
All experiments are performed using the GCND corpus [16] 1,
comprising 411 hours of spontaneous Dutch dialect speech
recorded in Belgium, the southern Netherlands, and French
Flanders. Each recording segment is manually transcribed and
annotated with accurate geographical coordinates correspond-
ing to the interview location. Speakers were selected based on
strong local attachment, having lived most of their lives in their
respective villages, thereby ensuring the presence of stable and
representative regional dialects. Sessions contain an average of
3.8 speakers and span a wide range of informal conversational
topics. These characteristics make GCND a suitable resource
for geographically informed ASR adaptation.
4. Pretrained Model
The backbone of the system is the Cascaded Encoder Dual
Features framework [17, 18]. The model follows an en-
coder–decoder architecture with a 12-layer Conformer [19] en-
coder and a 6-layer Transformer decoder. Model training uses
a hybrid objective that integrates weighted cross-entropy and
CTC losses [20, 21]. It was trained on 14k hours of Dutch
speech data and further fine-tuned for five epochs on dialectal
Dutch. The Dutch-only instantiation contains 180M parameters
and achieves superior performance on dialectal Dutch compared
to Whisper large-v3 [22] and OWSM-CTC-V4 1B [23], while
using fewer parameters.
5. Method
We extend GLoRIA to the setting where geographical coordi-
nates are unavailable at inference time by inferring a speaker
location from the speech signal and then applying the standard
geo-conditioning using the inferred coordinates. Concretely,
given an utterancexand transcripty, training data provides a
locationc∈R 2, while at test timecis unknown. We there-
fore estimateˆc=ϕ(x)and decode with the geo-conditioned
modelp(y|x,ˆc). The proposed solution adds a lightweight
Coordinate Regression Head (CRH) on top of the encoder and
performs inference in two passes: (i) predictˆcwith GLoRIA
disabled, and (ii) enable GLoRIA and decode conditioned onˆc.

5.2. Coordinate Regression Head (CRH) + GLoRIA
We attach a lightweight Coordinate Regression Head (CRH) on
top of the frozen encoder to infer speaker location directly from
speech. LetH= [h 1,...,h T ]∈R T×D denote the encoder
output sequence. CRH mapsHto a coordinate estimate ˆc∈R 2
using multi-head attentive pooling followed by an MLP regres-
sor. The pooling mechanism follows attentive (statistics) pool-
ing used for utterance-level representation learning [24].

5.3. Two-pass inference with inferred coordinates
5.3.1. Pass 1: coordinate inference (GLoRIA off).
We disable GLoRIA contributions in the encoder (i.e., bypass
low-rank updates or equivalently set the gates to zero) and com-
pute encoder outputsH. CRH then predictsˆc=ϕ(H).
5.3.2. Pass 2: geo-conditioned ASR (GLoRIA on).
We enable GLoRIA, provideˆcto the gating networks, and run
the ASR forward pass to decode the transcription using the geo-
conditioned encoder.
This design adds only one extra encoder pass and the small
CRH; since the computationally dominant decoder/beam search
runs once, measured two-pass inference latency overhead re-
mains below 3%. Fig. 1 depicts the training and inference.
6. Experimental Setup
6.1. Dataset and Dialect Partitioning
The corpus contains nine dialect regions. Dialects with≥50
recordings are split 80/10/10 into train/validation/test; smaller
regions are excluded from training and contribute 10% of
their data to test only. Training dialects are Brabants, Frans-
Vlaams, Oost-Vlaams, Oost-Vlaams>Brabants, and West-
Vlaams. Test-only dialects are Limburgs, Limburgs>Brabants,

Vlaams>Zeeuws, and West-Vlaams>Oost-Vlaams. The splits
are strictly disjoint to prevent data leakage, with no shared
speakers or coordinates across train, validation, and test sets.

6.2. Model and Training Configuration
Audio is resampled to 16 kHz. We extract 80-dim mel fil-
terbanks with 3 pitch features (25 ms window, 10 ms shift),
apply utterance-level normalization. SpecAugmentation [26]
is applied with time warping (window=5, bicubic interpola-
tion), two frequency masks (width range 0–30), and two time
masks (width range 0–40). Models are implemented in ESP-
net [27]. The encoder is a 12-layer Conformer(d model = 512,
df f = 2048, 8 heads, kernel size 31) with 6-layer Conv2d sub-
sampling and relative positional encoding. The decoder is a
6-layer Transformer (8 heads,d f f = 2048, dropout 0.1). A
2-layer subtitle encoder shares the same dimensions. GLoRIA
is applied to conformer feed-forward layers and cross-attention
modules. GLoRIA’s Gate-MLPs are two-layer networks (hid-
den size 32) with GeLU activation and Softplus output. The
CRH uses 2 attention heads and an MLP with hidden size 64
with relu hidden activation.
Training uses gradient accumulation of 128. LoRA, GLo-
RIA and CRH run for 50 epochs. New parameters use Xavier
uniform initialization; gate-MLPs are zero-initialized. Opti-
mization uses Adam (lr=0.001) with WarmupLR (variant of the
Noam warmup followd by an inverse square-root decay sched-
ule [28]) and 1500 warmup steps.
7. Results
7.1. Utterance length vs. great-circle accuracy of CRH
We begin by analyzing how much speech is required for reliable
coordinate inference. Fig. 2 and Tab. 1 show a clear duration
effect: CRH great-circle error drops sharply as utterances grow
from a few seconds to∼20–30 s, after which gains saturate. The
same trend holds across splits, with consistently higher error
under dialect shift (held-out dialects) than on training dialects.
To disentangle geographic support effects from dialect
identity, we further distinguishinterpolationandextrapolation
ranges. Interpolation—where evaluation locations lie within
the spatial support of training coordinates—achieves markedly
lower errors and saturates early (≈15.44 km at 30 s). In con-
trast, extrapolation remains consistently harder (≈38.17 km at
30 s), and the gap persists across durations. This indicates that
CRH operates reliably within the trained coordinate manifold,
whereas projecting beyond it is substantially more challenging
even with longer speech segments.

sharpen further and outliers reduce. Overall, the maps corrob-
orate a trend: increasing duration not only reduces average er-
ror, but also yields predictions whose spatial organization better
matches established dialect geography at a sub-regional level.

7.3. ASR without coordinates: CRH+GLoRIA vs. LoRA
We next compare our proposed CRH+GLoRIA pipeline against
a rank-matched LoRA baseline. Both methods adapt the same
180M-parameter backbone with comparable parameter effi-
ciency: LoRA and GLoRIA update just under 10% of the pre-
trained parameters during adaptation, while CRH introduces
only∼150k additional parameters (<0.1%of the backbone).
Importantly, CRH+GLoRIA uses only the speech signal (10 s
segments in this experiment), making the comparison with
LoRA fair in terms of test-time inputs.

8. Conclusion and Discussion
We addressed a key limitation of geo-conditioned ASR: the re-
liance on coordinate metadata at inference. We removed GLo-
RIA’s reliance on coordinate metadata at inference by infer-
ring speaker location from speech. A lightweight Coordinate
Regression Head (CRH) is trained on frozen encoder repre-
sentations to predict coordinates, which are then used to acti-
vate GLoRIA geo-conditioning in a two-pass inference scheme.
This retains GLoRIA’s parameter efficiency and interpretability
while requiring only the input utterance at test time.
CRH localization improves with utterance duration and sat-
urates around 20–30 s, reaching 15–25 km mean error. GLo-
RIA is robust within this range: perturbations up to 15 km have
negligible impact and remain below 1 absolute WER point at
25 km, with degradation increasing rapidly beyond that. Thus,
CRH accuracy lies well within GLoRIA’s tolerance to coor-
dinate mismatch. In the no-metadata setting (10 s segments),
CRH+GLoRIA improves over rank-matched LoRA by 3.05%
WER on average and remains within 0.58% WER of oracle geo-
conditioning, while large general-purpose ASR models exhibit
substantially higher error rates on this dialectal task.
Future work should target extrapolation by incorporating
uncertainty-aware conditioning (e.g., top-k or distributional co-
ordinates). In addition, it should conduct a component-level
analysis of GLoRIA to understand what each gated low-rank
rank component is doing—how these updates capture dialect
shifts—and whether the cues driving those adaptations align
with the cues CRH uses for coordinate inference.
