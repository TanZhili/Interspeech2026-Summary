Abstract
In constructed languages, creators often represent the personal-
ity or character traits of a fictional people linguistically through
phonaesthetics, e.g., Tolkien’s Orkish, developed to sound harsh
and evil, vs. the Elvish languages Sindarin and Quenya, de-
signed to sound elegant and pleasant. The present study investi-
gates whether these design principles are applied to the creation
of character names for fictional heroes and villains in German.
Participants were asked to invent names for good or evil fic-
tional characters based on two syllable-sized building blocks
they selected out of four alternatives. These syllables varied in
levels of sonority, nucleus length and CV-complexity. Results
show significant differences between the characteristics of syl-
lables selected for naming good and evil characters. GLMERs
show significant effects of character (good or evil) on the sonor-
ity of syllables selected for naming as well as an influence of the
other selected syllable’s sonority.
Index Terms: speech production, phonaesthetics, sonority,
vowel length, naming, sound symbolism
1. Introduction
In the context of fantasy and science fiction literature, the
process of world-building frequently involves the creation of
new languages. These so-called constructed languages (hence-
forth ‘conlangs’) frequently employ design principles based on
phonaesthetics and sound symbolism in order to evoke spe-
cific attitudes towards the protagonists [1, 2]. Most famously,
Tolkien invented the Elvish languages Sindarin and Quenya to
sound elegant and pleasant, whereas Orkish was designed to
sound evil and harsh [3]. The aim of this study is to investi-
gate whether lay people use similar design principles for cre-
ating names for villains and heroes. Previous studies showed
that names ofgoodcharacters in Tolkien’s Middle Earth [4]
are constructed with a larger proportion of front vowels (also
in diphthongs), voiced obstruents and sonorants thanevilchar-
acters. This is in agreement with previous rating studies on the
phonaesthetics of natural languages [5, 6] and conlangs [2, 1]
which found that the percentage of voicing, the overall sonority
and the ratio of vowels to consonants significantly increased the
perceived pleasantness of a stimulus. Further evidence comes
from the field of ‘Pok´emonastics’ dealing with the evolution of
Pok´emon character names [7, 8, 9], showing that shorter names
with bilabial consonants indicate smaller characters, whereas
longer names with voiced obstruents are associated with more
powerful and older characters. For Disney characters, [8] found
that the names of villains were composed with significantly
more voiced obstruents and less bilabials than names of non-

overall sonority seem to play a role for associations with good-
ness and pleasantness [5, 10, 1] with more complex syllables,
including clusters, being rated more negatively.
While the influence of phonaesthetics has been established
for a few existing conlangs, open questions remain. In particu-
lar, it is yet unclear how far the described principles generalise
and what causes them. Findings on sound symbolism support
a certain universality of speakers to match the perceptual prop-
erties of sounds or letters with the properties of named entities
[11, 9]. Similar observations have been found in the phonetic
coding of gender in first names, with femininity often expressed
with –highly sonorous– voiced consonants and rounded vowels,
while masculinity is expressed more often with –less sonorous–
voiceless consonants and unrounded vowels [12, 13, 14]. How-
ever, the world’s languages allow for wildly different syllable
structures, and it is unclear how the above-mentioned trends
generalise cross-linguistically and cross-culturally [14].
In our study, we explore whether phonaesthetic principles
generalise across speakers when they are tasked toinvent new
namesexpressinggoodorevilfantasy characters. In particular,
we expect to find a preference for more sonorous syllables in
naming heroes (good) and a preference for less sonorous syl-
lables for naming villains (evil). Given the above-mentioned
influence of gender, we will furthermore investigate the effect
of character gender on character naming.
2. Methodology
The task design of the presented study is inspired by [15]’s
product naming task. Instead of fictitious products, as in [15],
participants were given character images of eithergoodorevil
characters as their subject for naming. Participants were pro-
vided with predefined syllable-sized building blocks as their
initial inventory for developing character names. These were
varied with respect to their phonaesthetic features. Our study is
comprised of two experiments:
Experiment I (Section 2.1) was a pre-test, conducted to find
a set of images that embody the character traitsgoodandevil
and are clearlyfemale,androgynous 1 ormale. The best rated
images (cf. Fig. 1) were then shown to participants as the char-
acters to name in Experiment II.
Experiment II, the naming task (Section 2.2), was designed
with two parts: first, participants (always in a dyad) were to se-
lect two out of four given syllables to create the name for the
character image they are shown. Second, the speech produc-
tion part, where the selected syllables were to be used to create
names for the characters. The present study investigates the

2.1. Experiment I: Image Rating
2.1.1. Image Generation
Images of male, female and androgynous looking villains and
heroes were generated using the AI Flux Image Generation
Demo [17], hosted at the local university. To reduce gender
and age biases, the prompts had to be very specific. Here is an
example for a heroine: “Create the picture of a female hero with
fantasy elements in a fantasy world. She should be between 30
and 40 years old. The image should be bright, taken in daylight
and have little background. The focus should be on the figure
and the face should be clearly visible from the front, with the
head fully visible.” Altogether 45 pictures were generated, with
an additional one as an example for the instructions.
2.1.2. Implementation
The experiment was programmed using jsPsych [18] and imple-
mented as a browser-based web experiment 2. After receiving
brief instructions to the task, participants were presented with a
character image and asked to rate the character in each of four
categories. Each category was presented as a free slider with
labeled poles for the extreme values 0 and 100. The categories
were labeled as:
• Good vs. Evil
• Male vs. Female
• Young (20 years) vs. Old (70 years)
• Likable vs. Unlikable
The experiment followed a fully within-subjects design:
Every participant rated every character. To avoid learning or
repetition effects, the design was maximally randomised. The
order of the trials was randomised per-participant. The order of
the categories was randomised per-trial. Additionally, the poles
of the scales were rearranged at random per-trial separately for
each category.

The experiment was hosted on-premises using JATOS [19].
An open link to the experiment was propagated through insti-
tutional channels and mailing lists. Participation was anony-
mous and voluntary and participants did not receive compensa-
tion for completing the experiment. No demographic data was
collected.
2.1.3. Ratings & Analyses
28 participants completed the ratings of 45 images each. Three
ratings, each one by different participants, were excluded be-
cause of an inattentiveness check in data preprocessing (when
all scales remained rated at 50), yielding a total of 1,257 ratings.
For the optimal set for Experiment II, the images rated
as most evil-male, evil-androgynous, evil-female, good-male,
good-androgynous, good-female were selected. In case of two
pictures with identical median values, the one that was rated as
more un-/likeable was chosen as villain or hero, respectively.
The median of all ratings of each image was used. Based on the
distribution of the data, the categories for each scale were set at:
• gender:male≤9;androgynous≥10 &≤79;female≥80
• character:good≤36;evil≥64
For the male villain, the likeability score was additionally used
to decide between two identically rated images. The final set of
6 images used for the naming experiment is shown in Fig. 1.

2.2. Experiment II: Naming Heroes and Villains
2.2.1. Experimental Design
The six character images in Fig. 1 encode the two factors gen-
der (levels: androgynous, female, male) and character (levels:
good/hero, evil/villain; (3×2=6)).
Potential phonaesthetic effects are controlled using the
phonological features sonority index and nucleus length. All
items are canonical German syllables following the definition
of [15], based on syllables by [21]. Sonority is represented via
an SI, according to the numeric values given to phone classes
in [20] (cf. Table 1). Each syllable’s sonority was computed
by averaging over the sonority indices of its consonants. A
syllable’s sonority was categorised as high (hi) with an SI>
10.5 and low (lo) with an SI<8.5. All vowels are excluded
from the syllables’ SI, as nucleus length is manipulated inde-
pendently. Nucleus length differentiates between short vow-
els (V) and phonologically long vowels and diphthongs (VV).
Phonaesthetics is, thus, designed as 2×2=4, yielding a total
factorial design of 6×4, requiring 24 syllables. Syllable bins

2.2.2. Implementation
Similarly to Experiment I, Experiment II was programmed in
jsPsych and hosted locally. The two levels of the experiment
were implemented using consecutive trials. In the first, partici-
pants were presented with a character image as well as an indi-
cation whether this character wasgoodorevil. They were then
tasked to select two syllables from a 2×2 matrix, containing ex-
actly one syllable from each of the predefined syllable bins, by
clicking on them.
The selected syllables reappeared in order of selection high-
lighted below the character image. The participants were then
prompted to come up with a name for this character utilising
the syllables. If they did not finish the trial after 90 seconds, a
message appeared on screen instructing them to move on to the
next trial.
Each dyad saw each character and each syllable exactly
once. The mapping of syllable choice to character image was
randomised per-dyad. Additionally, the position of the syllables
within the 2×2 matrix was randomised per-trial. Through the
randomisation process, 93 different combinations of syllables
were presented for selection in the 96 trials. The three repeated
combinations had different positions in the 2×2 matrices they
were presented in. The task itself consisted of two parts, the
first being the selection process, the second a speech production
task which is not considered here.
2.2.3. Recordings & Set Up
For Experiment II, 32 native speakers of German (f 19, m 12,
non-binary 1; age range 19–35, mean/median = 25) participated
in 16 dyads. Audio recordings of the entire task were made on
campus and each recording session lasted about 10 to 15 min-
utes per dyad. Participants were sat at opposite sides of a desk
in one sound booth with a transparent plastic divider between
them for channel separation. The laptop running the experiment
was positioned at one end of the desk.

3. Results
Across all 96 trials (6images×16dyads) with two selected syl-
lables each (n = 192), each of the 24 syllables was chosen at
least once with the most frequent one chosen 13 times (mean
= 7.01, median = 7). Looking at the syllable bins (Table 2),
25.52% of these syllables have high sonority and a long nu-
cleus (hi.VV;n= 49), 31.77% high sonority and a short vowel
(hi.V;n= 61), 12.50% low sonority and a long nucleus (lo.VV;
n= 24) and 30.21% low sonority and a short vowel (lo.V;
n= 58). The distribution of selected syllables for heroes and
villains differs significantly (χ2 :p<0.001; cf. Fig. 2) with a
preference of hi.V for heroes and lo.V for villains, confirming
an effect of sonority.

4. Discussion
The results show that the syllable selections for character names
of heroes and villains differ both in syllable bins and in syllable
combinations. As expected, there is a substantial increase in
low sonority syllables in villains compared to heroes (Table 2,
Fig. 2). It shows mainly in the amount of syllables with short
vowels and less in those with long nuclei. This result is partially
in line with, partially contrary to our expectations, as syllables
with lo.V were expected to be the preferred choice for villains,
and hi.VV syllables expected as the preferred ones for heroes,
not the hi.V ones.
While there are significantly more combinations made with
low sonority syllables of both nucleus lengths for villains than
heroes, the most selected combination for villains includes both
ends of the sonority spectrum as lo.V hi.VV . For heroes, it
seems that including an hi.V syllable in the combination was
most important.
Thus, while sonority appears to be the most influential fac-
tor for the selection task, it is not sufficient to explain all of our
findings. Rather, speakers tend to combine strongly alternating
sonority profiles when building names for villains, while heroes
tend to have names with a more balanced and higher sonority
profile. Somewhat surprisingly, and contrary to previous re-
search (see Section 1), there was no clear-cut gender effect in
our results. This may indicate that in line with arguments from
[14], it is necessary to investigate cultural and cross-linguistic
factors contributing to gender effects on proper name building.
[14, 13] also found evidence for individual segments indicat-
ing gender across languages, which was not investigated here.
Similarly, the differences found in [8] regarding the use of more
voiced obstruents or less bilabials in Disney villains could have
influenced the selected syllables and their combination. Fur-
thermore, the nuclei could have influenced the selections in a
more differentiated fashion [20].
Future research will concentrate not only on the selection of
syllables as building blocks in a character naming task, but also
regard the materials collected in the second part of our study,
where two speakers co-construct a suitable name for fantasy
characters in an interactive fashion. This analysis will take into
account the final name that is being created, but also consider
the suggestions until this consensus has been reached. Here,
both syllable-level and segment level sonority features will be
taken into account in the analyses.
