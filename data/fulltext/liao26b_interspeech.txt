Abstract
Prosodic boundary detection has traditionally relied on man-
ual annotations such as ToBI labels, which can be resource-
intensive and are not always available in large speech corpora.
This paper presents a weakly supervised framework that de-
rives high-confidence prosodic boundary anchors from acoustic
cues, without depending on manually labeled prosodic bound-
aries. We adopted a hierarchical anchor construction proce-
dure in which long pauses were first used to identify a con-
servative set of boundary candidates, and pitch reset and en-
ergy reduction were then used to refine these candidates into
progressively stricter anchors. These anchors were designed to
emphasize precision over coverage and served as reliable posi-
tive instances for positive–unlabeled (PU) learning. By framing
prosodic boundary detection as a boundary strength estimation
problem, we employed PU learning to infer continuous bound-
ary strength scores over all candidate junctures under weak su-
pervision. Experiments on a large-scale Japanese speech cor-
pus demonstrated that meaningful prosodic boundary patterns
can be recovered from acoustic cues using PU learning, thus
providing an interpretable, data-efficient approach to prosodic
boundary modeling.

1. Introduction
Prosodic boundaries play a crucial role in structuring spoken
communication, enhancing intelligibility, discourse coherence,
and expressive intent [1, 2]. Accurate modeling of these bound-
aries supports a range of downstream speech and language pro-
cessing tasks, including speech synthesis, automatic speech
recognition (ASR), punctuation restoration, and spoken lan-
guage understanding [3, 4, 5, 6]. However, most existing bound-
ary detection systems depend on manually annotated prosodic
frameworks such as ToBI, which are labor-intensive, require
expert knowledge, and remain unavailable for many languages
and corpora [7, 8, 9].
To reduce reliance on manual labels, prior work has ex-
plored automatic and weakly supervised boundary detection,
often combining acoustic and linguistic cues [10, 11, 12, 13,
14]. Classic phonetic studies identify pause duration, pitch re-
set, energy variation, and boundary-adjacent lengthening as ro-
bust correlates of boundary strength [15, 16, 17, 18, 19, 20].
Perceptual work also shows that pitch and timing cues can in-
teract in judgments of duration and prosodic grouping [21]. De-
spite progress, many learning-based approaches still assume la-
beled training data or adopt dataset-specific heuristics.

In parallel, positive–unlabeled (PU) learning provides a
principled framework for learning when reliable negatives are
difficult to define [22, 23, 24]. This setting is a natural fit for
prosody: strong boundaries can be identified with high con-
fidence, while “no-boundary” cases are often ambiguous and
context-dependent. A key open problem is how to construct
reliable positive anchors for prosodic boundary learning under
weak supervision.
We propose an interpretable, hierarchical framework that
constructs high-confidence prosodic boundary anchors directly
from acoustic evidence, prioritizing precision over coverage.
We treat each word juncture as a candidate boundary and aim
to infer a continuous boundary strength score rather than re-
produce a specific manual label inventory (e.g., ToBI break in-
dices). First, we define a conservative anchor set (B1) using
long pauses [15, 16]. Second, we refine B1 using additional
acoustic constraints on pitch reset and energy change with data-
driven (quantile-based) thresholds [18, 19], yielding a looser
anchor set (B2 base) and a stricter subset (B2 strict). We then
use PU learning to propagate from these sparse positives and
produce graded boundary strength estimates over the full can-
didate set.
We evaluate the method on a large-scale Japanese speech
corpus and show that the resulting anchors and PU scores ex-
hibit consistent, interpretable separation across acoustic dimen-
sions, supporting the validity of the proposed anchors as high-
precision boundary candidates. Our contributions are: (1) a
weakly supervised approach for constructing high-confidence
prosodic boundary anchors without manual labels; (2) a hier-
archical anchor design that explicitly controls the precision–
coverage trade-off; and (3) empirical analyses demonstrating
that meaningful prosodic boundary structure can be recovered
from acoustic cues alone.
2. Data and Methods
2.1. Speech Corpus
Experiments in this study are conducted on a large-scale
Japanese speech corpus derived from the JVS corpus, which
consists of recordings from 100 native Japanese speakers with
balanced gender distribution [25]. The corpus contains studio-
quality read speech covering a wide range of phonetic contexts
and speaking styles. After segmentation, we extract a total of
164,323 candidate word junctures (boundaries between consec-
utive word intervals) for analysis. No manual prosodic bound-
ary annotations, such as ToBI labels, are used at any stage of the
proposed framework. Word-level time boundaries are obtained
automatically from the provided transcripts using an energy-
based segmentation and a token-to-time allocation procedure to
generate TextGrids. We retain only the word tier and define

2.2. Acoustic Feature Extraction
For each candidate juncture at timetb, we extract acoustic cues
commonly associated with prosodic boundaries: pause duration
P , pitch reset magnitude |∆F 0|, and energy change ∆RMS
[15, 19, 18]. Pause duration is computed within the inter-word
gap (using silence phones when available, otherwise the word-
gap duration).
Fundamental frequency (F0) and intensity are extracted at
a 10 ms step using Praat/Parselmouth with an autocorrelation-
based pitch estimator (75–500 Hz) [26, 27, 28]. We compute
robust pre-/post-boundary statistics using median values in two
windows: [tb−0.3, tb) and [tb, tb+0.3] s. Pitch reset is defined
in semitones as|∆F 0| =

2.3. Stage 1: Pause-Based Boundary Anchors (B1)
In the first stage, we identify a highly conservative set of
prosodic boundary candidates based solely on pause duration.
Intervals with pause duration exceeding a fixed threshold of 200
ms are labeled as B1 boundaries. This threshold is chosen to
prioritize precision over recall and reflects widely observed ten-
dencies in prosodic boundary realization, where long pauses are
strongly associated with major prosodic breaks [15, 16]. Pause
is used here only as an anchoring cue, not as a complete account
of duration-related boundary marking such as final or prebound-
ary lengthening.
Intervals not meeting this criterion are treated as unlabeled
and are not assumed to be negative examples. The resulting B1
set serves as a high-confidence pool from which more refined
boundary anchors are derived.
2.4. Stage 2: Acoustic Refinement Within the B1 Pool (B2)
In the second stage, we further refine boundary candidates
within the B1 pool using additional acoustic constraints. Specif-
ically, we consider pitch reset magnitude and energy reduction
across the boundary [19, 18, 17]. Rather than fixing absolute
thresholds, we adopt a data-driven approach in which cutoffs
are determined using high quantiles of the corresponding fea-
ture distributions computed within the B1 set.
Applying a pitch reset constraint yields a looser refined an-
chor set, denoted as B2 base. A stricter anchor set, denoted as
B2strict, is obtained by additionally enforcing an energy reduc-
tion constraint. This hierarchical design allows explicit con-
trol over the trade-off between anchor coverage and confidence
while maintaining interpretability. We use quantile thresholds
to obtain corpus-adaptive cutoffs without manual tuning: by
selecting the high tail (for |∆F 0|) and low tail (for ∆RMS )
within an already conservative pool, we keep the rule inter-
pretable while controlling anchor sparsity.
2.5. Anchor Design for Weak Supervision
The extracted anchors are designed for learning under weak su-
pervision. B1 provides a high-precision pool based on long
pauses, and B2 refines B1 using quantile-based thresholds on
|∆F 0| and optionally ∆RMS , enabling explicit control over
the precision–coverage trade-off [15, 19]. In our PU setting, we
use B2base as the positive set for training, while the remaining
candidates are treated as unlabeled [24, 22, 23]. The stricter
set B2strict is retained as a higher-precision subset for diagnos-
tic analyses (e.g., sanity checks), rather than as the sole train-

3. Experiments
3.1. Training data and labeling scheme
PU training and scoring are conducted on the pitch-valid sub-
set of candidate junctures, where reliable F0 is available in both
the pre- and post-boundary windows. Following the hierarchi-
cal anchor design, we treat B2 base as the positive set P and
the remaining pitch-valid candidates as unlabeled U, which is
a mixture of boundary and non-boundary instances. B2 strict
is reserved as a higher-confidence anchor set for analysis and
diagnostics due to its small size.
3.2. Model and nnPU objective
We train a lightweight MLP scorer under the non-negative PU
(nnPU) risk estimator with logistic loss[23, 22, 24]. Let f(·)
denote the model output logit. The PU score is defined as
σ(f), whereσ(·) is the sigmoid function, and is interpreted as
a continuous prosodic boundary strength. We use class prior
π = 0.01 in nnPU training; π is treated as a hyperparameter
and we report sensitivity results across multipleπ values in Ta-
ble 2 [29].
3.3. Implementation details
The scorer is a 1-hidden-layer MLP with 32 hidden units and
ReLU activation, followed by a linear output layer. We in-
tentionally use a lightweight architecture so that the evaluation
focuses on the anchor construction recipe and PU formulation
rather than on model capacity. We optimize nnPU risk using
AdamW with learning rate10−3 and weight decay10−4. Train-
ing proceeds for up to 4000 steps with early stopping (patience
8) based on the nnPU risk estimated on a speaker-disjoint de-
velopment set. We split speakers into 80% training and 20%
development sets, and enforce that both splits contain positive
anchors whenever possible. At each update, we sample 256
positive and 256 unlabeled instances with replacement. In-
put features for the MLP include five standardized cue vari-
ables: zP (pause duration), zF0 times v (pitch reset× voic-
ing), zRMS (∆RMS ), and the pre-/post-boundary voicing ra-
tios (pre voiced ratio, post voiced ratio). Feature
standardization statistics are computed on the training split and
applied to all subsets.
Missing feature values (rare) are imputed with the column
median.

4.2. Sanity check: score separation
Figure 2 shows PU score distributions for mutually exclusive
strata in the pitch-valid subset: U, B1-only, B2 base-only, and
B2strict. Scores exhibit a clear monotonic ordering from U to
B2strict, consistent with the intended confidence hierarchy of
the anchors. Notably, B2 anchors concentrate near high scores,
while the broad distribution over U suggests that the model gen-
eralizes beyond sparse anchors and produces graded strength
estimates over the full candidate set.
4.3. Cue-score consistency
Figure 3 examines how acoustic cues vary across PU-score
deciles. Pause remains near zero for most deciles and spikes
only at the highest decile, suggesting that long pauses charac-
terize only the strongest boundary instances. In contrast,|∆F 0|
increases more steadily with score, indicating that the learned
strength scale captures graded pitch-reset effects beyond pause
alone. For intensity, we define ∆RMS =RMS post−RMS pre
(dB), where negative values indicate post-boundary attenuation.
Across deciles, ∆RMS trends toward zero and can become
slightly positive at high scores, consistent with the fact that
strong boundaries may be followed by intensity reset (phrase-
initial strengthening) rather than uniform attenuation; this does
not contradict the stricter B2 strict rule, which serves only as a
conservative positive anchor. Crucially, ∆RMS is used only
to define a conservative anchor subset (B2 strict) and is not as-
sumed to be a monotonic correlate of boundary strength across
all contexts. Overall, these cue–score trends support an inter-
pretable boundary strength scale learned from sparse anchors.

4.4. External proxy check: punctuation enrichment
As an external consistency check without manual prosodic la-
bels, we use punctuation in the transcript as a noisy proxy
for major junctures in read speech. A candidate boundary
is marked as punct if a Japanese punctuation mark (e.g.,
a Japanese comma or full stop) occurs immediately adjacent
to the corresponding word boundary in the transcript. Ta-
ble 3 shows strong enrichment of punct boundaries in high-
confidence anchors, while unlabeled candidates remain mostly
non-punctuation. Ranking by PU score gives high top precision
(Prec@1%=0.956; Prec@5%=0.926) and correlates with this
proxy (AUC=0.740).

4.5. Perceptual audit: blinded break judgments
To complement the punctuation proxy with a perception-based
validation, we conducted a lightweight blinded perceptual audit.
We randomly sampled 60 boundaries from the high-confidence
anchor set B2 strict (High) and 60 from a low-score unlabeled
set (Low; unlabeled candidates with near-zero pauses and PU
scores in the bottom quantile). For each boundary, we presented
raters with a short audio clip centered at the juncture, including
0.6 s of context before and after the inter-word gap; for very
long pauses, the silent interval was compressed to a brief 0.15 s
silence to avoid excessively long clips. Three raters indepen-
dently judged whether a perceptible prosodic break was present
using a three-way label set: B (break), N (no break), and U (un-
certain). For the main analysis, we conservatively mapped U to
N (i.e., treating uncertain cases as no-break) and used majority
voting across raters.
As shown in Table 4, perceived-break rates were substan-
tially higher for B2 strict than for the low-score unlabeled set
(0.467 vs. 0.033), with substantial inter-rater reliability (Fleiss’
κ = 0.657). These results provide perception-based support
that the proposed anchors correspond to clearly perceivable
boundary events, while low-score unlabeled candidates largely
lack perceptible breaks.

5. Conclusions
This study demonstrates that weak supervision can recover an
interpretable boundary-strength signal from acoustic data with-
out manual prosodic labels. The anchor-driven PU learning
recipe leverages high-precision acoustic anchors to infer graded
boundary strength while leaving ambiguous non-anchor junc-
tures unlabeled. The results show that pause-based anchors,
pitch reset, and energy-related refinements recover meaning-
ful boundary structure in Japanese read speech. Future work
will incorporate lexical and ASR-derived information, evalu-
ate expert-labeled and spontaneous-speech benchmarks, model
speaker-specific cue weighting, and test cross-linguistic gener-
alization.
