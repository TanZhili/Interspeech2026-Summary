Abstract
People with mild cognitive impariment tend to perform
speech-based tasks differently than the healthy population. In
picture description tasks, they often describe a narrower por-
tion of the scene, missing details and spatial relations. We
propose a multimodal framework for estimating cognitive as-
sessment scores (MoCA and MMSE) from speech-based cog-
nitive tasks. The model uses pretrained encoders for audio,
text, and optional vision, followed by modality-specific projec-
tions and task-conditioned fusion. We compare staged variants
from acoustic features to foundation-model embeddings, late
fusion, mid-level cross-attention fusion, and a final mixture-of-
experts model. We also introduce a label-conditioned image-
text alignment loss for picture description samples to model dif-
ferences in transcript-image consistency between healthy con-
trols and patients with mild cognitive impairment. The best
model achieved 2.11(+-0.03) MoCA RMSE and 1.85(+-0.08)
MMSE RMSE in picture description task.

1. Introduction
Deep learning is increasingly used to support the diagnosis
of neurodegenerative disorders, especially Alzheimer’s disease
(AD) and mild cognitive impairment (MCI), because standard
diagnostic pathways (clinical assessments, cerebrospinal fluid
biomarkers, MRI/PET) are costly, invasive, and difficult to scale
for early screening, while also showing limited sensitivity to
subtle early-stage impairment [1, 2]. For MCI, recent work in-
creasingly uses pretrained speech and language representations.
This is reflected in multilingual, multi-picture settings such as
TAUKADIAL [3], where studies report promising results for
both MCI discrimination and Mini-Mental State Examination
(MMSE) [4] score regression, but with substantial variability
across languages, features, and evaluation protocols [4, 5, 6].
To address this, many studies analyze speech elicited during
the Picture Description Task (PD), where participants describe
a complex image and produce semi-spontaneous speech that re-
flects narrative organization, semantic retrieval, executive plan-
ning, visual attention, and speech production [7, 8]. In this
setting, earlier computational work was mostly unimodal (au-
dio or text)[1], but more recent systems combine handcrafted
acoustic features (e.g., MFCCs, eGeMAPS [9]) with pretrained
speech embeddings (e.g., Whisper [10, 11], Wav2Vec2 [12, 8])
and long-context modeling (e.g., DSTC-Net [13]), while text

Including the prompt image as an active modality enables
explicit modeling of speech-image alignment rather than using
the image only as a feature source. Vision-language multimodal
systems (e.g., CLIP/BLIP) [20] have improved over speech-text
baselines, including graph-based and co-attention approaches
on ADReSSo that link picture regions with spoken or tran-
scribed content [21, 22]. Zhu et al. model picture relevance
at the sentence level using CLIP-based image-text similarity,
including a sub-image variant that selects dementia-informative
regions. Their best model achieves 83.44% accuracy, outper-
forming both the text-only baseline (79.91%) and full-image
relevance filtering (80.63%) [23].
In this study we check if latent space of encoded pictures
can elicit varying levels of narrative complexity. We evaluate
how specific visual information and contextual features of each
picture that are integrated into the evaluation of speech can ac-
curately differentiate mild cognitive impairment from healthy
aging by predicting MMSE and Montreal Cognitive Assess-
ment (MoCA) [24] scores. We present series of experiments
on our custom dataset, with different architectures and modali-
ties: text, audio and vision. The main contributions of our work
are as follows: (1) We propose multimodal neurocognitive sta-
tus estimation with audio-image-text fusion pipeline and label-
conditioned image-text alignment loss for picture description
task. (2) We evaluate a staged sequence of models, from acous-
tic and unimodal baselines to multimodal fusion and mixture-
of-experts, showing progressive improvements in MoCA and
MMSE prediction.(3) We provide task-level analysis across five
speech-based cognitive tasks and show that picture description
is the most informative setting for score estimation.
2. Methodology
In this section we describe the dataset collection protocol used
in our study and speech-based tasks performed by participants.
We further describe our methods for cognitive score estimation.
Due to the significant class imbalance, our work focuses on re-
gression performance.

2.1. Dataset
The dataset was collected in a clinical trial at three medical
centers in Poland. It includes 88 participants: 69 with mild,
early-stage cognitive impairment confirmed by standardized
neuropsychological, and 19 healthy controls (HC). The MCI
group included pure MCI and MCI associated with Alzheimer’s
disease, multiple sclerosis, or Parkinson’s disease. All partici-
pants completed MoCA and MMSE screening. Demographic
and clinical characteristics are shown in Table 1, and the study
was approved by the local Ethics Committee.
2.2. Participants and Collection Protocol
Eligible MCI participants had a clinically confirmed diagno-
sis, preserved daily functioning, and the ability to use a smart-
phone and smartwatch with minimal assistance; written in-
formed consent was required. Exclusion criteria included mod-
erate/severe dementia, major psychiatric disorders, cognitive-
enhancing medication, and severe sensory or motor impairment.
HC participants were neurologically healthy and free of cogni-
tive disorders; cardiovascular problems and obesity were exclu-
sion criteria. Participants unable to comply with study proce-
dures or provide consent were excluded.
All participants used a Samsung Galaxy A55 smartphone to
complete tasks. Data collection lasted about six months for MCI
and one month for HC, with in-clinic onboarding/final visits and
most recordings collected at home.
2.3. Tasks and Experimental Setup
We investigate prediction of clinical assessment scores from
speech-based cognitive tasks. Each sample consists of a speech
recording from a specific task and is associated with a patient
identifier, task label, and two continuous targets (MoCA and
MMSE). We consider five tasks:
1. Answer verbally (A V): answer to the question showed on the
screen - uniform display of 18 texts during the study period,
2. Memorize answer (MA): memorize story played by the
speakers and then retell it - uniform display of 13 stories dur-
ing the study period,
3. Memorize words (MW): memorize 3 words shown on screen,
then tell the words - uniform display of 9 triples during the
study period,
4. Picture description (PD): describe picture shown on the
screen - uniform display of 18 images during the study pe-
riod
5. Read aloud (RA): read text shown on the screen - uniform
display of 13 texts during the study period.
Only the PD task includes a paired visual stimulus. All
tasks provide audio, and each sample additionally includes an
ASR transcript generated with Whisper Large v3 [10].

2.4. Data Preprocessing
We resample audio waveforms to 16kHz and amplitude-
normalized. Transcripts are tokenized with the BERT tokenizer.
For the PD task, images are preprocessed with the CLIP [20]
processor.
2.5. Model Architecture and Fusion
We use pretrained foundation encoders for each modality (au-
dio, text, and vision). Since encoder outputs differ in dimen-
sionality and distribution, modality-specific projection heads
map them into a shared fusion space of dimensiond model. For
token-level representations, we apply a learned linear projection
followed by LayerNorm.
We evaluate two fusion strategies.Late fusionoperates
on pooled modality vectors and provides a simple multimodal
baseline: pooled audio and text embeddings are projected into
the shared space, concatenated with a task embedding, and
passed to a small MLP regressor.Mid-level fusionenables
token-level interactions via task-conditioned cross-attention.

masked, ensuring consistent input structure without affecting
predictions. In the final stage, we additionally place a mixture-
of-experts (MoE) layer on top of the fused representation to al-

2.7. Experimental Protocol
We use a staged experimental design to isolate the effects of
representation learning, modality composition, and fusion:
•E0: eGeMAPS + MLP baseline.
•E1: frozen wav2vec2 audio encoder, train projection + re-
gression head.
•E2: frozen BERT text encoder, train projection + regression
head.
•E3: bimodal audio+text late fusion.
•E4: bimodal audio+text task-conditioned mid-level fusion.
•E5a:E4+ visual tokens for PD only (masked elsewhere).
•E5b:E5a+ image-text similarity objective with label-
conditioned alignment.
•E6:E5b+ MoE on top of the fused representation.
To prevent information leakage, we use patient-level 5-fold
cross-validation. We report mean and standard deviation of test
metrics across folds. Within each fold, target normalization pa-
rameters are computed on the training split only, applied to val-
idation and test sets.
Training uses AdamW with 5-step warmup and cosine de-
cay. To limit overfitting, we apply staged adaptation: encoders
are initially frozen, and only projection, fusion, and prediction
layers are trained; later, a small number of top encoder blocks
are unfrozen with a lower learning rate. Model selection uses

4. Discussion
The results show a consistent improvement from E0 to E6 (Fig-
ure 4), indicating that each component contributes to clini-
cal score prediction: stronger pretrained representations (E1–
E2), multimodal fusion (E3–E4), visual grounding for picture
description (E5a–E5b), and expert specialization (E6). The
text-only model (E2) outperforming the audio-only model (E1)
across all aggregate metrics suggests that semantic and lexical
information is more directly related to MoCA/MMSE variation
than acoustics alone in the studied tasks.
The comparison between E3 and E4 shows that fusion strat-
egy is important: task-conditioned mid-level fusion performs
better than late fusion, suggesting that token-level interactions
preserve useful cross-modal structure. Adding visual informa-
tion further improves results, especially when using the label-
conditioned alignment (E5b), and the best overall performance
is obtained with E6. These gains indicate that the proposed ar-
chitecture improves both accuracy and robustness.
Task-level results analysis on the Table 5 show that the
largest improvement is obtained forPD, with a1.34reduction in
MoCA RMSE relative to E1, compared with the next-largest re-
duction of0.95forMA. Since PD is the only task with paired vi-
sual input, this result highlights the benefit of transcript–image
grounding.PDalso gives the best overall performance for both
MoCA and MMSE estimation, whereasRAyields the highest
errors. The remaining non-visual tasks (AV,MA,MW) show
stable intermediate performance and consistent improvements
across model variants, withAVranking second-best for both
MoCA and MMSE estimation.
Several limitations remain. The cohort is relatively small
and it includes only Polish-speaking participants, which may
limit generalization. Clinical scores are predicted from many
recordings but measured less frequently, making supervision
relatively coarse at the sample level. Performance also depends
on ASR quality, and visual grounding is available only for PD.
Although patient-level cross-validation reduces leakage, exter-
nal validation on an independent cohort with a similar proto-
col, such as those in the DementiaBank [26] initiative, is still

Future work should focus on external validation, improved
patient-level/longitudinal aggregation, and quantifying predic-

e.g., to identify cases in which the model is less confident and
predictions should be interpreted more cautiously.
5. Conclusions
This work presents a multimodal framework for predict-
ing cognitive assessment scores (MoCA and MMSE) from
speech-based cognitive tasks using audio, text, and task-
dependent visual information. We evaluated series of mod-
els and experiments, moving from handcrafted acoustic fea-
tures to foundation-model embeddings, multimodal fusion, vi-
sual grounding for picture description, and a final mixture-of-
experts extension. These findings support the use of task-aware
multimodal fusion and selective visual grounding in heteroge-
neous cognitive assessment settings. Future work will focus on
external validation, stronger longitudinal/patient-level aggrega-
tion, and testing generalization across clinical subgroups and
languages.
