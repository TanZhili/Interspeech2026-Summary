M-LAMA: Multimodal Automated Scoring of Long-form Spoken English

Abstract
Automatic speech assessment (ASA) for real-world exams
requires evaluating multi-minute spontaneous responses across
acoustic delivery, linguistic quality, and task fulfillment. How-
ever, most prior work focuses on short utterances, leaving long-
form comprehensive assessment underexplored. We investigate
whether reliable scoring of extended speech requires structured
multimodal modeling. We propose M-LAMA, a cross-modal
framework integrating raw audio, ASR transcripts, and ques-
tion context through dual encoders and a discourse-aware fusion
decoder. A part-based audio structure and question-aware text
encoding capture both how and what is spoken, while a three-
stage training strategy addresses the bell-curve score distribu-
tion of high-stakes exams. Experiments on 87,226 full-length
sessions show that structured multimodal alignment substan-
tially improves scoring reliability across five criteria, suggesting
that effective long-form ASA requires principled integration of
acoustic, linguistic, and contextual evidence.
Index Terms: automated speech assessment, multimodal align-
ment, long-form spontaneous speech
1. Introduction
Automatic Speech Assessment (ASA) plays an essential role in
language proficiency evaluation [1], supporting education [2],
certification and large-scale standardized testing [3]. Recent
progress in speech technology has led to significant improve-
ments in automatic speech recognition and speech representa-
tion learning [4, 5, 6]. These advances have been driven by self-
supervised pretrained models such as wav2vec 2.0 [7], which
learn robust acoustic representations from large-scale unlabeled
data, and by systems like Whisper [8] that deliver high-quality
multilingual transcription. In addition, large audio-language
models (ALMs), including Qwen2-Audio [9], Flamingo [10],
and GPT-4o Audio [11], have demonstrated strong capabili-
ties in speech recognition and broader speech understanding
tasks. Despite these advances, most prior research has con-
centrated on short-form speech tasks or isolated pronunciation
evaluation [12, 10]. In contrast, real-world examinations such
as IELTS, TOEFL, and TOEIC require assessing multi-minute
spontaneous responses across multiple dimensions, including
fluency, grammar, vocabulary, and task fulfillment. These
long-form, multi-criteria requirements remain insufficiently ad-
dressed by current general-purpose models.
A central challenge in advancing ASA research is the ab-
sence of datasets that reflect the complexity of long-form, exam-
style speaking. Existing resources either provide fine-grained

assessment insufficiently studied. In addition, data from high-

confidentiality and privacy policies, preventing their use in open
research. This limited access to exam-authentic long-form data,
coupled with the absence of transparent scoring systems in com-
mercial platforms, has slowed methodological progress and hin-
dered reproducibility in ASA research.
To advance the study of long-form ASA under realistic con-
ditions, two elements are essential: access to exam-authentic
long-form responses and models explicitly designed to integrate
acoustic and linguistic evidence for multi-criteria evaluation. In
this work, we conduct experiments on a large-scale dataset of
certification-style English speaking sessions collected in col-
laboration with an institutional partner. Due to confidentiality
constraints, the dataset cannot be publicly released; however,
it reflects real exam conditions with multi-minute responses
and multi-criteria scoring. We propose M-LAMA (Multimodal
Long-form Audio Assessment), a structured cross-modal ar-
chitecture explicitly designed for multi-minute, multi-criteria
speech scoring. Rather than relying on naive feature concatena-
tion, M-LAMA introduces (i) part-aware audio modeling that
preserves discourse-level structure across exam segments, (ii)
question-aware text encoding to explicitly assess task fulfill-
ment, and (iii) bidirectional cross-modal attention that captures
the interaction between acoustic delivery and linguistic content.
A gated multimodal fusion mechanism further integrates these
complementary views into a unified proficiency representation.
In addition, we develop a progressive three-stage training strat-
egy: (i) contrastive alignment, (ii) range-aware classification,
and (iii) fine-grained regression, to address the mid-score dom-
inance characteristic of bell-curve distributed exam data. This
design enables stable optimization across the full proficiency
spectrum and supports reliable long-form scoring. Following
Open Science principles, all source code, experimental scripts,
trained checkpoints, and replication materials are publicly avail-
able in our artifact repository [16].

▷ Dual-stream Encoding. Given raw audio Xa and its ASR
transcript Xt, M-LAMA employs a dual-stream encoder. The
acoustic branch leverages a frozen Whisper-Large encoder [17]
to extract frame-level representations, which are refined through
a lightweight bottleneck adapter to suppress task-irrelevant vari-
ability while retaining prosodic and phonetic cues essential for
proficiency evaluation. These acoustic features provide direct
evidence for Pronunciation and Fluency assessment, where de-
livery quality and temporal stability are critical. In parallel,
a frozen Qwen2-1.5B encoder [18] processes both transcripts
and question prompts to derive semantic and grammatical repre-
sentations. This stream captures lexical diversity and syntactic
structure, supporting evaluation of V ocabulary and Grammar,
while also contributing to higher-level discourse analysis.
▷ Discourse-aware Audio Modeling. To preserve long-range
temporal dependencies, each 3–4 minute response is segmented
into fixed-length chunks and grouped into three structural parts
{pi} corresponding to exam discourse sections. Within each
part pi, attention-based pooling aggregates chunk-level fea-
tures, augmented with positional embeddings to retain temporal
order. This produces a structured audio representation that en-
codes discourse organization rather than treating the recording
as a flat sequence. This structured representation supports eval-
uation of Fluency and Discourse Management, which depend
on sustained temporal coherence beyond isolated acoustic cues.
▷ Question-aware and Cross-modal Interaction. Task ful-
fillment requires evaluating whether a response addresses the
exam prompt. We therefore employ a question-aware text mod-
ule, where transcript representationsXt attend to question em-
beddingsXq to produce a task-conditioned semantic view. In
this formulation, transcript representationsXt serve as queries,
while question embeddingsXq are projected into key and value
spaces through learnable matrices WK andWV (Equation 1).
The resultingHtext therefore encodes how well the spoken con-

2.2. Distribution-aware Training Strategy
Exam scores typically follow a bell-curve distribution, with
strong mid-score dominance. Direct regression under such skew
can lead to mean-biased predictions and reduced sensitivity at
distribution tails. To address this, we adopt a progressive three-
stage optimization strategy:
Stage 1: Contrastive Alignment. Audio and transcript embed-
dings are aligned through a contrastive objective, establishing a
shared multimodal space before scoring.

3. Experimental Settings
3.1. Dataset
Experiments are conducted on a large-scale English speak-
ing certification dataset containing 86,491 exam sessions from
29,034 candidates (approximately 4,845 hours of speech). Each
session corresponds to a complete three-part speaking exam (3–
5 minutes), and is scored by certified examiners on five criteria:
Pronunciation, V ocabulary, Grammar, Fluency, and Discourse
Management (0–10 scale). The score distribution is heavily
concentrated in the mid range (4–7), accounting for 85.38% of
samples, while low (0–3) and high (8–10) scores represent only
9.51% and 5.10%, respectively. This imbalance motivates our
distribution-aware training strategy (Section 2.2). To prevent
speaker leakage, data are split at the candidate level, with 2,647
candidates reserved for testing and no speaker overlap across
splits. Due to confidentiality restrictions associated with certifi-
cation exams, the dataset cannot be publicly released. However,
in support of Open Science and reproducible research, we are
able to share the data upon reasonable request and subject to
approval by the data provider.
3.2. Evaluation Plan
Our evaluation addresses three research questions:
▷ RQ1: How does M-LAMA perform relative to state-of-the-
art audio-language models? We compare against commercial
APIs (GPT-4o Audio [11], Gemini 2.5 Flash and Gemini 2.5
Pro [20]) and open-weight ALMs (Qwen-2 Audio [21], Qwen-
2.5 Omni [22] and Flamigo 3-Audio [10]) to assess competitive
performance in long-form speech scoring. Open-weight models
are fine-tuned on the training set, whereas commercial APIs are
evaluated via prompting. Owing to API costs and rate limits,
commercial baselines are evaluated on a random subset of 1,000
test samples. For reproducibility, all prompts and evaluation
materials are released in our artifact repository [16].
▷ RQ2: How does structured audio-text fusion affect scor-
ing reliability in long-form speech assessment? We conduct
unimodal ablations, and remove the question-aware module to
quantify each component’s contribution.
▷ RQ3: How does discourse-level structural modeling influence
scoring performance in long-form speech assessment? We per-
form a leave-one-out analysis across the 18 audio chunks to
measure the influence of temporal structure on final predictions.
We evaluate the performance using Mean Absolute Error
(MAE) [23], Quadratic Weighted Kappa (QWK) [24], and Ac-
curacy within Error 1 (Acc@1) across all five scoring criterias.
Due to space constraints, detailed definitions of these evaluation
metrics are omitted.
3.3. Implementation Details
We use Qwen2-1.5B-Instruct [18] ( dt=1536) and Whisper-
large-v3-turbo [17] (da=1280) as text and audio encoders. Text
is tokenized to 2048 tokens; audio is split into six 30 s chunks at
16 kHz and processed by a bottleneck adapter (db=512). Both
modalities are projected to a shared space ofd=512. The cross-
modal decoder uses 2-layer, 8-head self-attention blocks. The
regression head produces a 21-bin distribution over scores 0–10
(step 0.5). All models are trained with AdamW [25] (lr=10−6,
batch size 4) on a single NVIDIA A100 80GB GPU.

4.2. RQ 2: How does structured audio-text fusion affect scor-
ing reliability in long-form speech assessment?
To examine the role of modality integration, we compare uni-
modal (Text-only, Audio-only) and multimodal configurations
under identical architectural settings. Results are reported in
Table 2. Unimodal models exhibit substantial performance
degradation, with Acc@1 dropping to 49.30% (Text-only) and
43.46% (Audio-only), indicating that neither linguistic content
nor acoustic delivery alone is sufficient for reliable proficiency
assessment. In contrast, the multimodal configuration achieves
90.82% Acc@1 and reduces MAE to 0.6033, demonstrating
a large synergistic gain rather than incremental improvement.
The magnitude of this gap suggests that acoustic and textual
representations encode complementary but non-redundant in-
formation. Long-form assessment requires simultaneous mod-
eling of phonetic realization, lexical choice, grammatical struc-
ture, and discourse coherence, which are dimensions that cannot
be fully inferred from a single modality. These findings confirm
that structured audio-text fusion is a structural requirement for
robust multi-criteria speech scoring.

5. Related Work
Research on automated speech assessment remains limited
by both data availability and modeling capabilities. Ex-
isting datasets are either restricted to short utterances with
fine-grained annotations (e.g., Speechocean762 [13]), large-
scale ASR corpora without proficiency labels (e.g., Lib-
riSpeech [14], Common V oice [26]), or speech-quality bench-
marks focused on acoustic distortions rather than L2 proficiency
(e.g., NISQA [27], QualiSpeech [28]). Despite recent efforts
using speech synthesis to reduce speaker bias [29, 30], no public
benchmark provides long-form spontaneous speech with multi-
criteria proficiency annotations. From a modeling perspective,
self-supervised encoders such as Wav2vec 2.0 [31] and Whis-
per [17] capture local acoustic patterns effectively but strug-
gle with long-range dependencies in multi-minute recordings.
In contrast, recent Audio-Language Models (ALMs), including
Qwen2-Audio [21], Audio Flamingo [10, 32], and Gemini-2.5
Pro [20], support long-context reasoning yet are not optimized
for the fine-grained phonetic and prosodic cues required for ob-
jective proficiency assessment. These limitations motivate a
specialized framework that integrates robust acoustic encoding
with higher-level linguistic reasoning for long-form spoken En-
glish evaluation.
