Abstract
End-to-end neural speech separation systems have achieved
strong average scale-invariant signal-to-distortion ratio (SI-SDR)
scores, yet still struggle with mixtures where speakers have
highly similar timbre. These difficult cases reduce robustness
and raise concerns about real-world generalization. To address
this, we propose a curriculum hard-pair sampling strategy. Dur-
ing training, a pairwise speaker-speaker difficulty matrix is built
and updated online based on the SI-SDR score of separating each
speaker pair. Each mixture for model training is constructed by
first randomly sampling an anchor speaker, and the interference
speaker is sampled based on a temperature-controlled Softmax
which gives more weights to speakers that are, as indicated by
the difficulty matrix, difficult to be separated with the anchor
speaker. Results on Libri2Mix show that the proposed method
consistently improves performance, especially for similar-timbre
mixtures, without additional training overhead.

1. Introduction
Monaural speech separation aims to recover the speech signal of
each speaker from a single-channel multi-speaker mixture [1–15].
It is a core problem to solve in automatic speech recognition,
meeting transcription, and voice interaction [16 –19]. Despite
rapid progress in neural networks based models, performance
still degrades for mixtures with heavy overlap, similar voice
timbre, low signal-to-noise ratio (SNR), and strong reverberation.
Popular models such as Conv-TasNet [20], DPRNN [21] and
TF-GridNet [22] are trained by uniformly sampling training
mixtures and typically optimizing the mean SI-SDR metric [23].
As a result, rare but difficult mixtures inevitably receive less
emphasis during training, leaving large errors on a small subset
of mixtures. These errors undermine the robustness of the models
in real applications, and often dominate downstream failures and
users’ perception of the performance of the separation models.
When the speakers in a mixture have very similar timbre,
current models tend to produce large residual interference [24,
25]. We refer to these similar speaker pairs as hard pairs and
all other pairs as easy pairs. Hard pairs typically form only a
small portion of the training data, yet accounting for most tail
errors. They lower overall scores and, more importantly, harm
generalization to real recordings. However, common training

While this can reduce errors on hard examples, it inevitably al-
ters the effective training distribution and often sacrifices mean
performance on the original test distribution. For the task of tar-
get speaker extraction, Liu et al. [28] propose a curriculum that
gradually increases target–interferer similarity, but the sched-
ule is hand-crafted rather than driven by the model’s actual
errors, making its hyper-parameters difficult to tune. In addi-
tion, both the global hard-mining strategy proposed by Wang
et al. [26] and the curriculum proposed by Liu et al. [28] re-
quire pre-computing difficulty scores and partitioning the entire
training set into easy and hard subsets, which becomes cum-
bersome and time-consuming on large corpora. Differently, the
curriculum hard-pair sampling algorithm proposed in this paper
maintains an online speaker–speaker difficulty matrix that is up-
dated during training based on the SI-SDR scores of each speaker
pair. For each training mixture, we sample an anchor speaker uni-
formly and then select the interferer via a temperature-controlled
Softmax that gradually shifts from uniform to hard-biased sam-
pling. This design automatically separates easy and hard pairs
on the fly, avoids loss-level distribution shift, preserves average
performance, and introduces essentially no additional computa-
tional overhead.
Compared with uniform sampling, our proposed curriculum,
motivated by [29], focuses learning on rare but important cases
without letting them dominate from the start. Compared with
the approach by Wang et al. [26], which increases the presence
of hard mixtures through fixed re-weighting or re-sampling, our
schedule adapts the hardness over time, and balances easy and
hard cases more naturally. In practice, this yields better lower tail
performance while preserving mean SI-SDR, since the model
first builds a strong base on easy pairs and then learns to resolve
mixtures with similar timbres as the proportion of hard pairs
rises.

In many speech separation studies [22, 30–35], speaker tu-
ples are sampled uniformly from a pre-mixed set of mixtures
provided with the tasks or from mixtures created via dynamic
mixing on the fly, and the model is trained on this fixed sampling
distribution. This uniform sampling strategy treats all the mix-
tures equally, regardless of their difficulty (for example, heavy
overlap or similar timbre). As a result, during training, rare but
difficult combinations would occupy only a small fraction of
updates, and large errors can persist on a small subset of hard
mixtures.
In this work, we replace (static) uniform sampling with a
dynamic curriculum over speaker pairs. During training, the
sampler adapts the probability of selecting each speaker com-
bination based on an online difficulty estimate (derived from
SI-SDR scores or related statistics) so that early epochs stay
close to uniform sampling for stable, initial learning, while later
epochs gradually allocate more emphasis on hard pairs. This
section describes the construction of the difficulty measure and
the curriculum hard-pair sampling scheme.

the SI-SDR scores do not yet provide a reliable estimate of
pair difficulty. We therefore use only standard dynamic mixing
at the beginning of training. In the final stage of training, we
switch back to standard dynamic mixing, since continuously
over-sampling the hardest pairs may over-bias the model toward
a small hard tail and degrade its performance on easy pairs.

The curriculum hard-pair sampling is implemented entirely

For each mini-batch, the extra computation consists of reading a
row of the difficulty matrix for the chosen anchor, applying the
Softmax operation in Eq. (4), and drawing a categorical sample.
The forward and backward passes of the separation network

the evolving notion of pairwise difficulty and to allocate more
updates to hard mixtures, without modifying the loss function or
introducing additional network components.
3. Experimental Setup
We evaluate the effectiveness of different models using the
Libri2Mix dataset [36], a standard benchmark for two-speaker
speech separation constructed based on clean speech signals in
LibriSpeech [37] and noise signals in WHAM! [38]. In our ex-
periments, we follow the Libri2Mix configuration and use both
the 100 h and 360 h training subsets. According to the propri-
etary specification, the two subsets contain 13, 900 and 50, 800
two-speaker mixtures with 251 and 921 speakers, respectively,
yielding a total of 64, 700 mixtures and 1, 172 distinct speakers.
Instead of relying on pre-mixed audio, we perform on-the-fly
dynamic mixing [30]. For each training example we randomly
select two speakers from train-clean-{100,360}, pick
one utterance per speaker, and mix them at 16 kHz. The inter-
speaker SNR is drawn uniformly from the range [−5, 5] dB.
To verify whether our proposed algorithm can be effec-
tive for different speech separation models, we employ TF-
GridNet [22], Conv-TasNet [20] and BSRNN [39] as the back-
bone separation networks. All models are trained with the Adam
optimizer [40], using an initial learning rate of 10−3, which
is halved if the validation performance does not improve for 4
consecutive epochs. For STFT, we use a square-root of the Hann
window, with a window size of 16 ms and a hop size of 8 ms.
4. Evaluation Results
4.1. Effectiveness of Curriculum Hard-Pair Sampling
We evaluate the impact of curriculum hard-pair sampling on
Libri2Mix. For each backbone model, we compare a dynamic-
mixing baseline with uniform speaker-pair sampling against
several curriculum variants with different initial sampling tem-
peratures. All systems share the same training configuration, so
that any performance difference can be attributed to the sampling
strategy.
Table 1 reports the results for Conv-TasNet. The dynamic-
mixing baseline without hard-pair sampling achieves an average
SI-SDRi of 16.7 dB. When curriculum hard-pair sampling is
enabled, low initial temperatures with τ0 = 10 andτ0 = 15
slightly degrade the mean SI-SDRi to 16.6 and 16.5 dB, respec-
tively. With a higher initial temperatureτ0 = 20, the proposed
sampler matches the baseline at 16.7 dB. This indicates that
overly-aggressive deviation from uniform sampling can hurt the
average performance, whereas a milder curriculum that keeps
the sampling distribution close to uniform in early epochs can
preserve the mean SI-SDRi.

dynamic-mixing baseline attains 21.0 dB SI-SDRi. Again, low
initial temperatures (τ0 = 10 and τ0 = 15 ) lead to slightly
worse averages of 20.6 and 20.9 dB. In contrast, usingτ0 = 20
not only avoids degradation but improves the mean SI-SDRi

4.3. Visualization of Speaker-Speaker Difficulty Matrix
To better understand how the proposed sampler interacts with
the data, we visualize the speaker–speaker difficulty matrix D at
different training stages. For each matrix, we apply hierarchical
clustering [41] to group speakers with similar difficulty profiles.
This re-ordering places speakers with similar interaction patterns
next to each other, making block structures in Fig. 1 easier to
analyze. Entry (i,j ) encodes the difficulty score for the speaker
pair (si,sj), with warmer color indicating higher difficulty. The
diagonal is masked out because a speaker is never paired with
itself.
Panels (a) and (d) correspond to the early stage of training.
With standard dynamic mixing and uniform pair sampling (in
Fig. 1(a)), we observe several broad, light-colored blocks along
the diagonal. These blocks indicate large groups of speakers
that are mutually hard to separate from each other, i.e., many
pairs inside each block receive high difficulty scores. When
curriculum hard-pair sampling is enabled (in Fig. 1(d)), the
pattern becomes more concentrated: high-difficulty regions are
confined to a few sharper clusters, while most pixels in the matrix
is dominated by cooler colors. This suggests that the sampler
quickly finds a small set of difficult speakers and prioritizes
mixtures involving them in early training, rather than distributing
hard mixtures evenly across all speakers.
Panels (b) and (e) show the matrices in the middle of the
training process, after the models have largely converged on
easy and moderately-hard mixtures. For dynamic mixing with
uniform sampling (Fig. 1(b)), the matrix appears more homoge-
neous than that in the early stage, but moderately difficult regions
still spread across many speakers. In contrast, with curriculum
