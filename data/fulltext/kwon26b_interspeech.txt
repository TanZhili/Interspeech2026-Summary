Investigating ASR for Low-Intelligibility Dysarthric Speech 
Jinuk Kwon1,2, Beiming Cao1, Carl Sigmond1, Kristin Teplansky1, and Jun Wang1,2 
1 Department of Speech, Language, and Hearing Sciences, University of Texas at Austin, USA 
2 Department of Neurology, Dell Medical School, University of Texas at Austin, USA 
{jinuk.kwon, jun.wang}@austin.utexas.edu 
 
Abstract 
Despite the recent advances in automatic speech recognition 
(ASR) for dysarthria, it remains challenging for low -
intelligibility speech, as most studies used datasets that are 
dominated by mild to moderate dysarthric speech. In this study, 
we investigated ASR for severe dysarthria by utilizing a unique, 
large dataset from an individual with low-intelligibility speech. 
First, we implemented a bidirectional long short-term memory-
hidden Markov model (BLSTM-HMM) and fine-tuned large-
scale pretrained Whisper models in speaker-dependent settings. 
Both models achieved substantially low word error rates (< 
14%), indicating severe dysarthria can be effectively modelled 
with sufficient data. Second, we generalized a fine-tuned 
Whisper model to other patients, achieving a 6.4 percentage 
point improvement for severe dysarthria compared to the non-
fine-tuned model without performance degradation for mild and 
moderate cases, suggesting the potential of generalization of 
ASR for severe dysarthria.

1. Introduction 
Dysarthria is a motor speech disorder caused by neurological 
injury to the motor speech system [1]. Several neurological 
conditions can cause dysarthria, including cerebral palsy (CP) 
[2], amyotrophic lateral sclerosis [3], and Parkinson’s disease 
[4]. Individuals with dysarthria experience limited control over 
various motor subsystems such as respiration, phonation, 
resonance, and articulation. Consequently, their speech is 
characterized by symptoms including slurred speech, poor 
articulation, irregular speaking rate, breathy voice, nasal 
sounding, and monotonic intonation [1].  
The development of accurate automatic speech recognition 
(ASR) systems for dysarthric speech has the potential to 
improve communication accessibility, enabling affected 
individuals to use voice-driven technologies effectively. When 
integrated with text-to-speech (TTS) technology, recognized 
text could also be converted into speech, further streamlining 
communication. However, the severity and specific speech 
characteristics of dysarthria vary widely among individuals, 
presenting significant challenges for ASR [5]. Specifically, 
impaired motor control can result in inconsistent 
pronunciations, altered vowel and consonant sounds, and 
atypical prosody [6], all of which pose significant obstacles for 
current ASR systems. These challenges are especially 
pronounced in conversational speech; Tobin et al. [7] reported 
that recognition accuracy deteriorates  even further in 
conversational speech from individuals with speech disorders, 
highlighting the need for specialized modeling approaches. 
With recent advances in deep learning, numerous studies 
have strived to improve the recognition performance of 
dysarthric speech [8-12]. Nevertheless, a fundamental 
challenge remains: the difficulty of collecting large -scale 
speech data from a single patient. Individuals with dysarthria 
often experience fatigue, limited stamina, or difficulty 
producing extended speech, making it challenging to obtain 
long recording sessions. Several public datasets have been 
released to support research on dysarthric speech, such as 
UASpeech [13] and TORGO [14]. However, most existing 
datasets focus mainly on isolated word tasks and a limited 
number of sentences from single participants , and these 
speakers are typically dominated by mild or moderate cases. 
For example, the TORGO dataset has been widely employed, 
as it includes approximately 23 hours of acoustic recordings 
from eight individuals with dysarthria and seven control 
speakers. Even in more recent large -scale (regarding the 
number of speakers) initiatives such as the Speech Accessibility 
Project [15] and Google Project Euphonia [16], recordings from 
individuals with mild to moderate dysarthria remain dominant, 
while severe cases are underrepresented. Although these 
projects have significantly expanded the overall amount of 
atypical speech data, from hundreds to over a thousand hours 
across many participants, the amount of data available per 
speaker is still relatively limited (often around one hour), 
constraining their ability to model the wide variability of  
articulatory distortions of dysarthria, particularly for speakers 
with severe dysarthria, the population who have the greatest 
need for assistive communication. 
To address data-scarcity, additional efforts have explored 
various strategies to enhance recognition performance. These 
approaches have relied on pooled data across multiple speakers, 
combined with data augmentation, speaker normalization, or 
adaptation techniques [8, 9, 17, 18]. Recently, self-supervised 
and large-scale pretrained models such as wav2vec 2.0 [10, 12] 
and Whisper [11, 19, 20], which have achieved strong 
performance in typical ASR, have also demonstrated potential 
for dysarthric speech recognition. Moreover, Green et al. [21] 
showed that personalized ASR models could even surpass 
human listeners on short phrases from disordered speakers, 
underscoring the promise of speaker -specific approaches. 
Nevertheless, it remains unclear how effectively deep learning-
based ASR models can be trained on severely dysarthric speech 
when sufficient recordings from a single individual are 
available [22]. With the recent rapid advancements in deep 
learning techniques, such models might learn structured 
patterns in severe dysarthria, but performance may still be 
limited by highly variable pronunciation characteristics. A 
prevailing assumption in the field has been that severe 
dysarthric speech is too variable to model effectively, 
particularly under realistic data collection conditions [23]. 
In this study, we investigated the feasibility of ASR for 
severe dysarthric speech by leveraging a unique, large single-
speaker dataset from an individual with severe dysarthria.  Our 
central hypothesis is that severely dysarthric speech contains 
structured, learnable acoustic–phonetic regularities that can be 
learned through large amounts of single-speaker data and 
selectively transferred to other speakers with similar severity. 
To test this hypothesis, we employed two ASR models: a 
conventional hidden Markov model (HMM) [24] with a 
bidirectional long short-term memory (BLSTM) acoustic 
model [25], and a large-scale pretrained model (Whisper). The 
BLSTM-HMM model was trained from scratch, while the 
pretrained Whisper was fine-tuned using our large dysarthric-
speaker dataset. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first 
study to systematically investigate ASR using a large -scale 
single-speaker dataset of severe dysarthria.

2. Methods 
2.1. Data Collection 
A 30-year-old male, a native American English speaker who 
has had athetoid CP since birth, participated in the data 
collection. The intelligibility of his speech is 20.9%, as 
measured by two speech-language pathology students using the 
standard Sentence Intelligibility Test (SIT). His speaking rate is 
approximately 37 words/minute. This study and its protocol 
were approved by the local ethics committees of the University 
of Texas at Austin.  
During the data collection sessions, the participant read 
commonly used speech stimuli such as Harvard sentences [26], 
mngu0 corpus [27], MACHO-TIMIT [28], Rainbow Passage 
[29], GrandFather Passage [30], AAC (daily conversation) 
sentences designed by the last author’s lab for silent speech 
interface [31], as well as real-world spontaneous, diary-style 
stories (with private information removed). The data collection 
was conducted for 45 to 60 minutes per session at the 
participant’s home. The full data collection period lasted 
approximately one year. Data were securely transferred to UT 
Austin. The total recording time was about 100 hours. In the 
current study, 21.6 hours of data were used. The audio was 
initially recorded at a sampling rate of 44100 Hz, and then 
downsampled to 16000 Hz.  
2.2. Speaker-dependent models 
We employed two models: a conventional BLSTM-HMM [24, 
25] and a more recent pretrained large-scale model (Whisper). 
These two models were intentionally selected to have 
complementary representations of ASR models. BLSTM -
HMM represents a smaller-scale architecture that provides a 
transparent and controllable baseline that is straightforward to 
train and fine-tune. Whisper (English version) represents a 
robust, higher-capacity pretrained approach with strong 
potential for cross-speaker generalization. We also performed a 
data scale analysis using Whisper.  
2.2.1. BLSTM-HMM 
The BLSTM [32] is a recurrent neural network that incorporates 
memory blocks to contain both long- and short-term contextual 
information from previous inputs. BLSTM processes input 
sequences in both forward and backward directions using two 
separate hidden layers, which provides strong performance for 
time-series data. 
The downsampled audio signals were converted into Mel-
frequency cepstral coefficients (MFCCs), consisting of 12 
cepstral coefficients and one energy term. The first - and 
second-order derivatives of MFCCs were extracted with a 
frame length of 25 ms and a hop length of 10 ms. For training, 
9 frames (4 previous + 1 current + 4 future frames) of MFCCs 
and their derivatives were concatenated as the input, yielding a 
351-dimensional feature vector (9 frames × (13 MFCC + 13 ∆ 
+ 13 ∆∆)).  
The BLSTM-HMM architecture consisted of two hidden 
layers, each comprising 320 LSTM cells along with 200 
recurrent projection units. The output layer modeled 800 tied 
states (senones) of left-to-right triphone HMMs, where the 
senones were derived using a decision tree-based state tying 
method [33]. A bigram phoneme-level/word-level language 
model was trained with the training dataset. The speech 
recognition results were measured with the word error rate 
(WER). All the experiments were implemented with the Kaldi 
speech recognition toolkit [34]. 
All hyperparameters were empirically determined using the 
13.5-hour development dataset. For the final evaluation, the 
model was trained on a combined dataset of 19.6 hours 
(development dataset plus an additional 6 hours of 600 phrases) 
and tested on a separate dataset of approximately 2.1 hours. 
2.2.2. Large-scale pretrained model (Whisper) 
Pretrained Whisper models were adopted to investigate whether 
fine-tuning a recent large-scale ASR model can improve the 
recognition performance of dysarthric speech. Whisper , 
developed by OpenAI [35], employs an encoder-decoder 
transformer architecture and has demonstrated strong 
performance on typical speech. The model takes in raw 16 kHz 
audio and transforms it into an 80 log-Mel spectrogram. The 
encoder and decoder blocks use an identical number of layers. 
Several variants of Whisper are available, ranging from tiny to 
large architectures, as listed in Table 1, trained on hundreds of 
thousands of hours of speech data. Two types of Whisper 
models are provided: English-only and multilingual. In this 
work, we used only English-only models, as multilingual 
models showed no improvement in WER on our validation set. 
Specifically, we fine-tuned four English-only Whisper variants 
- tiny.en, base.en, small.en, and medium.en - using our dataset. 
Hereafter, the “.en” suffix, which denotes the English-only 
versions of the models, is omitted for brevity.  
For fine-tuning, only sentences shorter than 30 seconds 
were used. This restriction arises from Whisper’s 30-second 
receptive field, as fine-tuning with longer inputs is not officially 
supported [35]. Among our 19.6-hour datasets, 18 hours 
satisfied this constraint. This subset was divided into a 17-hour 
development dataset (15 hours for training and 2 hours for 
validation) and a 1-hour test set. For each training condition, the 
test WER was evaluated using the model checkpoint that 
achieved the lowest validation WER. 
Each model was fine-tuned for 15 epochs, with a learning 
rate of 1e-5. The batch size and gradient accumulation step were 
both set to 4, yielding an effective batch size of 16. To avoid 
excessive gradient magnitudes during training, gradient 
clipping was applied with a maximum gradient norm of 1, and 
all audio inputs were normalized to the range -0.95 to 0.95. All 
hyperparameters were determined based on the WER on the 
validation set and the physical constraints of GPU memory. For 
decoding, the parameters “ num_beams” and 
“no_repeat_ngram_size” were set to 5 and 3, respectively. All 
implementations were conducted using the Hugging Face 
Transformers library [36] with an M4 Pro chip and 64 GB  
RAM.

2.3. Cross-speaker Generalization 
To investigate whether the fine-tuned Whisper can generalize 
to other unseen speakers, we evaluate it on the TORGO dataset. 
The TORGO database [14] contains aligned acoustic and 
articulatory recordings from 15 speakers, including eight 
dysarthric speakers (five males) and seven healthy control 
speakers (four males). The severity levels of dysarthric speakers 
were evaluated by a speech-language pathologist based on 
overall clinical intelligibility and articulatory motor function 
according to the Frenchay Dysarthria Assessment [37], making 
TORGO a widely used benchmark for evaluating ASR 
performance on pathological speech. In this study, we used only 
acoustic speech recordings from dysarthric speakers.

3. Results 
3.1. Speaker-dependent ASR  
3.1.1. BLSTM-HMM 
The development dataset (13.5 hours) was used to investigate 
the optimal number of HMM states, starting from 3 states for 
non-silence phonemes and 5 states for silence. The phoneme 
error rate (PER) decreased as the number of states increased. As 
a result, we adopted 6 states to represent non-silence phonemes 
and 8 states for silence. In the final evaluation, BLSTM-HMM 
was trained using a 19.6-hour dataset and achieved a PER of 
14.2% and a WER of 13.4% on the 2.1-hour test set.  
3.1.2. Fine-tuned Whisper models 
Figure 1 illustrates the WERs on the validation sets for each 
fine-tuned Whisper model, evaluated at every 0.5 epoch. Red 
circles indicate the epochs at which the lowest WER was 
achieved. As expected, larger models generally achieved lower 
validation WERs. The lowest WERs were obtained at 3.5, 2, 3, 
and 10.5 epochs for the tiny (19.48%), base (12.19%), small 
(7.26%), and medium (6.56%) models, respectively. Notably, 
the validation WER difference between the small and medium 
models was less than 1%, which was smaller than the 
performance gaps observed among the other model sizes.

3.1.3. Scaling analysis using the Whisper-medium model 
To understand how the data scale affects the performance of 
speaker-dependent ASR, we also performed a scale analysis 
using the Whisper-medium model. Figure 2 presents the 
training size-WER curve. As the training data size increased 
from 1 to 15 hours, the WER steadily decreased on both the 
validation and test sets. Notably, test WER drops from 25.40% 
to 10.52%, corresponding to a 58.6% relative reduction. These 
results suggest that even severe dysarthric speech exhibits 
learnable acoustic patterns when sufficient speaker-dependent 
data is available.

3.2. Cross-Speaker Generalization 
Table 3 shows the generalization performance on the TORGO 
dataset, evaluated using the Whisper-medium model before and 
after fine-tuning on our dataset. For speakers with severe 
dysarthria, WERs decreased by 6 to 12 percentage points (pp) 
for three out of four patients (F01, M01, and M02). For speaker 
M05 (moderate/severe), fine-tuning also reduced WER by 
about 7.1pp. In contrast, fine -tuning did not improve 
performance for M04, whose baseline WER exceeded 80%, and 
resulted in a 4.8pp increase.  For speakers with moderate or 
mild dysarthria (F03, F04, and M03), the WER differences after 
fine-tuning were minimal (less than 1pp). Overall, the mean 
WER decreased from 45.8% to 41.7%.

and between ih and iy (21 and 17). A small number of 
bidirectional consonant substitution between d and t (17 and 15) 
was also observed. For insertion errors, the most frequently 
inserted consonants were t (66), n (59), d (40), and s (26).  In 
addition, vowel insertions, including ah (62) and iy(35), were 
also observed. Deletion errors were mainly associated with 
plosives and sonorants, particularly t, r, and n.

4. Discussion 
In this study, we investigated the feasibility of ASR for severe 
dysarthric speech using over 21 hours of single-speaker data 
with very low intelligibility (approximately 20%). Two 
different models were used: BLSTM -HMM and Whisper 
(English). The experimental results demonstrated that fine-
tuning the pretrained Whisper model achieved the lowest WER 
of 10.5%, highlighting the effectiveness of personalized fine-
tuning when sufficient single-speaker data are available. These 
results approach a performance level that may enable practical 
daily communication support for individuals with severe 
dysarthria. 
Notably, the conventional BLSTM-HMM model, which 
contains approximately 3.5M parameters, achieved 
performance comparable to the larger fine -tuned Whisper 
variants, whose parameter sizes range from tens to hundreds of 
millions. This finding suggests that when sufficient single -
speaker data are available, even conventional architectures can 
remain highly effective for dysarthric ASR. These results 
highlight the importance of data scale in achieving robust 
dysarthric ASR performance. In addition, from a practi cal 
perspective, a substantially smaller model size may offer 
advantages in terms of computational efficiency, memory 
footprint, and real-time deployment, which are critical factors 
for assistive communication devices. 
The generalization results reveal that the fine -tuned 
Whisper model is effective in improving performance of ASR, 
specifically for speakers with severe dysarthria, when baseline 
intelligibility remains above an extreme impairment threshold. 
The 6-12 pp reductions observed for three severely dysarthric 
speakers demonstrate that a large single speaker dataset can 
significantly enhance performance even under highly degraded 
acoustic conditions. A performance decrement was observed 
for speaker M04, whose baseline WER exceeded 80%, which 
is substantially higher than the baseline WER of our dataset 
before fine-tuning (63.4%). This result suggests that extremely 
severe articulatory impairment may reduce the effectiveness of 
model adaptation. For speakers with moder ate or mild 
dysarthria, the limited impact of fine-tuning likely reflects the 
strong representational capacity of the pretrained Whisper 
model, which already achieves near-optimal performance on 
more intelligible speech. These findings further suggest that 
effective cross-speaker generalization depends on the degree of 
similarity in severity characteristics between the fine-tuning 
data and the target speaker, which might be further investigated 
in future studies. 
The observed phoneme confusion patterns provide insight 
into the articulatory characteristics of dysarthric speech. The 
predominance of vowel substitutions  (e.g., /ao/→/aa/) 
particularly among central vowels, may reflect reduced vowel 
distinctiveness, a phenomenon often reported in dysarthric 
speech. Frequent insertions of neutral vowels such as /ah/ and 
/ih/ suggest a tendency toward unintended vowel epenthesis in 
severely impaired speech. In addition, the high deletion rates of 
plosives (e.g., /t/) and sonorants indicate that consonants 
requiring precise articulatory timing and closure are especially 
vulnerable to omission. Collectively, these patterns highlight 
the articulatory challenges inherent to CP-induced dysarthric 
speech and their impact on ASR performance. 
5. Conclusions 
In this study, we investigated ASR for severely dysarthric 
speech using over 21 hours of single-speaker data with very low 
intelligibility and evaluated its generalization to other patients. 
Our results demonstrated that, in a speaker-dependent setting, 
even the conventional HMM -based approach achieved 
competitive performance (WER 13.4%), while fine-tuned 
Whisper models, particularly small and medium variants, 
further improved recognition accuracy. Generalization of a 
fine-tuned Whisper model to other speakers in the TORGO 
database showed clear benefits for severe cases without 
degrading performance for moderate or mild speech, which 
suggests severe dysarthria has distinct patterns compared to 
mild and moderate dysarthria.
