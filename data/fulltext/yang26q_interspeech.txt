The Perception of Korean Stop Coda Consonants by Mandarin Speakers in 
Taiwan: A Comparative Study of Proficiency Levels 
Shu-Wei Yang1, Jung-Yueh Tu2 
1 Seoul National University, Republic of Korea 
2 National Chengchi University, Taiwan 
ja1993ck@snu.ac.kr, jytu@nccu.edu.tw 
 
Abstract 
This study investigates the perceptual identification of Korean 
stop codas (/p, t, k/) by Mandarin speakers in Taiwan, focusing 
on preceding vowel context and learning experience. Motivated 
by reports that these learners often produce Korean unreleased 
codas with audible release, a four -alternative coda 
identification task was conducted with 30 learners (17 
beginners, 13 intermediate learners). Results showed an 
accuracy hierarchy of /p/ > /t/ > /k/. Vowel context shaped 
accuracy and confusion patterns: /p/ was best identified after /i/ 
and /u/, /t/ was best identified after /a/, /t/ was often 
misperceived as /k/ after non-front vowels (/a, u/), and /k/ 
showed vowel-dependent confusions. Proficiency group did not 
significantly affect accuracy, indicating no reliable beginner-
intermediate difference in the present sample and task. These 
findings suggest the need for targeted perceptual training that 
integrates vowel context and unreleased coda contrasts in 
Korean teaching.

1. Introduction 
Many Taiwan Mandarin-speaking learners of Korean report 
persistent difficulty in perceiving Korean stop codas /p, t, k/, 
and such difficulty can directly lead to lexical confusion in 
communication (e.g., /pat.ta/ misperceived as /pak.ta/). From a 
phonological perspective, Korean stop codas are obligatorily 
unreleased and contrast only in place of articulation (labial, 
coronal, dorsal). In contrast, Taiwan Mandarin permits only 
nasal codas (typically /n, ŋ/) and lacks oral stop codas, making 
Korean stop codas perceptually unfamiliar for these learners. 
To frame the learning problem, Table 1 summarizes the coda 
inventories of Korean and Taiwan Mandarin, highlighting the 
absence of labial codas in Taiwan Mandarin and the overall 
mismatch in manner categories. 
From an L2 speech learning perspective, the Perceptual 
Assimilation Model for L2 (PAM-L2; [1]) proposes that L1 and 
L2 categories coexist in the learner’s phonological space and 
may be restructured as experience accumulates, either by 
mapping to existing L1 categories or by forming new phonetic 
categories. The Revised Speech Learning Model (SLM-r; [2]) 
emphasizes that both the quantity and quality of phonetic input 
influence category formation, with contextual properties of the 
input (e.g., vowel environment) systematically shaping the 
distributional patterns from which phonetic categories emerge. 
These models motivate investigating (i) cross -category 
asymmetries among /p, t, k/, (ii) systematic effects of preceding 
vowel context, and (iii) whether additional learning experience 
yields measurable improvement. 
Previous work on Taiwan Mandarin-speaking learners has 
primarily focused on production errors from a contrastive 
perspective. For example, [3] reported that learners show higher 
error rates for stop codas than for nasal codas, suggesting that 
unfamiliar coda manner is challenging. Longitudinal perception 
work on Mandarin-speaking learners in other contexts (e.g., [4]) 
indicates that the developmental trajectory may differ across 
coda places, and that confusion among stop codas can persist. 
In addition, a large body of research shows that vowel context 
can modulate coda place perception by altering coarticulatory 
cues (e.g., [5], [6]). For Korean, [7] demonstrated that even 
native Seoul Korean listeners’ place perception varies by vowel 
context, which may indicate that learners may be particularly 
sensitive to (or disrupted by) vowel-conditioned cues.

Against this background, the current study examines how 
Taiwan Mandarin learners of Korean perceive Korean stop 
codas /p, t, k/ in different preceding vowel contexts, and 
whether perception patterns differ by learning experience 
(beginner vs. intermediate). The research questions are: 
1. How accurately do Taiwan Mandarin-speaking learners 
identify Korean stop codas /p, t, k/? 
2. Does the preceding vowel (/i, u, a/) modulate stop-coda 
identification and confusion patterns? 
3. Does learning experience (beginner vs. intermediate) 
affect identification accuracy for /p, t, k/? 
2. Methods 
2.1. Participants 
Thirty Taiwan Mandarin speakers enrolled at National 
Chengchi University (Taiwan) participated. All participants 
were minoring in Korean in the Department of Korean 
Language and Culture. Because participants did not provide 
standardized CEFR or TOPIK (Test of Proficiency in Korean) 
scores, proficiency was operationalized based on institutional 
course placement and the level of instructional materials. Based

2.2. Materials 
Stimuli consisted of monosyllabic Korean items produced by 
native Seoul Korean speakers. Recordings were first collected 
from eight speakers (five males, three females) in a quiet 
environment using a Sony digital recorder. To ensure clear and 
typical productions, two additional native Seoul Korean 
listeners completed a dictation check; the male and female 
talkers with the highest transcription accuracy were selected, 
and their recordings were used as the final stimuli. 
The stimulus set crossed three preceding vowels (/i, u, a/) 
with three stop codas (/p, t, k/) and a liquid coda /l/ used as a 
filler option, yielding 12 syllables in total: /ip/, /it/, /ik/, /il/, /up/, 
/ut/, /uk/, /ul/, /ap/, /at/, /ak/, /al/. With two talkers (male, 
female), the full set contained 24 tokens. 
2.3. Procedure 
The perception experiment was implemented in OpenSesame 
(v3.3.9). Participants listened to each token once over 
headphones and then selected the perceived coda consonant 
from four on-screen options (“ㄹ” /l/, “ㅂ” /p/, “ㄷ” /t/, “ㄱ” 
/k/). Trials were presented in randomized order, and 
participants completed the same procedure individually on a 
laptop computer. 
2.4. Data analysis 
Analyses focused on stop-coda targets (/p, t, k/); /l/ target trials 
were included as fillers and were not included in the inferential 
analyses. Identification accuracy was computed by target coda 
consonant, preceding vowel context, and proficiency group. 
Response distributions were also calculated as percentages 
within each target-coda × vowel-context cell. Statistical 
analyses were conducted in R. 
For participant-level accuracy, within -participant 
differences across target codas or vowel contexts were 
evaluated using Friedman tests, followed where appropriate by 
paired Wilcoxon signed-rank tests with Holm correction. 
Between-group differences by proficiency were tested using 
Wilcoxon rank-sum tests. To examine response-distribution 
patterns, vowel × response chi-square tests of independence 
were conducted within each stop-coda target. Because /l/ 
responses were sparse, they were retained in the descriptive 
confusion matrices but excluded from chi-square inferential 
analyses. Chi-square p-values were adjusted using the Holm 
method. 
3. Results 
3.1. Overall identification of stop codas 
Figure 1 summarizes mean identification accuracy for the three 
Korean stop codas across vowel contexts. Overall, learners 
showed a clear accuracy hierarchy: /p/ was identified most 
accurately, followed by /t/, with /k/ showing the lowest 
accuracy. A Friedman test confirmed a significant difference in 
accuracy across the three stop codas (χ²(2) = 36.07, p < .001). 
Pairwise Wilcoxon tests with Holm correction showed that all 
three codas differed significantly from one another (/p/ vs. /t/: p 
= .006; /p/ vs. /k/: p < .001; /t/ vs. /k/: p < .001).

confusions of /p/ with /t/ and /k/ were comparatively similar in

Figure 4 compares beginners and intermediate learners. While

4. Discussion 
4.1. Overall perception asymmetry among /p, t, k/ 
Overall, when accuracy was collapsed across vowel contexts, 
Taiwan Mandarin-speaking learners identified /p/ most 
accurately and /k/ least accurately. This asymmetry lends 
support to the view that dorsal place cues for unreleased stop 
codas are comparatively less salient in the available acoustic 
signal. In the absence of a corresponding L1 oral-stop coda 
category, learners may therefore rely more heavily on vowel-
conditioned coarticulation cues when making perceptual 
judgments. 
Furthermore, the cross-linguistic mismatch in coda 
inventories (Table 1) indicates that Korean stop codas cannot 
be straightforwardly assimilated to an existing Taiwan 
Mandarin coda category. This structural gap likely increases 
learners’ reliance on subphonemic acoustic detail and may 
underlie the systematic confusion patterns observed in the data. 
From the perspective of the PAM-L2 [1], L2 contrasts that 
cannot be readily assimilated to existing L1 categories are 
predicted to be difficult to discriminate. Korean stop codas 
likely constitute non -assimilable contrasts for Taiwan 
Mandarin-speaking learners, resulting in weaker perceptual 
consistency and increased reliance on subphonemic acoustic 
cues. Complementarily, the SLM-r [2] posits that the formation 
of new L2 categories depends on the phonetic dissimilarity 
from L1 categories and the salience of available acoustic cues. 
Under this framework, the lower accuracy for /k/ compared 
with /p/ likely reflects the relatively weaker perceptual cues 
associated with dorsal place, rather than the presence of an L1 
coda category for /p/. Taken together, both PAM-L2 and SLM-r 
highlight that cross-linguistic mismatches in phonological 
inventories can lead learners to rely disproportionately on fine-
grained acoustic detail, which in turn gives rise to systematic 
and asymmetric perceptual confusion patterns observed in this 
study.  
4.2. Vowel-conditioned shifts in confusion patterns 
Preceding vowel context systematically modulated both 
identification accuracy and the dominant error category among 
Taiwan Mandarin learners. As shown in Figure 2, /p/ showed 
the highest accuracy in the /i/ and /u/ contexts, whereas /t/ 
showed the highest accuracy in the /a/ context. In contrast, /k/ 
was the least accurately identified coda across all three vowel 
contexts for the learner group. The row-normalized confusion 
matrices in Figure 3 further show that vowel context affected 
not only overall accura cy but also the direction of 
misperception. In particular, /k/ was frequently identified as /t/ 
after /i/, but as /p/ in the /a/ and /u/ contexts. For /t/, the 
dominant error was /p/ in the /i/ context, but /k/ in the /a/ and 
/u/ contexts. 
Table 2 summarizes the accuracy ranking by vowel context 
for the current learners and, for comparison, Seoul Korean 
native listeners reported by [7]. The learner pattern did not fully 
replicate the native ranking in any vowel context. Nevertheless, 
the two groups showed partial overlap: both groups showed a 
/p/ advantage after /i/, and both showed relatively low /k/ 
accuracy after /u/. The clearest divergence occurred in the /a/ 
context. Taiwan Mandarin learners showed a /t/ advantage and 
a marked /k/ disadvantage, whereas Seoul Korean listeners 
were near ceiling and ranked /p/ slightly above /k/ and /t/.

       Because Korean stop codas are typically unreleased, 
release-burst cues are weak or absent, and listeners must rely 
heavily on vowel-to-closure transitions. These transitions may 
be viewed as the VC counterpart of consonant -to-vowel 
transitions that cue stop place in CV syllables. When the 
transition into a target coda acoustically resembles the pattern 
associated with another place category, learners may be biased 
toward that non-target response. This account helps explain 
why /k/ after /i/ was often identified as /t/: the high front vowel 
context may have made dorsal closure cues sound relatively 
coronal-like to learners. In the /a/ and /u/ contexts, by contrast, 
dorsal cues may have been weak or ambiguous, leading to more 
frequent /p/ responses. 
4.3. L1 background and L2 cue weighting 
The learner-native difference in Table 2 suggests that Taiwan 
Mandarin learners did not weight vowel -conditioned 
coarticulatory cues in a fully native-like manner. Because both 
accuracy rankings and dominant error patterns varied across 
vowel contexts, this difference should not be interpreted as a 
fixed preference among /p, t, k/. Rather, it reflects an interaction 
between acoustic salience, L1 coda structure, Korean-specific 
coda categories, and L2 cue weighting. 
       Taiwan Mandarin has nasal codas /n, ŋ/ but lacks oral stop 
codas. Therefore, learners do not have an L1-based mapping 
between vowel-to-closure transitions and unreleased oral stop 
coda categories. The learner advantage for /p/, especially in the 
/i/ and /u/ contexts, is informative in this respect: since Taiwan 
Mandarin lacks labial stop codas, the relatively high accuracy 
for /p/ is unlikely to result from direct L1 coda transfer. Instead, 
it may reflect comparatively robust labial cues in unreleased 
coda position, or a learner strategy in which /p/ becomes a more 
stable response when coronal and dorsal cues are ambiguous. 
By contrast, the consistently low accuracy for /k/ suggests that 
dorsal coda cues were especially vulnerable when learners had 
to rely on vowel-conditioned acoustic detail. 
       Native Korean listeners, in contrast, have established 
phonological categories for unreleased /p, t, k/ codas and 
extensive experience compensating for vowel -conditioned 
coarticulation. This experience may allow them to interpret 
vowel-to-closure transitions more categorically rather than 
relying directly on surface acoustic similarity. At the same time, 
the native data do not support a simple alveolar-bias account: 
/t/ was the most accurate native category only in the /u/ context, 
not in the /i/ or /a/ contexts, although coda typology more 
generally tends to favor alveolar over velar codas [8]. Thus, the 
learner-native difference is better understood as an interaction 
between acoustic salience, Korean-specific coda categories, 
and L2 cue weighting. 
4.4. Learning experience and a potential perceptual plateau 
No significant proficiency effect was observed between 
beginners and intermediate learners. From an SLM -r 
perspective, this pattern may reflect the limited availability of 
high-quality phonetic input for stop codas in everyday 
communication. Even when learners misperceive a stop coda, 
lexical knowledge and contextual cues can often “repair” the 
intended word, thereby reducing the pressure to refine the 
corresponding L2 phonetic category [2]. Under this view, 
additional months of general exposure are unlikely to yield 
substantial improvement without targeted perceptual training. 
Pedagogically, the results indicate that stop codas should 
not be taught as isolated segments. Instead, instructional 
approaches should explicitly incorporate vowel-conditioned 
minimal pairs and targeted perceptual training that highlights 
coarticulatory cues (e.g., /i/ vs. /u/ contexts) as well as 
commonly confused contrasts in non-front vowel contexts, such 
as /t/–/k/ after /a/ and /u/. Such training may help learners 
recalibrate cue weighting and facilitate more robust L2 category 
formation.

5. Conclusion 
This study examined how Taiwan Mandarin-speaking learners 
of Korean perceive Korean stop codas /p, t, k/ as a function of 
preceding vowel context and learning experience. Three main 
findings emerge: 
(1) When collapsed across vowel contexts, learners showed an 
overall accuracy hierarchy of /p/ > /t/ > /k/. 
(2) Preceding vowel context systematically modulated both 
accuracy and confusion patterns: /t/ was often heard as /k/ after 
/a/ and /u/, whereas /k/ was often heard as /t/ after /i/ but as /p/ 
after /a/ and /u/. 
(3) No reliable improvement was observed from beginner to 
intermediate learners in the present task. This absence of a 
proficiency-related difference should be interpreted cautiously, 
given the course-based proficiency grouping and the cross-
sectional design. It may point to a possible lack of spontaneous 
improvement without targeted phonetic training, but 
longitudinal or training-based evidence is needed to evaluate 
this interpretation directly. 
The findings imply that Korean pronunciation pedagogy 
for Taiwan Mandarin-speaking learners may benefit from 
explicit perceptual training that integrates vowel context and 
focuses on high-confusion pairs. Future work should test 
advanced learners, employ larger and more diverse talker sets, 
and evaluate whether targeted training can reshape stop-coda 
categories in both perception and production.
