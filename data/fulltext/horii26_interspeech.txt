How does children’s pronunciation develop? Capturing syllabic change
with children’s growth using unsupervised syllable discovery
Koharu Horii1,∗∗, Naohiro Tawara1, Atsunori Ogawa 1, Shoko Araki 1
1 NTT, Inc., Japan
koharu.horii@ntt.com
Abstract
Understanding children’s pronunciation development is crucial
for both speech science and robust automatic speech recogni-
tion (ASR) for children. However, conventional ASR-based
top-down analyses forcibly map highly variable child speech
onto adult-defined phoneme categories, limiting the capture of
child-specific and intermediate pronunciations. We propose a
bottom-up framework analyzing children’s developmental pro-
nunciation variation using an unsupervised syllable discovery
(USD) model, Sylber, to objectively verify how their speech
converges toward a standard style. Analyzing 957 children aged
5–15, we reveal a clear developmental pattern: expansion of
syllable patterns (ages 5–8), followed by gradual stabilization
toward adult-like speech. This approach captures fine-grained
changes in pronunciation repertoire and stability difficult to ob-
serve via top-down methods, demonstrating USD as an effective
tool for investigating child speech development at scale.
Index Terms: child speech, unsupervised syllable discovery,
phonetic variation, speech development, bottom-up analysis
1. Introduction
Accurate child speech processing is expected to play a key role
in emerging technologies such as social robots [1], educational
ICT [2], and disorder screening [3]. To improve such systems,
it is essential to accurately understand and model pronunciation
variation, including acoustic and phonological variations across
developmental stages [4, 5].
However, analyzing children’s pronunciation variation re-
mains challenging. In studies of language development, au-
ditory assessments by speech-language pathologists have tra-
ditionally been regarded as the gold standard [6, 7]. For ex-
ample, Smit et al. [6] clarified the phoneme acquisition pro-
cess from 3 to 9 years of age by presenting pictures to chil-
dren and asking them to pronounce the corresponding words.
Their recorded utterances were then phonetically transcribed
by trained specialists and compared with the correct pronun-
ciations. Although such expert-based evaluation provides de-
tailed insights into phoneme development, it is labor-intensive
and costly, limiting scalability, data size, and reproducibility.
Recently, large-scale analyses using automatic speech
recognition (ASR) have also been explored [8, 9, 10, 11]. Horii
et al. [11] analyzed recognition error patterns using a phoneme
recognizer adapted for child speech, demonstrating that mis-
pronunciation tendencies change with development. However,
ASR-based analyses in previous studies [8, 9, 10, 11] inher-
ently function astop-downapproaches because most ASR sys-
tems are trained on adult-defined phonological label invento-

ries. During inference, speech is interpreted within these prede-
fined categories, obscuring child-specific or intermediate pro-
nunciations that do not match adult phoneme distinctions.
To address this limitation, we adopt abottom-up paradigm
for child speech analysis. Bottom-up approaches originated
in zero-resource speech research, aiming to discover linguistic
structure directly from raw speech without predefined phono-
logical labels [12, 13]. Such approaches have been discussed
as a computational analogy of infant language acquisition [14].
Kamper et al. [15, 16] further explicitly discussed the distinc-
tion between bottom-up and top-down paradigms. Despite this
conceptual connection, bottom-up approaches have rarely been
applied to large-scale child speech analysis. Our study extends
this paradigm to characterize developmental pronunciation vari-
ation beyond adult-defined phoneme categories.
To implement this framework, we use the latest unsuper-
vised syllable discovery model, Sylber [17], which discovers
syllable-level acoustic clusters directly from speech on the ba-
sis of similarity, without phoneme supervision. While prior
work [6, 9, 10, 11] has primarily focused on phonemes to an-
alyze pronunciation variation, linguistic research suggests that
the syllable plays a fundamental role as a minimal unit of pro-
duction [18, 19] and perception [20, 21] from early infancy.
Motivated by this perspective, we conduct a syllable-level anal-
ysis of developmental pronunciation variation.
We evaluate the effectiveness of this bottom-up approach
through a large-scale analysis of speech from 957 children aged
5–15. For this purpose, we introduce two variants of Sylber: an
adult-trained model that reflects proximity to adult-like speech
organization and a child-adapted model designed to capture
child-specific acoustic variation. Using these two models, we
investigate developmental trends from three perspectives: (1)
speaking style, analyzed by measuring how closely bottom-up
syllable boundaries align with top-down reference boundaries
estimated by the Montreal Forced Aligner (MFA) [22] (which
relies on standard pronunciation), (2) pronunciation repertoire,
measured by the number of discovered syllable-level clusters,
and (3) pronunciation stability, measured by the consistency be-
tween acoustic clusters and corresponding target syllables.
Our main contributions are twofold. First, we propose a
bottom-up speech analysis framework for investigating devel-
opmental changes in children’s pronunciation from these three
perspectives. Second, through large-scale analysis, we reveal
a fine-grained developmental pattern in which children’s pro-
nunciation initially becomes more diverse and then gradually
stabilizes, providing empirical evidence that aligns with previ-
ous language development studies [6, 23]. We believe that the
proposed framework could be broadly applicable beyond child
speech analysis to other large-scale datasets that are challenging
to annotate, such as dysarthric and dialectal speech.

2. Methodology
2.1. Unsupervised Syllable Discovery Using Sylber
Unsupervised syllable discovery aims to identify syllable-level
acoustic units directly from raw speech without relying on pre-
defined phoneme inventories. Unlike top-down approaches that
refer to linguistic categories during segmentation, this method
derives units purely from acoustic regularities. While vari-
ous unsupervised syllable discovery methods have been pro-
posed recently [24, 25, 26], in this study, we use Sylber [17],
a state-of-the-art unsupervised syllable discovery framework
built upon self-supervised learning (SSL). Sylber extends SD-
HuBERT [27] and enhances syllabic representation learning
through sentence-level tokenization and self-distillation, en-
abling more accurate syllable detection.

2.2. Speaking Style Change: Boundary Agreement with
Top-Down Method
To quantify developmental changes in temporal organization
(e.g., speaking rate and rhythm), we measure the agreement be-
tween (i) target syllable boundaries estimated by MFA [22] and
(ii) bottom-up boundaries estimated by Sylber. Specifically, we
compute the following metrics:
•Jaccard index[30]: Evaluates the similarity between (i) and
(ii) with a 50-ms tolerance.
•Over-segmentation ratio[31, 32]: Measures the relative dif-
ference in the number of segments between (ii) and (i), cal-
culated as(N (ii)/N(i))−1, whereNdenotes the segment
count.
These measures serve as indicators of approximation to adult-
like temporal organization rather than segmentation accuracy.
Higher boundary agreement indicates closer alignment with
adult-like syllable timing patterns.

the 1,024 discovered syllable clusters, we count, for each age
group, the number of clusters that contain at least one speech

3. Experimental Setup
3.1. Datasets

parison, we sampled the data to match the minimum number of
speakers across all ages (87 speakers at age 5). Additionally,
we balanced the distribution of utterance content across ages.

The official Sylber model [17] builds on a HuBERT [36]
model pre-trained on 960 hours of LibriSpeech [28], a large-
scale read speech dataset that matches the speaking style of the
OGI Kids corpus. It is further fine-tuned on a 10k-utterance
subset [37] (34 hours) to learn syllable-level acoustic units with-
out supervision. To enable robust child-speech adaptation, we
used the large-scale MyST corpus [29], which consists of spon-
taneous speech from children aged 8–11 interacting with a vir-
tual tutor. We used the 315-hour training set for adaptation and
the 40-hour development set for validation. Although MyST
differs from OGI in speaking style and age range, its large scale
enables robust adaptation to child-specific acoustic characteris-
tics.
To generate top-down syllable labels and boundaries, we
converted the OGI Kids prompt text into ARPABET phonemes
using g2p en [38], grouped them into syllables with a rule-
based tool [39], and aligned the speech to the resulting syllable
sequences using MFA [22]. The resulting boundaries were used
only as analytical references, not for training.
3.2. Implementation Details
We used the official Sylber [40] and SD-HuBERT code-
bases [41] for syllable discovery and metric calculation, re-
spectively. Default hyperparameters were used during infer-
ence, except for the silence detection (0.9) and similarity (1.9)
thresholds, which were adjusted to mitigate noise-induced over-
segmentation on the basis of development data from the adult-
trained model.
We explored agglomerative cluster sizes from 512 to 4096
in powers of two and selected 1024 clusters on the basis of the
clustering stability and interpretability of the resulting inven-
tory.
For child-speech adaptation, we performed additional train-
ing using Stage 1 of Sylber [17], omitting Stage 2. The learning
rate was set to5×10 −4 with early stopping (patience of 10) to
prevent overfitting.
For target generation, we used MFA [22] (english us arpa
v3.0.0), which is trained on adult speech, together with a
syllable-level pronunciation dictionary. To ensure reliable
alignments, we excluded utterances with abnormally low align-
ment confidence. Specifically, utterances were removed if ei-
ther the alignment log-likelihood or the speech log-likelihood
fell below the interquartile range (IQR) criterion, defined as
Q1−1.5×IQRover the entire analysis dataset, whereQ1
is the first quartile.

4.2. Speaking Style Change
Figure 1 illustrates the age-wise trends in boundary agreement
with MFA [22]. Boundary agreement consistently increases
with age in both models, suggesting children’s pronunciation
progressively approaches standard speech. Compared with the
adult-trained model, the child-adapted model achieved a higher
Jaccard index and substantially lower over-segmentation across
all ages. This suggests that adaptation improves discrimina-
tion between speech and background noise, thereby suppressing
spurious boundary detections.
While over-segmentation in the adult-trained model fluctu-
ates without a clear age-dependent pattern, the adapted model
shows a steady increase in over-segmentation with age. One
plausible interpretation is that younger children tend to produce
reduced or ambiguous syllable realizations, often articulating
fewer syllables.

4.3. Pronunciation Repertoire
Figure 3 illustrates the age-wise trend in the number of dis-
covered syllable clusters, representing acoustically distinguish-
able syllable-like patterns. Both models exhibited an inverted
U-shaped curve: clusters increase from age 5 to 6–8, then de-
crease. This initial increase is consistent with the phoneme ac-
quisition period reported by Smit et al. [6] and may reflect a pe-
riod during which children explore different ways of producing
sounds before their pronunciation becomes more stable. Mean-
while, the subsequent decrease suggests progressive stabiliza-
tion and consolidation of the pronunciation repertoire. Thus,
the proposed method captures developmental convergence to-
ward a standardized syllable inventory in a data-driven manner.
In the child-adapted model, the number of clusters de-
creases at age 7 and increases again at age 8. Inspection of the
cluster composition suggests that children consolidate the pro-
nunciation of sounds acquired by age 6 when they reach age 7,
followed by the acquisition or refinement of additional sounds
at age 8. This pattern aligns with a cyclical process of stabiliza-
tion and expansion during development [23].
4.4. Pronunciation Stability
Figure 4 illustrates the age-wise trend of cluster purity relative
to the target syllables. In both models, cluster purity increases
monotonically with age, quantitatively supporting the hypoth-
esis that children’s pronunciations of the same target syllable
gradually converge into more acoustically consistent regions.
Although the child-adapted model generally achieves higher pu-
rity across age groups than the adult-trained model, both models
converge to nearly identical purity levels for ages 12 and older.
This suggests that children’s acoustic realizations aged 12 and
older are already close to those of adults.

5. Conclusion
In this study, we proposed a bottom-up framework for analyzing
developmental changes in children’s pronunciation using the
unsupervised syllable discovery model Sylber. Through a large-
scale analysis of 957 children aged 5–15, we identified a clear
developmental pattern characterized by an early expansion of
syllable-level variability (ages 6–8) followed by gradual stabi-
lization. Our results demonstrate that unsupervised syllable dis-
covery can capture fine-grained changes in pronunciation reper-
toire and stability that are difficult to observe with conventional
top-down phoneme-based approaches. These findings highlight
the potential of bottom-up acoustic modeling as an objective
and scalable tool for investigating child speech development.
Future work includes rigorously evaluating the estimated
syllable boundaries to enable more precise analyses. Ulti-
mately, we aim to uncover the syllable inventories of each de-
velopmental stage.
