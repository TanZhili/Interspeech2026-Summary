Abstract 
Voice control of a surgical -assistant robot must map short 
utterances to structured actions in the presence of surgical 
masks and operating -room (OR) noise, where misrecognition 
can result in unsafe motions. Each utterance in this task 
specifies either a m ovement direction with a discrete step 
magnitude (1–5) or a functional command. Treating each 
composed command as an independent class disregards this 
compositional structure. We propose a lightweight command -
spotting model that factorizes commands into direction, mode, 
and magnitude. This model uses head -wise temporal attention 
pooling on a shared encoder and learns step magnitude via 
ordinal regression with per-sample loss masking and a training-
only auxiliary head. On a Korean OR -command dataset 
evaluated using leave -one-speaker-out under clean/noise and 
mask/no mask conditions, our model achieves 95.36% joint 
command success, with a MAE of 0.0338 steps and a 
catastrophic step error rate of 0.60% (|Δ|≥3) on step commands. 
Index Terms : command spotting, keyword spotting, ordinal 
regression, surgical robotics, safety-critical speech 
1. Introduction 
Voice interaction in operating rooms (OR) is challenged by 
personal protective equipment and high background noise. 
Mask use and OR -like noise can reduce  speech intelligibility, 
motivating more reliable communication and assistance 
interfaces in the OR [1, 2]. Recent work has explored smart-OR 
speech assistants and in -OR multimodal dataset for command 
recognition [3, 4], and surgical robot assistants have been 
controlled via ASR-based speech-to-action pipelines integrated 
with robot control software stacks [5, 6]. 
However, for safety -critical robot control, a closed -set 
command interface is often preferable to free-form transcription, 
as it enables bounded on-device latency, reduces error pathways 
introduced by downstream parsing, and simplify verification in 
a safety case. [7]. Keyword spotting (KWS) has therefore been 
extensively studied for on -device triggers and small command 
sets using compact CNN backb ones [8, 9, 10]. For surgical 
robots, edge deployment is a strict practical constraint rather 
than a mere preference, as reliance on external servers 
introduces unacceptable network latency and patient privacy 
risks. Deployment-oriented work further targets efficient 
inference on edge devices, including microcontroller settings 
and lightweight optimization strategies [11, 12].  
More recent efforts improve robustness to noisy and 
mismatched conditions t hrough stronger pooling/attention 
designs and explicit robustness techniques, including 
multichannel processing and test -time adaptation [13, 14, 15, 
16]. Streaming formulations based on transducer -style models 
have also been explored for low-latency spotting [17]. 
In this task, most commands are compositional: a 
movement direction combined with either a discrete step count 
(1–5) or a continuous -motion indicator, plus a few functional 
commands such as “stop.” Treating each composed command 
as an independent class ignores this structure and, critically, the 
ordinal nature of step magnitude: confusing five steps with four 
steps is less hazardous than confusing it with one step. Yet 
standard softmax training does not explicitly encode ordinality 
and penalizes these confusions similarly, making it difficult to 
suppress large-step (catastrophic) errors. 
We propose a lightweight shared -encoder model that 
factorizes each command into direction, mode, and magnitude. 
We use head -wise temporal attention pooling to aggregate 
factor-specific evidence without timestamp labels, and we 
model discrete magnitude with consistent rank logits (CORAL) 
ordinal regression [18], trained with per -sample loss masking 
and a training-only auxiliary 51-way head for stabilization. We 
evaluate unseen -speaker generalization using leave -one-
speaker-out (LOSO) across Clean/Noise and Mask/No-mask 
conditions and report joint command success together with 
safety-oriented magnitude metrics (MAE and catastrophic step 
error rate). We also study English pretraining on Google Speech 
Commands v2 [19] followed by Korean fine-tuning. 
Our contributions are summarized as: 
 We formulate safety-critical surgical-robot command 
spotting as factorized prediction of direction, mode, 
and magnitude with head -wise temporal attention 
pooling. 
 We introduce ordinal magnitude learning and safety -
aware evaluation that distinguishes near -miss from 
catastrophic step errors. 
 We validate robustness under OR room conditions 
with unseen speakers and assess the effectiveness of 
auxiliary guidance and cross -lingual initialization 
from English to Korean. 
2. Command Set and Task Definition 
We consider a fixed vocabulary of 51 Korean commands. 
Forty-eight commands are compositional movement 
commands formed by eight movement directions (left, right, up, 
down, forward, backward, clockwise, counterclockwise ) and 
six magnitude (one step, two steps, three steps, four steps, five 
steps, continue). The remaining three commands are functional 
single commands (cleaning, return, stop). 
Each utterance is annotated with a single command label 
from the 51 -command vocabulary , such as  left three-steps, 
forward_continue, or stop, rather than separate  labels. As 
summarized in Table 1 , we deterministically parse the

3. Method 
Given an input waveform sampled at 16 kHz, we extract a 
40-bin log -mel spectrogram X and feed it to a shared 
lightweight encoder that produces frame -level representations 
H. We then apply head -wise temporal attention pooling to 
obtain three task-specific summary vectors for direction, mode, 
and magnitude. Direction and mode are predicted by softmax 
classifiers, while discrete magnitude is learned via CORAL 
ordinal regression. 
Figure 1 provides an overview of t he proposed model. A 
shared lightweight encoder transforms the log-mel spectrogram 
into frame-level features, which are summarized by head -wise 
temporal attention for direction, mode, and magnitude 
prediction. An auxiliary 51 -way head is used only during 
training to stabilize learning.

3.3. Ordinal magnitude modeling via CORAL 
Discrete magnitude (1 to 5 steps) is an ordinal variable. We 
adopt CORAL ordinal regression, which learns K−1 
thresholded binary decisions (here K  = 5) such as “magnitude 
≥ 2”, “≥ 3”, and so on. We decode the magnitude by counting 
how many CORAL thresholds exceed 0.5 and adding 1.  We 
decode magnitude as:

for discrete-motion commands via per-sample weights. Ordinal 
learning encourages near -miss errors over catastrophic errors, 
aligning with safety goals.

3.5. Encoder pretraining (English → Korean) 
To stabilize training in a low -speaker regime, we initialize 
the shared encoder via supervised KWS pretraining on Google 
Speech Commands v2 (English) [19] using the same log -mel 
spectrogram. The pretraining classifier is discarded and the 
encoder is fine-tuned on the Korean OR-command dataset. For 
fair comparison, we apply the same initialization setting to all 
baselines and include an ablation without pretraining. 
4. Dataset and Experimental Setup 
We collect ed a Korean OR -command dataset under four 
controlled conditions defined by Clean/Noise × Mask/No-mask. 
OR background noise was sourced from real operating -room 
recordings and added via a replay -and-re-recording procedure 
to emulate acoustic playback and microphone capture. The 
dataset contains 7,140 utterances from five speakers (3 male, 2 
female) at 16 kHz, covering all 51 commands. 
For data augmentation, we adopt the training augmentations 
recommended in [20] for all models . During training, we 
randomly shift the waveform in time within ±100 ms  [21], and 
we apply Gaussian-noise mixing to 80% of examples. We also 
apply SpecAugment by masking two time regions and two 
frequency bands, without time warping [22]. 
We evaluate unseen -speaker generalization using a leave -
one-speaker-out (LOSO) protocol. We report joint command 
success as the primary metric, and quantify safety using 
magnitude MAE (steps) and catastrophic step error rate (CSE), 
computed on discrete s tep commands. Results are reported as 
mean ± standard deviation over the five LOSO folds. 
We define CSE as |Δ| ≥ 3  because the magnitude range is 
limited to five discrete steps. This corresponds to the 3 –4 step 
errors in the upper tail of the error distribution, which can cause 
substantial overshoot or undershoot in commanded motion. In 
contrast, smaller errors are treated as near misses, motivating a 
safety-aware distinction between large and small magnitude 
mistakes. 
For preprocessing of the input signals , we compute 40 bin 
log-mel spectrograms using an STFT with a 25 ms analysis 
window and 10 ms hop, and an FFT length of 512. The mel 
filterbank spans 60 -7600 Hz. We apply per -utterance mean –
variance normalization to the log-mel features. The models are 
trained with the Adam optimizer at a learning rate of 0.0001 for 
150 epochs, with a batch size of 16. The same preprocessing 
and training schedule is used for all models. 
5. Results and Discussion 
We compare against three baselines to isolate the effect of 
factorization, pooling, and ordinal magnitude learning: (A) a  
flat 51-way command classifier only with shared encoder , (B) 
a factorized model with a single shared attention pooling 
module (no head-specific attention), and (C) a factorized model 
with head -wise attention but a softmax magnitude head (no 
ordinal learning). Our full model combines head-wise attention 
with CORAL-based ordinal magnitude learning and a training-
only auxiliary 51 -way head for stabilization. We additionally 
include BC -ResNet-6 as a ex ternal KWS baseline  [20]. Its 
width parameter is chosen to yield comparable parameter 
numbers to our model  (τ = 6) . Unless otherwise stated, all 
models use the same log-mel front end, data splits, and training 
schedule (optimizer, learning rate, epochs, an d batch size), as 
well as the same English initialization setting, when applicable. 
As shown in Table 3, exploiting the compositional structure 
consistently improves command-level performance. Compared 
with the monolithic command classifier, factorized prediction 
increases joint success from 93.14% to 93.68%, and introducing 
head-wise attention pooling further improves joint success to 
94.57%. BC -ResNet-6, included as a parameter -matched 
external baseline, achieves a comparable joint success o f 
93.65%. Importantly, the best joint success does not necessarily 
coincide with the best safety profile. When magnitude is 
modeled with a standard softmax head, CSE increases to 0.83% 
and MAE remains at 0.0445 steps. By incorporating CORAL 
ordinal learnin g, our full model achieves the best overall 
safety–success trade -off, reaching 95.36% joint command 
success while reducing MAE to 0.0338 steps and CSE to 0.60% 
on step commands.

evidence pooling for compositional commands.

6. Conclusion and Limitations 
We presented a safety-critical command spotting approach 
for OR robot voice control that factorizes each utterance into 
direction, mode, and magnitude, applies head -wise temporal 
attention pooling, and models discrete magnitude using 
CORAL ordinal learning. Under LOSO evaluation on a Korean 
OR-command dataset with Clean/Noise and Mask/No-mask 
conditions, our final model achieves 95.36% joint co mmand 
success while maintaining low magnitude error rates on discrete 
commands (0.0338-step MAE and 0.60% CSE). These results 
support the practicality of safety -aware evaluation that 
distinguishes near-miss from large step-magnitude mistakes in 
compositional command interfaces. 
This study is limited by the number of speakers and by 
evaluation on pre -segmented 2 s utterances, which does not 
capture the full complexity of always -on, streaming operation 
in the operating room. Our noise condition is created via replay-
and-re-recording to emulate acoustic capture, but it may not 
fully reflect distribution shifts in real OR workflows .  
Consequently, while our model handles standard variance well, 
generalizing to some outliers remains a challenge for low -
speaker clinical datasets. 
Future work will expand speaker and environmental 
diversity, and evaluate streaming detection and rejection 
policies in a closed-loop robot control setting with deployment-
oriented profiling.
