Abstract
Auditory attention decoding (AAD) is often evaluated on static,
simplified speech scenes that poorly match everyday listening.
We introduce MOV-AAD, a large-scale dataset for studying au-
ditory attention under moving, naturalistic conversations. MOV-
AAD combines 64-channel EEG with synchronized physiolog-
ical recordings, including eye tracking, respiration, galvanic
skin response, heart rate, peripheral oxygen saturation, body
temperature, body motion, and photoplethysmography, enabling
analysis of cross-modal neural and physiological markers of
attention and listening effort. This dataset uses a more eco-
logically valid listening paradigm with dynamically moving
conversational speech sources and behavioral measures of at-
tentional engagement. MOV-AAD supports research on robust
AAD in realistic spatial dynamics, multimodal attention model-
ing, listening effort, and intersubject neural responses, providing
a resource for benchmarking selective auditory attention in natu-
ralistic listening.

naturalistic speech, EEG, physiological signals
1. Introduction
Selective auditory attention enables listeners to focus on a tar-
get speaker in noisy environments, commonly known as the
“cocktail party effect” [1]. Neural studies using EEG and MEG
have demonstrated that cortical activity preferentially tracks the
speech envelope of the attended speaker [2, 3, 4], forming the
basis of auditory attention decoding (AAD) in multi-speaker
scenarios. These findings have motivated research on neural
decoding algorithms and neuro-steered hearing technologies.
Attentive listening, however, is not solely a cortical phe-
nomenon. Selective attention and listening effort engage dis-
tributed neural and autonomic systems. Peripheral physiological
markers such as pupillometry, heart rate variability, respiration
dynamics, and electrodermal activity (e.g. galvanic skin re-
sponse) have been linked to cognitive load, arousal regulation,
and sustained attention during speech processing [ 5, 6, 7, 8].
Eye-tracking measures including pupil dilation and gaze sta-
bility similarly reflect task engagement and top-down control
processes [9, 10, 11]. Intersubject correlation (ISC) analyses
further reveal shared stimulus-driven neural dynamics during
attentive engagement [12, 13, 14]. These findings suggest that
auditory attention involves coordinated interactions across neural
and peripheral physiological systems.

However, most publicly available AAD datasets focus pri-
marily on EEG and lack synchronized autonomic and behavioral
measurements. Moreover, many existing datasets rely on spa-
tially stationary speakers, synthetic mixtures, or constrained
acoustic paradigms that only partially approximate real-world
listening environments [15, 16]. The lack of naturalistic multi-
modal recordings limits investigations of how neural and physio-
logical processes jointly support attention and effort in dynamic
acoustic scenes.
Here we introduceMOV-AAD 1, a large-scale multimodal
dataset designed to support research on auditory attention decod-
ing and cognitive state assessment under naturalistic listening
conditions. The dataset integrates 64-channel EEG with syn-
chronized physiological and behavioral modalities, including
heart rate, respiration flow and effort, galvanic skin response
(GSR), peripheral oxygen saturation (SpO2), body temperature,
body motion (accelerometry), photoplethysmography (PPG),
and binocular eye tracking (pupil dilation and gaze location),
recorded from 50 participants. By combining neural and auto-
nomic measures under dynamic listening conditions, MOV-AAD
provides a foundation for investigating how top-down attention
is coordinated across cortical and peripheral systems.
To position MOV-AAD within the existing literature, Table 1
summarizes representative publicly available non-invasive AAD
datasets. While datasets such as DTU [16, 17] and KULeuven
[18, 19] provide high-density EEG recordings in two-speaker
paradigms, most employ spatially stationary speech mixtures
and lack synchronized peripheral physiological measurements.
The PhyAAt dataset [ 20] incorporates selected physiological
signals but primarily focuses on single-speaker listening with
noise rather than competitive multi-talker attention. Compared
to these resources, MOV-AAD uniquely combines (i) dynamic
spatial movement of conversational speech sources with turn-
taking, (ii) a substantial participant cohort (N=50), and (iii)
comprehensive multimodal recordings integrating EEG with
autonomic and behavioral measures.
Each participant completed both single-conversation and
multi-conversation tasks. In the single-conversation condition
(40 trials), participants listened to conversational narratives pre-
sented from a spatially moving source. In the multi-conversation
condition (56 trials), they attended to one speaker among dy-
namically moving competing conversational streams spanning
±90◦ azimuth. To behaviorally track attention, participants per-
formed a repeated-word detection task during listening, which
has been shown to correlate with attentional engagement and task
difficulty [23, 24]. The inclusion of spatial movement and natu-

Across all tasks, synchronized 64-channel EEG and multi-
modal physiological signals were recorded from 50 participants,
comprising 4,800 attentive-listening trials. As one of the largest

tory attention research, MOV-AAD enables dynamic-condition
benchmarking, listening-effort analysis, and within- and cross-
subject modeling.
2. Dataset Description
2.1. Participants
The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review
Board of the authors’ university. Fifty participants (28 male at
birth; age 18-38 years, mean = 24, SD = 4.5) with self-reported
normal hearing and no history of neurological or attention dis-
orders were recruited between October 2022 and June 2025.
Written informed consent was obtained prior to participation,
and all participants were compensated.
2.2. Speech Materials and Spatialization
Speech stimuli were constructed as naturalistic conversational
streams spoken by native American English talkers. Each con-
versation consisted of temporally continuous speech segments
with occasional embedded repeated words used for attention
monitoring. The mean interval between repeated-word onsets
within a stream was 7.0 s (SD = 1.0 s).
All speech streams were spatialized using head-related trans-
fer functions (HRTFs) and presented through earphones. Dur-
ing moving-speaker conditions, conversations moved continu-
ously within the frontal-horizontal plane (azimuth range: -90◦ to
+90◦). The perceived distance of each stream was held constant
across trials. In multi-conversation trials, streams were matched
in root-mean-square (RMS) power.
Diotic background noise (pedestrian noise or speech bab-
ble) was added at levels 9 or 12 dB below the power of each
conversation stream.

their spatial perception accuracy.

perceived direction of the sound source using a mouse-click

2.3.3. Single Moving Speaker Conversation (SC)
Participants completed 40 trials in which a single conversational
stream moved continuously within the frontal horizontal plane
(-90◦ to +90◦). Speaker identity varied within trials, and par-
ticipants responded to embedded repeated words in a 1-back
detection task.
2.3.4. Multiple Moving Speakers Conversation (MC)
This task extended the SC framework by presenting two indepen-
dent conversational streams simultaneously. Participants com-
pleted 56 trials in which two spatially separated conversational
streams moving within the same azimuth range. Participants
were instructed to attend to the conversation that began first and
to press a button upon detecting repeated words in the attended
stream. The unattended stream began 3 s after the onset of the
attended stream. In the attended conversation, one talker switch
occurred at approximately 50% of the trial duration, whereas in
the unattended stream, two switches occurred at approximately
25% and 75%. Assignment of male and female talkers was coun-
terbalanced across trials. Repeated-word events did not overlap
across streams.
2.4. Data Acquisition and Preprocessing
EEG (64 channels) and peripheral physiological signals were
acquired using g.tec sensors and a g.HIamp amplifier (g.tec
medical engineering GmbH, Austria) at 1200 Hz with a 60 Hz
notch filter. The peripheral modalities included GSR, triaxial
accelerometry, respiratory flow (nasal airflow), respiratory effort
(thoracic belt), SpO 2, PPG, heart rate, and skin temperature.
Eye-tracking signals, including pupil diameter and instantaneous
horizontal and vertical gaze coordinates, were acquired using
a Tobii Pro Nano eye tracker (Tobii AB, Sweden). All neural,
physiological, and eye-tracking streams were synchronized and
recorded through Simulink.
Behavioral responses (repeated-word detections and local-
ization reports) were collected through a customized MATLAB-
based interface and synchronized to physiological recordings.
Audio playback was simultaneously recorded, and temporal
alignment was established by cross-correlating the recorded au-
dio with the original stimulus waveform.
The released dataset includes recordings from the repeated-
sentence task (∼12 min per participant), SC tasks ( ∼30 min
per participant), and MC tasks (∼45 min per participant); and
behavioral reports from the localization task, SC and MC tasks.
Specifically, for SC and MC trials, we provide EEG and periph-

3.1. Behavioral Validation Metrics
3.1.1. Localization Perception
The localization task included nine discrete azimuth locations
spanning -90° to +90°. For each participant, localization per-
formance was quantified by accuracy and error. Localization
accuracy was computed as the proportion of correctly identified
source locations across trials, with chance performance defined
as 1/9. Localization performance was further quantified using
the mean absolute error (MAE) between the reported and the
ground-truth stimulus location. For each participant, MAE was

3.1.2. Repeated-word Detection Metrics
Repeated-word performance was used to assess sustained atten-
tion during the conversation tasks. A repeated word was con-
sidered correctly detected if a space-bar press occurred within
2 s of the onset of the repeated word in the attended stream.
A space-bar press was counted as a false alarm if no attended
repeated-word onset occurred in the preceding 2 s window.
For each participant, we computed hit rate, precision, and
F1 score separately for the SC and MC conditions.
Statistical comparisons were performed using two-tailed
Wilcoxon signed-rank tests. F1 scores were compared between
MC and SC conditions as well as two streams within the MC
condition (Attended vs. Unattended). For all tests, we report

3.2. Physiological Data Quality Assessment
We perform a data quality sanity check by extracting a set of
basic physiological features from the MC session, which in-
clude heart rate, skin temperature, skin conductance level (SCL,
extracted from GSR), non-specific skin conductance response
rate (NS-SCR, extracted from GSR), respiration frequency, ac-
celerometer magnitude, SpO2, heart rate variability (HRV , ex-
tracted from PPG) features, and pupil dilation.
GSR was characterized using both tonic and phasic mea-
sures. The slow-changing tonic component (SCL) was computed
by low-pass filtering and averaging the raw GSR signal to cap-
ture baseline level. The phasic component (NS-SCR rate) was
computed as the number of spontaneous SCR events per minute.
Accelerometer signals were summarized using the magni-
tude of tri-axial acceleration, expressed in units of gravity (g), as
a proxy for body movement during the task. Since this measure
includes the static gravitational component, a baseline around 1
g is expected, and deviations from this value reflect changes in
movement and/or orientation.
HRV metrics were derived from PPG signals by detecting
pulse peaks and computing inter-beat intervals. From these in-
tervals, standard HRV metrics were calculated: SDNN (standard
deviation of NN intervals), reflecting overall variability; and
RMSSD (root mean square of successive differences), reflecting
parasympathetic activity.
All features were computed within fixed-length analysis
windows (40 s) and averaged across trials and participants. The
resulting distributions were compared against expected physio-
logical ranges based on prior literature[31, 32, 33, 34] and device
specifications.
3.3. Baseline Analyses
3.3.1. Envelope Reconstruction
For each trial, the speech envelope was extracted using the mag-
nitude of the analytic signal (Hilbert transform), followed by
4th-order Butterworth low-pass filtering at 8 Hz. This approach
follows common practice in cortical speech tracking studies
targeting slow amplitude modulations [14, 35].
To reconstruct the speech envelope from EEG, data were
resampled from 1200 Hz to 100 Hz, with an embedded anti-
aliasing filter. Signals were normalized and nonlinearly com-
pressed using 5 tanh(x/5) to mitigate extreme outliers. EEG
was then band-pass filtered to 1-8 Hz using a zero-phase FIR
filter (order 256). This frequency band captures low-frequency
neural tracking of the speech envelope widely reported in EEG
and MEG literature [4, 36].
Envelope reconstruction was performed using a backward
model implemented in the mTRF Toolbox v2.3 [37]. Decoders
were trained with time lags from 0 to 400 ms. Ridge parame-
ters were logarithmically spaced from 10−2 to 104 and selected
via leave-one-trial-out cross-validation. Reconstruction perfor-
mance was quantified as the Pearson correlation between the
reconstructed and true envelopes on the held-out trial. Single-
speaker and competing-speaker sessions were modeled and eval-
uated separately.
3.3.2. Trajectory Reconstruction
Spatial trajectories were defined as the time-varying azimuth
positions of each conversational stream. Trajectories were gener-
ated during stimulus design following the procedure described
in [26]. Briefly, speaker positions were constrained to the frontal
half of the horizontal plane (-90° to +90°) and were generated
using a first-order Markov chain with 1° spatial resolution. The
resulting sequences were smoothed and upsampled to 10 Hz
prior to spatial rendering via HRTF-based processing. The final
trajectories were further interpolated to 100 Hz to match the
neural sampling rate.
Trajectory reconstruction was performed following the de-
coding framework described in [25]. EEG signals were resam-
pled to 100 Hz and robustly normalized prior to band-specific
filtering. For delta-band analysis, EEG was band-pass filtered
between 0.05-2 Hz using a zero-phase Butterworth filter. For

3.3.3. Auditory Attention Decoding (AAD)
For multi-conversation trials, AAD was performed separately
for each participant using linear discriminant analysis (LDA)
following [25]. For each trial, rA andrU denote the Pearson
correlations between the reconstructed signal and the attended
and unattended stimuli, respectively.
Trial-wise feature vectors were constructed as [rA,r U]. To
form a balanced binary classification problem during training,
swapped feature vectors [rU,r A] were included as an alternative
class.
Decoding was evaluated using leave-one-trial-out cross-
validation across trials. In each fold, the held-out trial was tested
using the true-order feature vector, while training was performed
on both true-order and swapped-order samples from the remain-
ing trials. Accuracy was defined as the proportion of correctly
classified held-out true-order trials. Multiple feature setups were
tested, including envelope-only, trajectory-only (delta or alpha),
