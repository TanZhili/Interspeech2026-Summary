Frontend for Dysarthric Speech Conformer ASR

Abstract
Recent speech foundation models have advanced automatic
speech recognition (ASR) systems, particularly for clean or
controlled speech. However, no foundation models are specif-
ically designed to capture the characteristics of dysarthric
speech, making adaptation of existing models necessary. A
common approach is to utilize pretrained self-supervised learn-
ing (SSL) models to extract robust speech representations for
ASR. In this work, we propose an efficient adaptation method
that conditions self-supervised HuBERT features on speaker in-
telligibility using channel-wise modulation through FiLM-only
and Gated-FiLM mechanisms. By training only 6.3M (FiLM)
to 15.78M (Gated-FiLM) parameters, the proposed method
achieves WERs of 21.3% and 21.0%, respectively, introducing
intelligibility-aware channel modulation of HuBERT features in
an end-to-end Conformer ASR system.
Index Terms: Intelligibility, channel conditioning, SSL fron-
tend, conformer, ASR, dysarthria
1. Introduction
Dysarthria is a neuro-motor speech disorder resulting from con-
ditions such as cerebral palsy (CP), amyotrophic lateral sclero-
sis (ALS), brain injury, etc., that affect the central nervous sys-
tem [1]. These impairments disrupt control over the muscles
responsible for speech production, leading to speech that is of-
ten inarticulate, slurred, or monotonous. Recognizing such im-
paired speech poses a significant challenge for automatic speech
recognition (ASR) systems, primarily due to limited availabil-
ity of annotated training data and inter speaker variability due
to differences in level of speech intelligibility [2].
The advent of transformer-based architectures and attention
mechanisms has greatly advanced progress in ASR systems [3].
In particular, large pretrained speech foundation models lever-
aging self-supervised learning (SSL) have opened new path-
ways for task-specific adaptation [4]. These models are typi-
cally employed in two ways: either by extracting rich speech
representations as input features or by fine-tuning them on
domain-specific data [5]. Moreover, publicly available check-
points of SSL models trained on large datasets have made their
use more accessible. However, a critical limitation is that these
foundation models are predominantly trained on typical (con-
trol) speech, which does not adequately capture the characteris-
tics of dysarthric speech [6].
Recent research in dysarthric ASR has predominantly fo-
cused on developing end-to-end systems and fine-tuning ap-
proaches utilizing large speech foundation models. Notable
studies include investigations into adversarial data augmenta-
tion for fine-tuning pre-trained ASR systems [7], evaluation
of fine-tuning strategies using diverse data sources [8], explo-
ration of multistage fine-tuning approaches [9], and applica-
tion of meta-learning for model re-initialization [10] to improve
recognition accuracy. More recently, end-to-end architectures,
including the dysarthric transformer [11] and dysarthric con-
former [12], have been proposed for dysarthric speech recog-
nition. These models have shown strong performance, high-
lighting the effectiveness of transformer-based end-to-end ap-
proaches in handling the variability of dysarthric speech.
Despite progress in dysarthric ASR, very few studies have
explored the use of pretrained speech foundation models as fea-
ture frontends in ASR systems [13, 14]. In this work, we uti-
lize pretrained self-supervised speech representations from Hu-
BERT [15] and adapt them for dysarthric speech recognition us-
ing intelligibility-aware feature conditioning. Specifically, we
introduce channel-wise modulation [16] through FiLM [17] and
Gated-FiLM to incorporate speaker intelligibility information
into the SSL representations without extensively fine-tuning the
encoder. This conditioning enables the model to better cap-
ture severity-dependent acoustic variations present in dysarthric
speech. The resulting representations are integrated into an
end-to-end Conformer ASR system [18], leading to improved
recognition performance and generalization under the leave-
one-speaker-out (LOSO) evaluation [19] setting.
2. Database and Intelligibility level
The TORGO database [20] is currently the only publicly avail-
able, open-source English dysarthric speech corpus that con-
tains bothsingle-wordandsentence-level utterances, specifi-
cally designed for research in dysarthria and speech disorders.
It consists of recordings from 8 dysarthric speakers diagnosed
with Amyotrophic Lateral Sclerosis (ALS) or Cerebral Palsy
(CP), as well as 7 age- and gender-matched control speak-
ers with typical speech. The data preprocessing followed the
methodology outlined by Hermann et al. [19], which involved
eliminating empty audio files, those with complex transcrip-
tions, and files lacking transcriptions. It resulted in 5.42 and
9.71 hours of dysarthric and control speech, respectively.
A key feature of the TORGO database is the inclusion of
intelligibility ratings for each dysarthric speaker, provided at
theword,sentence, andconversationallevels [21]. These rat-
ings follow a standardized scale from‘a’ to ‘e’, where‘a’in-
dicates high intelligibility and‘e’represents severely impaired
intelligibility. These annotations are crucial for evaluating and
benchmarking speech recognition and assessment systems tai-
lored for dysarthric speech. For clarity, Table 1 summarizes the
intelligibility ratings provided in the TORGO database.

First, we extract utterance-level HuBERT mean features
and train a neural classifier to generate compact intelligibility
embeddings (128 dim.) representing utterance-wise dysarthria
severity. For classifier’s training, speaker-level intelligibility

grouped IA (IS≤1.5), IB (1.5<IS≤3.0), or IC (IS>3.0),

inherits its speaker’s class. The classifier is trained on these

split, achieving 89-to-95% validation accuracy. Eight classi-
fiers, each independent of the test speaker, were built to fol-
low the LOSO setup used in ASR. A 128-dimensional embed-
ding is extracted from the penultimate layer. These embeddings
along with speech utterances and corresponding transcripts is
then used to build ESPnet-compatible data manifests, enabling
effective conditioning during ASR model training.

jects the learned intelligibility embeddings (at interval of 10
transformer layers, total of 3 modules) into the HuBERT fron-
tend features using a gated FiLM-style mechanism [17], en-

dysarthria intelligibility. We train thechannel conditionerlay-

3.2. Intelligibility-aware channel conditioning
To incorporate dysarthria intelligibility information into the
acoustic representations, we extend the pretrained HuBERT
frontend with achannel conditioner[16] module. This mod-
ule injects speaker-level intelligibility embeddings into the Hu-
BERT features using a FiLM-style conditioning mechanism
[17], which allows the model to adaptively scale and shift fea-
ture activations based on external control signals. Given the in-
telligibility embeddings of an utterance, conditioner modulates
each feature channel as per the scale and shift vectors. These
vectors are then updated as per the ASR objective.

3.3. Conformer ASR setup
The severity-conditioned HuBERT features are passed to an ES-
Pnet encoder built with Conformer layers [18], which capture
both local and global temporal dependencies in the acoustic
signal. The encoder consists of 12 Conformer blocks with 4
attention heads, linear projection units of size 2048, and a CNN
module with kernel size 31. Dropout and positional dropout of
0.1 are applied, and relative positional encoding is used to im-
prove sequence modeling [23]. The input to the encoder is a
linear projection of the conditioned HuBERT features, ensuring
compatibility with the pretrained frontend [15].
The encoder outputs are decoded using a Transformer de-
coder [24] with 6 layers, 4 attention heads, and 512 linear units
per layer. Dropout rates are set to 0.03 to regularize training,
and the decoder attends to the encoder outputs to generate text
sequences. Training is guided by a joint CTC/attention objec-
tive [25], with the CTC loss weighted at 0.7 and the attention
cross-entropy loss implicitly weighted by the remaining 0.3, al-
lowing the model to balance alignment-free sequence modeling
with sequence-to-sequence learning.
Beam search is employed at inference with a beam size
of 20, a CTC weight of 0.7, and an LM weight of 0.3 to ef-
fectively combine acoustic and language model information.
Additional constraints such as maximum and minimum output
length ratios and length penalties are set to 0 to avoid biasing
sequence length. SpecAugment [26] is applied during training
with frequency and time masking to improve robustness on the
dysarthric speech dataset. The Adam optimizer with a learning
rate of 0.0003 and a warm-up schedule of 5000 steps ensures
stable convergence, while early stopping with a patience of 5
prevents overfitting.
This configuration enables efficient training on smaller
dysarthric datasets while leveraging severity-aware HuBERT
features for improved recognition performance.
4. Result
Table 2 summarizes the ASR performance in terms of WER,
CER, and SER across dysarthric speakers (F01–F04 and M01–
M05) under the leave-one-speaker-out (LOSO) [13] setup, com-
paring FBANK, HuBERT, and the proposed intelligibility-
conditioned HuBERT variants (FiLM-only and Gated-FiLM).
The FBANK baseline yields the highest error rates, particularly
for more severe speakers such as M01, M02, M04, and M05.
Replacing FBANK with HuBERT significantly improves recog-
nition performance; for example, for F01 the WER drops from
54% to 31.3% and the CER from 41.4% to 21%, confirming the
advantage of self-supervised speech representations.
Incorporating intelligibility-aware conditioning further im-
proves the HuBERT-based system. For instance, for F01 the
WER decreases from 31.3% (HuBERT) to 21.6% with FiLM-
only and 21.2% with Gated-FiLM, while for M01 it improves
from 28.4% to 25.0% and 22.4%, respectively. Similar gains
are observed for M04, where the WER reduces from 43.6%
to 30.3% using FiLM-only. Overall, FiLM-only performs best
for F03, M02, and M04, whereas Gated-FiLM shows better
results for F01, F04, M01, M03, and M05, indicating that
gating helps regulate intelligibility information and improves
dysarthric ASR performance.

with the variability inherent in pathological speech. To fur-

5. Conclusion
In this paper, we proposed an intelligibility-aware channel con-
ditioning framework to adapt pretrained HuBERT features for
dysarthric speech recognition within an end-to-end Conformer
ASR system. By incorporating speaker intelligibility through
FiLM and Gated-FiLM modulation, the proposed approach
generates more informative and severity-aware acoustic repre-
sentations without extensively fine-tuning the SSL encoder. Ex-
perimental results on the LOSO evaluation setup show that the
proposed methods reduce the average WER from 28.9% with
HuBERT and 54.0% with FBANK to 21.3% and 21.0% using
FiLM-only and Gated-FiLM, respectively, achieving the best
overall performance among compared systems. Furthermore,
the ablation study demonstrates that intelligibility-aware con-
ditioning provides competitive or better results than partially
fine-tuning HuBERT while requiring fewer trainable param-
eters (6.3M–15.78M). These findings highlight the effective-
ness and parameter efficiency of conditioning SSL representa-
tions for dysarthric ASR. Future work will explore extending
