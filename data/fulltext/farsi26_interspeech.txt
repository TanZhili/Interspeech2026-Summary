Abstract
Automatic speech recognition (ASR) is widely used, but its
progress in low-resource languages like South Azerbaijani,
a Turkic language with Arabic script, remains limited due
to scarce data. To address this gap, we introduce the first
community-driven ASR datasets for South Azerbaijani. These
include: a dataset comprising +255,743 transcribed utterances
from multiple sources; a dataset collected by the authors from
the South Azerbaijani-speaking community; and the AZB ASR
GoldSet, a challenging benchmark dataset for future evalua-
tion. To establish strong baselines, we benchmark eight models,
including MMS-1b-all and seven Whisper-family models fine-
tuned on our datasets. Our results demonstrate that using the
full dataset improves performance, and language-specific fine-
tuning is crucial for low-resource settings. Error analysis further
reveals that Arabic script ambiguity and deletion-heavy decod-
ing are the primary error sources. The code and datasets are
also publicly available 1 2 .

1. Introduction
The development of end-to-end automatic speech recognition
(ASR) systems has progressed significantly in recent years, par-
ticularly for high-resource languages, achieving high accuracy
and efficiency [1, 2, 3]. These advancements have been driven
by the availability of large annotated datasets, improvements
in deep learning algorithms, and access to high-performance
computational resources. However, ASR development for low-
resource languages continues to lag behind, primarily due to the
scarcity of annotated speech data and the limited availability of
pre-trained models tailored to these languages [4].
South Azerbaijani (AZB) is one such underrepresented lan-
guage. It is a variety of Azerbaijani written in the Arabic script,
in contrast to North Azerbaijani, which uses the Latin script.
The language is predominantly spoken by over 15 million Tur-
kic speakers in Iran [5], with smaller communities in Turkey,
Iraq, and Syria [6, 7, 8].
The lack of pre-trained ASR models for low-resource lan-
guages makes the development of reliable speech-to-text sys-
tems particularly challenging [9]. As an extremely low-resource
language, South Azerbaijani lacks publicly available annotated

To address this gap, we introduce a set of three ASR
datasets developed with the active involvement of the South
Azerbaijani-speaking community. These datasets provide high-
quality transcriptions specifically tailored to the linguistic and
orthographic characteristics of South Azerbaijani. The first
dataset (Community dataset) consists of over 25 hours of speech
paired with transcriptions, constructed following key principles
of community-driven data collection, such as ensuring speaker
diversity and cultural relevance. The texts are sourced from
books written in South Azerbaijani. The second dataset (Exter-
nal dataset) is a large-scale resource derived from publicly avail-
able North Azerbaijani speech corpora, comprising approxi-
mately 250,000 transcribed utterances (447.64h) recorded by
native speakers of the Northern variety under diverse acous-
tic conditions [11, 12]. To simulate South Azerbaijani ortho-
graphic conditions, the original Latin-script transcriptions are
systematically transliterated into Arabic script following South
Azerbaijani writing conventions. While the acoustic content re-
flects North Azerbaijani speech, the resulting transcripts adopt
South Azerbaijani orthography. Finally, we introduce the AZB
ASR GoldSet, a more challenging dataset consisting of 3,021
transcribed utterances (17.49 hours), designed to support robust
evaluation and benchmarking of South Azerbaijani ASR sys-
tems.

conduct a comprehensive benchmark across eight ASR mod-

Our benchmark results show that training on the full
dataset yields improvements across evaluation conditions, and
language-specific fine-tuning is essential for this severely low-
resource language. In addition, cross-lingual fine-tuning strate-
gies are also beneficial, as some of these models perform better
than Whisper-Small fine-tuned only on our Community dataset.
Moreover, among all evaluated models, MMS fine-tuned on the
Community dataset achieves the best performance on the Gold-
Set, although its performance on the External dataset remains
limited. MMS also exhibits a lower CER compared to Whisper,
which may be attributed to its non-autoregressive architecture
and the absence of an explicit autoregressive language model-
ing component.
To further understand the sources of transcription errors, we
perform a detailed error analysis of the model outputs. Our find-
ings indicate that phonemic and orthographic ambiguity in Ara-
bic script, instability on very short utterances, and challenges in
number transcription are the primary contributors to errors.
The key contributions of this work are as follows:
• We introduce the first publicly available large-scale ASR
datasets for South Azerbaijani, consisting of three comple-
mentary datasets designed to address different challenges in
low-resource speech recognition.
• We provide a comprehensive benchmark across eight ASR
models, including multiple fine-tuned Whisper variants, to
evaluate performance on South Azerbaijani.
• We conduct an in-depth error analysis to identify the main
sources of word- and character-level errors, offering insights
for future research and model development.
Finally, we release all datasets and fine-tuned models to
support future research in South Azerbaijani ASR and other
low-resource languages. We hope that our contributions will
facilitate future research and enable the development of more
robust ASR systems for South Azerbaijani and other under-
represented languages. Beyond South Azerbaijani, we envi-
sion the proposed community-driven pipeline for dataset cre-
ation, model fine-tuning, and systematic evaluation as a scal-
able blueprint for advancing speech technologies in other low-
resource languages.
2. Related Works
The field of ASR has witnessed significant progress with the
advent of E2E architectures such as Connectionist Temporal
Classification (CTC) [16], and Transformer-based models [17].
These models have demonstrated state-of-the-art performance
on high-resource languages, mainly due to the availability of
vast annotated datasets [1]. These advancements are evident
even in some subsets of Turkic languages [18]. Nonetheless,
low-resource languages, such as South Azerbaijani, continue to
be underrepresented due to the lack of annotated speech corpora
and domain-specific pre-trained models. Although North Azer-
baijani and South Azerbaijani are micro languages for Azerbai-
jani [19] and their auditory features are moderately analogous,
they are completely different in terms of their orthographic fea-
tures [5].
Accordingly, some of the recent research has focused on
the creation of ASR datasets on low-resource languages. One
of the proposed approaches for creation of these datasets is us-
ing the books and their relevant voice recordings such as au-
diobooks [20]. However, there is a notable challenge regarding
this approach which is the long length for the pairs of audio and
transcriptions [20], which can be resolved by clipping text and
audios into smaller chunks.
Moreover, several notable efforts have focused on collect-
ing ASR datasets for Turkic languages [21]. These studies pro-
vide valuable insights into data preprocessing techniques, in-
cluding text normalization, punctuation removal, and charac-
ter encoding standardization. Given the phonetic and gram-
matical similarities between South Azerbaijani and other Tur-
kic languages, these findings are directly relevant to research
on South Azerbaijani. Furthermore, as the writing system of
South Azerbaijani is based on the Arabic script, insights from
ASR dataset construction for Arabic can also be leveraged [22].
For example, preprocessing steps such as reducing punctuation
and orthographic inconsistencies, as well as ensuring diversity
in speaker attributes (e.g., gender and age) and applying manual
transcription and validation procedures, have been shown to sig-
nificantly improve data quality [22]. Additionally, recent efforts
have explored dataset and resource creation for low-resource
languages in the geographic region of Iran [23, 24]. However,
despite the large population of South Azerbaijani speakers in
Iran, there are currently no publicly available ASR datasets for
this language.
To address the scarcity of labeled data in low-resource
languages, prior work has explored both pseudo-labeling and
cross-lingual techniques. Pseudo-labeling enables the auto-
matic generation of speech-text pairs by leveraging existing
ASR models, significantly improving performance in data-
scarce settings [25]. Recent studies have shown that mod-
els trained with pseudo-labeled data can achieve performance
comparable to fully supervised systems in low-resource scenar-
ios [26]. Furthermore, combining pseudo-labeling with cross-
lingual transfer techniques, closely related to transliteration and
script normalization, has been shown to substantially reduce er-
ror rates. These findings highlight the importance of leveraging
linguistic similarities across languages to improve ASR perfor-
mance in low-resource settings [27].
Research on ASR development for South Azerbaijani fur-
ther highlights the limited progress in this area. To the best of
our knowledge and based on publicly available documentation,
the only large-scale ASR model that explicitly reports inclu-
sion of South Azerbaijani in its pre-training data is the Face-
book MMS model [19], which is trained using a CTC objective.
However, due to the absence of standardized and publicly docu-
mented benchmarks for South Azerbaijani, there is currently no
systematic evaluation of its performance on this language. In
addition, the Whisper family of models, which covers several
Turkic languages and has demonstrated strong performance,
does not include South Azerbaijani in its pre-training [28]. To
address this gap, prior work has shown that transfer learning is
highly effective in low-resource ASR, particularly when lever-
aging data from linguistically related languages. Models trained
on higher-resource languages can learn general acoustic and
phonetic representations that transfer effectively to languages
with shared characteristics [29]. These findings are further sup-
ported by broader cross-lingual ASR research, which demon-
strates that shared phonetic inventories, scripts, and acoustic
patterns enable effective knowledge transfer across languages
[30].

3. Datasets
To support automatic speech recognition (ASR) research for
South Azerbaijani (AZB), we curate a multi-source speech cor-
pus designed to balance scale, diversity, and annotation quality.
Given the severe scarcity of publicly available, script-consistent
South Azerbaijani resources, our dataset integrates newly
collected community speech (Community dataset), expert-
transcribed external audio (External dataset), and a carefully
designed evaluation benchmark (GoldSet). This design enables
both model training and robust evaluation under realistic low-
resource conditions.
The complete corpus consists of three complementary
datasets:
• Community Dataset: A newly Community-driven collected
and fully annotated speech data created by our team.
• External Dataset: A large-scale dataset curated through
the transliteration of North Azerbaijani voices from V oxLin-
gua107 [12] and BHSOAI Pseudo-Labelled Azerbaijani
V oices [11], followed by expert review and correction.
• AZB ASR GoldSet Dataset: A high-quality evaluation
set containing longer, more spontaneous speech designed to
stress-test ASR systems.
Each component serves a distinct role in training, validation,
or evaluation, and all splits are constructed to prevent speaker
overlap across experimental partitions. In the following, we dis-
cuss the details of each dataset and its curation process.
3.1. Community Dataset
In response to the scarcity of datasets for South Azerbaijani in
ASR tasks, we have developed a comprehensive community-
driven dataset. To capture the richness of diverse voices, we
have gathered speech recordings from individuals of various
ages and genders, using a range of devices to ensure variabil-
ity. Our text data is crafted to include a broad spectrum of
words and grammatical structures to reflect linguistic diversity.
This dataset was created in two main stages: first, by selecting
suitable texts from books, and second, by recording the corre-
sponding spoken versions of these sentences. Our completed
dataset features more than 13,000 sentences and over 25 hours
of recordings. Further details about this dataset are presented in
Table 1.
3.1.1. Text Collection and Preprocessing
In the first step, we collected texts from books written in Arabic
script. These texts were then segmented into smaller sections
by splitting them at punctuation marks such as ”?”, ”!”, and
”.”. Afterward, preprocessing was performed on the segmented
texts. preprocessing includes several steps as follows:
• We convert digits to words (e.g., 2 becomes ”two”).
• We remove all punctuation marks from the text.
• Symbols are converted into words (e.g., ”$” becomes ”dol-
lar”).
• We standardize Unicode characters with equivalent represen-
tations (e.g.,\u1734 and\u1605) into a consistent form.
Given that our dataset originates from books in Microsoft
Word format, it initially contained various Unicode control
characters, which we have removed from the text.
• Abbreviations are expanded to their full forms.
• We remove any words, containing characters outside the Ara-
bic script.
• We eliminate segmented texts that contain fewer than five
words.
• Finally, we manually review the segmented texts to address
issues like spelling errors and other discrepancies.
Although modern end-to-end ASR systems are increasingly ro-
bust to orthographic variation, text normalization remains im-
portant in extremely low-resource settings to reduce unneces-
sary output variability and to stabilize training. In the absence
of large-scale data, inconsistencies in written form, such as mul-
tiple representations of numbers, symbols, punctuation, or Uni-
code variants, can disproportionately increase label sparsity and
degrade convergence. Therefore, we apply a set of normaliza-
tion steps to minimize transcription ambiguity, ensure consis-
tent alignment between speech and text, and reduce the effective
vocabulary size. These preprocessing decisions are particularly
important for South Azerbaijani, where orthographic conven-
tions are not fully standardized and digital texts often contain
heterogeneous Unicode encodings.
3.1.2. Speech Collection and Recording Process
We collected recordings of texts from 14 native speakers with
gender balance (7F/7M) and age variability, as shown in Table
1. All speakers recorded using their own smartphones (multi-
ple device models), which introduces realistic microphone and
channel variation. As a recording protocol, the speakers were
instructed to:
• record in a quiet environment when possible,
• read each sentence naturally (not exaggerated), and
• repeat a sentence only if misread or interrupted.
Each recording is paired with the corresponding normalized text
prompt. The dataset metadata includes speaker ID, device type,
total duration, and sentence counts (Table 1). This structured
design enables controlled training/evaluation splits and analysis
by speaker/device if needed.
3.2. External Dataset
We curated a large-scale dataset using speech data from North
Azerbaijani, based on the fact that South Azerbaijani and North
Azerbaijani share most core linguistic features, as they repre-
sent two standard varieties of the macrolanguage Azerbaijani
[19]. To construct this dataset, we used North Azerbaijani data
from two different sources, which we describe as follows.
3.2.1. Expert-Validated Pseudo-Labeled Azerbaijani Speech

collected for automatic speech recognition without human-
provided labels [11]. This dataset consists of over 200,000
speech segments derived from publicly available YouTube

tent, which is typically associated with Latin-script orthogra-

3.2.2. Expert-Transcribed VoxLingua107 Azerbaijani Audio
To augment training data with naturally occurring speech, we
extracted approximately 58 hours of Azerbaijani audio from
V oxLingua107 [12]. While this corpus is commonly used for
language identification, it does not provide ASR-ready tran-
scripts. Dataset resources.8
Inspired by the pseudo-labeling approach proposed by
BHOSAI [11], we generated Latin-script transcriptions for
the V oxLingua107 audio samples using pseudo-labeling with
Whisper-large-v3. Then, for transliteration from North Azer-
baijani to South Azerbaijani, we collaborated with a South
Azerbaijani publishing company, whose linguists manually
transcribed the selected audio segments using Arabic script con-
ventions consistent with South Azerbaijani orthography. This
process resulted in high-quality labeled data suitable for ASR,
obtained through transliteration from North Azerbaijani tran-
scripts into South Azerbaijani.
Ultimately, to ensure data quality and validity for the ASR
task in both datasets, the same preprocessing and normalization
steps applied to the Community dataset were also performed
on these two datasets. The External dataset introduces greater
acoustic and stylistic variability, including spontaneous speech,
background noise, and diverse speaking conditions that are un-
derrepresented in read-speech corpora. At the same time, ex-
pert transcription ensures that orthographic consistency is main-
tained across all dataset components. Although the acoustic
content of this dataset reflects North Azerbaijani speech, the re-
sulting transcripts adopt South Azerbaijani orthography, which
is particularly valuable for a language like South Azerbaijani
that is an almost zero-resource language.

3.3. AZB ASR GoldSet Dataset
To enable rigorous and realistic evaluation of South Azerbaijani
ASR systems, we introduce the AZB ASR GoldSet, an indepen-
dently collected benchmark designed to be more challenging
than the training corpus. The GoldSet was not sampled from
the Community or External training datasets. Instead, it was
specially collected by the authors through a community-based
recording campaign organized by Kartal OL. Speaker recruit-
ment and data are publicly documented.9
The prompts were manually selected and curated to evalu-
ate generalization, robustness, and domain transfer. Unlike the
main training corpus, which is dominated by read speech and
source-specific material, GoldSet was designed as a controlled
evaluation set with broader lexical and stylistic variation. The
selected prompts include everyday expressions, informal com-
munication, news-style and political discourse, historical and
cultural content, scientific and educational passages, personal
narratives, short stories, and descriptions of places, people, ob-
jects, and daily events.
These topics were chosen to include both common vocab-
ulary and less frequent words, as well as different sentence
lengths, syntactic structures, and speaking styles. The goal
was not to create an exhaustive domain-balanced corpus,
