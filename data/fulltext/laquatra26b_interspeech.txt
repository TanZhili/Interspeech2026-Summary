Abstract
This paper investigates audio-to-audio retrieval using self-
supervised learning (SSL) models to generate audio represen-
tations without labeled data. To enhance retrieval accuracy,
we explore the use of SSL embeddings with sequence match-
ing techniques, including Dynamic Time Warping (DTW), and
clustering methods, such as K-Means combined with TF-IDF
and BM25. We evaluate our framework on two distinct tasks:
music retrieval via query-by-humming and spoken content re-
trieval via query-by-example. Experimental evidence shows
that clustering-based methods, which reduce SSL embeddings
into discrete hidden units, are particularly effective for speech
retrieval. Conversely, DTW applied directly on SSL embed-
dings, which preserves full sequence information, excels in mu-
sic retrieval. Extensive experiments demonstrate that combin-
ing SSL representations with appropriate sequence matching
improves retrieval accuracy across different audio domains.
Index Terms: audio information retrieval, self-supervised
learning, audio representations
1. Introduction
Audio-to-audio retrieval is a fundamental task in audio process-
ing, where the goal is to identify a matching audio clip from
a database given a query audio [1, 2]. Unlike text-based re-
trieval, which relies on metadata or transcriptions, audio re-
trieval directly compares audio signals and requires robust fea-
tures that handle variations in pitch, tempo, background noise,
and speaker characteristics [3]. The lack of labeled data in
many audio domains makes supervised approaches impractical
for large-scale applications. Furthermore, unlike classification
tasks such as keyword spotting [4], retrieval requires matching
arbitrary queries against a database without prior knowledge of
the content.
To address these challenges, various audio representations
have been explored. CNN-based embeddings [5] and con-
trastive learning approaches [6, 7] transform high-dimensional
signals into compact representations for efficient comparison.
More recently, transformer-based SSL-based models such as
HuBERT [8], WavLM [9], Wav2Vec2 [10], and Data2Vec
[11] learn robust representations from unlabeled data, show-
ing strong adaptability across speech, music, and environmental
sounds [12, 13]. MERT [14] extends SSL to the music domain
by generating pseudo labels that capture tonal variations, while
voc2vec [15] targets non-verbal vocalizations by pre-training
on open-source non-verbal audio datasets. MCR-Data2Vec 2.0
[16] improves robustness to input variations through output-
consistency strategies. Task-specific refinements have also been
proposed: LASER [17] uses alignment and temporal regulariza-
tion to improve ASR and phoneme recognition, while SCORE
[18] fine-tunes SSL models for phoneme recognition. These
developments demonstrate that SSL embeddings encode rich
acoustic structure, but their application to fully unsupervised
audio retrieval remains underexplored [19].
Given these representations, a key design choice is how to
measure similarity between audio sequences. Dynamic Time
Warping (DTW) [20] computes an optimal alignment between
two sequences, making it robust to temporal distortions such as
tempo variation. DTW has been combined with SSL embed-
dings for pronunciation error detection [21] and speech intelli-
gibility assessment [22], where capturing fine-grained temporal
variations is essential. An alternative approach derives discrete
tokens from SSL models [23, 24, 25] by quantizing continuous
embeddings into discrete units [26, 27]. These discrete repre-
sentations bridge audio and text, enabling the application of text
retrieval techniques such as TF-IDF [28] and BM25 [29, 30] to
audio matching.
Several retrieval systems have been built on top of these
components. Wav2Tok [19] learns discrete tokens for audio re-
trieval using a BiLSTM encoder trained on pairs of similar au-
dio generated through data augmentation, combined with CTC
loss and expectation-maximization. While Wav2Tok avoids
the need for explicit transcriptions or labels, it still requires
a dedicated training phase with augmentation strategies and
knowledge of which audio segments are semantically equiva-
lent. SpeechDPR [31] employs SSL-based encoders for spoken
question answering but requires speech-text pairings for train-
ing. ByteHum [32] applies weakly supervised models to query-
by-humming but relies on domain-specific annotated data. The
SUPERB benchmark [33] provides a standardized evaluation of
SSL representations across speech tasks, including query-by-
example [34]. However, SUPERB evaluates DTW as a fixed
protocol without exploring alternative matching strategies or
multiple audio domains. TF-IDF-based discrete token matching
has also been applied to query-by-example spoken term detec-
tion [35, 36], but these systems focus on speech only and do not
analyzewhya given strategy works for a given domain.
In this work, we propose a fully unsupervised framework
for audio-to-audio retrieval that requires no labeled data or
task-specific training. 1 Our approach combines SSL embed-
dings with both continuous alignment (DTW) and discrete to-
ken matching (TF-IDF, BM25), and evaluates them on two dis-
tinct tasks: query-by-humming (QbH), which retrieves songs
from hummed melodies, and query-by-example (QbE), which
retrieves spoken phrases from a speech database. Unlike
Wav2Tok, which requires a training phase on paired audio, and
SUPERB, which evaluates a single matching strategy on a sin-
gle domain, we systematically study how different matching

2. Methodology
The proposed framework performs unsupervised audio-to-
audio retrieval using frame-level embeddings from transformer-
based SSL models. Given an input audio, the SSL model pro-
duces a sequence of embeddingsX= [x 1,x 2,...,x T ], where
xt∈R d is thed-dimensional vector for thet-th frame (typi-
cally 20 ms), andTis the number of frames.
2.1. Clustering for Discrete Representation
Continuous frame-level embeddings are high-dimensional and
variable in sequence length, making direct comparison expen-
sive and sensitive to fine-grained acoustic variation. To obtain
a more compact representation, we apply K-Means clustering
[38] to the frame-level vectors. LetC={c 1,...,c K}denote
the set ofKcluster centroids. Each frame embeddingx t is as-
signed to the nearest centroid:i t = arg mink∥xt−ck∥2. This
produces two types of sequence representations:Cluster Em-
beddings (C-Emb), where each frame is replaced by its centroid
cit, forming the sequenceC X = [ci1,...,c iT ]; andCluster
IDs (C-IDs), where each frame is mapped to a discrete indexit,
forming the sequenceI= [i 1,...,i T ]. C-Emb preserves con-
tinuous distance structure while reducing granularity, whereas
C-IDs enable the application of text-based retrieval techniques.

2.3. Retrieval Process
Given a query audioQ, the system transforms it into the cho-
sen representation (embeddings, C-Emb, or C-IDs) and com-
putes its similarity to each database entryD i. For DTW-based
methods, the score is a distance (lower is better), while for text-
based methods the score is a similarity (higher is better). In both
cases, database entries are ranked accordingly, and retrieval per-
formance is evaluated based on the rank of the correct match.
3. Experimental Setup
We evaluate our framework on two retrieval tasks: query-by-
humming (QbH) and query-by-example (QbE).
3.1. Datasets
MIR-QBSH2 is used for the QbH task, where a hummed or
whistled query retrieves a target song from a database. The
dataset contains 4,206 queries covering 48 unique songs and a
search database of 225 samples covering the same songs. Each
song is performed by multiple speakers, testing the system’s ro-
bustness to different vocal styles and recording conditions.
QbE-LibriSpeechis a custom dataset we created from the Lib-
riSpeech corpus [40] for the QbE task. Queries are bigrams
(two-word phrases) extracted from transcriptions, with stop-
words removed and a minimum occurrence threshold of four
across all splits. For each qualifying bigram, the first occurrence
is designated as the query, and subsequent utterances containing
the same bigram serve as positive examples. Word-level times-
tamps are obtained using WhisperX [41] to extract the exact
audio segment corresponding to each bigram. Queries whose
source utterance also appears as a positive for another query are
removed to prevent data leakage. The final dataset contains 27
unique queries and 107 positive examples, with an average of
3.70 positive matches per query.

4.2. Query-by-Example (QbE)
Table 2 reveals a stark contrast with QbH: TF-IDF on cluster
IDs is the most effective strategy for speech retrieval.HuBERT-
LSagain leads (Accuracy: 0.633, MRR: 0.723). The advantage
of discrete matching can be explained by how SSL models learn
their representations. Models pre-trained with masked predic-
tion, such as HuBERT, produce embeddings that cluster natu-
rally into phone-like units [8]. When quantized via K-Means,
these clusters approximate phonetic categories, and matching
based on shared cluster frequencies effectively compares pho-
netic content across speakers. DTW, by contrast, operates in a
continuous embedding space where speaker-dependent acous-
tic variations (e.g., pitch, speaking rate, vocal tract differences)
introduce noise that does not reflect phonetic identity, resulting
in lower performance (Accuracy: 0.300).
TF-IDF outperforms BM25 in Accuracy and MRR
(0.633/0.723 vs. 0.467/0.636), though BM25 achieves compet-
itive Recall at higher cutoffs (R@3, R@5). We attribute TF-
IDF’s advantage in top-1 retrieval to the short, similar-length
segments in our setup, where BM25’s document length nor-
malization provides little benefit. TF-IDF’s simpler weighting,
which directly reflects cluster importance, is more effective at
identifying the single best match.

4.3. Temporal Sensitivity Analysis
The results in Sections 4.1 and 4.2 showwhatworks per do-
main: DTW for music, TF-IDF for speech. To understandwhy,
we conduct two complementary analyses that isolate the role
of temporal sensitivity along the continuous and discrete repre-
sentation axes, respectively. All experiments in this section use
HuBERT-LSwithK=10,000.
Soft-DTWγ-sweep.Soft-DTW [37] replaces the hard min-
imum over alignment paths in DTW with a smooth soft-
minimum parameterized byγ. Whenγ→0, it recovers hard
DTW; whenγ→∞, all alignment paths contribute equally, ef-
fectively removing temporal ordering (bag-of-frames). Fig-
ure 3a shows the results. For QbH, accuracy degrades mono-
tonically asγincreases, dropping from 0.765 atγ=0.01to
0.579 atγ=100, confirming that strict temporal alignment of
pitch contours is essential for melody retrieval. For QbE, even
hard DTW (γ=0.01) achieves only 0.300, far below TF-IDF
(0.633), and performance degrades further asγincreases. This
shows that the advantage of TF-IDF over DTW for speech is
not explained by temporal invariance alone: relaxing temporal
alignment in continuous space does not help. The bottleneck is
the continuous representation itself, where speaker-dependent
acoustic variability masks phonetic identity.

discrete token space, we propose Temporal TF-IDF (T-TF-IDF),
which augments standard TF-IDF with a positional penalty con-

Summary.The two sweeps provide a unified explanation for
the domain-dependent results. Music retrieval requires tem-
poral ordering at both the continuous level (γ ⋆→0) and the
discrete level (λ⋆>0). Speech retrieval is maximally position-
invariant in discrete token space (λ⋆=0), and DTW fails not be-
cause of temporal alignment but because discretization removes
speaker-dependent acoustic noise that masks phonetic identity
in continuous space.
5. Conclusion
This work presents a systematic study of how SSL represen-
tations interact with different sequence matching strategies for
unsupervised audio retrieval. Our cross-domain evaluation re-
veals a clear trade-off: continuous alignment through DTW cap-
tures melodic contour variations essential for music retrieval,
while discrete token matching via TF-IDF captures phonetic
identity patterns that drive speech retrieval. The temporal sensi-
tivity analysis provides a principled explanation for this finding:
music retrieval requires temporal ordering at both the continu-
ous (γ-sweep) and discrete (λ-sweep) levels, whereas speech
retrieval is maximally position-invariant in discrete space, with
discretization itself being the key factor that removes speaker-
dependent noise from continuous embeddings. Additionally,
SSL models pre-trained on speech data outperform music-
specific models even on hummed queries, suggesting that vo-
cal production patterns learned from speech transfer effectively
to sung or hummed content. These results motivate the devel-
opment of adaptive retrieval strategies that automatically select
between continuous and discrete matching based on input audio
characteristics.
Limitations.Our evaluation is limited to two datasets (MIR-
QBSH and QbE-LibriSpeech). Validating these findings on
larger-scale benchmarks and additional audio domains (e.g., en-
vironmental sounds, overlapping speech, polyphonic music) is
an important direction for future work [42]. Also, we focus on
base-size SSL models, and exploring larger variants may further
improve retrieval performance or exhibit different behavior. Fi-
nally, our framework highlights that optimal matching strategies
differ across audio domains; selecting the best strategy automat-
ically at deployment time remains an open problem.
