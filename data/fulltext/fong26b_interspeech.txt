Abstract
Speech-based Large Language Models (SpeechLLMs) are mov-
ing towards supporting multiple languages and tasks. However,
prior work often studies multilinguality and multitask learning
separately or use large training corpora. We present a study of
multilingual multitask SpeechLLMs under low-resource condi-
tions, extending beyond ASR to speech translation and topic
classification. Building on evidence that high-resource ASR
pretraining enables cross-lingual transfer, we examine whether
this generalizes to multitask SpeechLLMs. Using only 3-5
hours of labeled data per task and language, we show that
bootstrapping from ASR-pretrained projectors enables effec-
tive multitask adaptation. We find that cross-lingual perfor-
mance is influenced by language similarity, while zero-shot
generalization does not extend to unseen tasks. Overall, our
results demonstrate that ASR-pretrained bootstrapping and lim-
ited target-language finetuning enable multilingual multitask
SpeechLLMs in low-resource settings.
Index Terms: speechllm, multilingual, multitask, low-
resource, data scarcity, transfer learning, automatic speech
recognition, speech translation, topic classification
1. Introduction
Speech-based Large Language Models (SpeechLLMs) are in-
creasingly advancing towards supporting multiple languages
and tasks. Existing work investigates different aspects of this
challenge through multitask training approaches [1, 2, 3, 4, 5]
and cross-lingual transfer [6, 7, 8, 9]. However, these directions
are often studied in isolation or under settings with substantial
amounts of training data (e.g., [10] show that few-shot abilities
for tasks unseen during training emerge only after pretraining
on more than 100,000,000 hours of audio). Moreover, existing
frameworks remain heavily skewed toward high-resource lan-
guages, with large performance gaps observed for low-resource
languages across tasks [11].
Despite this progress, it remains unclear how SpeechLLMs
behave when adapted jointly to multiple tasks and multiple lan-
guages with minimal amounts of training data. In particular,
few studies examine multilingual multitask SpeechLLMs un-
der low-resource settings, or systematically analyze zero-shot
behavior across both languages and tasks. In more realistic
and inclusive deployment scenarios, models should be able to
support multiple tasks and multiple languages, even when only
limited task- and/or language-specific data are available. This
therefore motivates an investigation towards multilingual and
multitask SpeechLLMs in low-resource conditions, where only
a few hours of labeled data are available per task-language pair.

Building on prior findings that mono- or multilingual pre-
training enables cross-lingual transfer for low-resource Auto-
matic Speech Recognition (ASR) [7], the present work ex-
amines whether this strategy extends to multilingual multitask
SpeechLLMs and to tasks beyond ASR, including speech trans-
lation (ST) and topic identification/classification (TID). We fur-
ther analyze the extent to which such models exhibit zero-shot
cross-lingual and cross-task generalization, and how language-
family proximity can affect performance. We additionally
compare against a strong open-weight audio-language model
(Qwen2-Audio-7B Instruct) to contextualize our proposed low-
resource multitask finetuning approach.
Our experiments show that multilingual ASR pretraining
enables multitask transfer with less than 5 hours of data per
task and target-language. While zero-shot cross-lingual per-
formance is influenced by language-family proximity, zero-shot
generalization does not extend to tasks excluded during finetun-
ing. Our main findings are visually summarized in Figure 1.
The main contributions of our paper include:
1. Extending prior findings on high-resource ASR pretraining to
multilingual multitask SpeechLLMs under data scarce condi-
tions.
2. Demonstrating transfer to tasks beyond ASR, including
speech translation and topic identification/classification.
3. Showing that cross-lingual multitask transfer is influenced by
language similarity.
4. Showing that unseen tasks do not emerge zero-shot and in-
stead require task-specific supervision.
2. Methodology
We evaluate three tasks spanning recognition, generation, and
classification. For readability purposes, we present all metrics
as percentages.
Automatic Speech Recognition (ASR).The model transcribes
speech into text in the source language. Performance is mea-
sured using word error rate (WER), where lower values indicate
better recognition accuracy.
Speech Translation (ST).The model generates English trans-
lations from speech input (X→EN) . We report BLEU scores
[12], with higher values indicating better translation quality.
Topic Identification (TID).The model predicts a topic label
for each speech input. Performance is measured using accuracy
and macro F1 to reflect overall classification performance and
robustness to class imbalance. Higher values indicate stronger
classification performance.

2.1. Datasets
Experiments are conducted using the Italian (IT), Spanish (ES),
Galician (GL), Czech (CS), and Finnish (FIN) subsets of the
SIB-Fleurs dataset [13, 14], with approximately 3–5 hours of
labeled training data per task and language. SIB-Fleurs pro-
vides parallel annotations for ASR, ST, and TID on the same
speech segments, enabling multitask learning from identical au-
dio inputs with task-specific labels. This setting allows us to
isolate the effects of multitask and multilingual transfer without
confounding differences in acoustic content. We select these
five languages to cover multiple families (Romance, Slavic,
and Uralic) and varying degrees of relatedness. This facilitates
preliminary analysis of cross-lingual transfer and language-
family effects under low-resource conditions. For speech trans-
lation, we use theSeamlessM4T-derived English translations
provided in SIB-Fleurs. For topic identification, each utter-
ance is assigned one of seven dataset-provided topics: sci-
ence/technology, travel, politics, sports, health, entertainment,
and geography.
Following [7], we use CommonV oice 20.0 (CV) [15] for
high-resource ASR pretraining. We randomly select either 200
hours of IT for monolingual pretraining or 500 hours drawn
from a balanced combination of IT, ES, English (EN), French
(FR), and German (DE) for multilingual pretraining.
2.2. Model architecture and training configuration
We adopt a unified SpeechLLM architecture rather than a cas-
caded pipeline to reduce error propagation between components
and enable shared representations across tasks [16]. The imple-
mented architecture consists of a frozen pretrained speech en-
coder and a frozen pretrained large language model (LLM) con-
nected by a lightweight linear projection layer (Figure 2). The
speech encoder produces acoustic representations of the input
signal, which are mapped into the LLM embedding space via
the projector and processed using task-specific prompts.
We use the official SLAM-LLM repository for multitask
training1, selected for its open-source accessibility and strong
performance in single-task ASR. For all experiments, we em-
ployWhisper-large-v3-turbo 2 [17] as the speech encoder and
EuroLLM-1.7B-Instruct3 as the multilingual LLM. This choice
aligns with prior work employing these models as effective
modular components for multilingual SpeechLLM systems that
support European languages [7].

Training follows the SLAM-LLM multitask recipe with dy-
namic frame batching, where samples are batched based on
audio frame length rather than a fixed batch size. Based on
the default values defined upon initial release of the recipe,
train max frame length= 1500 andeval max frame length=
3000. A scheduler with 1,000 warmup steps is applied, after
which the learning rate is held constant. Training runs for a
maximum of 10 epochs and is stopped early when the validation
loss stops improving. Cross-entropy loss between the LLM-
predicted tokens and the ground-truth label tokens is used for
all tasks. During inference, decoding is performed withbeam
size= 4 andmax frame length= 1000. All experiments were
conducted on one NVIDIA Ada Lovelace L40S GPU.
The following task specific prompts are used: for ASR
[18, 7] (“Transcribe speech to text.”), ST [6] (“Perform speech
translation into English using the preceding audio:”), and TID
(“Classify the topic of the spoken utterance into one of the
following labels: science/technology, travel, politics, sports,
health, entertainment, geography.”).

2.3. Experimental Procedure
We consider several training configurations to study the effects
of ASR pretraining, multitask finetuning, and cross-lingual
transfer.
Qwen2-Audio Baseline.As a strong open-weight refer-
ence, we evaluateQwen2-Audio-7B-Instruct[19] across all five
languages and three tasks without any finetuning on SIB-Fleurs.
The model supports Chinese, English, Cantonese, French, Ital-
ian, Spanish, German, and Japanese. This baseline enables a
comparison between a general-purpose audio-language model
3470
and our task-adapted projector-based approaches under low-
resource multilingual conditions.
Train-from-Scratch Baselines.We train multitask
SpeechLLM projectors directly on SIB-Fleurs without any ASR
pretraining either monolingually (one model per language) or
multilingually (one model for all languages). These models
serve as low-resource multitask baselines.
ASR Pretraining.The linear projector is first pretrained on
high-resource ASR data from CV 20.0. Prior work has shown
that bootstrapping a linear projector pretrained on at least 100–
200 hours of high-resource language ASR data can mitigate
poor ASR performance in low-resource scenarios [7]. We there-
fore consider both monolingual ASR pretraining (200 hours of
IT) and multilingual ASR pretraining (500 hours across IT, ES,
EN, FR, and DE).
Zero-shot Transfer Settings.We evaluate cross-lingual
zero-shot transfer by finetuning on a single source language and
testing on unseen target languages. We additionally evaluate
cross-task zero-shot behavior by excluding a task during fine-
tuning and assessing performance on that unseen task.
Cross-lingual Finetuning.We compare finetuning mod-
els separately per language (monolingual multitask finetun-
ing) with finetuning a single model jointly on all five lan-
guages (multilingual multitask finetuning) for multitask train-
ing to quantify the extent to which multilingual finetuning is
beneficial despite limited target-language supervision.
In one experiment (Table 4), we also finetune an open-
source multilingual projector that supports 28 European lan-
guages (MEUSLI) 4. Following the original training configu-
ration of the released model, we align the projector with Eu-
roLLM 1.7B-Instruct by finetuning the query and value projec-
tion layers using LoRA adapters with the same parameters (r=
8,α= 32). This added an additional 1.4 million trainable pa-
rameters. LoRA is not used in the experiments with CV20 ASR
pretraining (Table 3) to maintain a consistent training setup and
to isolate the effects of ASR pretraining and multilingual fine-
tuning.
During pretraining, only ASR data is used. Multitask
learning is introduced exclusively during SIB-Fleurs finetuning
across ASR, ST, and TID with task-specific prompts. Finetun-
ing uses all available labeled data per task and target language
from SIB-Fleurs (3–5 hours).
3. Results
Training from Scratch With Limited Data.Table 1 shows
that monolingual multitask SpeechLLMs trained from scratch
with<5 hours of data per task and language perform poorly
(i.e., WER 129-162% and near-zero BLEU scores). Although
training a single multilingual model from scratch improves per-
formance for closely related Romance languages (IT, ES, GL),
it remains unstable for more distant languages (CS and FIN).
This suggests that limited multitask data is insufficient to learn
robust representations, and that the benefits of multilingual
training alone are constrained. However, performance may vary
depending on the LLM used [7, 20].
In contrast, theQwen2-Audio-Instructbaseline demon-
strates strong zero-shot performance in languages explicitly
supported during pretraining. In particular, higher BLEU scores
are observed in IT (52.6%) and ES (55.2%), outperforming
our from-scratch models. However, ASR performance remains

comparable to the multilingual from-scratch models. Moreover,
performance substantially decreases for unsupported languages
such as CS and FIN (WER>100%, BLEU<5%), indicating
that cross-lingual generalization may be limited when the target
language is not well represented during pretraining. For Topic
ID, Qwen2-Audio achieves higher accuracy in supported Ro-
mance languages, but lower macro-F1 scores suggest reduced
robustness across less frequent topic classes.
Overall, these results indicate that multitask alignment is
challenging to learn from scratch with limited data. This moti-
vates the use of ASR pretraining to initialize multitask speech-
LLM capabilities, where large-scale ASR data is typically more
readily available.
ASR Pretraining Enables Cross-Lingual Multitask
Transfer.Row 1 in Table 2 shows that pretraining the projector
on 200 hours of IT ASR data followed by multitask finetun-
ing enables performance across ASR, ST, and TID. Relative to
training monolingually from-scratch (Table 1), WER reduces
significantly from 129% to 5.4%, while ST and TID perfor-
mance substantially improves (BLEU from 0.8% to 48.2%, and
Accuracy from 71.8% to 81.5%). These results indicate that
ASR pretraining provides transferable representations that ef-
fectively support multitask learning with limited task-specific
data available. We note that this finding contrasts with the ob-
servations in [6], where incorporating ASR data did not consis-
tently improve speech translation performance when sufficient
ST data were available. In our low-resource multitask setting
where only 3–5 hours of ST data are available per language,
ASR pretraining appears to help establish more robust multitask
alignment. Therefore, ASR pretraining can provide a neces-
sary initialization for low-resource multilingual multitask adap-
tation.
Zero-Shot Cross-Lingual Transfer and Language Prox-
imity.We evaluate zero-shot cross-lingual transfer in two set-
tings: (1) a projector pretrained on IT ASR and finetuned on
IT multitask data, and (2) a projector pretrained on multilingual
ASR and finetuned on IT multitask data. Performance in both
conditions (Mono-PT and Multi-PT, respectively) is reported in
rows 2-5 of Table 2 for unseen target languages.
Across tasks, ES exhibits the strongest zero-shot perfor-
mance, GL shows moderate performance, while CS and FIN de-
grade substantially. With IT ASR pretraining, this pattern aligns
with language-family proximity to IT, suggesting that zero-shot
transfer degrades as linguistic distance increases. This pattern
of results is consistent with prior work showing that modeling or
grouping languages by similarity improves cross-lingual trans-
fer [8, 9]. However, multilingual ASR pretraining in this zero-
shot setting appears to be less stable across languages, indicat-
ing clear limits to zero-shot cross-lingual generalization.
Zero-Shot Generalization to Unseen Tasks.Rows 6–7
in Table 2 show that models finetuned without supervision for
a given task fail to perform that task zero-shot. Models fine-
tuned on ASR and ST fail TID (0% Accuracy and F1 Macro),
and models finetuned on ASR and TID fail ST (3.8% BLEU).
These results indicate that, unlike cross-lingual transfer, zero-
shot generalization does not extend to unseen tasks, and that
task-specific supervision remains necessary.

ASR pretraining setting (Table 3), monolingual finetuning does
not consistently outperform the multilingual one. Although

lingual performance.
Compared to the Qwen2-Audio baseline (Table 1), our
ASR-pretrained models with supervised multitask finetuning
achieve more consistent performance across all five languages.
Although Qwen2-Audio performs strongly on ST for supported
languages (IT and ES), performance decreases for unsupported
ones such as CS and FIN. In contrast,<5 hours of target-
language finetuning per task enables more balanced multilin-
gual multitask performance.

4. Conclusion
This study demonstrates that multilingual multitask Speech-
LLMs are viable in low-resource settings via ASR-pretrained
bootstrapping and under five hours of data per task. We found
that ASR transfer learning is essential for stability, though the
optimal finetuning strategy depends on the projector’s pretrain-
ing coverage. Specifically, multilingual finetuning is compet-
itive only with a strongly pretrained projector. While cross-
lingual zero-shot performance remains tied to language prox-
imity, zero-shot generalization does not extend to unseen tasks,
which still requires explicit supervision. Although focused on
three tasks and European languages, these results provide a
foundation for scaling SpeechLLM architectures to broader lin-
guistic contexts and more complex pipelines.
