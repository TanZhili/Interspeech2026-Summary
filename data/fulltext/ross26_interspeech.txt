Revisiting the NZE front vowel shift: evidence from New Zealand’s largest and 
most linguistically diverse city 
Brooke Ross1, Catherine Watson1, Elaine Ballard1, Miriam Meyerhoff2 
1 Waipapa Taumata Rau, University of Auckland 
2 University of Oxford 
brooke.ross@auckland.ac.nz 
 
Abstract 
Auckland is New Zealand’s largest and most demographically 
diverse city, yet it has been largely ignored in research on New 
Zealand English  (NZE). Recent work on Modern NZE in 
Auckland and the rediscovery of key  historical commentary  
suggests the city has played a role in leading sound changes that 
make NZE distinct . This data also raises questions about the 
trajectory of NZE’s much-discussed Short Front Vowel Shift. 
This paper explores 100 years of sound change in Auckland 
through a preliminary acoustic and statistical analysis of NZE’s 
iconic /e, æ, ɪ/  vowels. We compare contemporary 
Aucklanders’ (n=67) speech across two age groups with that of 
historical Aucklanders (n=8), integrating real and apparent -
time analyses. Results show a rise and subsequent fall of /e/ and 
/æ/ over time, and  early centralisation of /ɪ/ among  historical 
speakers, contrasting previous research . Further investigation 
suggests /ɪ/ centralisation was initially  phonologically 
conditioned by adjacent laterals. 
Index Terms: New Zealand English, Phonetic Analysis, Short 
Front Vowels 
1. Introduction 
Auckland, New Zealand’s largest and most linguistically 
diverse city, is home to one -third of the country’s population 
[1]. Yet until recently , speakers from Auckland  have been  
largely excluded from the New Zealand English (NZE)  
sociophonetic narrative. This omission is notable, given the 
body of sociolinguistic work showing that large, linguistically 
diverse urban centres are hubs of linguistic innovation [2-5]. 
However, recent work [ 6-11] on modern NZE, examining 
sound change in Auckland, shows that young Aucklanders lead 
a series of new sound changes , including shifts in the front 
vowel space. Our most recent studies  [6, 11-12] suggest that 
these vowel shifts are now spreading beyond Auckland, 
indicating that Auckland is a hub of  sound change in modern 
NZE.  
In addition, historical commentary from the recently 
rediscovered 1921 thesis on NZE, written by G.E. Thompson 
[13], suggests that Aucklanders may have played an important 
role in the early development and emergence of NZE. This 
primary historical source, by a trained phonetician, provides 
one of the earliest comprehensive phonetic descriptions  of 
NZE. In this work, Thompson makes a series of comments 
suggesting that NZE development was occurring fastest in the 
North Island, especially in Auckland, indicating Auckland was 
potentially important for sound change in early New Zealand as 
well. If Thompson’s observations are confirmed by acoustic 
analysis, they would suggest that Auckland has served as a 
locus of innovation not only in contemporary NZE but 
throughout its historical development. Before we can consider  
these claims, we need to verify Thompson’s historical 
commentary with phonetic data from historical Aucklanders.  
Changes in the NZE front vowel space have received 
significant attention in both local and international  
sociolinguistic research [e.g. 14-19]. These changes include the 
Short Front Vowel Shift  (SFVS), involving the raising and 
fronting of /e/ and /æ/, and the centralisation of /ɪ/. The most 
widely accept ed account of the  SFVS proposes that  early 
colonial immigrants imported relatively raised /e/ and /æ/ 
variants from Southern British dialects (including RP) [14,19]. 
These variants survived the dialect levelling process, instigating 
a chain shift in  the front vowel space, characterised by 
continued raising of /æ/ and /e/ and the centralisation and 
lowering of /ɪ/ [19]. 
This paper reports on changes in the front vowel 
space over time as part of a project tracing 100 years of sound 
change in Auckland. We  undertake two analyses  comparing 
modern NZE speech ( n=67; age groups: Under -25 and 40+, 
recorded 2010s) with preliminary historical data (n=8; speakers 
born 1896– 1914, recorded 1980 –2002). In t he first analysis 
(Experiment 1), we present group and individual formant means 
extracted from statistical models, focusing on the short front 
vowels /e, æ, ɪ/. Based on unexpected findings for /ɪ/ in 
Experiment 1, we ran a follow -up experiment (Experiment 2) 
to investigate the impact of adjacent laterals on /ɪ/ formant 
values and centroids. In this analysis , we focus on individual 
tokens of /ɪ/ from a single historical speaker  and overall 
centroid means for lateral and non -lateral environments across 
our three age groups. 
2. Corpora 
2.1 Auckland Voices Corpus (Modern Data) 
The modern data analysed in this study comes from the  
Auckland Voices corpus  [20], which was r ecorded by trained 
research assistants in 2017 -2018. The corpus consists of  67 
sociolinguistic interviews with NZE-speaking Aucklanders. 
The corpus was designed for apparent -time analysis, and as 
such, speakers were collected from two age groups: 16 -25 
(approx. mean birth year 1995)  and 40+  (approx. mean birth 
year 1952) . The interviews were recorded  on two devices: a 
Zoom H5 and a Marantz PMD 661, both of which used a TDK

2.2 Auckland Born Corpus (Historical Data) 
The historical data analysed in this study was sourced from oral 
histories held by Auckland Council Libraries and consists of 
interviews with Auckland born New Zealanders . In this paper, 
we examine a preliminary set of  8 speakers born 1900-1911 
(mean birth year 1906, interviews recorded 1980-2003). Using 
archival data means we do not have information about the 
recording equipment specifications or the digitisation process , 
but the .wav files provided are sampled at 44.1 kHz and 
quantised to 16-bit . Given the recording years , it is likely that 
the recordings were digitised from multiple media, including 
reel-to-reel tapes, cassette tapes, and CD -ROMs. While this is 
beyond our control, it is important to note the potential impact 
of recording quality differences on formant measures,  
especially when comparing historical and modern reco rdings 
[21].

3. Experiment 1 
Formant data was extracted from  10 minutes of speech for the 
modern corpus and 15 minutes of speech for the historical 
corpus. Modern speech was transcribed by research assistants 
shortly after recording using ELAN [22]; historical speech was 
transcribed specifically for this project using OCTRA [23]. All 
recordings underwent forced alignment using webMAUS 
(English NZ service)  [24]. Formants were calculated using 
forest in R [25] using EmuR [26]. forest settings used 
were the default male and female settings respectively.  
Phoneme boundaries and formant tracks of vowels with phrase 
stress were hand-checked and corrected where necessary using 
the Emu -Web-App [ 27]. A vowel target was identified and 
labelled for all vowels with phrase stress  following criteria 
adapted from [2 8-29]. First and second formant values were 
extracted as single values at the vowel target using EmuR. Plots 
were generated using ggplot2 [30]. Based on previous work 
[6-7], tokens implicated in the celery-salary  merger were 
excluded from this study.  
Statistical analyses were undertaken us ing methods 
outlined in [7 ,9] using linear mixed models.  Preparation for 
statistical modelling includes a linear transformation by gender, 
following [9], to normalise vocal tract differences between male 
and female speaker groups. This allows us to see how the 
sounds are changing in  the speech community, rather than in 
specific gender groups . Models were calculated using the 
lme() function in the nlme package [31], and comparisons 
between models were undertaken using the anova() function. 
For each vowel, three models were built for observations of the 
F1 and F2 with age ( Younger AV, Older AV, Historical) and 
gender as fixed effects and speaker as a random effect. Previous 
work on the Auckland Voices corpus shows gender is not 
significant [7,11] and will not be reported in this paper.

3.2 Experiment 1: Discussion 
The rise and fall  of /e/ and /æ/ across the three age groups 
reflects both established and emerging patterns in NZE. The 
difference between the Historical and Older AV  groups aligns 
with the widely documented  [14-19] raising and fronting of 
these vowels across the history of NZE and reflects patterns 
found across the history of NZ and NZE research [ 16-17,32-
35]. The Younger AV group exhibits a reversal of this pattern, 
with lowered realisations of /e/ and /æ/, reflecting recent work 
indicating Aucklanders are leading new sound changes in NZE 
[11-16]. Taken together, these results illustrate SFV movement 
across Auckland’s history. 
While the raising of /e/ and /æ/ between the Historical 
and Older AV groups reflects general NZE patterns, t he 
discovery of centralised /ɪ/ among historical Auckland 
speakers, without parallel raising of /e/ and /æ/, complicates the 
accepted account  of the Short Front Vowel Shift. Previous 
research positions /æ/ as the first vowel to move in the SFVS, 
raising and fronting towards /e/, which then raised and fronted, 
initiating /ɪ/ centralisation [ 16]. Our findings indicate the 
possibility that, at least in A uckland, /ɪ/ centralisation may not 
have been strictly dependent on prior raising and fronting of /e/ 
and /æ/. 
Furthermore, t he relative stability of /ɪ/ across our 
three groups is unexpected and warrants further investigation, 
given that the centralisation of this vowel is generally assumed 
to be a later development in NZE [2]. Initial evaluation suggests 
that the distribution of /ɪ/ among historical speakers differs from 
that of modern speakers  and is potentially phonologically 
conditioned. Thompson’s [18] observations regarding the 
influence of adjacent laterals  [pp.70] on /ɪ/ provide us with an 
indication of whe re to look and motivat e the analysis 
undertaken in Experiment 2 below.

4. Experiment 2 
We undertook a preliminary analysis of the impact of adjacent 
laterals on /ɪ/ formant values , as impressionistic analysis 
suggested that for historical speakers, this centralisation might 
be phonologically conditioned. This analysis follows the same 
data preparation and analysis methods as experiment 1 , except 
data is from raw formant values . Figure 3 below  shows 
individual tokens of  /ɪ/ for a single historical speaker, and  
demonstrates th at for historical speakers , the retraction of /ɪ/ 
usually occurs when /ɪ/ is adjacent to a lateral consonant . In 
contrast, tokens in other environments are usually fronted.

4.2 Experiment 2: Discussion 
The results of Experiment 2 suggest that the centralisation of /ɪ/ 
in the historical data is conditioned by phonetic environment.  
Tokens occurring adjacent to laterals  show notable retraction, 
and removing these tokens shifts the historical /ɪ/ centroids 
forward in the vowel space . If this pattern holds across our 
historical dataset, it might indicate that /ɪ/ centralisation 
emerged first in specific phonetic environments before 
generalising to all environments. If replicated in the full corpus, 
this pattern would support a model in which /ɪ/  centralisation 
began as allophonic variation  that later became phonologised 
and generalised across all environments.  
5.  Discussion 
In this paper , we examine 100 years of change in Auckland 
speakers’ front vowel system. Across three groups, we observe 
a rise and subsequent fall of /e/ and /æ/, with our Older AV 
speakers exhibiting the most raised variants  of these vowels . 
Surprisingly, /ɪ/ appears relatively centralised across all three 
groups, but further exploration indicates  that historical 
centralisation was  still a change in progress , potentially 
conditioned by adjacent laterals.  Cumulatively, these 
preliminary results also raise t he possibility that /ɪ/ 
centralisation did not initially arise as a direct consequence of 
push-chain pressure from raising and fronting of /e/ and /æ/ but 
may have initially developed independently before  ultimately 
integrating into the wider restructuring of the NZE front vowel 
that occurred through the latter half of the 20th century. 
Returning to the broader question of Auckland’s role 
in the development of NZE. We can contextualise our findings 
with previous historical work (primarily the ONZE corpus), 
which largely focuses on speakers from the South Island and 
more rural centres  [19]. While methodological differences 
make direct comparison challenging , a  visual comparison of 
Figure 1 with data from [3 2] indicates that ONZE speakers of 
a similar age to our historical  speakers have a high front /ɪ/ 
vowel and comparatively  higher /e/ and /æ/ vowels. Previous 
analyses on speakers from the ONZE corpus appear to support 
these findings  [15-16, 34]. While it is too early to establish 
whether our results indicate that Auckland led the centralisation 
of /ɪ/ in early NZE, they do  suggest that historical Auckland-
based NZE  speakers may have exhibited a different 
configuration of the short front vowel system  at this time 
compared with speakers from elsewhere in NZ. 
It is important to emphasise, however, that the results 
in this study  are preliminary. The historical dataset analysed 
here represents a subset of a larger corpus, and patterns must be 
confirmed across the whole corpus. Additionally, at this stage, 
we are still reflecting on how differences in recording media 
and digitisation processes may affect formant measurements, 
particularly in the high vowel space.  Furthermore, although 
gender-based normalisation and mixed -effects modelling 
mitigate some variability,  the substantial intra - and inter -
speaker variation in early NZE presents  an ongoing challenge, 
and we are still establishing best practices for comparing the 
historical and modern datasets. 
6. Conclusion 
This study has demonstrated the importance of incorporating 
Aucklanders' speech into research on the emergence and 
development of NZE . We have preliminary evidence that, in 
Auckland, the short front vowels have risen and then fallen over 
the last 100 years. We also have preliminary evidence that 
Thompson’s observations regarding /ɪ/ are correct. 
Furthermore, if the distributions of /e, æ, ɪ/  identified in this 
paper are replicated in our  full corpus, they have the potential 
to reshape our understanding of the Short Front Vowel Shift.
