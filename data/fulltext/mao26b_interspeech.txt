Age-Related Changes in Mandarin Lexical Tone Production:  
Acoustic Properties and Tonal Distinctiveness 
Zhongxuan Mao1, Yan Feng1,**, Chenyu Li2 
1 School of Foreign Studies, Nanjing University of Science and Technology, China 
2 Johns Hopkins University, United States

Abstract 
Despite growing interest in age -related speech changes, how 
aging affects the production of lexical tones remains 
underexplored. This study compared the acoustic properties and 
the tonal distinctiveness between younger and older speakers. 
Seventy-eight native Mandarin speakers produced 236 syllables 
covering all four lexical tones. The results revealed that older 
speakers exhibited gender -divergent F0 centralization  of 
different tones , contour flattening, greater F0 variability, and 
longer duration, with these age-related effects varying across 
tones and genders. Both Bhattacharyya distance and s upport 
vector machine classification accuracy showed decreased tonal 
distinctiveness of the tone system with age. Critically, 
classification confusion was concentrated on bidirectional 
T2↔T3 misclassifications. These production patterns 
paralleled previously reported perceptual difficulties  in older 
adults, supporting the auditory-motor integration model.  
Index Terms: speech production, aging, Mandarin tones, F0 
dynamics, tone distinctiveness 
1. Introduction 
Speech production serves as a primary means of conveying 
information in daily communication, requiring precise 
coordination among the respiratory, laryngeal, and articulatory 
systems [1]. As speakers age, these systems undergo structural 
and functional changes , such as reduced respiratory capacity, 
stiffened laryngeal tissues, and s lower articulatory movements 
[2, 3], which in turn alter the ir acoustic properties of speech 
production. These age -related changes may reduce speech 
intelligibility, degrade automatic speech recognition (ASR) 
performance [4], and complicate clinical assessment of speech 
disorders [3]. Understanding how aging reshapes speech 
production therefore remains an important issue for speech 
science and technology, and its clinical practice. 
Previous studies have investigated age -related changes in 
speech production from different perspectives. In terms of voice 
quality, which reflects the acoustic outcome of laryngeal 
function, research has shown that aging leads to increased 
irregularity and instability in vocal fold vibration, as indicated 
by higher jitter [i.e., cycle-to-cycle variation in fundamental 
frequency (F0)] and shimmer ( i.e., cycle-to-cycle variation in 
amplitude) values in older adults compared to younger speakers 
[5, 6].  
Besides voice quality, researchers have also examined how 
aging affects the acoustic realization of linguistic units at both 
the segmental and suprasegmental levels. At the segmental 
level, older adults tend to produce consonants with longer voice 
onset time and greater variability [ 7, 8], and their vowel 
production shows centralizatio n of the vowel space and 
compression of formant distances between vowel categories [9, 
10]. At the suprasegmental level, most of what we know about 
age-related F0 changes comes from non -tonal languages, such 
as English, where F0 mainly carries pragmatic information,

increase F0 variability [ 11] and prolong syllable duration by 
approximately 20-25% [12]. Gender also interacts with aging 
in shaping F0: Young adults show clear gender difference s, 
with males producing lower F0 due to longer and thicker vocal 
folds [13]. But this gap narrows with age: Older males tend to 
show elevated F0 while older females show lowered F0 relative 
to their younger counterparts [11, 14]. This convergence has 
been attributed to laryngeal tissue stiffening and hormonal 
changes, especially in postmenopausal women [15].  However, 
research on age-related F0 changes in tonal languages remains 
limited, despite F0 carrying an additional critical function in 
these languages, namely encoding lexical tone s to distinguish 
word meanings. 
In tonal languages, F0 is the primary acoustic cue for lexical 
tones. Previous research conducted a multidimensional scaling 
analysis on cross-language tone perception and revealed that F0 
height and contour direction were the primary dimensions along 
which listeners differentiated tones [16]. In Mandarin Chinese, 
lexical tones are also characterized by the two dimensions, 
yielding four tone categories: T1 (high -level tone), T2 (mid -
rising tone), T3 (low-dipping tone), and T4 (high -falling tone) 
[17]. These four tones form a tonal acoustic space defined by 
pitch height and slope, within which each tone occupies a 
distinct region; The degree of separation between regions 
determines tonal distinctiveness. Regarding gender, previous 
research found that Mandarin -speaking males produced lower 
mean F0 than females across all four tones, but they all showed 
similar contour shapes, indicating that gender primarily affects 
pitch height rather than tonal contour patterns [18].  However, 
how aging affects the F0 of lexical tone production in 
Mandarin, including potential changes in pitch height and 
slope, and whether such changes differ between genders, 
remains largely unexplored. Addressing this gap is especially 
urgent given China's demographic reality:  As of the end of 
2025, China has 224 million people aged 65 and above (15.9% 
of the total population) [1 9], for whom F0 -based lexical tone 
production is essential for communication. 
Although direct acoustic evidence on aging-related changes 
in Mandarin lexical tone production is limited, theoretical work 
and empirical studies on the perception-production association 
may provide a basis for prediction. The auditory-motor 
integration model proposes that speakers perceive, monitor and 
adjust their sounds when producing them  [20]. The brain 
generates an expectation of intended speech output, after which 
is produced, the auditory system compares it against actual 
feedback, and any mismatch triggers motor adjustments. 
Because age-related hearing loss is common, impaired auditory 
input could deteriorate speakers' ability to detect and correct 
errors of speech production. This may suggest that aging could 
affect both tone perception and production for Mandarin 
speakers. From the perspective of lexical tone perception, aging 
has been shown to selectively impair tonal perception. For 
individual tone identification, older listeners (60-70 years old) 
were found to be less accurate across all four Mandarin tones, 
with T4 best preserved and T3 most affected [21].  The T3's

Taken together, previous research show ed that aging 
affected speech production at multiple levels, including voice 
quality, segmental and suprasegmental production. How ever, 
evidence at the suprasegmental level has come mainly from 
non-tonal languages, whereas comparable evidence from tonal 
languages remains limited. It therefore remains unclear how 
aging affects Mandarin lexical tone production. Considering the 
aging effect on the perception of individual Mandarin tones and 
distinction of tone categories, tone production in older speakers 
may also vary in the two dimensions. To address the research 
gap systematically, the present study compared Mandarin 
lexical tone production between older and younger adults with 
the following two research questions: (1) How does aging affect 
the acoustic properties of individual Mandarin tones?  (2) How 
does aging affect the distinctiveness of the Mandarin tone 
system? 
2. Method  
2.1. Participants 
Seventy-eight native Mandarin speakers from Nanjing, China 
were recruited, including a younger group aged 18-30 years (N 
= 36; 27 females;  M = 22.35, SD = 2.64) and an older group  
aged 55-77 years (N = 42; 30 females; M = 66.30, SD = 5.44). 
All participants completed a language background 
questionnaire and reported speak ing Standard Mandarin in 
daily life.  All of them also  reported no history of speech, 
language, or neurological disorders. The younger participants 
were university students who self-reported normal hearing and 
no cognitive impairments. The older participants were 
community-dwelling adults with normal hearing (≤ 20 dB HL) 
or mild hearing loss  (20-40 dB HL)  from 125 Hz  to 3 kHz , 
measured by an audiometer (GSI 18). Cognitive function of the 
older group was screened using the Montreal Cognitive 
Assessment (M = 26.00, SD = 2.35, range : 20-30), indicating 
normal cognitive function (range: 26-30) or  mild cognitive 
decline (range: 20-25). 
2.2. Materials and procedure 
The materials consisted of 236 syllables (59 vowel-only or CV 
structures × 4 tones)  represented by common Chinese 
characters (e.g., 八 ‘eight’, /pa/ with T1; 拔 ‘pull out’, /pa/ with 
T2; 靶  ‘target’, / pa/ with T3;  爸  ‘father’, / pa/ with T4 ). 
Recordings w ere made in a sound -attenuated booth using a 
large-diaphragm condenser microphone (Neumann U87) 
connected to a microphone preamplifier (PreSonus BLUE 
TUBE DP V2) and an audio interface (RME Fireface UCX). 
Audio was digitized at a 44.1 kHz sampling rate wit h 16 -bit 
resolution. Participants were presented with a printed character 
list with a fixed syllable order and instructed to read each 
syllable once in isolation at a comfortable speaking rate. The 
target syllables with the same vowels or CV structures but 
different tones were organized together with a fixed order in the 
character list to minimize cognitive load for older speakers. 
 
2.3. Data analysis 
F0 contours were extracted using Praat [2 5] and converted to 
speaker-normalized T -values to deal with interspeaker 
variations [26]. A total of  18,408 tokens (236 syllables × 78 
speakers) were collected. After excluding 1,561 tokens with 
insufficient valid F0 sampling points, 16,847 tokens remained 
for acoustic analyses. Seven acoustic parameters were obtained: 
t_mean, t_slope, t_SD, t_range, t_onset, t_offset, and duration  
of F0 contour . Here, t_mean and t_slope indexed the pitch 
height and pitch slope. Tonal space shown as tone balloons was 
characterized using t_mean ×  t_slope [27]. The t_SD was the 
standard deviation of the normalized F0 values . The t_range 
was the distance between the min imum and max imum F0 
values of the F0 contour. The t_onset and t_offset represent ed 
the normalized F0 values at the beginning and end of the F0 
contour, respectively.  
To address the first research question, linear mixed -effects 
models on t_mean, t_slope, t_SD, t_range, t_onset, t_offset, and 
duration were fitted using lme4 [2 8] and lmer Test [29] in R, 
with Group, Tone, Gender, and their interactions  as fixed 
effects, and trial and subject as random intercepts. Post hoc 
comparisons used emmeans [30] with false discovery rate (FDR) 
correction.  
To address the second research question, we assessed tonal 
distinctiveness at the category level. Lexical tone production in 
tonal languages is categorical, and contrast clarity depends on 
the separability of tone categories in multidimensional acoustic 
space. The present study combined distribution al distance and 
machine-learning classification to assess the tonal 
distinctiveness of older and younger adults' Mandarin lexical 
tone production. 
Tonal distinctiveness was first quantified using 
Bhattacharyya distance (BD), which measures distributional 
separation between tone categories while considering both 
category means and covariance matrices [31–33]. BD was 
calculated for the six tone pairs (T1-T2, T1-T3, T1-T4, T2-T3, 
T2-T4, T3-T4) in older and younger groups. Larger BD values 
indicated greater tonal distinctiveness. 
Tone classification was then performed by a  radial basis 
function (RBF) kernel SVM to assess acoustic separability and 
capture nonlinear boundaries among correlated acoustic cues , 
following previous research [34]. The classifier was trained 
separately for each age group using seven acoustic parameters: 
t_mean, t_slope, t_SD, t_range, t_onset, t_offset, and duration. 
The final classifier dataset contained 16,847 tokens, including 
4,528 T1 tokens (26.88%), 4,469 T2 tokens (26.53%), 3,710 T3 
tokens (22.02%), and 4,140 T4 tokens (24.57%). Stratified 
train-test partitioning and stratified 10 -fold cross -validation 
were used to preserve tone proportions across folds. 
Classification accuracy and confusion patterns were use d to 
evaluate how well tone categories could be distinguished based 
on these acoustic properties.  
Feature importance was assessed using permutation 
importance [35]: Each feature was randomly shuffled while all 
other features remained fixed, and the decrease in classification 
accuracy was recorded. This procedure was repeated 100 times 
per feature, and importance scores were normalized to sum to 
1.0 for cross-group comparison.

4. Discussion 
4.1. Age-related changes in the acoustic properties of 
individual Mandarin tones 
To address the  first research question, the present study 
compared the pitch height  (t_mean), pitch slope  (t_slope), F0 
variability (t_SD), pitch range (t_range), onset and offset of F0 
(t_onset and t_offset), and duration between younger and older 
speakers. We found that older speakers exhibited gender -
divergent F0 centralization  of different tones , contour 
flattening, greater F0 variability, and longer duration, with these 
age-related effects varying across tones and genders.  
Older females showed lower F0 in T1, T2, and T3, while 
older males showed higher F0 in T4. The findings of gender-
divergent F0 centralization of different tones in Mandarin -
speaking older speakers indicated that the aging effect on 
lexical tone production interacted with gender and tone.  This 
was the first study to uncover such a complex interplay. Unlike 
the relatively stable and uniform pitch height changes in female 
and male older speakers from non-tonal languages [11, 14], the 
variations of pitch height were inconsistent across different 
tones. Besides, s lopes were significantly flattened in T2, T3, 
and T4 for older speakers of both genders, consistent with age-
related changes in laryngeal and respiratory control reducing 
the ability to produce dynamic F0 movements [2, 3, 11].  The 
interplay of pitch height and slope changes affected the tonal 
space of older speakers. 
Along with these spectral changes, F0 variability increased 
across different tones in older speakers , indicating their less 
precise cycle -to-cycle motor control. This might increase 
within-category tone dispersion. In addition, consistent with 
previous research where syllable duration became longer with 
age [1 2], older speakers in th is study also produced 
significantly longer duration across all four tones. 
4.2. Age-related changes in the d istinctiveness of the 
Mandarin tone system 
To address the  second research question, the current study 
calculated the BD and SVM classification accuracy of younger 
and older speakers . We found that the tonal distinctiveness 
decreased in older speakers. The results also showed that 
acoustically similar tone pairs were more vulnerable to aging, 
with the T2-T3 contrast being the most affected. T2-T3 showed 
the lowest BD among all six pairs in younger speakers, and the 
BD of T2 -T3 further reduced i n older speakers . SVM 
classification also revealed an obvious bidirectional T2↔T3 
misclassification in older adults. This T2 -T3 convergence in 
production align ed with perceptual findings reported in 
previous research. Previous research reported that categorical 
perception was specifically weakened for the T2-T3 contrast in 
older adults [22]. Another study also found that T3 was the most 
vulnerable tone in older listeners' identification, attributing T3's 
perceptual difficulty to its complex and non -monotonic pitch 
contour that placed heavier demands on temporal processing 
[21]. Our tone production data suggested a parallel explanation: 
T3's dipping -rising contour p laced the greatest demands on 
laryngeal motor control, making it the most susceptible to age-
related decline. The consistency of production and perception 
findings supported the auditory-motor integration model [20] .
