Abstract
Advancing speech technology for underrepresented lan-
guages requires carefully collected and well-documented data
resources. In this work, we introduce a spontaneous multilin-
gual speech corpus consisting of 320 native speakers of Bodo,
Dzongkha, Gorkhali (Nepali), and Sherpa. These languages
span the Tibeto-Burman and Indo-Aryan families and include
both tonal and non-tonal systems. The corpus contains sponta-
neous speech recorded under consistent recording and curation
protocols. We describe the participant selection process, record-
ing design, and metadata organization used to maintain consis-
tency across languages. Acoustic analysis highlights system-
atic differences in pitch, energy, and temporal dynamics among
the languages. In addition, language identification experiments
show that these distinctions remain learnable, achieving up to
92.95% accuracy and demonstrating the usefulness of the cor-
pus for computational documentation and modeling of linguis-
tically diverse speech communities.
Index Terms: low-resource languages, speech corpus, lan-
guage identification, acoustic analysis, prosodic features, Hi-
malayan languages
1. Introduction
The development of speech technologies relies heavily on the
availability of carefully collected and well-documented speech
corpora [1–4]. While high-resource languages benefit from
large annotated datasets, many linguistically diverse regions
remain underrepresented in computational research due to the
lack of systematically collected data. In such settings, the chal-
lenge is not limited to data scarcity; it also involves determining
how speech resources should be collected, organized, and eval-
uated under practical constraints.
The Eastern Himalayan corridor, which spans parts of
Northeast India, Nepal, and Bhutan, represents one such lin-
guistically rich yet under-documented region. Within relatively
small geographic areas, languages and dialects vary consider-
ably, and patterns of intergenerational language use continue to
evolve [5, 6]. Despite active speaker communities, spontaneous
multilingual speech corpora suitable for computational analysis
remain limited [7–10]. Existing datasets are often small or lack
consistency in recording design and documentation.
Building speech datasets in such environments involves
challenges beyond simply increasing data volume [11]. Record-
ings frequently take place in acoustically variable settings with
diverse speaker demographics and limited technical infrastruc-
ture. In addition, large-scale transcription is often impracti-
cal due to dialectal variation, restricted annotation capacity,
and associated costs [12]. These factors motivate data collec-
tion approaches that emphasize structured spontaneous speech
while maintaining consistent recording procedures and well-
organized metadata.
In this work, we present a spontaneous multilingual speech
corpus containing 146 hours of speech from 320 native speak-
ers of Bodo, Sherpa, Gorkhali (Nepali), and Dzongkha. These
languages represent both Tibeto-Burman and Indo-Aryan fam-
ilies and include tonal as well as non-tonal systems, allowing
comparative analysis within a geographically coherent region.
The recordings were collected in natural indoor environments
and are accompanied by structured metadata describing speaker
age, gender, and recording conditions.
To examine dataset consistency, we perform validation
along two complementary directions. First, acoustic and
prosodic distributions are analyzed to determine whether sys-
tematic language-level patterns appear under natural recording
conditions. Second, speaker-independent language identifica-
tion (LID) [13,14] experiments are conducted to evaluate cross-
language separability. These experiments are intended as vali-
dation tools for the dataset rather than as benchmarks for model
performance.
The main contributions of this work are:
1. The collection and structured organization of a spontaneous
speech corpus for four under-resourced languages in the East-
ern Himalayan region.
2. A documented recording and metadata framework designed
to support consistent cross-language analysis under realistic
data collection conditions.
3. An acoustic characterization of language-level pitch, energy,
and temporal structure in spontaneous speech.
4. Empirical validation demonstrating that the corpus supports
reliable speaker-independent language discrimination.
By focusing on structured spontaneous speech collection
and systematic validation, this work contributes to ongoing ef-
forts toward the computational documentation of linguistically
diverse and evolving speech communities.
2. Data Collection and Curation
Framework
2.1. Language Selection and Regional Scope
The corpus includes four languages spoken across the East-
ern Himalayan corridor: Bodo, Sherpa, Gorkhali (Nepali), and
Dzongkha. These languages were chosen to represent both ty-
pological and sociolinguistic diversity within a geographically
connected region. Bodo, Sherpa, and Dzongkha belong to the
Tibeto-Burman family and display tonal characteristics, while
Gorkhali (Nepali), an Indo-Aryan language, is non-tonal. This
selection allows comparisons across different phonological sys-

The languages also differ in their level of digital and com-

2.2. Participant Recruitment and Recording Protocol
Participants were recruited through outreach within educational
institutions and community networks across the region. Re-
cruitment focused on voluntary participation rather than strict
demographic targeting. Individuals who expressed interest in
contributing were recorded and provided compensation for their
participation. Written informed consent was obtained before
each recording session, and every speaker was assigned an
anonymized identifier to protect personal identity.
The corpus contains recordings from 320 native speakers,
with 80 speakers per language. Demographic details are sum-
marized in Table 1. Although the number of speakers per lan-
guage was balanced by design, gender distribution varies no-
ticeably across languages. Sherpa recordings show a strong
male predominance (74 male, 6 female), while Bodo and
Dzongkha include a larger proportion of female speakers. These
imbalances reflect practical recruitment conditions rather than
deliberate sampling choices. Speaker ages range from early
adulthood to late middle age, introducing natural variability in
vocal characteristics. No personally identifying information be-
yond age and gender was retained.

Recordings were carried out primarily in indoor environ-
ments such as schools and colleges. Although indoor locations
were preferred to limit excessive environmental noise, the ses-
sions were not conducted in acoustically treated studios. As a
result, some recordings contain varying levels of background
noise.
Speakers were asked to produce spontaneous monologues
without predefined scripts or prompts. They were encouraged
to speak freely about topics of their choice so that natural dis-
course structure, prosodic patterns, and lexical usage could be
captured. Each participant completed five separate recording
sessions, with every session forming an independent sponta-
neous utterance. The average duration of each recording is ap-
proximately 5.5 minutes, resulting in about 27–28 minutes of
speech per speaker and a total corpus duration of 146 hours.
Two recording configurations were used. Some sessions
were captured using a portable digital recorder operating at 44.1
kHz, while others used a headphone–microphone setup config-
ured at 16 kHz. All audio files were preserved at their original
sampling rates during data collection.
2.3. Data Organization and Curation
The dataset is organized hierarchically by language, with sep-
arate subdirectories for individual speakers. Each audio file is
accompanied by structured metadata that includes the language
label, speaker age, gender, and recording environment. Con-
sistent file naming conventions and anonymized speaker identi-
fiers were maintained to support controlled experimentation and
straightforward data management.
No recordings were removed based on perceived quality,
background noise, or speaking style. All collected material
was retained to preserve the natural variability present in spon-
taneous speech. Metadata consistency and directory structure
were carefully maintained to ensure that the dataset can be reli-
ably partitioned for speaker-independent evaluation and related
experimental protocols.
3. Acoustic Structure of the Corpus
This section examines the internal acoustic structure of the cor-
pus as an intrinsic validation step. Since the dataset does not in-
clude transcriptions, distributional analysis of pitch, energy, and
temporal dynamics provides an objective way to assess whether
consistent language-level patterns emerge under natural record-
ing conditions. Frame-level fundamental frequency (F0) was
extracted using the YIN algorithm [19], with unvoiced frames
excluded from the analysis. For each recording, the mean and
standard deviation of F0 were calculated over the voiced seg-
ments. Speaker-level mean F0 was then obtained by averaging
the five recording-level means for each speaker, which helps re-
duce session-specific variability. In addition, speaking rate was
estimated from voiced-frame segmentation statistics derived us-
ing WebRTC-based speech activity detection.
3.1. Language-Level Pitch and Energy Statistics
Table 2 summarizes recording-level statistics aggregated by lan-
guage. The mean F0 values show clear differences across the
four languages: Bodo has the highest average pitch (173.7 Hz),
followed by Dzongkha (163.4 Hz), Gorkhali (156.5 Hz), and
Sherpa (141.9 Hz) [20]. The difference between the highest and
lowest averages exceeds 30 Hz, indicating noticeable variation
in average pitch across the recorded speech.
Pitch dispersion, measured through the standard deviation
of F0, ranges from 63.7 Hz to 69.2 Hz. Although these values
exhibit some overlap, they indicate differences in pitch variabil-
ity across the language subsets under the current recording con-
ditions [21]. Together, the mean and standard deviation of F0
provide complementary information about the acoustic charac-
teristics of the corpus [22].
Mean intensity values also vary across languages. Although
intensity can be influenced by recording conditions, the ob-
served variation suggests that amplitude-related characteristics
contribute additional acoustic information beyond pitch.
Taken together, these observations indicate that language-
level acoustic variation remains evident despite heterogeneous

3.2. Speaker-Level Pitch Distribution
Figure 1 shows the distribution of speaker-level mean F0 val-
ues. Clear vertical clustering can be observed across the four
languages. Bodo speakers occupy the highest pitch range, fol-
lowed by Dzongkha, while Gorkhali forms a relatively compact
cluster in the mid-range. Sherpa speakers appear in the lowest
pitch band.
Although some overlap between languages is present, dif-
ferences in the central pitch ranges remain visible at the speaker
level rather than being driven solely by individual recordings,
indicating consistent acoustic variation across the corpus.
Pitch distributions are also influenced by demographic
composition [23]. For instance, the Sherpa subset includes only
six female speakers, while the Bodo group contains a larger
proportion of female speakers. Since average F0 is strongly
influenced by speaker gender, the observed separation likely re-
flects both demographic composition and language-level acous-
tic variation within the corpus.
3.3. Temporal Organization and Speaking Rate
In addition to spectral characteristics, temporal organization
provides insight into the rhythmic patterns of discourse in each
language [24]. Speaking rate was approximated using the num-
ber of voiced speech segments per second, derived from frame-
level speech activity detection [25]. Speaker-level averages
were calculated across the five recordings available for each in-
dividual.
Figure 2 shows the distribution of speaker-level speaking
rates. Noticeable differences appear across languages. Bodo
displays the highest median segmentation rate, followed by
Dzongkha, Sherpa, and Gorkhali. These differences comple-
ment the pitch and energy characteristics, indicating that the
language subsets also differ in the temporal organization of
spontaneous speech [21].
These variations may reflect community-specific patterns of
discourse pacing and conversational style rather than controlled
phonetic design. Importantly, the differences remain visible
despite identical recording procedures, indicating that the tem-
poral organization is an inherent characteristic of the collected
spontaneous speech rather than an artifact of the recording pro-
tocol.

namics suggests that the dataset encodes a multi-dimensional

4. Empirical Validation via Language
Identification
The acoustic analyses in Section 3 revealed structured pitch,
energy, and temporal organization across the four languages.
To evaluate whether these patterns support learnable language-
level distinctions, we conduct language identification (LID)
experiments under a strict speaker-independent setting. The
goal is not to maximize classification performance, but to as-
sess whether the collected spontaneous speech encodes suf-
ficient acoustic structure for computational modeling without
transcription.
4.1. Experimental Setup
All recordings were standardized to 16 kHz mono format to en-
sure cross-language consistency. WebRTC voice activity de-
tection (aggressiveness level 3) was applied to remove non-
speech segments, and continuous speech was segmented into
non-overlapping 10-second chunks. Only complete segments
were retained to maintain uniform input duration across sam-
ples.
A speaker-independent 80/20 split was taken, preventing
any speaker overlap between training and test partitions. This
protocol ensures that evaluation reflects language-level acoustic
structure rather than speaker memorization [27]. Performance is
reported using overall accuracy, balanced accuracy, and macro-
averaged precision, recall, and F1-score, which provide robust
multi-class evaluation under demographic imbalance [28].
4.2. Spectral and Prosodic Feature Analysis
We first establish a spectral baseline using 40-dimensional
MFCC representations extracted from 10-second speech seg-
ments. Tables 3 and 4 summarize results for SVM and CNN
classifiers under the same speaker-independent protocol.

To examine the role of different acoustic cues, we eval-
uate pitch (F0 contour), intensity, and RMS loudness sepa-
rately. Across both classifiers, spectral representations remain
the most effective, indicating that spectral structure provides the
strongest signal for distinguishing languages.
Pitch on its own produces relatively low performance
(54.06% for SVM and 56.86% for CNN), even though Sec-
tion 3 showed clear pitch-band separation. This suggests that
speaker-level pitch variation exists but does not provide enough
information by itself for reliable four-language classification.
Energy-related features prove more informative than pitch.
Loudness reaches 59.54% accuracy with SVM and 76.09% with
CNN, indicating that amplitude variations capture meaningful
temporal structure across languages [29]. Intensity produces
moderate results (50.26% for SVM and 66.30% for CNN),
showing that energy cues contribute useful but partial informa-
tion.
These results reveal that the corpus exhibits a layered
acoustic structure in which spectral information provides the
strongest basis for language discrimination, while energy and
prosodic cues contribute complementary evidence. The consis-
tent trends observed across both classifiers indicate that these
patterns reflect intrinsic properties of the recorded speech rather
than artifacts of a particular feature representation, further sup-
porting the suitability of the corpus for computational modeling.

From the perspective of corpus validation, these results
show that the spontaneous speech data contains stable multi-
dimensional acoustic structure. Strong performance under
speaker-independent evaluation, together with balanced clas-
sification metrics, indicates that consistent language-level pat-
terns are present across speakers and recording conditions. This
supports the suitability of the corpus for downstream computa-
tional studies of under-resourced Himalayan languages.
Together, these results demonstrate that the corpus encodes
structured and learnable acoustic patterns, confirming its suit-
ability as a computational resource for documenting and model-
ing linguistically diverse Himalayan speech communities. Pre-
liminary experiments with representations from multilingual
pre-trained speech models did not yield competitive results un-
der the same experimental setting. This observation likely re-
flects the absence of task-specific adaptation, and future work
will investigate fine-tuning such models using the available data
for language identification and related downstream tasks.
5. Conclusion
We introduced a spontaneous speech corpus containing 146
hours of recordings from 320 native speakers of Bodo, Sherpa,
Gorkhali (Nepali), and Dzongkha. Along with the data, we de-
scribe the recording protocol, metadata structure, and curation
process used during collection under practical field conditions.
Acoustic analysis showed clear differences in pitch and energy
across languages. Speaker-independent language identification
experiments further confirm that these differences are learnable
from the recordings. Spectral features provide the strongest
signal for discrimination, while energy and prosodic cues add
complementary information. These results were obtained with-
out transcription, showing that carefully curated spontaneous
speech can still support computational analysis in low-resource
settings. Overall, the corpus offers a useful resource for fu-
ture work on Himalayan languages and illustrates a practical
approach for collecting and validating speech data in underrep-
resented linguistic communities.
