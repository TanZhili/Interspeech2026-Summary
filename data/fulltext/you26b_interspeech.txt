Diarization

Abstract 
End-to-end neural diarization with vector clustering (EEND -
VC) has emerged as a robust solution for real -world 
applications due to its ability to handle overlapped speech and 
an arbitrary number of speakers.  Existing EEND -VC 
segmentation back ends typically employ recurrent neural 
networks (RNNs), attention-based architectures, or Mamba . In 
this paper, we propose a novel segmentation model that 
integrates a WavLM -based front end with a bidirectional 
Retention Network (BiRetNet) back end. RetNet provides dual 
parallel and recurrent formulations with favorable 
computational scaling with respect to sequence length, 
motivating its use as a promising segmentation bac k end for 
speaker diarization. We evaluate the potential of BiRetNet by 
benchmarking it against established RNN, attention, and 
Mamba-based variants. Experimental results demonstrate that 
the proposed model outperforms these baselines, achieving  
state-of-the-art performance on two widely used diarization 
datasets. 
Index Terms : sp eaker diarization , EEND-VC, bidirectional 
retention network, WavLM 
1. Introduction 
Speaker diarization is the essential task of determining “ who 
spoke when”  within a multi -speaker audio recording.  It 
partitions an audio recording into time -stamped regions and 
assigning each region a speaker label.  
Traditional diarization pipelines perform speech activity 
detection, extract speaker embeddings for short segments (e.g., 
i-vectors [1] or x-vectors [2]), and group them with clustering 
such as agglomerative hierarchical clustering  [3] or Bayesian 
HMM [4] variants. These cascaded systems are robust and 
naturally support long recordings and an unknown number of 
speakers, yet they struggle to model overlapped speech. End-
to-end neural diarization (EEND) instead predicts frame -level 
activity for multiple spea kers directly with a single network, 
handling overlap by design. However, EEND typically assumes 
a fixed maximum speaker count, generalizes poorly beyond it, 
and incurs high memory cost when processing long sequences 
during training and inference. 
EEND with vector clustering [5, 6] (EEND-VC) reconciles 
these trade -offs by running an EEND model on overlapping 
short windows to obtain local, permutation-ambiguous speaker-
activity tracks, then extracting per-track embeddings and 
clustering them to derive consistent global speaker identities. A 
stitching stage aggregates overlapping window predictions into 
a full -recording diarization. Because the neural model only 
needs to operate on short chunks, computation and memory 
scale gracefully to hours -long audio, while clustering enables 
an arbitrary number of speakers. Compared with fully end-to-
end EEND, EEND-VC better handles long-form recordings and 
speaker count s, without sacrificing overlap awareness in 
practical deployments. Target-speaker voice activity detection 
(TS-VAD) [7] is another effective diarization paradigm that 
estimates each target speaker’s activity conditioned on speaker 
profiles, whereas this work follows the EEND -VC route and 
focuses on improving its local segmentation backbone. 
In a typical EEND-VC pipeline, exemplified by the widely 
used Pyannote [8] framework, the EEND segmentation model 
can be viewed as comprising a front end and a back end module. 
The front end converts raw speech waveforms into frame-level 
acoustic representations, using either signal processing based 
features (e.g., Fbank), learnable waveform front ends (e.g., 
SincNet [9]), or self-supervised pretrained speech encoders 
such as WavLM  [10]. These features are subsequently 
processed by the back end, which models speaker activity over 
time. Common back end architectures include recurrent neural 
networks (RNNs), such as bidirectional long short -term 
memory (BiLSTM) networks  [8], as well as attention -based 
models, including Transformer  [5] and Conformer [11]. More 
recently, the Mamba  [12] architecture, based on state -space 
models, has emerged as a promising alternative back end 
backbone for speaker diarization [13]. 
In recent years, Retention Networks [14] (RetNet) have 
emerged as a compelling alternative to Transformer-style self -
attention for sequence modeling, introducing a retention 
mechanism that bridges the inductive bias of recurrence with 
attention-like global dependency modeling while maintaining 
competitive performance. A key property of retention is its dual 
form (parallel and recurrent), which further enables a 
chunkwise recurrent implementation, allowing GPU -friendly 
parallel training while enabling RNN -style state updates at 
inference. In particular, the recurrent formulation maintains a 
compact, fixed- size state at inference and does not rely on 
attention-style key-value caching. For long-context settings, the 
chunkwise recurrent form encodes local windows in parallel 
and propagates cross -window information through recurrent 
summarization, yielding linear complexity with respect to 
sequence length. These architectural properties suggest that 
RetNet is well-suited for long-context modeling in diarization 
back ends. 
In this work, we adopt the EEND -VC framework and 
implement it within the Pyannote pipeline.  We introduce a 
novel EEND segmentation model that combines a WavLM -
based front end with a bidirectional Retention Network 
(BiRetNet) back end.  For the proposed model, training is 
conducted in two stages: we first optimize the segmentation 
model while keeping the pre-trained WavLM parameters fixed, 
and then optionally unfreeze WavLM to jointly fine -tune the 
system in a second stage . Furthermore, we conduct a 
comprehensive ablation study on the RetNet architecture, 
evaluating the influence of block depth and recurrent chunk 
size. We additionally provide a controlled efficiency study 
reporting real-time factor (RTF) and peak runtime memory 
across window durations.  Experimental results demonstrate 
that the proposed method surpasses established back  ends, 
including LSTM, Attention, and Mamba , across diverse 
datasets, ultimately achieving state-of-the-art performance on 
two widely used diarization corpora. The model source code is 
available at : github.com/frankyoujian/BiRetNet-
Diarization.

3. Proposed Method  
3.1. BiRetNet-based Segmentation Model 
The proposed BiRetNet -based segmentation model is 
illustrated in Figure 1. In the following, we first introduce the 
Retention Network, followed by a detailed description of the 
proposed segmentation model. 
3.1.1. Retention Network 
The Retention Network (RetNet) replaces the standard self-
attention mechanism with a multi-scale retention  module. 
Unlike Transformers, RetNet is built upon a dual form that 
enables three distinct computation paradigms:  parallel, 
recurrent, and chunkwise recurrent . We refer readers to the 
original RetNet paper [14] for the full theoretical derivation and 
implementation details; here, we focus on the functional 
representations relevant to our segmentation back end.  Given 
an input  sequence 𝑋𝑋 ∈ ℝ|𝑥𝑥|×𝑑𝑑model , it is first projected into 
queries (𝑄𝑄), keys (𝐾𝐾), and values (𝑉𝑉): 
𝑄𝑄 = 𝑋𝑋𝑊𝑊𝑄𝑄,   𝐾𝐾 = 𝑋𝑋𝑊𝑊𝐾𝐾,   𝑉𝑉 = 𝑋𝑋𝑊𝑊𝑉𝑉 (1) 
where 𝑊𝑊𝑄𝑄 , 𝑊𝑊𝐾𝐾 , 𝑊𝑊𝑉𝑉  are learnable weights. These projections 
are utilized across the three paradigms: 
Parallel Representation: As shown in Figure 2(a), this form is 
primarily used for efficient training on GPUs. It utilizes a causal 
mask matrix 𝐷𝐷 that incorporates exponential decay  𝛾𝛾 based on 
relative distance: 
Retention(𝑋𝑋) = (𝑄𝑄𝐾𝐾⊤⨀𝐷𝐷)𝑉𝑉 (2) 
where 𝐷𝐷𝑛𝑛𝑛𝑛 = 𝛾𝛾𝑛𝑛−𝑛𝑛 for 𝑛𝑛 ≥ 𝑚𝑚, and 0 otherwise.

Chunkwise Recurrent Representation: This hybrid approach 
divides the sequence into chunks, using parallel computation 
within chunks and recurrent updates across them to handle long 
audio recordings efficiently.  
3.1.2. Proposed Segmentation Model 
In this paper, we employ the BiRet Net to replace the BiLSTM 
module in Pyannote  segmentation model. As illustrated in 
Figure 1, t he BiRetNet  comprises dual parallel Retention  
blocks, one of which processes a temporally reversed feature 
sequence. These outputs are subsequently  added with the 
original input, effectively instantiating a residual skip 
connection. For the diarization task, we adopt the chunkwise 
recurrent representation of Retention. By segmenting an audio 
sequence of length L into chunks of length B, the model enables 
parallel intra -chunk computation while  maintaining cross -
chunk dependencies via fixed- size recurrent state updates. 
Consequently, with B  as a fixed hyperparameter, the system 
achieves linear time complexity relative to the total sequence 
length. 
We refer to the official RetNet 1 python implementation to 
build up the Retention block in Figure 1. Concretely, the 
Retention bloc k utilizes a four- head Retention module  and a  
position-wise feed-forward network with hidden size 1024. Pre-
LayerNorm and residual connections are employed to stabilize 
training. The Retention computation operates in chunkwise 
recurrent mode with a chunk size of 100 frames . Notably, to 
maintain consistent speaker embeddings across the entire audio 
stream, we follow [19] and set the decay factor 𝛾𝛾 to 1, thereby 
eliminating temporal information decay. Finally, four BiRetNet 
blocks are stacked sequentially to process the input features. 
3.2. Diarization Pipeline 
The Pyannote diarization pipeline is typically composed of a 
local EEND segmentation model , a speaker embedding 
extractor and a clustering module. 
3.2.1. Local EEND Segmentation Model 
In the Pyannote framework, the EEND component is primarily 
composed of a SincNet front end followed by four Bi LSTM 
layers. Our proposed approach largely adheres to the Pyannote 
processing pipeline, with one key modification: the local EEND 
segmentation model  is replaced by a model built upon a 
WavLM-based front end and a BiRet Net-based back end, as 
illustrated in Figure 1. In accordance with the strategy 
introduced in SUPERB [22], the representations extracted from 
all WavLM layers are combined through a learnable weighted 
sum at each time frame to form a unified feature representation. 
These fused WavLM features are subsequently projected 
through a linear layer and normalized vi a layer normalization 
before being fed into back end . Finally, a linear projection

performed in the powerset space [23] . Under the powerset 
formulation, a separate class is assigned to non-speech, to each 
individual speaker, and to every distinct pair of simultaneously 
active speakers. Training is done using the powerset loss [23]. 
In all experiments, we use the publicly available pre-trained 
WavLM Base+ model, which produces 768-dimensional frame-
level representations. Given 16 -kHz audio stream, WavLM 
outputs approximately 50 frames per second; therefore, a chunk 
size of 100 frames represents a 2s window.  The resulting 
WavLM representations are then passed through a linear 
projection that reduces their dimensionality from 768 to 256, 
producing features that serve as the input to back end. 
3.2.2. Speaker Embedding Extractor and Clustering 
For speaker embedding extractor, we use ResNet34-LM2 which 
was trained using WeSpeaker toolkit [24] on the VoxCeleb2 
dataset [ 25]. Speaker embeddings are computed exclusively 
from single  speaker audio segments, with automatically 
detected overlapping speech regions (as inferred by the local 
segmentation model) discarded prior to extraction.  For 
clustering approach, the VBx clustering [4] is applied on the 
aforementioned embeddings. The clustering parameters are 
optimized in experiments.  After each local active speaker has 
been mapped to a corresponding global cluster, the final global 
speaker diarization output can be assembled. 
4. Experiments 
4.1. Datasets 
We train all models on a compound dataset which is composed 
of the training partitions of the AMI-SDM (AMI)  [26], 
AISHELL-4 (AIS-4) [27], AliMeeting  far (AliM) [28], 
NOTSOFAR-1 (NSF) [29], MSDWild  Few (MSD) [30], 
VoxConverse v0.3 (VoxC) [31], RAMC [32], LibriConvo [33], 
CallHome [34] and a synthetic dataset. For  CallHome, we use 
part13 for training. The total duration of training dataset is 952  
hours. For recordings captured with multichannel microphone 
arrays, we retain only the first channel  for use. Following the 
recipe presented in [ 35], t he synthetic dataset is created by 
simulating speech mixtures using LibriSpeech [ 36]. 
Background noise from MUSAN [37] and simulated room 
impulse response [38] are applied to the synthetic mixtures . 
DIHARD III [39] is excluded from the compound training set 
and used solely for out-of-domain evaluation and post-domain-
adaptation evaluation. 
4.2. Configurations 
For training, each audio recording is partitioned into fixed-
length 10-second segments. Following the settings in [23], we 
use N=4 (maximum speakers per segment) and K=2 (maximum 
concurrent speakers per frame) for powerset models, which can 
cover at least 97% of the data.  Training is performed in two 
stages. In the first stage, the WavLM parameters are kept 
frozen. All models are trained for 100 epochs with a batch size 
of 128 using the AdamW  [40] optimizer with default 
hyperparameters and an initial learning rate of 1e-3. We employ 
a ReduceLROnPlateau scheduler that halves the learning rate 
after 10 epochs without improvement. In the second stage, the 
WavLM parameters are unfrozen to jointly fine-tune the entire 
system. Owing to memory constraints, the batch size is reduced 
from 128 to 64. The initial learning rate is decreased from 1e-3 
to 1e-5, and the scheduler patience is adjusted from 10 to 8. 
During this stage, models are trained for up to 60 epochs . 
Domain adaptation is done per dataset, which relies on the 
corresponding training and development sets. For domain 
adaptation, early stopping is employed with a patience of 10 
epochs and a constant learning rate of 1e-5. 
The DiariZen 4 framework based on Pyannote pipeline is 
utilized for inference. The final model is derived by averaging  
the last 15 checkpoints. Hyperparameters are optimized through 
Optuna [41] on development subset  of each dataset . The 
hyperparameter search is performed for 300 iterations. More 
specifically, four hyperparameters are tuned:  the intra-speaker 
gap-bridging threshold Δ, the clustering threshold δ, and the 
acoustic scaling factor 𝐹𝐹𝐴𝐴 and speaker regularization coefficient 
𝐹𝐹𝐵𝐵 in the VBx algorithm. System performance is assessed using 
the diarization error rate (DER) without collars. In addition, we 
report the macro-averaged DER  to provide a comprehensive 
measure of overall performance and cross-dataset robustness.

4.3.2. Effect of Model Size 
The WavLM -based front end contains 94.3M parameters. 
Excluding this, the proposed BiRetNet back end (8.6M) is 
larger than the LSTM (1.6M) and Mamba (7.4M) baselines. To 
ensure BiRetNet’s gains are not due to an unfair parameter 
advantage, we evaluated two reduced configurations. First, by 
decreasing the back end blocks from 4 to 3, a 6.5M -parameter 
BiRetNet achieves a 1 5.9% in -domain average DER, 
outperforming the larger Mamba baseline (16. 2%). Second, 
reducing the back end to 1.4M parameters by decreasing feature 
dimensionality to 64 yields a 16.5% in -domain average DER. 
This remains superior to the LSTM system despite a smaller 
parameter budget. 
4.3.3. Ablation Studies 
Figure 3 exhibits the DER results of the BiRetNet block number 
ablation study. The four-block configuration achieves the best 
overall performance, consistently producing the lowest DER 
across datasets. Increasing the depth to five blocks often 
degrades performance  (e.g., DER on AliMeeting rises from 
16.1% to 16.7% ), indicating that additional complexity offers 
limited benefit and may introduce optimization overhead. 
Missed detections and speaker confusion remain  the dominant 
error sources. The highest DER occurs  on NOTSOFAR -1 
(~22%), reflecting its high speaker density and challenging 
acoustic variability. In particular, the severe speaker confusion 
on NOTSOFAR-1 corresponds to its large maximum speaker 
count (N=7), highlighting the difficulty of resolving 
overlapping speech in dense scenarios. In contrast, the model 
performs most effectively on VoxConverse and AISHELL -4, 
where DER remains stable at approximately 10–11%. 
Figure 4 presents the DER result s of RetNet chunk size 
ablation study. The result illustrates that a chunk size of 100 
generally achieves the lowest DER across most corpora.  This 
empirical performance aligns with the underlying 
conversational dynamics. Specifically, we find  that 92% of 
speaker turn intervals in the AliMeeting dataset occur within 2s. 
This observation suggests that a 2s window can cover most turn 
transitions, enabling the model to capture speaker transitions 
effectively without the information loss  seen at a 1s window 
(chunk size=50). While a larger 5s window (chunk size=250) 
may slightly improve missed detection in certain contexts, it 
often introduces higher speaker confusion and false alarms by 
aggregating over-extended temporal data, thereby validating 
the 2s window  (chunk size=100) as the ideal balance for 
maintaining local precision. 
4.3.4. Computational Efficiency Analysis

factor (RTF) and peak runtime memory during segmentation 
inference. Experiments are run on one hour of audio recordings

sliding-window step ratio of 0.1. All systems are evaluated in a 
unified CPU‑only setting without GPU acceleration, using

model processing time and audio duration.
