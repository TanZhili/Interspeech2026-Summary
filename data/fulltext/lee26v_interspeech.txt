Abstract
Silent Speech Interfaces (SSIs) offer an alternative communi-
cation pathway by reconstructing intelligible speech from non-
acoustic biosignals such as facial electromyography (EMG). In
particular, EMG can capture muscle activity associated with ar-
ticulatory movements even in the absence of vocalization, serv-
ing as a suitable modality for silent speech synthesis. A ma-
jor challenge in EMG-to-speech (ETS) research is the natu-
ral absence of ground-truth speech for silent EMG, along with
the scarcity of available data. To address this, we propose a
novel Silent-V oiced Alignment (SV A) loss, which introduces
a multi-level representation-based alignment objective between
utterance-parallel silent and voiced EMG pairs. Extensive ex-
periments show that our method significantly enhances content
reconstruction and can be seamlessly integrated into existing
ETS frameworks. Furthermore, we demonstrate enhanced in-
telligibility and robustness under a standardized evaluation pro-
tocol.

1. Introduction
Speech is one of the most fundamental and efficient modalities
of human communication. However, in circumstances where
normal vocalization is not possible due to laryngeal injury,
neurodegenerative disorders, or acoustically constrained envi-
ronments, spoken communication becomes substantially lim-
ited. In such cases, a Silent Speech Interface (SSI) offers
an alternative means of communication by using non-acoustic
biosignals to reconstruct intelligible speech. Recent studies
[1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6] have explored the feasibility of SSI by develop-
ing EMG-to-Speech (ETS) techniques that directly synthesize
audible speech from facial electromyography (EMG) signals.
Typically, these systems are implemented using deep learning
architectures, where ETS models consist of an EMG encoder
that converts EMG signals into acoustic features and a vocoder
for waveform reconstruction.
Silent EMG refers to EMG signals that capture muscle
activity during non-vocal articulation, which serves as the
core modality for ETS applications. Nevertheless, synthesiz-
ing speech from silent EMG is considerably more challenging
than from voiced EMG. The main reasons are as follows: (1)
Publicly available datasets for silent speech suffer from data-
scarcity [7], and the sample size of silent EMG is significantly
smaller than that of voiced EMG, causing data imbalance [2].
(2) While voiced EMG is paired with time-aligned acoustic
targets, silent EMG offers no direct speech supervision due
to the absence of audible speech [8]. To address these chal-
lenges, recent studies [2, 3, 5] have adopted a target-transfer
strategy, leveraging datasets with paired voiced and silent ut-
terances recorded from the same transcripts. However, these
approaches focus solely on direct transfer of acoustic targets
from voiced EMG to silent EMG and overlook representation
consistency between silent–voiced EMG pairs.
In this study, we propose a novel training strategy that ex-
plicitly exploits utterance-parallel EMG pairs by enforcing a
representation mapping between silent and voiced conditions,
thereby guiding silent EMG embeddings to better capture artic-
ulatory content. This mapping encourages silent EMG repre-
sentations to align more closely with those derived from voiced
EMG, facilitating more accurate prediction of downstream
acoustic targets such as mel-spectrograms or soft speech units
[9]. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first work to use
parallel silent–voiced EMG signals as explicit representation-
based supervision for ETS training. Moreover, we demonstrate
that the performance of conventional ETS frameworks varies
substantially across trials, and our method consistently achieves
superior results with trial-wise statistical significance—an as-
pect that has not been previously addressed in ETS research.

2. Related work
Prior ETS studies [2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 10] have focused on improv-
ing the prediction of mel-spectrograms or soft speech units, ex-
ploiting the complementary information provided by utterance-
matched voiced EMG recordings. Earlier efforts [2, 3, 5] trained
EMG encoders to predict acoustic representations from silent
EMG, including MFCCs [2], mel-spectrograms [3], and soft
speech units, which were used for voice conversion in [5, 10].
These predicted representations were then employed to synthe-
size speech via either a pre-trained WaveNet [2] or HiFi-GAN
speech vocoder [11]. A self-supervised pretraining strategy was
first applied in [4] to improve encoder representation learning
under limited labeled data conditions, and the pretrained en-
coder was subsequently fine-tuned in a supervised manner to
predict mel-spectrograms. In [6], a diffusion model was trained
on top of a pretrained EMG encoder to improve the quality of
predicted log mel-spectrograms, with the primary objective re-
maining the enhancement of acoustic representations such as
log mel-spectrograms. Despite the availability of utterance-
parallel EMG pairs (i.e., silent and voiced recordings of the
same utterance), most existing approaches have not utilized
such paired information during ETS training, relying solely on
speech-derived acoustic targets as supervision.

account for such trial-to-trial variance and validate the statistical
significance of our improvements, thereby ensuring the empiri-
cal robustness of our proposed method.
3. Method

3.2. EMG Encoder Architecture
We adopt the EMG encoder proposed in [3], which comprises a
set of residual convolutional layers that down-sample the EMG
signal to either 50Hz features for soft speech unit prediction
or 86.13Hz features for mel-spectrogram prediction. Subse-
quently, a transformer module is employed with two output
heads that predict phoneme probabilities, along with either soft
speech units following [5], or mel-spectrograms following [3].
To train the aforementioned encoder, we employ a two-stage
training strategy: the EMG encoder is first pretrained using only
voiced EMG data with standard acoustic and phoneme losses,
and then fine-tuned on both voiced and silent EMG data with
the additional SV A loss.
4. Experiments
4.1. EMG Datasets
We conducted our experiments using the Gaddy & Klein
dataset [2], which consists of 1,289 silent-voiced parallel
pairs and 5,477 non-parallel voiced utterances for training.
The dataset contains 8-channel facial EMG signals originally
recorded at a sampling rate of 1000 Hz. Following the original
implementation [16], we applied a 60 Hz notch filter to remove
power-line interference and a 2 Hz high-pass filter.
To ensure a fair comparison, we strictly followed the data
configuration and preprocessing protocols specific to each base-
line. For Gaddyet al.[3] and SU-ETS [5], the signals were
downsampled to 689.06 Hz and 800 Hz, respectively. The 98
test samples were used for evaluation, excluding those with no
alphanumeric characters.
4.2. Implementation Details
Given that EMG signals are highly sensitive, model perfor-
mance can vary depending on the random seed, leading to large
fluctuations in speech intelligibility metrics, such as Character
Error Rate (CER) and Word Error Rate (WER). To address this
variability, we employed an evaluation protocol that has not
been investigated in previous studies. Specifically, we trained
the model with 10 different seeds for 200 epochs each and saved
a model every 10 epochs. For each seed, the model with the
lowest WER on the validation set was selected for test evalua-
tion. The final model performance was reported as the average
of the test CER and WER across all 10 seeds. All models were
trained with a batch size of 80 for 200 epochs using the AdamW
optimizer with a learning rate of 3e-4 and weight decay of 1e-5.
A ReduceLROnPlateau scheduler [17] decreased the learning
rate by a factor of 0.5 when validation performance plateaued.
In this work, we further utilized a sample-wise soft schedul-
ing weightω=σ(τ−L out
SV A)based on the silent–voiced align-
ment, whereσdenotes the sigmoid function andτis a threshold
hyperparameter. Samples with better alignment receive larger
weights, so thatL SV Ais emphasized when the silent–voiced cor-
respondence is reliable, especially in early training. The loss
weightsλ T andλ P in the DTW cost matrices were both set to
0.5, and the total loss weightsα,β, andγwere all set to1.0,
which were empirically chosen. For mel-spectrogram targets,
we used the same HiFi-GAN vocoder [11] to synthesize speech
waveforms from the predicted mel-spectrograms.

5.1. Objective Intelligibility Evaluation
Table 1 presents the results of the objective intelligibility eval-
uation. Notably, we observed that the standard deviation for
the WER metric across different seeds is approximately 1%.
This variance underscores the necessity of a multi-seed eval-
uation protocol, as the limited dataset size and the inherent
non-stationarity of EMG signals can lead to inconsistent per-
formance across individual training runs.
Our proposed method consistently outperforms the base-
lines across both acoustic target settings. When compared to
the Gaddy’s [3], our model (Mel) achieves a lower CER and
WER, yielding a relative WER reduction of 6.0% (from 26.75%
to 25.14%). This improvement is found to be statistically sig-

perior performance over SU-ETS [5], reducing the WER from
27.78% to 26.55% (p=0.009). These quantitative assessments
indicate that our silent-voiced alignment mechanism effectively
enhances ETS performance, regardless of whether the acoustic
target is continuous (Mel) or discrete (SU). Furthermore, the
lower standard deviation highlights our method’s robustness,
ensuring that the intelligibility improvements are not only sig-

6. Conclusion
In this study, we devised Silent–V oiced Alignment (SV A), a
training strategy that explicitly leverages parallel silent–voiced
EMG data to improve the intelligibility of EMG-to-speech
(ETS) synthesis. By enforcing representation-level consistency
between silent and voiced EMG signals at both hidden and
output layers, our method facilitates effective mapping toward
speech-derived acoustic targets. Across both mel-spectrogram
and soft speech unit targets, our experimental results show
that the SV A loss consistently yields statistically significant
reductions in WER under multi-seed evaluation, demonstrat-
ing robustness to training variability. Ablation studies fur-
ther confirm that output- and hidden-level alignments provide
complementary benefits. These findings highlight that explicit
silent–voiced alignment offers an effective and easily integrable
approach for enhancing ETS systems, suggesting a promising
direction for future silent speech research.
