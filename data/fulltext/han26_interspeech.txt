A Transcript-anchored Pipeline With Large Language Models For Detecting
Inappropriate Pauses In Dysarthric Speech
Minsu Han1,∗, Insung Lee2,∗, Taeyoung Jeong2, Myoung-Wan Koo2,∗∗
1 CTO, LG Electronics, South Korea

Abstract
Dysarthria, a motor speech disorder common after stroke, re-
duces intelligibility and often involves prosodic abnormalities
such as inappropriate pauses (IPs). These provide critical cues
for clinical assessment and treatment; however, reliable auto-
matic evaluation remains challenging because transcript-level
information is essential. We present an automated frame-
work forIPdetection using large language models (LLMs).
The framework integrates verbatim automatic speech recogni-
tion, the Montreal Forced Aligner with refined temporal bound-
aries, LLM-guided lexical adaptation, and LLM-based classi-
fication with rationale generation. This pipeline enables accu-
rate identification ofIPsand provides precise time alignment in
dysarthric speech. Experimental results demonstrate strong per-
formance against expert evaluation, highlighting the effective-
ness of LLM-guided processing. Overall, our study introduces
a scalable and explainable approach for clinical and research
assessment.
Index Terms: speech recognition, human-computer interac-
tion, computational paralinguistics
1. Introduction
Dysarthria is a motor speech disorder caused by neurological
conditions such as cerebral palsy, Parkinson’s disease, and espe-
cially stroke. Impairments in respiration, laryngeal control, and
articulation often yield prosodic errors that reduce intelligibil-
ity [1, 2, 3]. Clinically, these deficits are tracked via maximum
phonation time, diadochokinetic rate, and standardized reading
passages [2, 3, 4]. In passage reading, patients frequently ex-
hibit articulatory imprecision, disfluency, and prosodic errors;
a salient manifestation isinappropriate pauses (IPs)at irreg-
ular locations—e.g., inside noun phrases or even words rather
than at syntactic boundaries. Such pauses are clinically impor-
tant because their location, duration, and context strongly affect
perceived intelligibility and naturalness [5, 6, 7]. In Korea, the
Autumn Paragraphis commonly used to elicit these phenom-
ena [8].
Automating the assessment of pause appropriateness re-
quires both reliable pause detection and accurate alignment
to transcripts. V oice activity detection (V AD) [9, 10, 11] ef-
fectively identifies non-speech regions but does not map di-
rectly to linguistic units [11, 12]. Forced alignment can at-
tach timestamps to text, yet pipelines that combine an au-
tomatic speech recognition (ASR) transcript with a separate
aligner—e.g., Whisper transcription followed by wav2vec 2.0
CTC alignment [13, 14], as in WhisperX—often degrade on

Aligner (MFA) [16] offer high temporal resolution, provid-
ing precise, phonetically-grounded alignment [17]. End-to-end
models such as TDT jointly predict transcripts and token dura-

also support timestamp prediction [19]; however, these meth-
ods are difficult to apply in dysarthric speech due to the se-
vere scarcity of annotated data. Because pause appropriateness
depends not only on the presence and duration of silence but
also onwhereit occurs relative to linguistic units, represent-
ing pause evidence in a transcript-anchored, text-readable form
can enable an LLM to interpret pauses jointly with linguistic
context. Recent work has shown that prosodic cues—including
pauses—can be represented as transcript-anchored textual units
that LLMs can leverage for speech assessment [20].
Recent work ondisfluency-aware verbatimASR has im-
proved timestamp accuracy for disfluent speech by explicitly
preserving fillers and self-repairs [21]. Related efforts for de-
mentia or aphasia speech similarly target filler-inclusive tran-
scription and timestamping [22, 23, 24]. To our knowledge, no
prior work has systematically studied verbatim ASR with times-
tamp prediction for dysarthric speech, despite its prevalence.
Motivated by this gap, we propose a transcript-anchored
pipeline tailored to dysarthric speech. First, a Whisper [25]
model is trained to generate verbatim transcripts that pre-
serve fillers, repetitions, and self-repairs. These transcripts
are aligned with speech using the MFA; however, since the
MFA lexicon cannot represent many atypical tokens produced
in dysarthric speech, a large language model (LLM) is first em-
ployed to map such tokens in the Whisper transcripts into forms
compatible with forced alignment. The resulting candidate
pause timestamps are then refined with frame-level V AD, yield-
ing duration-marked pause tokens (e.g.,[PAUSE 0.82s]).
A second LLM evaluates pause appropriateness by considering
both duration and linguistic context, guided by definitions from
speech–language pathologists (SLPs). We benchmark multiple
transcriber–aligner configurations and adopt the most accurate
setup for both timing and downstream IP detection.

2. Inappropriate Pause Categories
For systematic assessment of prosodic errors in dysarthric
speech, we defineIPsbased on their linguistic positioning and
phonetic context. Our classification framework categorizesIPs
into three types using the standardizedAutumn Paragraphread-
ing task:
•Intra-word Pauses:Pauses occurring within a word that dis-
rupt natural syllable structure, based on original text segmen-
tation.
•Pauses Following Vocal Fillers:Pauses succeeding vocal
hesitations (e.g.,“uh”, “um”) when unclear articulation, in-
cluding excessively prolonged pauses surrounding fillers.
•Pauses During Pronunciation Corrections:Pauses occur-
ring during repetitive attempts to correct mispronunciations,
including cases where repetitions deviate from target pronun-
ciation regardless of internal consistency.
3. Methods
Our approach comprises four sequential stages as shown in
Fig 1: (1) verbatim transcription with a fine-tuned Whisper
model; (2) Forced alignment using MFA with frame-level V AD
fusion to obtain precise pause timestamps; (3) LLM-guided
lexical adaptation that maps out-of-lexicon tokens to MFA-
compatible forms while preserving a stable correspondence to
the original transcript; and (4) LLM-based evaluation of pause
appropriateness.
3.1. Verbatim ASR
Standard Whisper models tend to suppress fillers and self-
repairs [25], which conflicts with the clinical requirement to
capture dysarthric phenomena verbatim. We therefore re-
annotated theAutumn Paragraphcorpus to preserve disfluen-
cies (fillers, repetitions, cut-offs) and fine-tuned Whisper for
verbatim output onAutumn Paragraph.
3.2. MFA Alignment and V AD-Based Timestamp Fusion
MFA is run on the adapted transcript to obtain phone-/word-
level timestamps. Candidate pauses are identified as inter-token
silences exceeding a minimum duration. In parallel, a frame-
level V AD detects non-speech spans. For each alignment-
derived pause, if any overlap with a V AD non-speech span ex-
ists, the pause boundaries are replaced with the corresponding
V AD times; otherwise, the MFA timings are retained. The final
durations are injected into the transcript as explicit tokens (e.g.,
[PAUSE 0.82s]).
3.3. LLM-Guided Lexical Adaptation for MFA
While the MFA is a powerful tool for generating accurate,
fine-grained alignments [17], its direct application to dysarthric
speech presents significant challenges. The frequent pronunci-
ation errors in such speech cause the dictionary-based aligner
to often fail, tokenizing many words as unknown<unk>. This
issue is particularly problematic in unspaced languages like Ko-
rean, where mapping<unk>tokens back to the original tran-
script is non-trivial. A straightforward solution—building a
new pronunciation dictionary—is infeasible due to the scarcity
of in-domain data. To bridge the gap between verbatim ASR
and MFA outputs, we employ an LLM-based post-editing step.
Using the MFA word list and the original transcript as in-
put, the model preserves transcript words, inserts pause tags
at alignment-indicated positions, and substitutes<unk>tokens
with their corresponding words.
3.4. LLM-Based Pause Appropriateness Assessment
Given the verbatim transcript with accurate pause durations
marker, we prompt an LLM to (i) classify each pause asap-
propriate pause (AP)or one of threeIPcategories defined in
Section 2, and (ii) produce short rationales grounded in those
definitions and the local linguistic context. These labels and
rationales are used in downstream analyses and evaluation.
4. Experiments Details
4.1. Dataset
The experimental dataset consists of 743 Korean utterances,
with each participant reading theAutumn Paragraph[8]. The
dataset includes speech recordings and verbatim transcriptions,
along with detailed pause and temporal boundary annotations.
Dysarthria severity is categorized into three levels based on
the National Institute of Health Stroke Scale: Healthy Control
(HC), Mild-to-Moderate, and Severe. The distribution of the
dataset by severity is as follows: there are 221 HC, 467 Mild-
to-Moderate, and 55 Severe utterances, totaling 15.7 hours of
audio. On average, the duration of utterances is 58.4 seconds
for HC, 79.9 seconds for Mild-to-Moderate, and 114.9 seconds
for Severe groups, with an overall average duration of 76.1 sec-
onds. The dataset was divided into training, validation, and test
sets in an 8:1:1 ratio, ensuring a speaker-independent setting for
evaluation.

4.2. Experimental Setup
We trained three models for transcription and pause alignment:
a V AD model [11], wav2vec 2.0 [14], and Whisper [25]. Ad-
ditionally, we utilized GPT-5 [26], for the LLM-guided lexical
adaptation component and pause appropriateness classification.
All training was conducted on NVIDIA A100 GPUs.
•V AD: We employed Silero-V AD [11]. Training was con-
ducted using the Adam optimizer [27] with a learning rate
of 5e-4, batch size of 128, on a single GPU without warm
up steps for a maximum of 5 epochs. For inference, optimal
thresholds were determined on the validation set.
•wav2vec 2.0: We utilized a Korean-trained version [28].
Training was conducted using the AdamW optimizer [29]
with a learning rate of 3e-4, batch size of 128, across 4 GPUs.
We employed a 16.7% warm up rate for a maximum of 15
epochs.
•Whisper: We used the small version. Training was con-
ducted using the AdamW optimizer [29] with a learning rate
of 1e-5, batch size of 16, across 4 GPUs. We applied a 10%
warm up rate for a maximum of 20 epochs.
4.3. Pause Alignment Evaluation
4.3.1. Configuration
We evaluated pause alignment performance using different
combinations of transcription models and forced aligners. For
transcription, we employed wav2vec 2.0 [14], Whisper [25],
and WhisperP (Whisper trained with pause tokens in previous
study [6]). For alignment, we used MFA, wav2vec 2.0 CTC de-
coder, and DTW. Model-aligner combinations were determined
by architectural compatibility: wav2vec 2.0 used only its own
CTC decoder, WhisperP employed the wav2vec 2.0 CTC de-
coder for pause token processing, while Whisper was evaluated
with all three alignment methods.
Additionally, we evaluated both pre-trained and fine-tuned
V AD models to assess the impact of domain adaptation on
dysarthric speech, using their respective optimal thresholds.
4.3.2. Evaluation Metric
To quantitatively assess pause alignment accuracy, we calcu-
lated F1-scores. Following the methodology in [21], we ap-
plied a 0.2-second tolerance window (or ‘collar’) to both the
onset and offset of predicted pauses, accounting for minor tim-
ing variations that are acceptable in practical applications.

5. Results
5.1. Pause Alignment Performance
As shown in Table 1, fine-tuning the V AD resulted in a signif-
icant performance improvement of approximately 18 percent-
age points (pp) overall in pause alignment. The V AD refine-
ment method, which forcibly replaces the boundaries of over-
lapping pauses with V AD-detected values, leads to improved
pause alignment performance even when the aligner alone per-
forms poorly. This is because relying solely on the aligner
would depend on the poor dysarthric speech transcription per-
formance, as evidenced in Table 2. However, this approach is
limited by its strong dependence on the performance of the V AD
itself. While MFA performs better than V AD for pause align-
ment on non-dysarthric speech, its performance does not hold
up as the severity of dysarthria increases. Our approach, which
combines MFA’s alignment capability with V AD’s robustness,
achieves consistent pause alignment performance regardless of
severity. It also outperforms combinations of other alignment
methods with V AD. Furthermore, it aligns better with human-
perceived pauses, as confirmed by expert assessments.
5.2. SLPs assessment for IP Classification
To evaluateIPclassification performance, we report macro-F1
scores for the binary classification task (Appropriate Pauses
(AP)vs.IP), stratified by dysarthria severity and referenced
against SLP judgments. As shown in Table 3, the MFA-based
approach achieves the highest scores for both HC (0.64) and
mild-to-moderate groups (0.68). In contrast to wav2vec 2.0 and

5.3. Case studies
Fig. 2 illustrates how our pipeline determines pause appropri-
ateness and the types of errors it makes. In the HC examples,
the model incorrectly judges an intra-word pause as appropri-
ate. Under Yale Romanization, ‘issnun tushhan’ is written with
a space, which may prompt the LLM to treat the pause as inter-
word and thus acceptable. In Korean, however, the phrase is
typically read as a single word-like unit, so a pause between
‘issnun’ and ‘tushhan’ is unnatural. This highlights a failure
mode of LLM-based judgment: over-reliance on romanized to-
ken boundaries without sufficient knowledge of Korean reading
conventions.

and ‘swuka’) as inherently unacceptable, whereas the SLP may
judge it asAPwhen the pause can be interpreted as a compen-
satory breath in dysarthric speech. Second, even when both the
model and the SLP agree that a pause isIP, the causal anno-
tation can diverge: for example, the SLP may assign a com-
posite cause (following vocal filler and during pronunciation

5.4. Ablation Study: Impact of IP on Dysarthria Detection
To validate that the proposedIPdetection pipeline captures
clinically meaningful characteristics, we conducted an ablation
study using a LightGBM [30] classifier. The baseline model
used six features: WER from fine-tuned Whisper transcriptions
and five V AD-based pause features (number of pauses, aver-
age/max pause duration, pause rate, and pause ratio). We then
augmented this with threeIPfeatures from our pipeline (IPdu-
ration,IPratio, andIPmax duration), yielding nine features
trained on the same data splits with Sec. 4.
As shown in Table 4, incorporatingIPfeatures improved
macro-accuracy by 8.4 percentage points and macro-F1 by 7.2
percentage points. These gains demonstrate that ourIPde-
tection pipeline captures dysarthria-relevant prosodic patterns
beyond what conventional V AD-based pause features provide.
Moreover, because the features are derived from transcript-
anchored pause analysis, they offer interpretable cues that en-
hance both the accuracy and explainability of dysarthria assess-
ment.
6. Conclusion
In this study, we proposed an inappropriate pause detection
pipeline that leverages the text understanding capabilities of
LLMs. By adopting LLMs at two separate stages, our approach
overcomes the limitations of MFA—which is often difficult to
apply in domains with limited data such as dysarthria—and
enables effective detection of inappropriate pauses. Through
extensive experiments across diverse system combinations,
we identified the most suitable time-aligned pause detection
method for dysarthric speech. In addition, expert evaluation
and auxiliary experiments were conducted to assess the perfor-
mance of the proposed model and to demonstrate the role of
inappropriate pauses in improving both the accuracy and inter-
pretability of dysarthria assessment.
Nevertheless, our study has clear limitations. Because our
inappropriate-pause detection module is transcript-anchored
and relies on LLMs, we could not fully validate its effectiveness
for patients with severe dysarthria, where transcripts are more
likely to be unreliable. Future work should therefore extend
the proposed methodology to moderate-to-severe dysarthria and
improve robustness to transcription errors to achieve more com-
prehensive and clinically applicable results.
