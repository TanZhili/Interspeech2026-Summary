Abstract
African languages remain severely underrepresented in large-
scale speech resources, particularly for spontaneous speech
reflecting real-world use. We present WazobiaSpeech, an
ethically governed corpus spanning four Nigerian languages,
2,540.8 hours of audio, and 2,865 speakers, with emphasis on
naturally occurring speech across diverse sociolinguistic con-
texts. Although a small portion - 4% of the entire data includes
scripted recordings to ensure lexical and domain coverage, the
dataset is predominantly spontaneous. Unlike prior corpora
built mainly on scripted speech, WazobiaSpeech captures au-
thentic variation in accent, dialect, register, and code-switching,
enriched with demographic and contextual metadata. We detail
data collection and transcription workflows, and report baseline
ASR results with cross-lingual error analyses, including fine-
grained evaluation of tone sensitivity in speech recognition.
Index Terms: speech recognition, African languages, tone sen-
sitivity analysis, error analysis
1. Introduction
Despite recent advances in multilingual speech and language
models [1, 2, 3, 4], African languages remain systematically un-
derrepresented in the datasets used to train and evaluate speech
technologies. Existing large-scale speech resources often pri-
oritize read or scripted speech, provide limited demographic
annotation, and lack strong governance mechanisms, resulting
in models that perform poorly in real-world African contexts.
Spontaneous speech—characterized by natural prosody, disflu-
encies, dialectal variation, and code-switching—poses distinct
challenges for automatic speech recognition (ASR) systems, yet
remains largely absent from publicly available African language
datasets. This gap limits both model robustness and our ability
to study fairness, bias, and generalization across sociolinguistic
groups [5, 6, 7, 8].
To address these limitations, we introduce WazobiaSpeech,
a large-scale speech dataset designed to reflect the linguistic,
demographic, and contextual diversity of African languages. It
consists of∼2500 hours of speech data collected from 2,865 di-
verse speakers across four languages - Hausa, Igbo, Naij ´a, and
Yor`ub´a. WazobiaSpeech prioritizes spontaneous speech col-
lected from speakers across age groups, genders, regions, and
domains, and is accompanied by fine-grained metadata to sup-
port sociolinguistic and fairness-aware analysis. By grounding
speech data in real-world linguistic contexts, WazobiaSpeech
enables principled evaluation of ASR systems beyond read
speech benchmarks, supports analysis of demographic perfor-
mance disparities, and facilitates research on explainable and
robust speech technologies for low-resource languages.
In addition to its technological objectives, the project fore-
grounds ethical and participatory data practices. All speech data
is collected with informed consent, and transcriptions are pro-
duced by native and highly proficient speakers, ensuring both
linguistic accuracy and cultural sensitivity. By enabling the
development of robust ASR and TTS models for African lan-
guages, the WazobiaSpeech Project contributes to the broader
goals of linguistic inclusion, digital equity, and sustainable
language technology development in underrepresented regions.
Our contributions are as follows:(1.)We release Wazobi-
aSpeech1, a large-scale, multi-language African speech dataset
focused on spontaneous and scripted speech.(2.)We provide
rich demographic and contextual metadata enabling fairness,
bias, and sociolinguistic analyses.(3.)We present baseline
ASR results across languages.(4.)We perform error analysis
of model performance and fine-grained tone sensitivity analysis
for Yor`ub´a.
This paper is organized as follows. Section 2 surveys re-
lated literature on African language speech resources. Section
3 provides linguistic background on Hausa, Igbo, Naij ´a, and
Yor`ub´a. Section 4 describes our data collection methodology
and ethical governance framework. In Section 5, we discuss our
data quality evaluation framework. Section 6 presents baseline
ASR experiments and results. Section 7 includes error analyses
across languages while tone sensitivity analysis is described in
Section 8. Section 9 concludes and discusses future research
directions.
2. Literature Review
In this section, we synthesize prior work on multilingual speech
technology, African-language speech resources, and dataset
creation methodologies to contextualize the contribution of the
WazobiaSpeech project.

2.2. Data Quality and Methodological Challenges
Beyond scale, data quality and methodological rigor remain
critical challenges in African speech datasets. Comprehensive
audits by Lau et al [19] of widely used benchmarks—including
Common V oice 17, FLEURS, and V oxPopuli—identify sys-
tematic labeling errors and inconsistencies that disproportion-
ately affect low-resource languages. Although Common V oice
[20] covers a broad range of African languages, data qual-
ity, demographic balance, and recording conditions vary sub-
stantially across languages and locales. The FLEURS bench-
mark [15], while valuable for multilingual evaluation, provides
only approximately 12 hours of speech per language, render-
ing it insufficient for robust model training or detailed sociolin-
guistic analysis. Moreover, many existing datasets lack fine-
grained demographic and contextual metadata, limiting their
utility for studying bias, fairness, and performance variation
across speaker groups. These limitations underscore the need
for datasets that combine scale, quality control, sociolinguis-
tic diversity, and transparent documentation—particularly for
spontaneous speech, which remains underrepresented despite
its importance for real-world ASR robustness.
2.3. Spontaneous Speech and Sociolinguistic Variation
Spontaneous speech poses well-documented challenges for
ASR systems due to disfluencies, informal registers, prosodic
variability, and code-switching [21, 22]. Models trained pre-
dominantly on read or scripted speech frequently exhibit de-
graded performance when deployed in natural conversational
settings [23, 24]. These challenges are especially pronounced
in African linguistic contexts, where multilingualism, dialectal
variation, and frequent code-switching are common [25]. Nev-
ertheless, spontaneous speech remains sparsely represented in
publicly available African language corpora, limiting system-
atic investigation of these phenomena. Addressing this gap is
essential for developing robust and equitable speech technolo-
gies that generalize beyond laboratory conditions.
2.4. Dataset Documentation, Ethics, and Governance
The NLP community has increasingly emphasized the impor-
tance of transparent dataset documentation and ethical data
practices, as articulated in frameworks such as Datasheets for
Datasets [26] and Model Cards [27]. These concerns are partic-
ularly salient for speech data, which inherently contains per-
sonally identifiable and biometric information. Recent work
has further highlighted the societal risks of large-scale language
data collection, particularly for underrepresented communities
(Bender et al., 2021). In African contexts—where historical
data practices have often been extractive—explicit governance
frameworks are essential for ensuring responsible data steward-
ship. The WazobiaSpeech project builds on these principles
by integrating human-in-the-loop anonymization, and lifecycle
management into dataset design, positioning governance as a
core methodological component rather than a post-hoc consid-
eration. The WazobiaSpeech 2500 hours corpus uses demo-
graphic stratification, diverse speaking contexts, and rigorous
quality control. Table 1 compares WazobiaSpeech with exist-
ing speech datasets that include African languages.
3. Languages in the Corpus
3.1. Hausa
Hausa is a Chadic language of the Afroasiatic family spoken by
more than 80 million people across West and Central Africa. It
is characterized by a complex consonant system that includes
implosives, ejectives, and labialized or palatalized consonants,
as well as a rich vowel inventory with phonemic vowel length.
Hausa also employs a tonal system with high, low, and falling
tones, although tone is not represented in standard orthography.

4. Data Collection
4.1. Language Scope and Dialectal Considerations
For languages exhibiting substantial internal variation, a refer-
ence variety was adopted to ensure orthographic consistency
while preserving natural accentual and regional variation in
speech. For example, within the Hausa subset, the central di-
alect (Kananci) was used as the orthographic reference, while
speakers representing a wide range of regional accents were
included in the recordings. This approach balances linguistic
consistency with sociolinguistic realism and avoids collapsing
heterogeneous speech communities into a single homogeneous
variety.
4.2. Domains and Content Coverage
To ensure real-world applicability, data collection focused on
domains central to everyday life and public service delivery, in-
cludingagriculture,healthcare,business, andeveryday con-
versation. Speech data within these domains is particularly
important for applications such as agricultural extension ser-
vices, community health outreach, financial literacy tools, and
general-purpose conversational systems. A structured domain
taxonomy was developed for each domain to guide data collec-
tion and ensure semantic and pragmatic diversity. For instance,
the healthcare taxonomy included symptom description, diag-
nosis, treatment, vaccination, maternal care, and public health
messaging, while the agriculture taxonomy covered crop types,
planting cycles, pest control, climate variability, irrigation, and
market access. Similar taxonomies were developed for finan-
cial inclusion and everyday communication. These taxonomies
served as blueprints to avoid narrow or repetitive content and
to ensure broad thematic coverage across recording sessions.
Figure 1 illustrates the taxonomy of the health domain and the
relative prominence of its major thematic categories, while the
breakdown of domains in the dataset is presented in Figure 2f.
4.3. Speech Elicitation and Recording Protocol
We used a combination of multimodal prompts, including text-
based, image-based, and audiovisual stimuli. Each modality
was selected to encourage different types of linguistic pro-
duction. Text prompts were primarily used for controlled or
read speech in Hausa and Naij ´a, while visual and audiovisual
prompts were particularly effective in eliciting spontaneous and
semi-spontaneous responses. For spontaneous speech collec-
tion, participants were presented with prompts depicting realis-
tic scenarios—such as images of farming activities, short videos
simulating medical consultations, or visual representations of
mobile money transactions—and were asked to respond or nar-
rate freely in their own words without written scripts. This ap-
proach captures natural hesitations, reformulations, discourse
markers, and pragmatic phenomena that are typically absent
from read speech. For read speech, participants were provided
with carefully designed textual materials that were phonetically,
syntactically, and lexically rich. Source texts were either au-
thored directly in the target language or translated from En-
glish by professionally trained translators working in collabora-
tion with native speaker consultants. Particular care was taken
to ensure cultural appropriateness and to avoid literal transla-
tions that could introduce unnatural phrasing. Recordings were
conducted in quiet, naturalistic environments using commonly
available devices, including smartphones and portable micro-
phones. This variability in recording conditions was intentional
and reflects realistic deployment scenarios for speech technolo-

4.4. Speaker Recruitment and Metadata
All recordings were produced by native speakers of the target
languages. Speakers were recruited through community-based
networks and local institutions to ensure diversity across age,
gender, educational background, and geographic region. To
maximize speaker diversity, individual contributors were typ-
ically limited to approximately one hour of recorded speech
per language. In total, the dataset includes contributions from
2,865 unique speakers. For each recording, structured meta-
data was collected, including:age group: e.g., 15–29, 30–
45, 46–60, 61+;gender: self-identified;educational qualifica-
tion: e.g., no formal education, primary, secondary, tertiary.do-
main: agriculture, business, everyday conversation, and health;
modality: spontaneous, scripted. Figure 2d shows the distri-
bution of speaker’s ages across each language in the dataset.
Gender coverage in represented in Figure 2c while domain in-
formation is in Figure 2f.
4.5. Audio Quality Control
All recordings underwent a dual-layer quality assurance pro-
cess. Automatic checks verified technical criteria, including
signal-to-noise ratio (targeting approximately 40 dB) [28], sam-
pling rate (48 kHz) [29], and bit depth. In parallel, manual re-
view was conducted by trained native speakers who verified that
recordings contained authentic speech in the correct language,
were free from excessive background noise, and were represen-
tative of native speaker usage. Only recordings that passed both
automatic and manual checks were included in the final dataset.
4.6. Transcription Process
All spontaneous speech recordings were transcribed by native
speakers using a standardized set of transcription guidelines de-
veloped by professional linguists. These guidelines specify or-
thographic conventions, use of diacritics, treatment of disfluen-
cies, and annotation of code-switching. Disfluencies such as
hesitations and repetitions are marked using the placeholder tag
[um], unintelligible segments are marked as[?], and instances
of code-mixing are enclosed using the[cs]tag. Numbers and
special characters are spelled out in full to support downstream
speech processing tasks. Transcribers were instructed to tran-
scribe speech verbatim, preserving grammatical errors and in-
formal constructions as spoken. Each transcript was reviewed
by linguists with both native-speaker fluency and formal lin-
guistic training, enabling consistent handling of dialectal varia-
tion and subtle transcription errors.
4.7. Dataset Splits
To support fair evaluation and prevent speaker leakage,
the dataset was partitioned using a speaker-level stratified
split. Stratification was performed based on language, age
group, gender, and recording type. Speakers were uniquely
identified and assigned exclusively to one of four splits:
train(approximately 85%),development(approximately 5%),
development-test(approximately 5%), andtest(approxi-
mately 5%). For strata with insufficient representation, speak-
ers were assigned randomly according to predefined propor-
tions. After splitting, integrity checks verified that no speaker
appeared in more than one split, all clips were assigned, and de-
mographic balance was preserved. This procedure yields a re-
producible and balanced partitioning suitable for robust model
evaluation.

pipeline as an integral component of the WazobiaSpeech
Project. This pipeline combines automated statistical valida-
tion, algorithmic alignment checks, and expert linguistic review
