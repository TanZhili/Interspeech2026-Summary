Sub-band Cepstral Analysis of Speaker-Specific Information:  
A Case Study of Japanese Word /saN/ 
Shunichi Ishihara1, Frantz Clermont1, Can Zhang1,2 
1 Speech and Language Laboratory, Australian National University, Australia 
2 School of Literature, Languages and Linguistics, Australian National University, Australia 
shunichi.ishihara@anu.edu.au, dr.fclermont@gmail.com, can.zhang@anu.edu.au 
 
Abstract 
This study applies band-limited cepstral coefficients (BLCCs) 
to forensic voice comparison (FVC) to identify 
speaker-sensitive spectral regions for the Japanese fricative /s/, 
vowel /a/, and nasal /N/ produced by 306 male speakers. 
Derived from full-band cepstral coefficients via linear 
transformation, BLCCs allow flexible extraction of sub-band 
information without re-analysing the signal. Using 0.6-kHz 
sub-bands shifted in 0.2-kHz increments across 0–8 kHz, FVC 
experiments were run for each segment. The vowel /a/ and nasal 
/N/ outperformed the fricative /s/, and all segments showed 
weak speaker information in the 7 –8 kHz region. 
Speaker-sensitive sub-bands varied across segments and were 
not always predictable from their articulatory or acoustic 
characteristics. The findings also demonstrate the effectiveness 
of the BLCC method in accessing and a nalysing any local 
spectral region of interest.  
Index Terms: band-limited cepstral coefficient, forensic voice 
comparison, speaker sensitive sub-bands, fricative, and nasal 
1. Introduction 
Speaker-discriminative information is not distributed 
uniformly across the spectrum [1-3]. Although the precise 
patterns vary depending on the data and analytical methods 
used, studies based on running speech generally report that, for 
male speakers, the most informative regions tend to lie in the 
low (< 0.5–0.7 kHz), mid (~2–4 kHz), and mid-to-high (~3–
6 kHz) frequency ranges [4-8]. These regions are often linked 
to physiological characteristics and their acoustic 
manifestations, which differ according to the speech segment 
produced (e.g., vowels, nasals, fricatives). Source-related cues 
such as F0 and low-frequency harmonics contribute to the 
lower region; energy distribution and spectral shaping across 
vowels and sonorants dominate the mid region; and the 
mid-to-high region contains individuating cues for unvoiced 
fricatives and burst spectra. 
Most previous studies have focused on running speech, 
providing only an overall picture of speaker-sensitive spectral 
regions across broad phonemic contexts. In contrast, Clermont 
and Ishihara [9] examined Japanese vowels (/a, i, u, e, o/) 
produced in isolation and found that speaker -sensitive 
sub-bands do not necessarily align with formant regions [cf. 
10]. They also reported stronger speaker information below 
0.6 kHz and above approximately 2 kHz (at an 8-kHz sampling 
rate). However, it remains unclear where speaker -specific 
information is encoded for consonants, and to what extent. To 
address this gap, th e present study investigates 
speaker-sensitive sub-bands for the fricative /s/, vowel /a/, and 
nasal /N/ in the Japanese word /saN/ (“three”). Note that nasals 
and fricatives have been reported to carry useful speaker -
specific information [11-16]. In forensic voice comparison 
(FVC), understanding how speaker-specific information is 
distributed across the spectrum—and how this distribution 
varies across speech segments—is important for interpreting 
system outputs in a legal context. 
Previous sub-band studies have relied on filterbank-based 
analyses, where precise manipulation of filter characteristics 
can be difficult. The present study instead employs the 
band-limited cepstral coefficient (BLCC) method [9, 17], which 
allows flexible and precise extraction of sub-band information 
through a linear transformation of full -band cepstral 
coefficients (CCs), eliminating the need to re -analyse the 
original signal. A series of likelihood-ratio (LR)-based FVC 
experiments were conducted with the individual BLCC vectors. 
2. Band limited cepstral coefficients 
Standard CCs provide a compact representation of the full 
speech spectrum, but their global nature limits access to specific 
frequency regions. The BLCC method addresses this by 
deriving sub-band information directly from full-band CCs. As 
shown in Figure 1, the method involves: (1) short-time spectral 
analysis of the signal, (2) cepstral extraction, and (3) a linear 
transformation that maps the full -band cepstrum 𝑐 to a 
sub-band cepstrum 𝑐′ for any desired frequency range [𝜔!,𝜔"]. 
This transformation enables efficient sub-band analysis without 
re-processing the signal. Although various spectral analyses 
and frequency scales can be used in Step (1), this study adopts 
linear-frequency CCs (LFCCs) to ensure equal weighting 
across all sub-bands. For mathematical details, see [9, 17].

3. Experimental procedure  
3.1. Speech samples and parametrisation 
The speech materials were sourced from Japan’s National 
Research Institute of Police Science database [18], which 
contains microphone recordings from 306 adult-male native 
speakers of Japanese recruited across Japan (mean age: 39.9 
years, SD: 15.5). The target word /saN/ was recorded in citation 
form, with each speaker producing two tokens in each of two 
sessions separated by 3–5 months. The original 44.1-kHz 
recordings were down-sampled to 16 kHz, providing an 
analysis range of 0–8 kHz. Although this range is wider than 
that typically encountered in FVC —where telephone and 
mobile-phone channels limit usable frequencies—the broader 
range is appropriate for the present scientific investigation. In 
particular, access to higher-frequency components is necessary 
for analysing the fricative /s/, which exhibits its strongest and 
most structured energy in this region [19, 20]. 
A filter-bank analysis was then applied to each segment 
using 25 triangular filters spaced uniformly on the linear 
frequency scale, with 50% overlap between adjacent filters. The 
signal was analysed using 25-ms frames with a 5-ms shift. For 
each frame, 14 LFCCs were extracted, as illustrated in Step 2) 
of Figure 1. To obtain a stable representation of each segment, 
the LFCCs from the three central frames were averaged and 
retained as the full-band LFCC vector. 
The target word is a two-mora item whose final segment /N/ 
functions as a moraic nasal. When followed by another 
segment, /N/ assimilates to the place of articulation of the 
succeeding sound, while in utterance-final position it is often 
described as uvular (e.g., [N]) [21, 22]. However, instrumental 
studies show considerable variability in its production. Most 
commonly, speakers produce an oral closure influenced by the 
preceding vowel [23], although speaker-dependent realisations 
vary widely across individual speakers, ranging from alveolar 
to uvular. 
The full 0–8 kHz frequency range was examined using a 
0.6-kHz sub-band shifted in 0.2-kHz increments. For each 
sub-band, a BLCC vector was generated by applying a linear 
transformation to the full-band LFCCs (see Step 3) of Figure 
1), yielding 38 BLCC vectors in total. The value of 𝑀𝑊 was 
1.05 and was rounded to 1, resulting in each BLCC vector 
containing two coefficients, including the 0th-order term. 
3.2. Experimental descriptions 
The 306 male speakers were randomly divided into three 
batches of 102. These batches were systematically rotated so 
that, in each cycle, one batch served as the test set, another as 
the background set, and the third as the calibration set, yielding 
six folds (see Table 1). All reported metrics are averaged across 
these six experiments.

Two sets of FVC experiments (Experiments 1 and 2) were 
conducted on a per-segment basis using BLCCs derived for 
each of the 38 sub-bands. For every sub-band, LRs were 
estimated with a multivariate kernel density model [24] and 
calibrated via logistic regression [25]. System performance was 
quantified using the log-LR cost 𝐶##$, its decompositions, and 
the 𝐸𝐸𝑅 (as reference), all computed separately for each 
sub-band. Each rotation of the test set yielded 102 same-speaker 
and 10,302 different-speaker comparisons. 
Experiment 1 examined how speaker -discriminative 
information is distributed across the spectrum for each segment 
by evaluating FVC performance on a sub-band-by-sub-band 
basis. Sub-bands containing stronger speaker information were 
expected to produce lower 𝐶##$values. Experiment 2 then 
combined the LRs derived from these individual sub-bands, 
systematically fusing them in different combinations and 
comparing the resulting systems with a full -band LFCC 
baseline. This allowed identification of the most informative 
sub-band groupings.

4.1. Experiment 1 
As the outcome of Experiment 1, the 𝐶##$ value for each 
sub-band is plotted against its centre frequency in Figure 2, 
shown separately for the three segments. Horizontal dashed 
lines represent each segment’s mean 𝐶##$. Note that lower 𝐶##$ 
values indicate better FVC performance, reflecting stronger 
speaker-discriminative information. 
All sub-band regions yield 𝐶##$ values below 1, indicating 
that each sub-band carries useful speaker -discriminative 
information. However, the variation in 𝐶##$ values shows that 
the speaker-discriminative information is unevenly distributed 
across the spectrum, consistent with observations reported in 
prior studies [1-8]. 
Another pattern observable across the three segments is an 
upward trend in 𝐶##$ values as the sub-band frequencies 
approach the upper end of the spectrum (8 kHz), although the 
onset of this trend differs by segment. In all cases, the sub-bands 
within the final 1 kHz show relatively high 𝐶##$ values 
compared with the rest of the spectrum . In the following 
sub-sections, the results for each segment are examined 
separately.  
4.1.1. Segment /s/ 
Fricatives have been reported to carry good 
speaker-discriminative information and have been used in FVC 
studies [11-15]. Among the three segments examined, 
however, /s/ exhibits the least overall speaker -specific 
information (i.e., higher 𝐶##$ values), as shown in Figure 2a. A 
pronounced dip in the 𝐶##$ trajectory appears around 1–2 kHz. 
Beyond approximately 2.5 kHz, the 𝐶##$values remain

relatively stable before rising again toward 8 kHz, with two 
smaller dips near 4 kHz and 6.5 kHz. 
The fricative /s/ typically exhibits its strongest spectral 
energy in the 4–8 kHz region [26], a key cue for its perceptual 
identification [27]. This high -frequency energy reflects 
front-cavity resonances and properties of the supraglottal 
constriction, both of which vary across speakers   [20]. As 
shown in Figure 2a, this region provides useful 
speaker-discriminative information, particularly around 
6.5 kHz. The strong speaker-specific information observed 
around 1–2 kHz is less expected. Keith and Kinoshita  [28] 
reported similarly robust cues near both 2 kHz and 6.5 kHz for 
English /s/, consistent with the present findings for Japanese /s/, 
whereas Hyon et al. [29] observed weak information near 2 kHz 
for Korean /s/. These cross-linguistic differences suggest that 
the location and strength of speaker-sensitive sub-bands for /s/ 
may depend on language-specific factors or coarticulatory 
influences from the following /a/. 
4.1.2. Segment /a/ 
The segment /a/ shows substantial fluctuations in 𝐶##$ across 
the spectrum (Figure 2b). The strongest speaker-discriminative 
information appears around 5–6 kHz, where the lowest	𝐶##$ 
value (0.76044 for the 5.2–5.9 kHz sub-band) is observed. 
Although this sub-band provides the most informative 
single-band cue for /a/, its articulatory basis remains unclear. 
The 5–6 kHz range likely corresponds to very high formant 
regions (e.g., F5 and F6), which are difficult to estimate directly

4.1.3. Segment /N/ 
Although the 𝐶##$ trajectory shows some fluctuations, 
speaker-discriminative information for /N/ is generally more 
concentrated in the lower half of the spectrum (< 4 kHz) than 
in the higher frequencies (Figure 2c). Within this 
lower-frequency region, the most prominent 𝐶##$ minimum 
occurs around 0.5–1 kHz. This range corresponds to the 
spectral concentration of nasal murmurs and lies close to the 
typical first nasal resonance for adult males (approximately 
0.3–0.5 kHz) [30]. Acoustic energy in this region reflects 
speaker-dependent characteristics of the nasal –pharyngeal 
tract—such as tract length, coupling, and damping—which are 
relatively stable within a given speaker [30]. 
Nasals are also characterised by anti-resonances, which 
produce pronounced reductions in spectral energy, particularly 
in the 1.5–2 kHz region [30]. The presence and frequency 
locations of these anti-formants vary across speakers and 
influence the overall spectral balance and tilt. A further local 
minimum appears around 3.5 kHz, likely reflecting 
speaker-specific variability in the place of articulation of 
utterance-final /N/  [23]. Overall, the distribution of 
speaker-sensitive sub-bands for /N/ aligns well with its 
articulatory characteristics. 
4.2. Experiment 2 
Beyond pinpointing the locations of speaker -sensitive 
sub-bands and their magnitudes in Experiment 1, it is also 
important to assess their relative contribution to system 
performance compared with the full -band condition. 
Experiment 2 addressed this by systematically fusing the LRs 
derived from individual sub-bands, combining them in groups 
of two up to four. This approach allows for a more detailed 
examination of how speaker-discriminative information is 
distributed across the spectrum for each segment, and how FVC 
performance changes as additional sub-bands are incorporated.

As an example, the three best - and worst-performing 
sub-band combinations are highlighted in pink and grey, 
respectively, in Figure 2 for each segment. Across all segments, 
combinations that include the highest-frequency sub-bands 
(shown in grey) consistently yield the poorest performance.

By contrast, the sub-bands that perform best in combination 
are spaced apart and generally coincide with the local minima 
in 𝐶##$. For /s/, these three high-performing sub-bands are 
widely distributed across the spectrum (Figure 2a), indicating 
that speaker-specific information for this fricative is encoded 
broadly rather than concentrated in one region. For /a/, the first 
two high-performing sub-bands span the F2 and F3 regions 
(Figure 2b), aligning with known speaker-sensitive frequency 
ranges for this vowel. For /N/, the three best -performing 
sub-bands are all located below 4 kHz (Figure 2c), consistent 
with the greater importance of lower-frequency energy for nasal 
acoustics. 
5. Conclusion and future work  
Consistently with previous studies [1-8], the present work 
confirms that speaker-specific information is unevenly 
distributed across the spectral range. Overall, the vowel /a/ and 
the nasal /N/ provide stronger speaker-discriminative cues than 
the fricative /s/. It further shows that the locations and relative 
importance of speaker-sensitive sub-bands differ across the 
speech segments analysed. For example, the main 
speaker-sensitive sub-bands occur around 1–2 kHz for /s/, 5–
6 kHz for /a/, and 1 kHz for /N/, with additional local minima 
in each segment. Although we attempted to relate these patterns 
to the articulatory and acoustic properties of each segment, the 
correspondence was not always clear, and phonological or 
language-specific factors may also contribute. 
This study is based exclusively on male speech samples and 
a limited set of speech segments. While the focus on male 
speakers is practically motivated —given their greater 
involvement in criminal investigations—future research should 
include a broader demographic range and a wider variety of 
speech materials. In addition, exploring other spectral analyses 
(e.g., linear prediction) and frequency scales (e.g., Mel) may 
offer further insights into the findings.  
BLCCs retain the advantages of traditional cepstral 
representations while enabling fine-grained, region-specific 
spectral analysis. This flexibility allows forensic practitioners 
to direct their attention to the sub-bands most relevant to a given 
case and to present FVC results to the trier of fact in a clearer 
and potentially more interpretable manner.
