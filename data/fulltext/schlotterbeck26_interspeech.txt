Abstract
Continued pre-training (CPT) of self-supervised learning (SSL)
models uses domain-specific unlabeled audio to improve down-
stream performance. However, existing work focuses on con-
trastive architectures, leaving discrete-unit models underex-
plored, and rarely examines catastrophic forgetting in multi-
task setups. To bridge this gap, we study CPT across three
SSL architectures on subsets of the MLCommons Unsuper-
vised People’s Speech dataset across three tasks. Re-computing
pseudo-labels on target data and resuming masked prediction
improves content tasks in label-scarce domains, though gains
are high-variance across seeds and data scale. In contrast, com-
paring discrete-unit and contrastive models reveals a systematic
trade-off: CPT degrades speaker-discriminative information in
discrete-unit models, an effect contrastive models do not ex-
hibit. These findings highlight undocumented task specializa-
tion patterns under CPT, serving as a reference for future do-
main adaptation.

1. Introduction
Self-supervised learning (SSL) is the dominant paradigm for
learning general-purpose speech representations. Modern SSL
speech models fall into two broad families:contrastivemod-
els, such as wav2vec 2.0 [1], which distinguish true quan-
tized targets from distractors; anddiscrete-unitmodels, such
as HuBERT [2] and WavLM [3], which predict offline k-means
pseudo-labels at masked positions. Both achieve strong down-
stream performance when fine-tuned with labeled data [4].
Resuming the self-supervised objective on new target-
domain data, known as Continued Pre-Training (CPT), im-
proves representations for domain-shifted audio [5]. However,
existing CPT studies focus predominantly on contrastive archi-
tectures, where resuming the loss suffices since quantizers up-
date on-the-fly. For discrete-unit models, CPT is less explored
because the original pseudo-labels were computed on source-
domain data and may not align with the target distribution. A
natural question arises:can one simply recompute k-means la-
bels on the target data and resume masked prediction training?
We investigate this through a controlled empirical study on
the MLCommons Unsupervised People’s Speech (UPS) Chal-
lenge [6] across three architectures: HuBERT (discrete-unit),
WavLM (discrete-unit with denoising), and OmniASR (con-
trastive, wav2vec 2.0 based). For the discrete-unit models, k-
means pseudo-labels were recomputed on the target audio and
a fresh projection head attached; for the contrastive model, its
native objective was resumed. 1 To make CPT feasible under
limited compute, we propose a data curation pipeline that con-
structs 100 h and 500 h subsets by scoring tar archives accord-
ing to V oice Activity Detection (V AD) density and language
scarcity, allowing all experiments to run on a single NVIDIA
A100 GPU within the challenge time frame.
Results show that CPT can improve content-related met-
rics such as character error rate (CER), though these gains are
high-variance across seeds and data scale. More systemati-
cally, CPT reveals a trade-off: speaker diarization performance
degrades substantially across discrete-unit models, suggesting
CPT shifts representations toward linguistic content at the ex-
pense of speaker-discriminative information. A pseudo-label
ablation further shows that label nature is decisive for discrete-
unit CPT: embedding-based labels retain content information,
while MFCC-based labels from the original first iteration tend
to preserve speaker identity instead.
Our contributions are:
• A controlled comparison of CPT across discrete-unit and
contrastive SSL paradigms on shared data and evaluation,
showing the strategy extends to discrete-unit models by re-
computing k-means pseudo-labels.
• Evidence that pseudo-label nature is critical for discrete-unit
CPT: embedding-based labels improve ASR (with notable
run-to-run variance), while MFCC-based labels degrade con-
tent tasks but mostly preserve speaker identification.
• A systematic content–speaker trade-off: content gains (CER,
language ID) are high-variance across seeds and subsets,
while speaker diarization degrades consistently in discrete-
unit models—not previously documented in a controlled
cross-paradigm CPT comparison.
• A lightweight, metadata-driven curation pipeline that selects
language-balanced subsets from the million-hour UPS corpus
without downloading the full dataset.
2. Related Work
2.1. Self-supervised speech models
Baevski et al. introduced wav2vec 2.0 [1], a contrastive frame-
work that quantizes latent representations and trains a Trans-
former encoder to distinguish true quantized targets from dis-
tractors at masked positions, matching prior state of the art
with 100 times less labeled data. SSL has since become the
dominant method to learn audio representations fromunla-
beleddata. For multilingual speech, the Massively Multilingual
Speech (MMS) [7] and Omnilingual ASR [8] projects scaled

Beyond contrastive objectives, HuBERT replaced the con-
trastive loss with an offline clustering step: MFCCs or
intermediate-layer embeddings are clustered via k-means, and
the model predicts the cluster assignment of masked frames via
cross-entropy. Iteratively refining pseudo-labels across rounds
proved stable and strong on the SUPERB benchmark [4].
WavLM extended HuBERT with a denoising objective: utter-
ances are mixed with overlapping speech at random SNR while
the model still predicts the clean pseudo-labels, yielding more
robust representations in noisy, multi-speaker conditions.
2.2. Continued pre-training for domain adaptation
Resuming the self-supervised objective to adapt transformer-
based models to new, often low-resource domains has been
widely studied. In NLP, Gururangan et al. [9] showed that ad-
ditional in-domain pre-training of BERT [10] consistently im-
proves downstream performance, especially in specialized do-
mains. In speech, Hsu et al. [11] found that including target-
domain data during wav2vec 2.0 pre-training substantially im-
proves ASR under mismatched conditions, highlighting the im-
pact of pre-training data distribution.
More recent work applies CPT to contrastive models with
techniques to limit catastrophic forgetting. Kessler et al. [12]
anchor divergence from the original wav2vec 2.0 via knowl-
edge distillation, and Lee et al. proposed CTRL [13], transfer-
ring to new domains with adapter layers while freezing original
weights—showing CPT is feasible for contrastive models with
minimal degradation. For discrete-unit models, CPT is harder
since pseudo-labels may need re-estimation; Ng et al. [14] pro-
posed De’HuBERT to disentangle noise and improve robust-
ness. Few studies, however, systematically examine content–
speaker trade-offs during CPT across paradigms. We fill this
gap, showing that recomputing k-means labels and resuming
masked prediction is viable for discrete-unit models, and pro-
viding a controlled cross-paradigm analysis that reveals previ-
ously undocumented trade-offs.
3. Methodology
3.1. Data curation
The Unsupervised People’s Speech (UPS) dataset [6] comprises
approximately one million hours of multilingual audio across
90+ languages, distributed over 10,000+ shards and 40TB+.
Given our compute constraints (a single A100 GPU), subsets
of 100 h and 500 h were curated using a two-stage scoring
pipeline that prioritizes high-quality, language-diverse audio
without downloading the full corpus.
V AD density filtering.First, a V AD density score was
computed for each shard, defined as the ratio of total speech
duration to total audio duration within it. Archives with density
below 70% were discarded, eliminating shards dominated by
silence, music, or noise.
Language-scarcity scoring.From the provided language
identification metadata, each language was assigned a scarcity
score inversely proportional to the logarithm of their representa-
tion (total time) in the dataset: languages with>1000 h receive
1 point, 100–1000 h receive 2, 10–100 h receive 3, and<10 h
receive 4 points. Each shard was then scored as:

handled batch randomization to avoid intra-batch correlation.
Pseudo-label assignment.Following HuBERT, discrete
pseudo-labels were assigned by clustering acoustic features.
The 9th layer HuBERT base embedding was extracted for each
10-second chunk, producing499frames per chunk. An online
MiniBatchKMeans [15] with500clusters was fitted incremen-
tally as tars were streamed, using Welford’s algorithm [16] for
running mean and variance normalization. Each frame was then
assigned its nearest cluster centroid as a pseudo-label.
Pseudo-label ablation.The original HuBERT work uses
39-dimensional MFCC features with 100 clusters for its first
training iteration, then refines the labels in subsequent iterations
by clustering intermediate Transformer representations. Since
the checkpoints were already pre-trained, training started di-
rectly from intermediate-layer representations; to test the effect
of pseudo-label quality in the CPT setting, a second set of labels
was produced by reproducing the MFCC procedure previously
described. The effects of usingembedding-basedlabels against
MFCC-basedones on downstream performance were compared
(Section 4.3).
3.2. CPT setup
Continued pre-training was performed on three SSL architec-
tures, following each model’s original pre-training recipe as
closely as possible while sharing the same curated data subsets.
Shared configuration.All models used span-based mask-
ing: 8% of encoder time-steps were sampled as span start points
and expanded to spans of length 10, masking approximately
57% of frames in practice. AdamW optimization was used with
linear warmup over 1500 steps, gradient clipping at1.0, a learn-
ing rate of5×10 −5, and adjusted gradient accumulation and
batch size to maximize GPU memory utilization while keeping
a minimum effective batch size of 48. Each model ran for 5
epochs over their respective curated subset. Checkpoints were
saved at the end of each epoch for downstream evaluation.

facebook/hubert-base-ls960checkpoint and at-
tached a linear projection head mapping the 768-dimensional

4. Results
All models were evaluated on the UPS Challenge held-out test
set, which measures representation quality through three down-
stream tasks spanning content, language, and speaker informa-
tion: language identification (Macro-F1), speaker diarization
(Adjusted Rand Index, ARI), and automatic speech recognition
(Character Error Rate, CER). This multi-task evaluation enables
us to examine whether CPT uniformly improves representations
or introduces task-specific trade-offs. For each base architec-
ture, the off-the-shelf checkpoint was compared against CPT
checkpoints at each epoch for the 100 h subset. WavLM-large
was additionally evaluated on the 500 h curated subset. Ex-
cept for HuBERT-base, whose figures are averaged over three
seeds2, all reported results are single runs, consistent with the
resource-constrained challenge setting; the speaker-information
degradation described below is observed across all four model
configurations.

feature-type choice above.

5. Discussion & Conclusion
The central finding of this work is that CPT on curated multi-
lingual data shifts representations toward linguistic content at
a consistent cost: a sharp degradation in speaker diarization
performance for discrete-unit models. While content-related
metrics often improve, we find these gains to be high-variance
across seeds and data scale, whereas the speaker degradation is
systematic. This pattern suggests that CPT does not uniformly
enhance all representational aspects but ratherspecializesthe
model toward linguistic content. The magnitude of this trade-
off also depends on the model’s pre-training history; HuBERT-
base, facing the largest domain shift (English to multilingual),
yielded the largest gains, whereas OmniASR, already multilin-
gual, benefited primarily from the shift to spontaneous speech.
Interestingly, WavLM models showed the most catastrophic for-
getting; we hypothesize this could stem from the lesser domain-
shift and the sensitivity of their denoising objective.
This study has several limitations worth noting. The
resource-constrained setup implied limited training schedules
and batch sizes compared to usual pre-training. While the pro-
posed data curation pipeline allowed for efficient experimenta-
tion, scaling these experiments to the full dataset would quantify
the specific impact of curation versus simply adding more data.
These limitations nonetheless open clear directions for future
work. Addressing the observed degradation in speaker tasks
is a critical next step; future research might explore adapter-
based methods [18] or multi-task learning to explicitly pre-
serve speaker information alongside linguistic content. Expand-
ing evaluation to broader benchmarks like ML-SUPERB [19]
would also provide a more comprehensive view of these shifts.
For practitioners, the implications are two-fold. First, CPT
can aid content-focused domain adaptation, but its gains are
variable across runs and it reliably sacrifices speaker informa-
tion, so it may require complementary strategies if speaker iden-
tity is relevant. Second, and perhaps most critically for discrete-
unit models, the nature of pseudo-labels is decisive. As shown
in the ablation study, MFCC-based labels, while sufficient for
