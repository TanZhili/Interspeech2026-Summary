Abstract
Public voice agents often underperform for accented English,
reducing usability and trust.LocalMATEis an end-to-end
accent-adaptive airport conversational agent that (i) estimates a
user’s accent with calibrated confidence, (ii) retrieves grounded
answers, and (iii) returns accent-conditioned speech with a syn-
chronized talking-face video, using a confidence-gated fallback
when uncertain. Our accent estimator (WavLM-Base-Plus with
layer-weighted statistics pooling) reaches 85% speaker-disjoint
accuracy on L2-ARCTIC (Indian/Korean/Spanish) and 92.3%
on out-of-domain Korean-accentedSpeech Accent Archive. In a
within-participant user study (N=20), correct accent adaptation
significantly improved confidence and trust (p <0.05) while
reducing user repair attempts. Conversely, incorrect adaptation
reduced trust and increased friction. A confidence-gated fall-
back policy mitigated these effects and restored user confidence.
Index Terms: Multimodal conversational agents, Accent clas-
sification, Accent-adaptive speech synthesis, Trust and user per-
ception
1. Introduction
Spoken conversational agents are increasingly deployed in
public-facing settings, such as kiosks, help desks, and informa-
tion robots, where users expect smooth, low-effort interaction.
In practice, user speech is acoustically diverse, and those with
accented English may face issues if systems are optimized for
a narrow range of speaking styles. Prior HCI work shows that
multilingual users’ needs are often under-considered in voice-
agent interaction and that social context can shape whether
users feel comfortable speaking in a particular language or ac-
cent to an agent [1, 2]. These concerns are intensified by ev-
idence that automatic speech recognition (ASR) errors are not
evenly distributed across demographic speech varieties [3, 4].
Such failures can go beyond usability costs: voice assistant
errors may be experienced as microaggressive in cross-race
interactions and can negatively affect users’ mood and self-
perception [5]. V oice assistant failures also influence long-term
dependence, with different failure types degrading user trust in
distinct ways [6]. Together, these observations motivate designs
that better accommodate accent and linguistic diversity.
Accent and dialect cues influence first impressions and per-
ceived identity, competence, and warmth, and can affect user
trust in human–agent interaction [7, 8]. Recent work sug-
gests that limited accent support in synthetic voice services
can contribute to users feeling excluded, motivating inclusive
accent-adaptive speech technologies [9]. At the same time, in-

Despite this progress, we still lack a systematic understand-

responses may be delivered as a realistic talking-face video. In
particular, it remains unclear how users perceive and prefer (i)

to match a user’s accent, and whether these judgments change
when the same audio is paired with an audiovisual representa-
tion.
In this paper, we study the perceptual impact of accent
adaptive responses in a multi-modal FAQ dialogue setting. Ini-
tially, we ran a pilot study that isolates presentation effects by
comparing four conditions: (1)Sourcehuman-recorded audio
(S-audio), (2)Modifiedsimulated audio designed to match ac-
cent (M-audio), (3) talking-face video withS-audio(S-video),
and (4) talking-face video withM-audio(M-video). Using
Likert-scale ratings, we measured perceived naturalness, ease
of understanding, accent/tone match, audiovisual synchroniza-
tion, and preference.
In addition to this pilot, we evaluated an end-to-end accent
adaptive conversational system,LocalMATE, with a user study
designed to answer three research questions:
RQ1: Does correct accent adaptation improve trust and
confidence?
RQ2: What is the cost of incorrect accent adaptation?
RQ3: Can confidence-gated fallback – fallback triggered
when accent-detection confidence is low – mitigate mis-
match harm?

generation improves user experience in conversational systems
and how incorrect accent adaptation affects trust. We further
evaluate whether a confidence-gated fallback strategy can miti-
gate the negative impact of accent misclassification.

2. Related Work
We review previous work on (1) Accent and social perception in
voice interaction, (2) Bias, harms, and repair in voice assistants,
and (3) Accent bias in synthetic voice services and multi-modal
representation.
2.1. Accent and social perception in voice interaction
Previous research demonstrates that in human–agent interac-
tion, voice design choices heavily influence social judgments
and user experience [7]. Furthermore, some studies note that di-
alect and accent cues can shape perceived competence, warmth,
and authenticity, motivating interest in agent speech that is di-
alect and accent-aligned [8]. Earlier studies on VUI(V oice User
Interface) also reported that English regional accents affect user
acceptance and preference, highlighting that accent is a promi-
nent factor in voice interfaces [13]. Despite the identified im-
portance of accent aligned responses in a human-centric inter-
action system, few studies have thoroughly implemented these
systems to evaluate their impact on user experience.
2.2. Bias, harms, and repair in voice assistants
ASR systems differ in their demographic performance, where
some variants of demographic speech show higher error rates.
[3]. These differences can translate into unequal user experi-
ences and affect downstream tasks that need correct speech-to-
text translation for aligned responses [14]. Wenzel and Kauf-
man showed that ASR failures in cross-race voice interactions
can be experienced as microaggressive, affecting users’ psycho-
logical responses [5]. Another study shows that identical (Au-
tomatic Speech Recognition) ASR outputs are perceived dif-
ferently depending on the speaker’s accent: listeners attribute
more blame to non-native speakers and judge them as less cred-
ible, even when transcription quality is constant [15]. This high-
lights that equal model performance does not ensure equal user
experience. V oice assistant failures degrade trust and alter fu-
ture reliance, with mixed-methods work indicating that failure
type and context shape trust dynamics [6]. Recent research sug-
gests harm reduction strategies based on communication repair
for multicultural voice interactions, highlighting designs that
help users recover from recognition errors and mitigate neg-
ative downstream impacts [2]. However, comparatively little
work has investigated how accent conditioned response strate-
gies such as, producing an incorrect accent versus falling back
to a neutral accent under uncertainty, may affect user trust and
confidence in end-to-end spoken systems.
2.3. Accent bias in synthetic voice services and multi-modal
representation
Although the quality of synthesis is high, synthetic voice ser-
vices can neglect certain accents and contribute to digital ex-
clusion [9]. In parallel, perceptual realism and embodiment in-
fluence user judgments: prosody and embodiment have been
shown to affect perceived naturalness and task outcomes in
agent interactions [11], while highly human-like outputs can
produce unintended social effects consistent with “unnatural”
responses [10]. These findings motivate controlled evaluations
of accent-adaptive audiovisual presentations.
3. System
We presentLocalMATE, an accent-adaptive dialog system for
general airport information. Given a user input utterance, the
system (i) estimates the user’s English accent using our fine-
tuned accent classifier and caches it to maintain a consistent
accent state across subsequent conversation, (ii) transcribes the
utterance using an ASR model, (iii) retrieves a response from
a fixed set of FAQs via embedding-based semantic retrieval,
(iv) generates a spoken response conditioned on the user’s ac-
cent, and (v) renders a talking-face video synchronized to the
response audio. Figure 2 summarizes the end-to-end pipeline.
Unlike prior accent-adaptive systems that always apply accent
conditioning, our approach introduces a confidence-gated per-
sonalization policy, where accent adaptation is applied only
when the classifier confidence exceeds a threshold.
3.1. Accent estimation and caching
The system records the user’s speech through a microphone and
predicts the speaker’s English accent using the trained classifier.
The classifier is trained on three accents: Spanish-accented En-
glish, Korean-accented English, and Indian-accented English.
The model outputs an accent label and confidence score, and
caches the result for ten minutes to stabilize multi-turn interac-
tion. The cached accent state is used for speech generation and
triggers a safe fallback when the accent estimate is uncertain
(Section 3.5).

3.2. Automatic speech recognition
We transcribe the input audio with the Whisper (small) model
[16]. A low-latency ASR model is used to support task-oriented
user queries and reduce processing time. The transcript and
metadata (timestamp, predicted accent, confidence) are passed
to the retrieval module.
3.3. FAQ-based response retrieval
The system retrieves responses to user queries from a curated
FAQ set to eliminate the risk of unverified information. The
ASR transcript is converted into a GPT-based text embedding
[17] and compared with the precomputed embeddings of all
FAQ questions. The FAQ entry with the highest cosine similar-
ity is selected, and its paired answer is returned as a response. If
retrieval confidence is low (e.g., the best similarity score is be-
low a threshold), the system asks a clarification question instead
of the wrong answer.
3.4. Accent-adaptive speech synthesis
The retrieved response text is converted to speech using VEVO
TTS model [18], which supports reference-conditioned voice
generation and enables accent-adaptive outputs from accented
reference audio for each target accent (Indian, Korean, Span-
ish). This process uses (i) a session-level cached accent label
from the accent estimator and (ii) reference-speech style repre-
sentation to transfer accent cues and prosody while keeping the
response content fixed.
We selected VEVO after preliminary trials with several re-
cent reference conditioned TTS systems, including XTTS [19],
F5-TTS [20], CosyV oice 2 [21], Vall-E [22], and MaskGCT
[23]. Although these models generate fluent prompted speech,
they struggle to maintain speaker identity and controlled ac-
cent transfer. In contrast, VEVO consistently generates de-
sired accent features from the reference audio without damag-
ing speaker timbre, hence, it was chosen asLocalMATETTS
backend.
We use a female-presenting voice for all responses, align-
ing with common design choices in widely deployed voice as-
sistants. We do not explore gender-based personalization; in-
stead, we focus on accent-adaptive speech output. This main-
tains a consistent system identity while adapting accent-related
cues that may influence perceived understanding and trust.
3.5. Safe fallback for uncertain adaptation
Incorrect accent adaptation can reduce user confidence. To
avoid this, we maintain a gating system based on the accent
classifier’s confidence. When confidence falls below a thresh-
oldτ, the system defaults to a neutral US accented TTS output
while preserving the response text. This approach ensures that
accent adaptation is applied only when the estimated confidence
is high, thus maintaining user trust.
3.6. Talking-face rendering
For audio-visual dialogue, the system generates a lip-
synchronized talking-face video using SadTalker [24]. It takes
the accented response audio and a portrait image that defines
the systems visual identity as input and generates a synchro-
nized talking-head video as the final response. The avatar is
a photorealistic female avatar that is fixed to control the inter-
face. This avoids demographic inference from speech and re-
duces appearance-related bias when evaluating accent-adaptive
speech output.
4. Experimental Setup
4.1. Accent Classifier Setup
Various off-the-shelf speaker embedding models (e.g., ECAPA-
TDNN classifiers, WavLM) can perform well on the datasets
they are trained on, but their accuracy does not necessar-
ily transfer to our target setting with Korean and Span-
ish accented English. Recent evidence in [25] shows that
self-supervised speech encoders (e.g., Wav2Vec2/XLSR) cap-
ture accent-relevant phonetic variation more effectively than
ECAPA-style embeddings and generalize better across ac-
cents when paired with simple utterance-level aggregation and
lightweight augmentation. Additionally, prior studies show that
accented TTS models can be finetuned with a limited amount
of accented speech data (General American English, Irish En-
glish, and British English) [26]. Hence, we adopt an SSL-based
approach and fine-tune a WavLM encoder with statistics pool-
ing (mean+std) on a speaker-disjoint split of L2-ARCTIC for
our three target accents (Indian, Korean, Spanish). This task-
specific fine-tuning provides robust preprocessing for accent-
adaptive response generation.

L2-ARCTIC [27] accented English speech, restricted to three
accents (INDIAN, KOREAN, SPANISH). To evaluate model
generalization to unseen speakers, we use aspeaker-disjoint

Input processing.All audio files are converted to mono and
resampled to 16 kHz. For WavLM experiments, inputs are stan-
dardized to fixed-length 7.0 s segments (112,000 samples) via
truncation or zero-padding. During training, we use random
cropping for utterances longer than 7.0 s; during dev/test we
use center cropping to reduce evaluation variance.
Model architecture.We fine-tuneWavLM-Base-Plus[28]
for 3-way accent classification. We output all hidden states and
learn a softmax-normalized weighted sum over 13 states (em-
bedding + 12 transformer layers). The resulting frame-level se-
quence is aggregated using statistics pooling (mean and stan-
dard deviation over time), producing a 1536-d utterance rep-
resentation. A 2-layer MLP classifier (1536→256→3) with
ReLU and dropout (p=0.5) predicts the accent label. The
WavLM convolutional feature extractor is frozen throughout
training.
Training.We optimize with AdamW using discriminative
learning rates:3×10 −5 for the classification head and layer-
weight parameters, and5×10 −6 for any unfrozen encoder pa-
rameters, with weight decay 0.02. We train for up to 50 epochs
with batch size 4 and gradient accumulation of 2 steps (effective
batch size 8), gradient clipping at 1.0, and label smoothing 0.05.
To stabilize fine-tuning in a small, speaker-disjoint setting, we
adopted gradual unfreezing [29], updating higher transformer
layers before lower layers to reduce catastrophic forgetting.
In our implementation, the WavLM encoder was ini-
tially frozen while training the classification head and layer-
aggregation weights to establish a stable decision boundary. We
then unfroze the last transformer layer at epoch 3 (near the
end of the 4-epoch augmentation warm-up) to introduce lim-
ited encoder adaptation under mild perturbations. After train-
ing stabilized under stronger augmentation, we unfroze the last
two transformer layers at epoch 6 (including the encoder layer
normalization), increasing adaptation capacity while balancing
generalization against overfitting. We select the best checkpoint
based on development-set macro-F1 and apply early stopping
with a patience of 6 epochs (minimum improvement10 −4).
On-the-fly augmentation.Augmentations [30, 31] are applied
during training only. We use stochastic gain perturbation (up
to±3dB), time shifting (up to 60 ms), additive Gaussian noise
(std up to 0.003), and random bandpass equalization. Augmen-
tation probability and strength follow a warmup+ramp schedule
(warmup 4 epochs, ramp 10 epochs; probability 0.10→0.50 and
strength 0.30→0.90).
Metrics.We report macro-F1 (primary) and accuracy on de-
velopment and held-out test sets. Macro-F1 weights classes
equally and is appropriate for speaker-disjoint evaluation where
per-speaker variability can be large.
Compute and runtime.Experiments are run on a NVIDIA
TITAN RTX GPUs (16 GB VRAM), using single-GPU train-
ing. For the WavLM setup, training time is approximately 291 s
per epoch (∼4.85 min/epoch), and the representative run shown
converges with early stopping at 15 epochs (∼73 min).
4.2. System Conditions
TheLocalMATEsystem response generation dynamically
adapts to the estimated user’s accent based on a set threshold.
We evaluate it under four response conditions that differ only in
the accent used for speech synthesis (other components, such as
ASR, FAQ retrieval, and response content, remain unchanged).
Baseline (US Default)In the baseline condition, the system al-
ways produces a default U.S.-accented response, where no ac-
cent adaptation is applied.
Correct Accent AdaptationThe system estimates participant’s
accent using the fine-tuned classifier, and the response voice
was experimentally controlled as EN-Korean to ensure a consis-
tent realization of the intended correct-accent adaptation across
all Korean-accented English participants.
Incorrect Accent AdaptationThe system intentionally uses a
mismatched accent that differs from the participant’s own ac-
cent (such as selecting a different accent than the one estimated
for the participant) to simulate incorrect adaptation.
Uncertain + Fallback.When the accent classifier is uncer-
tain (with confidence below a predefined threshold), the system
uses the default U.S.-accented voice. This fallback avoids non-
response under low-confidence cases and allows evaluation of
user perceptions of confidence-gated fallback strategy.
