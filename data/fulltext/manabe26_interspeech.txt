Abstract
Language-audio representation learning typically assumes one-
to-one correspondence between audio and text. However, the
relationship is inherently many-to-many: an audio segment can
be described by multiple levels of specificity and vice versa.
This oversimplification prevents models from learning the un-
derlying data hierarchy. Unlike in vision-language domains,
extending hierarchical modeling to audio is non-trivial due
to complex sound source superposition. To address this, we
propose Probabilistic Language-Audio Pre-training (ProLAP),
representing inputs as probability distributions in a joint em-
bedding space. For evaluation, we introduce two diagnostic
datasets (AudioCaps-HC, AudioCaps-EC) for hierarchical anal-
ysis. Through a novel audio traversal task and inclusion tests,
we demonstrate that ProLAP significantly outperforms deter-
ministic baselines in capturing semantic hierarchy and yields
intuitive uncertainty estimations, without sacrificing audio-text
retrieval performance.
Index Terms: contrastive learning, general-purpose audio rep-
resentation, audio-text retrieval
1. Introduction
Language-audio representation learning has emerged as a pow-
erful paradigm for open-vocabulary audio understanding. Con-
trastive Language-Audio Pre-training (CLAP) [1] aligns audio
clips with textual descriptions in a shared embedding space, en-
abling zero-shot retrieval and classification.
Most language-audio models [2, 3] implicitly assume a one-
to-one correspondence, mapping each input to a deterministic
point in the embedding space. In real-world settings, however,
the language-audio relationship is inherently many-to-many: a
single caption such as ’dog sound’ can correspond to diverse
audio instances, while a single audio clip can be described at
multiple levels of specificity, from coarse (’dog barking’) to
fine (’small dog barking indoors’). Such many-to-many map-
pings can be effectively formulated as inclusive relationships,
enabling the representation of the inherent hierarchical struc-
ture within the data. The one-to-one assumption prevents exist-
ing models from capturing this hierarchical structure, hindering
their ability to learn the underlying data hierarchy.
In the vision-language domain, understanding data hierar-
chy and many-to-many correspondences has significantly ad-
vanced [4, 5, 6, 7, 8], driving various practical improvements.
For example, hierarchical awareness aids in filtering ambiguous
samples [9], efficient coarse-to-fine retrieval [10], and multi-
granular evaluation [11]. To learn such structures, recent frame-
works utilize probabilistic representations; ProLIP [8], for in-
stance, captures hierarchies as inclusion relationships, remov-
ing the need for explicit hierarchical captions.
Achieving similar hierarchy-aware capabilities in the au-
dio domain is promising yet underexplored. While some work
has investigated hierarchical structures in audio-only tasks [12],
it remains unclear whether vision-language learning strategies
and evaluation frameworks can be successfully translated to
the audio domain. This difficulty arises from a fundamental
difference in modalities: unlike visual scenes, where objects
are generally spatially distinct, real-world audio is inherently
characterized by the complex superposition of multiple over-
lapping sound sources. Consequently, a straightforward appli-
cation of vision-language methodologies is insufficient, neces-
sitating domain-specific investigations into capturing semantic
hierarchies in audio-language representations.
Motivated by these domain-specific challenges, we pro-
pose Probabilistic Language-Audio Pre-training (ProLAP), a
probabilistic extension of CLAP [1]. ProLAP represents each
input (audio or text) as a probability distribution over the joint
embedding space rather than a deterministic vector. While a
probabilistic representation provides richer encoding than a de-
terministic vector, we empirically find that probabilistic mod-
eling alone has a limited ability to capture semantic hierarchy.
Therefore, we introduce hierarchical inclusion loss, which ex-
plicitly encodes hierarchical relations between general and spe-
cific concepts in the learned representations.
To explicitly evaluate the latent hierarchical structure of
ProLAP, we establish hierarchical analysis as our primary eval-
uation. For this purpose, we introduce two datasets extend-
ing AudioCaps: AudioCaps-HC (Hierarchical Captions) and
AudioCaps-EC (Event-level Captions). Using AudioCaps-HC,
we design an audio traversal task, analogous to the image
traversal task [4], to systematically analyze the learned hierar-
chical structure. Furthermore, we conduct inclusion tests on
AudioCaps-EC to verify that semantic inclusion relations are
accurately captured in both the audio and text modalities. Fi-
nally, to ensure that our hierarchical modeling does not compro-
mise basic cross-modal alignment, we perform standard audio-
text retrieval tasks. Our overall results demonstrate that ProLAP
captures semantic hierarchy significantly better than determin-
istic baselines and yields intuitive uncertainty estimations (e.g.,
longer texts exhibiting lower uncertainty), all without sacrific-
ing the foundational retrieval performance of standard CLAP.
2. Proposed Method
In this paper, we propose Probabilistic Language-Audio Pre-
training (ProLAP), which learns joint language-audio repre-
sentations with probabilistic modeling of inputs. Building on
ProLIP [8], we follow its probabilistic formulation, and our
baseline ProLAP is a straightforward application of ProLIP for
audio-text data. We also propose hierarchical inclusion loss to
promote hierarchical learning.

2.4. Implementation Details
Following the official implementation of CLAP, we use HTS-
AT [13] and GPT-2 [14] as audio and text encoders, respec-
tively. In the text encoder, the end-of-sentence token ([CLS])
estimates the mean, while[UNC]token estimates variance. In
the audio encoder, because HTS-AT is based on Swin Trans-
former [15], we cannot use token-level prediction or token drop-
ping. Instead, we add an head for uncertainty and a learnable
[MASK]token. Empirically, we found that simply applying the
[MASK]token fails to learn uncertainty, because naive mask-
ing causes the masked audio embeddings to collapse to near-
identical representations, hindering uncertainty learning. To
mitigate the effect of the[MASK]token, in the final adaptive
average pooling layer, we average only the unmasked tokens.
3. Experiments
3.1. Experimental Setup
To demonstrate the effectiveness of ProLAP, we evaluate it on
audio-text retrieval, audio traversal, and inclusion tests. In
our experiments, we fine-tune ProLAP initialized with pre-
trained CLAP [1] weights. In addition, we fine-tune CLAP
with the InfoNCE and CLAP with the sigmoid loss proposed
in SigLIP [16]. Our training settings is mostly aligned with that
of [17, 18]. We train for 50 epochs with a batch size of 256. For
training, we use Adam [19] as the optimizer. The learning rate
is scheduled with a cosine scheduler and one warm-up epoch.
The maximum learning rate is1×10−5. We setλ 1 = 5×10−3,
λ2 = 5×10−7, andγ= 1×10 −5 for all experiments. We use
L= 1for audio encoder andL= 2for text encoder. We apply
a masking probability of 75% to the first 12.5% of each batch
for both audio (qa) and text (qt) modalities.
3.2. Datasets.
We use two benchmarks for audio-text retrieval: Audio-
Caps [20] and ClothoV2 [21]. Furthermore, to quantitatively
evaluate hierarchical understanding, we add manually veri-
fied synthetic annotations for AudioCaps, providing two new
datasets: AudioCaps Hierarchical Captions (AudioCaps-HC)
and AudioCaps Event-level Captions (AudioCaps-EC).
AudioCaps-HCis used for an audio traversal task introduced
in Sec. 3.4. For each caption from AudioCaps, we generate cap-
tions at four abstraction levels using gpt-oss [22], Specifically,
Level 4 corresponds to the original caption (e.g., “Humming
and vibrating with a man and children speaking and laughing”),
and Levels 3, 2, and 1 are progressively more abstract, with
Level 1 being the most abstract (e.g., “Humming with people”).
We use these multi-level captions to evaluate the hierarchical
structure in the learned embedding space.
AudioCaps-ECis used for inclusion tests [8], which measure
how well the model learns hierarchical structure. For each Au-
dioCaps caption, we extract event-level captions from the orig-
inal caption using Ministral 3 [23] (e.g., “Food is frying, and
a woman talks”→[ “Food is frying”, “A woman talks” ]). We
then use SAM Audio [24] to extract corresponding audio seg-
ments given the event-level captions as queries. Using these
event-level captions and extracted audio segments, we evaluate
the learned hierarchical structure in both audio and text.

3.4. Performance on Audio Traversal
To assess the hierarchy learned by ProLAP, we introduce audio
traversals. Audio traversal evaluates whether, as an audio em-
bedding is shifted toward the most abstract point in the latent
space, the nearest text embeddings become correspondingly
more abstract. This task assesses how well the hierarchical
structures of audio and text representations align. Audio traver-
sal holds promise for practical applications, flagging audio-text
examples with abstraction mismatches to support dataset cura-
tion or other downstream applications.
Following image traversals [4], an audio traversal starts
from an audio embeddingaand moves toward the most abstract
anchor ([ROOT]) by sampling 50 nearest text embeddings via
linear interpolation in the shared space. The resulting sequence
of retrieved captions reveals how semantics evolve from specific
descriptions nearato more abstract concepts near[ROOT]. We
set[ROOT]as the empty caption ("") for deterministic mod-
els, and as the average of the empty and most inclusive caption
embeddings for probabilistic models following [8].
Table 2 shows traversal performance on AudioCaps-HC.
Overall, ProLAP can retrieve the corresponding hierarchical
captions in AudioCaps-HC more precisely and exhaustively
than deterministic methods, suggesting stronger capture of se-
mantic hierarchy. Moreover, we observe that our proposed loss
terms consistently improve hierarchical representation quality.

qualitative evaluation [8], we examine the relationship between
input text length and predicted uncertainty on the AudioCaps
dataset. Intuitively, longer captions tend to be more specific,
therefore the text length and uncertainty are expected to be neg-
atively correlated. We aim for a moderate or stronger negative
correlation (rTLU <−0.4) [26]. Fig. 2 shows the effect of hier-
archical inclusion loss. The hierarchical inclusion loss leads to
a clearer downward trend in uncertainty as text length increases.

3.6. Ablation Study
To examine how hierarchical inclusion loss and masking prob-
ability influence performance, we performed two experiments.
Impact of Hierarchical Inclusion Loss.We evaluated various
combinations of hierarchical inclusion loss levels for the audio
and text encoders, with the resulting AudioCaps audio-to-text
retrieval performance andr TLU summarized in Table 3. The
results show thatr TLU consistently improves as the hierarchical
inclusion level on the text side increases. In contrast, audio-
side level 1 maintains stable retrieval performance, while level
2 causes slight but consistent degradation.
Effect of Masking Probability.Although Table 3 demonstrate
the effectiveness of the hierarchical inclusion loss, it remains
unclear whether this effect is driven by the loss or a lower mask-
ing probability in the final hierarchical mask. Table 4 summa-
rizes ablations for hierarchical inclusion loss and the masking
probabilityq t ∈{0.5,0.75}. We choose 0.5 as it is close to
0.752. Note that we omit the ablations for the audio masking
probabilityq a as it had minimal impact on the performance,
and we fix it to 0.75. The results show that lowering the mask-
ing probability without hierarchical inclusion loss substantially
deterioratesr TLU, indicating that the loss promotes learning hi-
erarchical structure. Furthermore, we found that settingq t to
0.75 slightly improves retrieval performance compared to 0.5.
4. Conclusion
We proposed Probabilistic Language-Audio Pre-training (Pro-
LAP), to capture the inherent semantic hierarchies within audio-
language modalities. Within this framework, we introduce a hi-
erarchical inclusion loss that inherently encourages hierarchical
understanding. We evaluate our model on two newly introduced
diagnostic datasets, ProLAP significantly outperforms deter-
ministic baselines in modeling hierarchy and estimating uncer-
tainty, without sacrificing audio-text retrieval performance.
