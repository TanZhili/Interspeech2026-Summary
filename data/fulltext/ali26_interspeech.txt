Multilingual ASR

Abstract
SpeechLLMs show strong promise for multilingual ASR, yet
centralized training conflicts with privacy constraints over dis-
tributed, linguistically diverse data. This paper presents Fed-
SpeechLLM, a federated learning framework for multilingual
SpeechLLM-based ASR evaluated on a bilingual English and
Italian setting. We study federated optimization under com-
pounded non-IID conditions from acoustic and cross-lingual
heterogeneity. Standard FL methods degrade when clients
hold language-skewed distributions. Moreover, we propose
language-aware gradient aggregation and a sampling clustering-
based method to mitigate multilingual imbalance, using selec-
tive aggregation of speech encoders and projection layers with
a frozen LLM backbone. Results confirm competitive perfor-
mance against centralized training.
Index Terms: Federated Learning, Speech Recognition, Large
Language Models, SpeechLLM
1. Introduction
Large language models (LLMs) have driven significant progress
in natural language processing, with recent innovations extend-
ing these capabilities to speech through multimodal architec-
tures [1]. Speech language models (SpeechLLMs) integrate
pretrained LLMs with speech encoders via learned projection
layers (Figure 1(a)), demonstrating strong performance across
speech recognition, translation, and spoken question answer-
ing [2, 3]. A compelling frontier for these models is multi-
lingual speech understanding, enabling a single system to pro-
cess speech across typologically distinct languages, which dif-
fer substantially in phonology, morphology, and prosodic struc-
ture [4]. However, these models rely on centralized training
that aggregates vast, linguistically diverse corpora in a single
location, raising critical concerns about data privacy, regulatory
compliance, and data sovereignty [5]. In multilingual settings,
this problem is especially severe: speech data across languages
is inherently siloed across different institutions, jurisdictions,
and devices, each subject to distinct regulatory constraints that
prohibit centralized aggregation.
Federated learning (FL), depicted in Figure 1(b), partially
addresses these challenges by enabling collaborative training
across distributed datasets without centralizing raw data [6, 7].
Yet applying FL to multilingual SpeechLLMs introduces com-
pounded non-IID challenges: client data varies not only in
acoustic conditions and speaker demographics, but also along a
fundamentally new axislinguistic heterogeneity[8, 9]. Clients
may hold data in a single language or in heavily imbalanced lan-
guage mixtures, causing local updates to reflect divergent lin-
guistic statistics that severely degrade convergence and cross-
lingual generalization [10, 11]. Furthermore, the multimodal

Despite successful FL applications to monolingual ASR
models [14], federated optimization for multilingual Speech-
LLMs remains entirely unexplored [15]. Existing federated
ASR methods are predominantly monolingual and architec-
turally simpler, while SpeechLLM systems are trained centrally
without addressing the linguistic and acoustic heterogeneity of
real-world multilingual deployments. No existing framework
simultaneously tackles federated training, SpeechLLM archi-
tecture, and multilingual non-IID distributions.
To bridge this gap, we introduceFed-SpeechLLM, the
first federated framework for multilingual SpeechLLM-based
ASR, evaluated on a bilingual English and Italian setting. Fed-
SpeechLLM supports selective aggregation of projector train-
able parameters, while keeping the speech encoder and LLM
backbones frozen to preserve its pretrained multilingual linguis-
tic knowledge. This enables efficient federated optimization un-
der both linguistic and acoustic heterogeneity, without sacrific-
ing the expressive power of the underlying language model.
We conduct a comprehensive empirical study evaluating
federated optimization strategies for speech-language models
in terms of convergence, communication efficiency, and robust-
ness to data heterogeneity. The main contributions of this paper
are summarized as follows:
• We present a systematic study of FL for SpeechLLM-based
multilingual ASR.
• We propose a communication-efficient strategy for large
speech-language models that aggregates only trainable pa-
rameters on the server.
• We provide an extensive evaluation on different FL client se-
lection approaches for multilingual ASR tasks, demonstrat-
ing the effectiveness and stability of the proposed approach.

2. Model Architecture
2.1. Overview of SpeechLLM Architecture
The SpeechLLM architecture [3, 16], shown in Figure 1(a),
combines a frozen speech encoder with a pre-trained LLM via a
trainable projector. The input includes speech, a task instruction
prompt, and ground-truth text. Raw 16kHz audio is encoded
into the encoder dimensional embeddings, which are projected
and pooled by the projector into LLM-dimensional represen-
tations with pooling factork. The prompt and ground truth
(training only) are tokenized and embedded in the same LLM
dimensional space. All embeddings are concatenated along the
sequence dimension and fed into the LLM for text generation.
2.2. Speech Encoder
Our Fed-SpeechLLM adopts WavLM-Large [17] as the speech
encoder, a self-supervised model trained on 94K hours of un-
labeled speech comprising 24 transformer layers (317M pa-
rameters) that processes 16kHz raw audio. WavLM achieves
strong performance across ASR, speaker verification, and emo-
tion recognition [18]. The encoder remains frozen during feder-
ated training, with only the projector updated, enabling efficient
adaptation while preserving pre-trained representations.
2.3. Large Language Model
We employ TinyLlama-1.1B-Chat-v1.0 [19] as our LLM back-
bone, a 1.1B-parameter instruction-tuned model trained on 3
trillion tokens following the Llama 2 architecture, with a hid-
den size of 2048 matching our adapter output. Its compact size
ensures efficient training while maintaining competitive perfor-
mance for resource-constrained settings. We fine-tune it using
LoRA [20], which approximates weight updates via low-rank
matrices:∆W=AB, whereA∈R d×r andB∈R r×dk
withr≪d[21].
2.4. Projector
To bridge the dimensional mismatch between the speech en-
coder and the language model, we use a lightweight linear
adapter composed of a projection layer followed by 1D average
pooling [22]. The projection maps 1024-dimensional WavLM
embeddings to TinyLlama’s 2048-dimensional input space. We
then apply average pooling with kernel size and stridek= 2
along the temporal dimension, reducing sequence length and
computational cost while aggregating local temporal informa-
tion. The projector is trained from scratch, with both the speech
encoder and LLM kept frozen, enabling efficient adaptation be-
tween the two pre-trained components [23].

3.2. Federated Learning Setup
We implement our FL framework using Flower [26]. We de-
ploy a client for each speaker in the datasets. In each round,
30%of the clients are randomly instantiated and trained for 10
local epochs. The resulting gradients are centrally agglomer-
ated usingFedAvgstrategy [27]. FL is applied for 100 rounds.
All FL results marked as—-,—-for LS and MLS, respec-
tively, are compared with those achieved with central training
marked as⋆,⋆for LS and MLS, respectively, using the Adam
optimizer. More details related to training hyperparameters are
publicly available in our GitHub repository. 1.
4. Results
4.1. Monolingual FL Scenario
To the best of our knowledge, state-of-the-art research has not
yet investigated FL for SpeechLLM architectures; therefore, we
provide the first systematic analysis of federated adaptation in
this setting by examining the Word Error Rate (WER) across
federated rounds in the monolingual scenario as shown in Fig-
ure 2, using a trainable projector with frozen backbones and
LoRA-based adaptation on the LLM side. On LS, WER drops
sharply from approximately100%to approximately6-7%by
round 100, closely matching centralized training (approxi-
mately6%), confirming that federated adaptation nearly ap-
proaches the centralized performance. Turning to MLS Italian,
Fed-SpeechLLM achieved WER≈22%by round 100 versus
≈20%obtained centrally, with the small residual gap likely
attributable to FL hyperparameter sensitivity. Unlike conven-
tional ASR models that struggle to converge under FL from
scratch [6, 15, 28, 29], Fed-SpeechLLM converges stably with
low communication and computational overhead.

4.2. Bilingual FL with Random Client Selection
To study the impact of client participation and training strate-
gies in bilingual FL, we consider three scenarios. The Full-
Client scenario includes all 316 clients. Client-Balanced sce-
nario, where an equal number of speakers from each dataset
(65 per dataset) are used. The Full-Client with server-side fine-
tuning scenario further optimizes the aggregated global model
for the target language at the server.
❶Full-client Scenario:This scenario uses all LS and MLS
speakers, preserving the original dataset composition while in-
creasing the number of LS clients. This implies that the client
distribution per language is skewed towards English data while
each Italian client has more training hours. Figure 3(a) shows
the evolution of the WER between rounds. Starting from 100
WER, the federated model quickly reduces errors and gradu-
ally converges. By round 100, WER reaches 16.8 for LS and
19.7 for MLS, corresponding to absolute reductions of 83.2 and
80.3 points, respectively. The LS dataset exhibits slightly higher
variability, while MLS remains stable. Compared to centralized
training and the monolingual scenario, the federated model has
an absolute gap of≈10 WER for LS. This gap is due to the
non-IID distribution between both languages. While for MLS,
the gap is 1.3 of WER, showing effective model convergence.
❷Client-balanced Scenario:The client-balanced federated
learning scenario (Figure 3(b)) considers equal numbers of
speakers (65 per dataset) from LS and MLS to control client
participation in a cross-lingual federated SpeechLLM setting.
Although this strategy equalizes the number of participating
clients, the data distribution remains heterogeneous, as MLS
provides substantially more speech hours per speaker than LS.
Compared to centralized training (6.1 WER on LS and 18.4
WER on MLS), federated training converges to 33.8 WER on
LS and 22.7 WER on MLS after 100 communication rounds.
While MLS performance remains relatively close to the central-
ized baseline, LS exhibits pronounced degradation and higher
variability across rounds. This observed performance degrada-
tion can be attributed to the limited training hours available in
the LS dataset. In general, these results indicate that balancing
client counts alone is insufficient to mitigate data and language
imbalance, motivating language-aware aggregation and person-
alization strategies for federated multilingual ASR.
❸Full-clients with server fine-tuning Scenario:In this sce-
nario, we attempt to mitigate the gap introduced in the last
scenario, especially on LS. In detail, this scenario extends the
full-clients FL setup by introducing a lightweight server-side
fine-tuning step after each model aggregation. Specifically, fol-
lowing aggregation, the global model is fine-tuned on the LS
dev-cleandataset for English for up to three epochs. The
WER evolution across communication rounds is shown in Fig-
ure 3(c). Starting from a WER of 100 the model exhibits rapid
error reduction in early rounds and converges to 12.1 WER on
LS and 21.1 WER on MLS by round 100. This corresponds to
absolute WER reductions of 87.9 and 78.9 points, respectively.
Compared to the full-clients scenario without server fine-tuning,
server-side adaptation further improves LS performance and re-
duces variability across rounds, while MLS performance re-
mains relatively stable. When contrasted with centralized train-
ing, the remaining absolute gap is reduced to 6.0 WER for LS,
whereas the gap for MLS increases slightly to 2.7 WER. Over-
all, these results indicate that limited server-side fine-tuning can
effectively bias the global model toward a target language and
improve convergence for that language, while at the cost of a
modest degradation on non-target languages.
4.3. Bilingual FL with Clustering Client Selection approach
❶Full clients with sample-clustering Scenario.To mitigate
client-level data imbalance, we use size-aware sampling and ag-
gregation [30, 31]. Clients are partitioned into small and large
groups based on the median dataset size. At each round, client
selection is biased toward smaller clients, with approximately
70% sampled from the small group. Motivated by the adaptive
fairness-aware reweighting approach proposed in [32], we mod-
ify FedAvg by scaling each client’s contribution using a simple
static schemew k =α knk, wheren k denotes the number of
local samples andα k = 1.5for small clients andα k = 1
otherwise. This server-side adjustment increases the influence
of smaller clients without changing local training. Results are
shown in Figure 4(a). Compared to centralized training, the
federated setup achieves 9.7 on LS and 19.6 on MLS. Con-
sequently, the obtained results highlight the superiority of the
clustering-based approach over random client selection, demon-
strating that FL remains competitive in such scenarios.
❷Full clients with language-clustering Scenario.We inves-
tigate two temporal strategies for language biasing in federated
SpeechLLM training:Late Biasing(round 30 onset) andEarly
Biasing(round 5 onset). The gray vertical lines, as depicted in
Figure 4, mark the transition points of the English-biased client.
•Late Biasing
Pre-biasing Performance (Rounds 0-30):Prior to round
30, client participation is balanced at 50% English and 50%
Italian. This demonstrates rapid initial convergence from 100%
WER, with FL MLS Italian achieving≈25% WER by round 20
and FL LS English reaching 32% WER by round 30. This bal-
anced participation boosts the performance of both languages
prior to biasing to the target language.
Post-biasing Performance (Rounds 30-100):Following

5. Conclusion
In this work, we introduced Fed-SpeechLLM, a federated
framework tailored for SpeechLLM-based multilingual ASR
that enables privacy-preserving decentralized training through
selective parameter aggregation. Extensive experiments across
monolingual and bilingual scenarios demonstrate that feder-
ated adaptation of lightweight components can closely match
centralized training in homogeneous settings, while heteroge-
neous multilingual conditions require careful client selection
and biasing strategies. Our results show that clustering-based
sampling and early language-aware biasing significantly reduce
the federated–centralized performance gap, providing practical
guidelines for deploying SpeechLLMs in real-world, privacy-
sensitive environments. Future work will explore adaptive ag-
gregation, personalized federated strategies, and broader multi-
lingual scalability to further mitigate cross-lingual interference
and improve robustness.
