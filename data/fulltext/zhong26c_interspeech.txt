Abstract
Prior work on phoneme-level dysarthric speech assessment has
been limited to alignment-based methods and speaker-level in-
telligibility prediction. Here, we move to utterance-level, mul-
tidimensional assessment, investigating posterior-uncertainty
features from a frozen connectionist temporal classification
(CTC) phoneme recognizer as proxies for articulatory distor-
tion. We combine them with phoneme error rates to form the
Phoneme Error Decomposition (PED) feature family. PED is
evaluated via logistic regression probing on the Speech Acces-
sibility Project dataset with utterance-level expert ratings. We
find that uncertainty-based features provide the strongest indi-
vidual assessment signals. The full PED family achieves a mean
Area Under the ROC curve (AUROC) of 0.80 on four primary
dimensions, comparable to the performance of a strong SSL
baseline (AUROC of 0.81). We further present a transparent,
human-inspectable decision-tree pipeline based on PED, with a
mean AUROC of 0.78.
Index Terms: automatic dysarthric speech assessment,
phoneme error decomposition, interpretability
1. Introduction
Dysarthria is a motor speech disorder resulting from neurologic
disease or injury. Depending on lesion site and etiology, it can
impair speech subsystems to varying degrees, producing highly
heterogeneous impairment profiles with substantial inter- and
intra-speaker variability. Speech-language pathologists (SLPs)
typically assess dysarthric speech through auditory perceptual
analysis, a process requiring repeated listening to rate specific
speech dimensions [1]. This process is inherently subjective and
time-consuming, particularly when evaluating dozens of dimen-
sions for differential diagnosis [2]. Therefore, automated, ob-
jective methods are needed to support SLPs in comprehensive
multi-dimensional assessment.
Early automated assessments relied heavily on hand-crafted
acoustic features, such as jitter orf 0 [3]. These features quan-
tify specific acoustic properties and serve as acoustic correlates
for particular perceptual attributes [4, 5]. However, these mea-
sures capture a narrow signal scope and remain insufficient to
model the multidimensional perceptual impairment profiles re-
quired for comprehensive dysarthria assessment. Recently, self-
supervised learning (SSL) speech representations have achieved
strong performance on dysarthric speech assessment, includ-
ing dysarthria detection, voice quality assessment, and etiology
classification [6, 7, 8]. However, such SSL-based embeddings
are black boxes for humans. This lack of interpretability is a
barrier to clinical deployment [9].
Word error rate (WER) from automatic speech recognition
(ASR) has been explored as an interpretable proxy, and can
show moderate-to-high correlations with global intelligibility
ratings [10]. However, modern end-to-end ASR relies on lin-
guistic priors during decoding, which can bias transcriptions
toward plausible word sequences rather than the actual acous-
tic signal [11]. Moreover, as a coarse utterance-level summary,
global WER misses localized disruptions and phoneme-level er-
ror patterns that are clinically informative [12].
Phoneme transcriptions are interpretable and clinically in-
formative, making them attractive for automated speech assess-
ment. Manual clinical transcription is labor-intensive. Hence,
prior works have largely relied on automated alignment tools to
obtain phoneme-level features. These alignment-based meth-
ods include forcing alignment against canonical text [13], map-
ping decoded phone sequences to language-dependent dictio-
nary entries [14], and calculating Goodness of Pronunciation
(GoP) scores with uncertainty quantification [15]. However,
these alignment-based methods depend on reference transcripts
or dictionaries. Acoustic mismatch in dysarthric speech can de-
grade alignment quality [16], thereby biasing models toward the
intended phonemes and masking the diagnostic deviations.
Prior work has predominantly focused on speaker-level in-
telligibility assessment. This focus is driven by speaker-level
annotations in benchmark datasets such as UA-Speech [17] and
TORGO [18]. Given the substantial intra-speaker variation, ag-
gregating features across utterances from a single speaker can
mask utterance-level patterns [19]. SLPs find that consonant er-
ror patterns correlate with articulatory severity [20], suggesting
that phoneme-level features are beneficial beyond global intel-
ligibility. Moreover, constructing reliable speaker-level error
patterns may require a large number of samples per patient, ex-
acerbating the time constraints already faced by clinicians.
In this work, we investigate utterance-level, multidimen-
sional dysarthric speech assessment using posterior-uncertainty
features from a frozen connectionist temporal classification
(CTC) phoneme recognizer. Unlike the prior alignment-based
methods, we derive uncertainty features from free-decoding
CTC posteriors. Motivated by the known overconfidence of
CTC models on dysarthric speech [21], we propose three Ev-
idential deep learning (EDL) based scores [22] and a training-
free ME score (Eq. 2) to quantify phoneme-level uncertainty,
and combine them with six phoneme error rates to form a PED
feature set. The evaluation is performed on SAP [23] across
seven speech dimensions. Our contributions are:
1. We show that CTC posterior uncertainty features provide in-
formative signals for articulatory-related perceptual dimen-
sions, improving upon the alignment-dependent GoP without

2. Dataset
We use the publicly available Speech Accessibility Project
(SAP) [23] (release 2025-11-02), containing speech from 959
speakers across five etiologies: Parkinson’s disease (39.4%),
ALS (26.8%), Cerebral Palsy (18.7%), Down Syndrome
(9.3%), and Stroke (5.9%). Expert clinicians rated samples on
up to 45 perceptual dimensions (1–7 ordinal scale) following
the DAB framework [1]. We apply speaker-stratified splits to
the labeled subset (only 3% is rated): train (n=8,759), vali-
dation (n=1,017), and test (n=1,392), totaling 11,168 samples
with no speaker overlap. Of the 45 dimensions, only 25 have
≥1,000 labeled samples. In this work, we study seven repre-
sentative dimensions. These include four primary articulatory
and global metrics: Imprecise Consonants (IC), Distorted V ow-
els (DV), Intelligibility (Int), and Naturalness (Nat). To enable
direct comparison with the SSL baseline [6], we additionally in-
clude two voice quality dimensions: Breathiness (Br) and Harsh
V oice (HV), and one prosodic dimension: Prolonged Phonemes
(PP). Annotation distributions are shown in Fig. 1.
Our within-speaker analysis of the labeled data confirms
substantial intra-speaker variability, which challenges the use
of speaker-level labels for modelling. For speakers with≥5
utterances, the mean within-speaker standard deviation of per-
ceptual ratings reaches 55% of the between-speaker standard
deviation across the four primary dimensions.
3. Experiment Setup
Experiments were implemented in PyTorch [24] and scikit-
learn on an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 3090 GPU. All audio was
resampled to 16 kHz and peak-normalized, and all encoders
were frozen during probing. Performance was evaluated for
(i)binary screening(Normal: 1 vs. Impaired: 2–7) using Area
Under the ROC curve (AUROC) and (ii)ordinal grading(1–7
scale) using Spearman’s rank correlation (ρ) to measure rank
agreement. All reported metrics include 95% bootstrap confi-
dence intervals (1,000 iterations; seed = 42). Approximate run-
times were 81 s for PED probing, 3,149 s for HuBERT probing,
and 2 hours for EDL training. Our code is publicly available.1

3.1. PED Feature Family
PED comprises 11 interpretable features derived from
a frozen CTC phoneme recognizer (wav2vec2-xls-
r-300m-timit-phoneme[25], XLS-R fine-tuned on
TIMIT [26], 315M params). These include six reference-based
phoneme error rates and five reference-free confidence features
from the CTC head as described in Table 1.
3.1.1. Posterior Uncertainty Features
We hypothesize that distorted phoneme realizations can act as
an out-of-distribution (OOD) signal relative to typical speech.
While CTC-based phoneme recognizers achieve strong perfor-
mance on standard speech benchmarks, domain mismatch can
cause their posterior confidence to be overconfident and unre-
liable on dysarthric speech [21]. Uncertainty-related signals
may better reflect deviations from learned canonical patterns
than raw softmax confidence. We therefore use posterior un-
certainty as a proxy for articulatory distortion and investigate
two uncertainty quantification approaches.
Evidential deep learning (EDL).We propose applying a
lightweight EDL head [22] to the frozen CTC encoder, replac-
ing the softmax layer with a Dirichlet parameterization. Un-
like a softmax function, the Dirichlet head provides a structured
uncertainty estimate and allows decomposing predictive uncer-
tainty into epistemic and aleatoric components [22, 27]. The
head is a 2-layer MLP (1024→512→44, totaling 547K train-
able params) that outputs Dirichlet concentration parameters:

Evidence uncertainty is defined asu=K/S∈(0,1], which

The head is trained on the LibriSpeech train-clean-100 sub-
set [28] with AdamW (lr=1e-4), cosine annealing over 5 epochs,
early stopping (patience=3), and KL annealing (λ: 0 to 0.01).

3.1.2. Standard Phoneme Error Rates
Canonical phoneme sequences are derived from reference text
via G2P conversion (g2p-en[29]). Realized sequences are
obtained from CTC greedy decoding with IPA-to-TIMIT map-
ping [26]. Unlike alignment-based methods, where the canon-
ical transcription constrains the decoder, the two sequences
are derived independently and interact only at the Levenshtein
alignment step [13]. Six error rates are computed from the
alignment: PER, substitution, deletion, insertion, phonological
feature error rate (PFER), and length ratio. PFER is weighted
by articulatory distance in a 16-dimensional phonetic feature
space; e.g., /t/→/d/ (voicing only) incurs a smaller penalty than
/t/→/m/ (voicing, nasality, place, and manner).
3.2. Evaluation Protocol
For each perceptual dimension, we train independent probes for
(i) binary screening and (ii) ordinal grading via scikit-learn. We
use logistic regression (C=1.0,class weight=balanced,
solver=lbfgs,max iter=1000) for screening. For grad-
ing, we useLassoCV(5-fold CV ,max iter=5000) on stan-
dardized features. We use the same hyperparameters as Narain
et al. [6] for a fair comparison with the SSL benchmark.
Forsingle-feature benchmarks, we compare the proposed
ME and EDL-based uncertainty scores against GoP-maxlogit,
the best reported GoP baseline with uncertainty quantification
from Yeo et al. [15]. GoP-maxlogit baseline was reimplemented
using the same MFA forced alignment and temperature scaling
(T=7.8375) under the same frozen CTC recognizer. We also
include WER features extracted using Whisper Large-v3 [11]
(1.55B parameters) as an additional single-feature baseline.
Formulti-feature baselines, the PED suite is evalu-
ated against HuBERT Large [30] (315M parameters, 1024-
dimensional SSL embeddings) and anAcoustic12baseline. Hu-
BERT was selected for its strong performance in our benchmark
study across seven selected perceptual dimensions on SAP [6].
The Acoustic12 set was extracted via Parselmouth [31], com-
prising F0 and intensity statistics (mean, std, range), HNR, jit-
ter, shimmer, duration, pause ratio, and speaking rate.
To provide a fully transparent assessment alternative, we
also train shallow decision trees withmax depth=4, balanced
class weights. The depth was fixed for human-inspectability
rather than optimised for performance on PED features only.

5. Conclusion
In this work, we demonstrate that posterior uncertainty features
can serve as an informative proxy for articulatory distortion.
Uncertainty features are integrated with standard phoneme error
rates to form the PED feature family. The results show that PED
can match black-box representations on primary articulatory di-
mensions while preserving interpretability. The effectiveness of
PED highlights the potential of phoneme recognition systems
as diagnostic tools for dysarthric speech assessment. More-
over, we combine PED with a shallow decision tree to offer a
transparent pipeline for efficient automated speech assessment,
where each node is human-interpretable. Limitations include
skewed labels on secondary dimensions, the potential for lin-
ear models to underestimate feature interactions, and the exclu-
sive use of numerical features. Future work will explore deep-
learning-based approaches and investigate phoneme error pat-
terns with decoded transcripts. Understanding how phoneme-
level distortion patterns connect to specific etiologies and sever-
