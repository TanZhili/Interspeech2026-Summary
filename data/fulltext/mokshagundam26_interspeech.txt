Boundaryless Speech-to-Syllable Representations with Hierarchical CNN for
Linguistically Inspired Automatic Stress Detection

Speech Processing Lab, LTRC, IIIT Hyderabad, India
namrata.mokshagundam@research.iiit.ac.in, saiharshitha.aluru@research.iiit.ac.in,

Abstract
In stress-timed languages like English, word stress place-
ment can change meaning, making automatic syllable stress
detection vital for Computer Assisted Language Learning
(CALL) systems, as L2 learners often misplace stress. Ex-
isting approaches rely on manually annotated boundaries or
forced alignment, which require extensive labeling and suffer
from alignment inaccuracies. We propose a fully boundary-
independent framework using convolutional neural networks
(CNNs) for hierarchical time-compression, converting frame-
level embeddings into compact syllable-level representations
without explicit segmentation. To enforce the linguistic con-
straint that each word contains exactly one stressed syllable, we
also incorporate a Post-net2.0 loss. Experiments on the ISLE
corpus with German (GER) and Italian (ITA) learners show
consistent improvements over boundary-dependent and prior
boundary-independent SOTA models, achieving 94.86% (GER)
and 96.24% (ITA), with gains up to 18.67% and 16.12%.
Index Terms: speech recognition, human-computer interac-
tion, computational paralinguistics
1. Introduction
In stress-timed languages such as English, one syllable within
a word is typically produced with greater prominence than the
others, known as stressed syllable [1] . Misplacement of syl-
lable stress can alter word meaning. For example, the word
’conduct’ changes its meaning depending on stress placement:
conDUCT (verb) means to carry out or lead, whereas CON-
duct (noun) refers to behavior. This misplacement of stress is
a frequent error among second-language (L2) learners, whose
stress patterns are often influenced by their native (L1) lan-
guage. Computer-Assisted Language Learning (CALL) sys-
tems [2] have been developed to provide corrective feedback
on such challenges. A key component of these systems is au-
tomatic syllable stress detection and therefore, it is essential to
design accurate automatic syllable stress detection methods.
Despite its importance, automatic detection of stressed syl-
lables remains a challenging task. Stress is realized through
acoustic cues such as pitch, duration, and intensity [3] and the
early approaches relied hevily on handcrafted acoustic features
[2, 4, 5]. For example, Tepperman et al. [5] utilized the prosodic
cues to detect syllable stress. Yarra et al. [6] introduced ex-
plicit context features that integrate linguistic information with
acoustic cues, improving the performance of syllable stress de-
tection. Building on these acoustic and linguistic features, sta-
tistical machine learning methods such as support vector ma-
chines, decision trees, and ensemble approaches like bagging

and boosting have been applied to syllable stress detection [7].
However, these models are limited in capturing the sequential
and temporal dependencies inherent in syllable stress patterns.
To address these limitations, deep learning architectures have
been explored for syllable stress detection. Convolutional neu-
ral networks (CNNs) have been shown to perform well for stress
detection by combining spectral features with knowledge-based
prosodic cues [8]. Because stress on a syllable can influence
stress on the neighboring syllables, recurrent models such as
LSTMs and GRUs, have effectively captured these temporal re-
lationships, enhancing the performance on stress prediction [9].
In addition to these, hybrid strategies by combining variational
autoencoders (V AEs) with deep neural networks (DNNs), have
also shown to improve representation learning and stress classi-
fication performance [10]. In parallel to modeling architectures,
some studies have focused on designing loss functions to im-
prove performance. Notably, Post-net [11] and Post-net2.0 [12]
incorporate the linguistic constraint [13] that only one stressed
syllable per word exists. In [14], the authors have evaluated the
stress detection performance under discriminative and genera-
tive noisy conditions.
Existing approaches to syllable stress detection are con-
strained by their dependence on syllable boundary information.
Obtaining such boundaries is highly challenging, as manual an-
notation is very expensive, and automatic alignment is unreli-
able as it is prone to errors. A recent state-of-the-art model [15]
attempts to overcome this issue partially by using dual attention
over frame-level and phoneme-level features. Yet it continues to
rely on phoneme boundaries, thereby introducing the alignment
errors again.
The primary novelty of our work is a fully boundary-
independent framework that maps speech directly to syllable-
level structure, entirely eliminating the reliance on explicit syl-
lable or phoneme boundaries. Our approach employs a hierar-
chy of convolutional neural networks (CNNs) and pooling lay-
ers that progressively compress frame-level acoustic features
into compact syllable-level representations, thereby mapping
speech-to-syllables. The use of CNNs with pooling layers is
motivated by two hypotheses: (i) CNNs can capture local tem-
poral patterns that reflect syllabic structure, and (ii) pooling op-
erations combine information across neighboring frames while
preserving stress-relevant cues. Consequently, the proposed
framework mitigates errors arising from inaccurate boundary
annotations. Further, we incorporate the linguistically informed
Post-net2.0 loss [12] to enforce the linguistic constraint. We
evaluate our approach on English speech from German (GER)
and Italian (ITA) L2 speakers. Experimental results show that
the proposed Post-net2.0-based CNN model consistently out-
performs both boundary-dependent and boundary-independent
baselines, achieving maximum accuracies of 94.86% for GER

2. Proposed Approach
Figure 1 shows the overall workflow of the approach, which
comprises four stages: a) feature extraction and preprocessing,
b) modeling, c) loss design, and d) post-processing.
2.1. Feature Extraction and Preprocessing
Capturing prosodic variations at the frame level is crucial for
stress detection, as stressed syllables differ in pitch, inten-
sity, and duration. Instead of relying on handcrafted acous-
tic features such as MFCCs, we employ self-supervised rep-
resentations that learn context-dependent acoustic patterns.
These representations provide rich prosodic cues without the
need for labeled data. From each utterance, a sequence of
{T1, T2, . . . , Tx}frame-level representations (Figure 1a) are
extracted, wherexcorresponds to the number of frames in the
given utterance.
Padding and Masking:Generally, the length of these frame-
level sequences varies across each utterance, which is inconsis-
tent for model training. To address this, we pad all utterances
to the maximum sequence lengthyin the dataset, yielding se-
quences{T 1, T2, . . . , Ty}. To ensure that padding does not in-
terfere with the models’ learning, the additionalTy−T x padded
frames are masked to allow the model to focus on only valid
frames.
2.2. Modeling
Modeling syllable stress from frame-level representations in-
volves three core challenges: capturing contextual dependen-
cies across frames, compressing frame-level sequences into
syllable-level embeddings, and decoding stress patterns effec-
tively. To address these, our model is organized into three main
components: an encoder, a convolutional temporal reduction
block, and a decoder.
Context encoding (encoder block):Accurate stress detec-
tion requires considering not just the acoustic cues of the current
frame but also those of neighboring frames, since stress patterns
are influenced by both preceding and following syllables. To in-
corporate this information, we pass the frame-level embedding
sequence ({T1, T2, . . . , Ty}) through an encoder with sequen-
tial network. This provides enriched frame representations of
fixed lengthT y.
Temporal compression (convolutional block):The next
challenge is to compress frame-level sequences into syllable-
level embeddings while retaining stress-relevant cues. To pro-
gressively reduce the temporal resolution from frames to syl-
lables, we employ a CNN-based hierarchical sequence reduc-
tion module. Stacked convolutional filters act as local fea-
ture extractors, while pooling layers downsample the sequence
in a controlled manner. As shown in Figure 1b, throughk
successive convolution and pooling operations, the sequence
length is gradually compressed from{T 1, T2, . . . , Ty} →
{S1, S2, . . . , Sm}, wheremis the maximum syllable count in
the dataset. The resultant is now a representation of syllable-
level information.
Stress decoding (decoder block):Finally, the syllable-
level embeddings are mapped to stress predictions using a de-
coder. While we can model stress predictions independently,
stress on one syllable is often influenced by stress on other syl-
lables within a word. To address both cases, we experiment on
two decoding strategies:
Non-autoregressive decoder:This decoder predicts all syl-
lable stress labels simultaneously without depending on the
previous predictions, avoiding error propagation among the
syllables. As shown in Figure 1b (Non-autoregressive), the
msyllable-level representations{S 1, S2, . . . , Sm}are passed
through a stack of sequential layers, producing a sequence of
mprobability outputs(P 1, P2, . . . , Pm)in parallel. Since the
number of syllables per utterance varies, we retain only the first
nprobability outputs(P 1, P2, . . . , Pn), wherenis the true syl-
lable count of the utterance, and discard the remainingm−n
positions. During both training and inference, the true syllable
countnis obtained directly from the ISLE dataset annotations.
Autoregressive decoder:To account for the dependencies be-
tween syllables explicitly, we employ an autoregressive decoder
that generates stress predictions sequentially, one syllable at a
time. At each step, the model uses both the syllable embeddings
and its previous outputs, enabling it to capture the syllabic de-
pendencies within a word. During training, teacher forcing is
applied, where ground-truth stress labels are provided as inputs
instead of previously generated predictions. During inference,

the decoder relies on its own outputs. During training and infer-
ence, the decoder continues until allnsyllables are predicted,

In both decoders, a final classification layer maps the sylla-
ble embeddings into stressed/unstressed labels.
2.3. Loss Functions
To train our models, we consider two alternative loss functions,
i) Binary Cross-Entropy (BCE) and ii) Post-net2.0 loss. Models
are optimized using either BCE or Post-net2.0 individually, and
we compare their performance to evaluate the effect of the loss
formulation.
Binary Cross-Entropy (BCE):Stress detection at the syl-
lable level is formulated as a binary classification task, where
each syllable must be identified as either stressed or unstressed.
For this purpose, we employ the Binary Cross-Entropy (BCE)
loss, a standard choice for binary classification, as it directly
optimizes the likelihood of assigning correct stress labels. BCE

2.4. Post-processing
In English, according to the linguistic constraint, every word is
expected to contain exactly one primary stressed syllable. How-
ever, model predictions may assign stress to multiple syllables
or fail to assign it for all syllables. To enforce this linguistic
constraint, we apply a post-processing step after training. For
each word, we identify the syllable with the highest predicted
stress probability and mark it as stressed, while all other sylla-
bles are designated as unstressed.
3. Dataset
In this study, we use the ISLE corpus [16], which contains 7,834
utterances from 46 non-native English speakers (23 German
and 23 Italian), with approximately 160 utterances per speaker.
Phoneme-level alignments were obtained via automatic forced
alignment and manually refined by five expert linguists. Syllab-
ification was performed using P2TK [17], followed by syllable-
level stress annotation under the constraint that each word con-
tains exactly one primary stressed syllable. For our experi-
ments, we use only the audio recordings and stress labels, with-
out phoneme or syllable boundaries. The full dataset includes
48,868 stressed and 16,693 unstressed syllables. Following
[6], we restrict our analysis to polysyllabic words, resulting
in 12,388 stressed and 16,005 unstressed syllables. Speaker-
independent train/test splits are created separately for GER and
ITA, balanced across age, sex, and proficiency while preserving
the natural stress distribution [16].
4. Experimental Setup
In this section, we outline the features and give detailed descrip-
tion of the architecture details of the proposed methodology.
4.1. Self-supervised wav2vec 2.0 representations
Self-supervised speech models such as wav2vec 2.0 [18] and
HuBERT [19] have shown strong ability to capture rich acous-
tic and prosodic information from raw audio, eliminating the
need for handcrafted features. These models learns to en-
code cues, which are crucial for distinguishing stressed from
unstressed syllables. Following prior findings [20], we adopt
768-dimensional frame-level embeddings extracted from a pre-
trained wav2vec 2.0 model, which uses a 25ms window size
and a 20ms frame shift. These embeddings, obtained directly
from raw waveforms, serve as the input features to our en-
coder–decoder CNN architecture.
4.2. Architecture details
The proposed model consists of three main components:
Encoder block:For the sequential encoder, we utilize a
single bidirectional LSTM [21] layer to capture contextual in-
formation across frames. This encoder preserves the original
temporal resolution, meaning that no compression of features
or sequence length is applied at this stage.
Convolutional block:The encoded frame-level embed-
dings are processed through a hierarchy of six convolutional
[22] and pooling layers to progressively reduce the temporal
resolution. The sequence is downsampled from the maximum
number of frames (y) to the maximum number of syllables (m)
across the dataset. We achieve this using a series of 1D con-
volutions and max-pooling layers, resulting in the successive
temporal resolution reduction: (210→105→53→27→14
→7→5). The initial length of 210 frames accommodates
the longest polysyllabic word duration in the dataset, while the
target length of 5 aligns with the maximum expected syllable
count.
Decoder block:The syllable-level embeddings are passed
through a stack of five recurrent layers, BiLSTMs for the non-
autoregressive decoder and LSTMs [23] for the autoregressive
decoder. The layer dimensions progressively decrease from
(768→256→128→64→32→16). Finally, a dense layer
maps the outputs to syllable-level stress predictions.
Baseline:For comparative evaluation, we consider both ex-
isting boundary-dependent syllable stress detection models [20]
and a recent state-of-the-art partially boundary-independent
model [15]. The boundary-independent model leverages acous-
tic and contextual features along with attention-based mecha-
nisms for stress classification and has demonstrated strong per-
formance in detecting lexical stress errors in L2 English learn-
ers. So, it acts as a strong baseline for our experiments.
Both of the proposed and baseline models are trained using

binary cross-entropy (BCE) loss. We also train our proposed
model using the Post-net2.0 loss to evaluate the effectiveness
of the linguistic constraint. ReLU [24] activation function is
applied in all intermediate layers, and a softmax layer is used
at the output to predict syllable stress. We use Adam [25] as
the optimizer. The hyperparameters are selected based on the
performance of the model on validation set. We conduct evalu-
ations under three experimental settings:
Matched:Models are trained and tested on the same lan-
guage dataset (GER or ITA).
Combined:A single model is trained on the combined
GER and ITA datasets and evaluated separately on each lan-
guage.
Cross:Models are trained on one language dataset (GER)
and tested on the other (ITA) and vice versa.

6. Conclusion
In this work, we proposed a boundary-independent framework
that eliminates the reliance on explicit phoneme or syllable
segmentation, thereby avoiding the annotation costs and align-
ment errors. Our model leverages CNN-based hierarchical
time-compression to progressively transform frame-level em-
beddings into syllable-level representations. This architecture
allows us to effectively model local temporal dependencies
while retaining the linguistic cues necessary for stress assign-
ment. To further enhance the performance, we integrated the
Post-net2.0 loss, which enforces the linguistic constraint of ex-
actly one primary stressed syllable per word. Experiments on
the ISLE corpus of German and Italian L2 English learners
demonstrated that our approach consistently outperforms both
boundary-dependent and partially boundary-independent base-
lines across matched, combined, and cross scenarios. In future
work, we aim to extend this strategy to unsupervised modeling.
