Prosodic Realization of Focus in Yi-Mandarin Bilingual Speakers: On-Focus
Expansion without Post-Focus Compression
Ziyu Zhang1,∗, Chenyu Li 2,∗,∗∗
1 Flinders University, Australia
2 Johns Hopkins University, United States
zhan2915@flinders.edu.au, cli229@jh.edu
Abstract
Post-focus compression (PFC) is absent in Yi (Nuosu), a Tibeto-
Burman tone language, but present in Beijing Mandarin. We ex-
amined whether Yi–Mandarin bilingual speakers produce PFC
in L2 Mandarin, using delta-F0 and intensity as primary mea-
sures and duration as a secondary measure, across initial, me-
dial, and final focus conditions. Yi speakers produced on-focus
F0 expansion fully comparable to Beijing controls, yet showed
no post-focus compression across all conditions in both F0
and intensity. Individual variation in Mandarin language back-
ground did not predict post-focus F0 among Yi speakers. These
results demonstrate a complete dissociation between intact on-
focus expansion and absent post-focus compression, suggest-
ing that when the L1 entirely lacks PFC, the mechanism may
be suppressed in L2 production even under prolonged bilin-
gual contact—consistent with the view that on-focus expansion
and post-focus compression constitute functionally independent
prosodic modules with distinct acquisition trajectories.
Index Terms: post-focus compression, L2 prosody, Yi lan-
guage, Mandarin, focus prosody, L1 transfer
1. Introduction
Prosodic focus is a cross-linguistically widespread means of
marking informational prominence. In many languages, a fo-
cused element is realized with expanded F0 range, increased in-
tensity, and lengthened duration—a phenomenon known as on-
focus expansion (OFE) [1, 2]. In a subset of these languages,
OFE is accompanied by post-focus compression (PFC): a sys-
tematic reduction of F0 range and intensity in all words fol-
lowing the focus, relative to a neutral baseline [1]. PFC has
been documented in, among others, English [2], Korean [3],
Japanese [4], and Beijing Mandarin [1, 5]. Crucially, PFC is ab-
sent in a number of typologically diverse languages, including
Cantonese, Southern Min (Taiwanese), and Yi [6, 7], indicat-
ing that PFC is an independent linguistic feature not predictable
from the presence of lexical tone or other structural properties.
The present study examines focus prosody across three acoustic
dimensions—F0, intensity, and duration—following the multi-
cue framework of prior work on cross-linguistic PFC [6, 8].
The transfer of PFC in bilingual and L2 speech has at-
tracted considerable attention. PFC has proven resistant to
cross-linguistic transfer in both directions: it does not spread
from a PFC language to a non-PFC language through con-
tact [6, 9], and it is not easily acquired by L2 learners whose
L1 lacks PFC [10, 11]. Even when the L1 has PFC, transfer to
L2 does not occur automatically [12]. Notably, Chen et al. [13]

found that PFC in L1 Southern Min did not transfer to L2 Man-
darin even when Mandarin accounted for approximately 63% of
daily language use, underscoring that PFC production is gated
by L1 prosodic structure rather than amount of L2 experience.
Recent work further suggests that OFE and PFC constitute
functionally independent mechanisms with distinct acquisition
trajectories. Liu and Lee [14] showed that advanced Korean
learners of English produced acoustic cues in focused positions
largely comparable to those of native speakers, yet their post-
focus production remained significantly compressed relative to
native norms. Liu et al. [12] similarly found that PFC is not
automatically transferred from Korean to L2 English even at
advanced proficiency levels. These findings converge on the
view that OFE is acquired before PFC, and that PFC may be
suppressed entirely when the L1 lacks it. OFE operates locally,
enhancing acoustic cues within the focused word itself, whereas
PFC requires global reorganization of pitch range, intensity, and
duration across the remainder of the utterance—a computation-
ally more demanding operation that may be more strongly con-
strained by the prosodic grammar of the L1 [2, 14]. Within the
PENTA framework [15], these two operations are understood
as distinct communicative functions encoded in parallel, pre-
dicting that they can be selectively disrupted by L1 transfer.
These findings raise a more fundamental question: if the
L1 entirely lacks PFC—rather than merely realizing it differ-
ently from the target L2—can the mechanism emerge at all in
L2 production, even with extensive experience? Yi (Nuosu), a
Tibeto-Burman language of the Loloish branch spoken primar-
ily in Liangshan prefecture, Sichuan, China, has four lexical
tones (55, 44, 33, 21) and has been experimentally confirmed
to lack PFC [7, 5]. Speakers in Liangshan have been in pro-
longed contact with Mandarin through education and regional
administration, yielding a bilingual community with wide vari-
ation in Mandarin proficiency and usage—an ideal naturalistic
setting for examining the effects of L2 experience on prosodic
transfer. The present study addresses three questions: (1) Do
Yi–Mandarin bilingual speakers produce OFE in L2 Mandarin
to the same degree as Beijing Mandarin native speakers? (2) Do
they produce PFC in L2 Mandarin? (3) Does individual varia-
tion in Mandarin language experience (exposure, age of onset,
proficiency, current use) predict thedegreeof post-focus F0 re-
duction within the Yi group?
2. Methods
2.1. Participants

a second language (hereafter YI; 10 females, 11 males) par-
ticipated in this study, recruited in Puge County, Liangshan
Yi Autonomous Prefecture. Five Beijing Mandarin speakers

onset, self-rated proficiency (10-point Likert: 1 = Beginner,
10 = Native-like), and current Mandarin use frequency.
2.2. Materials and Procedure
Stimuli consisted of three-word Mandarin sentences in which
each syllable within a sentence bears the same lexical tone. This
design, following Chen et al. [8], isolates prosodic focus effects
from tone-driven F0 variation. The focused word appeared in
sentence-initial (W1), sentence-medial (W2), or sentence-final
(W3) position, yielding three focus conditions (Initial, Medial,
Final) plus a Neutral baseline elicited by a general question (ni
shuo shenme?‘What did you say?’). Focused conditions were
elicited by wh-questions targeting the subject, verb, or object.
Four target sentences in four standard Mandarin tones (T1–T4)
were used, giving 16 condition–sentence combinations. Each
was repeated five times per participant. BJ recordings were
made in a soundproof booth; YI recordings were made in a quiet
room at the local government office. All recordings were made
using a laptop-mounted microphone, sampled at 44.1 kHz, and
recorded via Praat [16].
2.3. Acoustic Analysis
Word boundaries were automatically aligned using the Mon-
treal Forced Aligner [17] with the official Mandarin acoustic
model, followed by manual correction in Praat to ensure ac-
curate segmentation of word onsets and offsets. Fundamen-
tal frequency (F0) was extracted using Praat’s PointProcess al-
gorithm [16]. Raw F0 values were converted to semitones
(st) referenced to each speaker’s median F0, normalising for
inter-speaker pitch-range differences. Time was normalised to
10 equidistant points per word token. In addition to F0, in-
tensity (dB) and duration (ms) were extracted for each word
token. Delta values for all three dimensions were computed
by subtracting the Neutral-condition mean from each focused-
condition token, yielding∆F0,∆intensity, and∆duration as
parallel dependent measures.
2.4. Delta-F0 Computation
Following Chen et al. [8], we computed a∆F0 value represent-
ing the difference between each focused-condition token and
the neutral baseline. For each speaker–sentence pair, the mean
F0 contour across all Neutral repetitions was subtracted from
the F0 contour of each individual focused-condition repetition,
separately for every word position. Positive deltas indicate F0
raised relative to neutral; negative deltas indicate compression.
Critically, subtraction was performed at the repetition level, pre-
serving trial-level variance for the mixed-effects models.
2.5. Statistical Analysis
Word-position means were computed by averaging the 10 time-
normalised points within each token, yielding one observation
per speaker×sentence×repetition×word (4,662 tokens to-
tal). Linear mixed-effects models (LMEs) were fitted in R us-
inglme4[18] andlmerTest[19]. The main model was fitted
separately for each acoustic dimension with the same fixed- and
random-effects structure:

3. Results
3.1. F0 Contours by Focus Condition
Figure 1 shows the time-normalised∆F0 contours for BJ and
YI speakers. BJ speakers produced a pronounced positive delta
at the in-focus word followed by strongly negative deltas at
post-focus words—the canonical PFC pattern documented for
Beijing Mandarin [1, 5]. YI speakers closely mirrored BJ at
in-focus positions but showed near-zero or positive deltas at
all post-focus positions, indicating a complete absence of PFC
across all focus conditions.

3.3. OFE–PFC Dissociation
The group×functional-position interaction was highly signif-
icant (F(1,2460.2) = 291.03,p < .001). Post-hoc con-
trasts showed that at OFE positions BJ and YI did not dif-
fer (estimate=−0.10st,t=−0.62,p=.536), while at
PFC positions the difference was large and significant (esti-
mate=−1.88st,t=−12.32,p<.001).
Figure 2 plots speaker-level OFE against PFC deltas. BJ
speakers clustered in the lower-left quadrant (OFE positive,
PFC negative); YI speakers occupied the upper-left quadrant
(OFE positive, PFC near-zero). The groups overlapped com-
pletely on the OFE axis but were entirely separated on the PFC
axis. Cohen’sdon speaker-level means was negligible for OFE
(d=−0.31;M BJ = 0.48st,M YI = 0.61st) and extremely

Both groups showed a position effect for OFE (Initial W1
>Medial W2; see Table 2), consistent with pre-focal declina-
tion reducing available pitch range in the medial position [8].
Crucially, this position effect was symmetric across groups and
did not interact with the PFC deficit. The dissociation between
intact OFE and absent PFC in the YI group aligns with the pro-
posal that OFE and PFC are functionally and acquisitionally
independent [14, 12]: OFE is acquired before PFC even among
learners whose L1 has PFC, and the present data suggest that
when the L1 entirely lacks PFC, the mechanism is suppressed
altogether while OFE remains unaffected.

3.4. Intensity and Duration
The OFE–PFC dissociation replicated across acoustic dimen-
sions. For intensity, the three-way interaction was significant
(F(4,4686.0) = 8.95,p < .001). At OFE positions, BJ and
YI did not differ (Initial W1: estimate= 0.40dB,p=.395;
Medial W2: estimate=−0.06dB,p=.899). At post-focus
positions, BJ showed significantly greater intensity reduction
than YI (Initial W3: estimate=−2.70dB,p < .001; Initial
W2: estimate=−1.19dB,p=.013), mirroring the F0 pat-
tern.
For duration, the three-way interaction was also significant
(F(4,4686.1) = 10.65,p < .001). BJ and YI did not differ
at the in-focus word in Initial focus (W1: estimate= 0.61ms,
p=.954), whereas BJ showed greater post-focus duration re-
duction at W3 (Initial W3: estimate=−45.4ms,p < .001;
Medial W3: estimate=−27.4ms,p=.011). However, group
differences also emerged at pre-focal and final-focus positions
(Medial W1:−23.0ms,p=.031; Final W1:−35.3ms,
p=.001), suggesting a general speech rate difference between
groups that complicates interpretation of duration as a pure in-
dex of PFC.

4. Discussion
Prior research has shown that even when the L1 has PFC, this
mechanism does not transfer automatically to the L2: advanced
Korean learners of English produce near-native OFE while their
PFC remains significantly below native norms [12, 14]. This
dissociation is generally attributed to the greater computational
demands of global pitch-range reorganisation relative to local
focus enhancement [2, 14]. However, the studies above all in-
volve the scenario in which the L1 has PFC but transfer is in-
complete; it has remained unclear whether, when the L1entirely
lacksPFC, the mechanism is merely attenuated or is suppressed
altogether. The present data provide the most extreme test of
this question to date. YI speakers produced on-focus F0 and
intensity expansion fully comparable to BJ speakers, while pro-
ducing no post-focus compression across any focus condition
or acoustic dimension. The complete overlap of the two groups
on the OFE axis combined with their complete separation on
the PFC axis indicates that when the L1 prosodic grammar con-
tains no PFC mechanism, the mechanism is not merely atten-
uated in L2 production but is suppressed entirely—while OFE
remains unaffected. This pushes evidence for the functional in-
dependence of OFE and PFC into a new extreme condition and
provides stronger support for the proposal that the two subsys-
tems have distinct acquisition trajectories [14], converging with
recent findings that Korean learners of L2 Mandarin also fail
to produce native-like PFC despite adequate OFE even at ad-
vanced proficiency [21].
The null effect of language experience on PFC requires
careful interpretation. On the surface, it appears to conflict
with Chen et al. [13], who reported that younger Southern Min–
Mandarin bilinguals in Quanzhou—speakers with higher Man-
darin use—produced Beijing-like PFC in their L2. We argue
that this conflict is resolved by a threshold effect: language ex-
perience predicts PFC only after speakers have crossed some
initial threshold and begun producing positive PFC variability.
In the Chen et al. [13] sample, younger bilinguals already ex-
hibited considerable PFC variation, so experience could account
for it; in the present YI data, post-focus deltas were overwhelm-
ingly positive and the vast majority of speakers produced no
post-focus compression at any level, leaving no variance for
experience to explain. The likely source of this fundamental
difference is input quality: the Quanzhou bilinguals received
sustained naturalistic Mandarin immersion from early child-
hood in a Mandarin-dominant educational and social environ-
ment, whereas the YI participants acquired Mandarin primar-
ily through formal schooling in a community where Yi remains
the dominant home language. Taken together, these factors sug-
gest that PFC acquisition is not a linear function of accumulated
exposure but a non-linear process requiring early, high-quality
naturalistic input to establish an initial representation, before
accumulated experience can consolidate it.
One limitation lies in the duration results. Although BJ
speakers showed post-focus shortening consistent with PFC,
group differences also emerged at pre-focal and utterance-final
positions where no PFC effect is theoretically predicted, sug-
gesting a systematic overall speech rate difference between
groups that complicates interpretation of duration as a pure
PFC index—an interpretive limitation noted in related PFC
work [6, 8]. This confound does not, however, affect the F0
and intensity conclusions: computing delta values against each
speaker’s own speaker-normalised neutral baseline controls for
overall rate at the individual level, and the between-group pat-
terns for F0 and intensity are fully consistent across conditions
and directionally align with the OFE–PFC dissociation hypoth-
esis. The duration results therefore constitute a limitation of the
present study but do not alter the core conclusion.
The complete dissociation observed here further supports
the parallel encoding view of the PENTA framework [15], in
which distinct communicative functions are encoded indepen-
dently in speech prosody. OFE and PFC emerge as two such
functions that can be selectively disrupted by L1 transfer, and
the present data demonstrate that this independence extends to
L2 acquisition under conditions of maximal L1–L2 prosodic
contrast.
5. Conclusions
This study provides experimental evidence for a complete
OFE–PFC dissociation in Yi–Mandarin bilingual speakers. YI
speakers produced on-focus F0 and intensity expansion indis-
tinguishable from Beijing Mandarin controls, while producing
no post-focus compression across any focus condition or acous-
tic dimension. The dissociation was robust across F0 and in-
tensity and was not modulated by any index of Mandarin lan-
guage experience, consistent with the view that PFC acquisition
is gated by L1 prosodic structure rather than by amount of L2
exposure. These findings support the functional independence
of OFE and PFC and suggest that when the L1 entirely lacks
PFC, the mechanism is suppressed in L2 production even under
conditions of prolonged bilingual contact.
