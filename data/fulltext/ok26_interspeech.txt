Abstract
Automatic Speech Recognition (ASR) systems are commonly
designed to transcribe linguistic content independently of
speaker identity. However, speaker-specific information can
persist within the latent representations of ASR models, rais-
ing privacy concerns when training data includes sensitive in-
dividuals. While machine unlearning has been studied in
several speech tasks, applying speaker unlearning to ASR
remains challenging due to the strong entanglement of lin-
guistic and speaker-related information in speech representa-
tions. In this work, we conduct a layer-wise analysis of Self-
Supervised Learning (SSL) based ASR representations, reveal-
ing that speaker identity is primarily encoded in intermediate
encoder layers. Based on this observation, we explore a feature-
level unlearning strategy that targets these identity structures.
Experiments on the VCTK corpus show that the proposed ap-
proach removes the influence of specific speakers while main-
taining robust transcription performance.
Index Terms: Machine Unlearning, Automatic Speech Recog-
nition, Privacy Preservation
1. Introduction
Machine unlearning has recently emerged as critical research
frontier, driven by the need to comply with regulatory man-
dates such as the Right To Be Forgotten (RTBF) under the Gen-
eral Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) [1]. These frameworks
grant individuals the right to request that their data be removed
from trained systems, posing a significant challenge when a user
withdraws consent after a model has been deployed. Unlearn-
ing has expanded into the speech domain, particularly for tasks
such as Keyword Spotting, Speaker Identification, and Text-to-
Speech [2, 3, 4]. In these applications, the information targeted
for unlearning are typically entangled with the primary goal of
the model. However, unlearning remains underexplored in Au-
tomatic Speech Recognition (ASR), which is one of the founda-
tional task of speech processing. As ASR fundamentally relies
on broad generalization to transcribe effectively, it is commonly
assumed that unlearning specific data could severely degrade its
general transcription performance.
Although ASR is typically formulated as a speaker-
independent task, empirical evidence shows that modern ASR
models still encode speaker-specific traits during training [5].
This effect becomes pronounced in modern ASR pipelines,
where large pretrained speech models are fine-tuned on rel-
atively small, application-specific datasets. While such ap-
proaches enable strong performance in data-constrained sce-
narios like clinical or low-resource applications [6, 7, 8, 9],
neural networks are known to memorize distinctive details of
their training data beyond what is required for generaliza-
tion [10, 11]. In context of ASR, this results in residual acoustic
fingerprints that persist within the model representations. These
signals can potentially be exploited through Membership Infer-
ence Attacks (MIAs) [12, 13], allowing an adversary to deter-
mine whether a particular speaker was included in the training
data [14]. Such risks raise serious privacy concerns [15], partic-
ularly when the training corpus involves sensitive cohorts, such
as patients in clinical studies, where a privacy breach could lead
to the re-identification of protected health information.
Despite these risks, to the best of our knowledge, achiev-
ing speaker-level unlearning in ASR while preserving transcrip-
tion performance has not yet been explored [16]. This difficulty
arises from the coupling of linguistic and paralinguistic infor-
mation within speech representations. An effective unlearn-
ing strategy must therefore selectively remove speaker-specific
traces while maintaining the general recognition performance,
ensuring that speech from those speakers is transcribed with the
same fidelity expected for any previously unseen speaker.
In this work, we investigate speaker-level unlearning in
ASR. Building on prior observations that speaker-related in-
formation tends to be localized within intermediate layers of
speech encoders [17], we explore a representation-level inter-
vention that targets these identity structures directly in the latent
space. To enable controlled manipulation of speaker represen-
tations, we attach an auxiliary representation head and perform
feature-level unlearning on the resulting embeddings. As one
concrete approach, we introduce a simple dispersion objective
that pushes representations of the forget speakers away from
their latent centroids. Our experiments demonstrate that such
targeted intervention can effectively removes speaker-specific
traces while maintaining robust ASR performance. By achiev-
ing a favorable privacy-utility trade-off compared to existing
unlearning baselines, our work provides a practical step toward
privacy-preserving ASR systems.
2. Related Work
2.1. Privacy Risks and Membership Inference in Speech
Models
Membership Inference Attacks reveal that training data can
leave measurable traces in deployed models [12, 14, 18], in-
cluding modern end-to-end ASR systems [19]. Moreover, SSL
encoders such as HuBERT [20] have been shown to retain
training-specific signals within their latent representations [13],
suggesting that memorization may originate at the representa-
tion level and propagate to downstream tasks. While prior stud-
ies highlight the privacy risks of speech models, they primarily
focus on auditing vulnerabilities rather than enabling mecha-
nisms for identity erasure from trained ASR systems.

2.2. Machine Unlearning
Machine unlearning [15] seeks to remove the influence of spe-
cific training data without retraining a model from scratch [21].
Existing approaches typically rely on parameter updates, such
as gradient ascent or label corruption applied to the forget
set [22]. More advanced methods introduce mechanisms such
as knowledge distillation or structural model editing [23, 24,
25, 26, 27]. Recent works has explored representation-level ap-
proaches that manipulate latent embeddings to control data in-
fluence more locally [28].
Machine unlearning has been studied in several speech
tasks [2, 3, 4]. However, applying unlearning to ASR re-
mains particularly challenging due to the strong entanglement
of linguistic and speaker-related information in speech repre-
sentations. Consequently, only a limited number of studies
have explored unlearning in ASR, including sample unlearning
on LLM-based ASR architectures [29] and optimization con-
straints during training [16], that suppress transcription perfor-
mance for targeted speakers. However, this objective differs
from the goal of general ASR systems, which are designed to
operate independently of speaker identity. Existing studies have
not yet demonstrated how to remove speaker-specific traces
from ASR models while preserving their ability to transcribe
speech for those speakers.
3. Method
3.1. Problem Definition
We define the task of speaker-level machine unlearning for
ASR. LetD={(x i,yi,si)}N
i=1 denote the original train-
ing dataset, wherex i is an input utterance,y i is its transcrip-
tiony i, ands i ∈ Srepresents the speaker identity. Follow-
ing the standard unlearning setup, we splitDinto a forget set
Df ={(x,y,s)|s∈S f}, specified by a set of forget speakers
Sf , and a retain setD r =D\D f . Given a modelf θ trained on
D, the objective is to update its parameters toθ ′ such thatf θ′
behaves similar to a gold modelf θ∗ trained exclusively onDr,
without requiring full retraining. A successful unlearned model
must satisfy the following three objectives.
•Utility preservationThe model must maintain its transcrip-
tion performance onDr, ensuring that the linguistic mapping
remains intact.
•Targeted Identity ErasureSpeaker-specific information as-
sociated withS f must be removed to defend against mem-
bership inference attacks, while identity information ofS r
remains unaffected.
•GeneralizationThe model should maintain robust ASR per-
formance on both forgotten and unseen speakers, treating for-
gotten identities as non-member data.
3.2. Auxiliary Speaker Representation Head
The overall architecture of ASR model consists of a pre-trained
HuBERT-base encoder followed by a Connectionist Temporal
Classifcation (CTC) [30] decoder. To obtain a controllable rep-
resentation of speaker-related information, we attach an aux-
iliary speaker classification head to the hidden states of the
6th encoder layer. This choice follows prior analyses show-
ing that speaker information tends to emerge in the early-to-
middle layers of SSL speech encoders [17]. As illustrated in
Figure 1(a), we first train this speaker head while freezing the
encoder. This step learns a speaker embedding spacezthat cap-
tures identity-related structure without modifying the linguistic
representations used by the ASR decoder. The subsequent un-
learning stage is operated on this embedding space to enable
representation-level interventions while minimizing disruption
to transcription performance.

3.4. Speaker-Aware Identity Dispersion
We instantiateL unlearn(Df)with a two-stage identity dispersion
procedure that targets geometric structure of speaker clusters in
the embedding space.
Stage 1: Centroid-based Distribution MappingUsing the
speaker embedding space defined in Section 3.2, we estimate
geometric anchors for the forget speakers. Although speaker
labels are available, we intentionally apply K-Means cluster-
ing to the embeddings of the forget set to identify latent cen-
troidsc s corresponding to each speaker distribution. Speaker
identity typically manifests as compact clusters in the represen-
tation space. Estimating the centroid of each cluster therefore
provides a natural geometric anchor that summarizes identity-
specific structure and serve as a reference points for targeted
removal during unlearning.
Stage 2: Latent Identity DispersionOnce the centroids are
estimated, we unfreeze the encoder and optimize the model to
push the embeddings of forget samples away from their cor-
responding centroid. For a forget utterancex j belonging to
speakers, we apply a margin-based dispersion loss between the
current embeddingΨθ(xj)and its centroid anchorc s. We refer
to this objective as Speech-Aware Identity Dispersion (SAID).

4. Experiments
4.1. Experimental Setup
DatasetsWe evaluate our method on the VCTK corpus [31],
a multi-speaker English dataset of high-quality read speech, to
simulate a realistic unlearning scenario where an ASR model is
fine-tuned to a new domain. We use the mic2 recordings and
exclude p280 and p315 due to reported technical artifacts, re-
sulting in a total of 108 speakers. All recordings are resampled
to 16kHz. To rigorously evaluate speaker-level forgetting, we
construct speaker-independent splits such that speaker identi-
ties are strictly disjoint across training and evaluation sets. The
speakers are randomly partitioned into a retain setD r of 95
speakers, a forget setD f of 5 speakers and a test setD t of 8
speakers. To mitigate potential speaker imbalance, we maintain
a balanced distribution of gender and accent across the splits.
To further distinguish between the removal of specific training
samples and the erasure of speaker identity, bothDr andDf are
divided into training and evaluation subsets with a 9:1 ratio.
Implementation DetailsWe first prepare the reference ASR
models by fine-tuning a HuBERT-base encoder with the CTC
loss. The Original ModelM orig is trained onD r,train∪Df,train,
while the Gold ModelM gold is trained exclusively onD r,train,
simulating retraining without exposure to the forget data. To
extract and manipulate speaker-specific information, we attach
a speaker classification head to the hidden states of the 6th
encoder layer. The speaker head is trained using a Cross-
Entropy loss for 15 epochs with the learning rate of3×10−4 on
Dr,train∪Df,train, while the ASR encoder remains frozen to pre-
serve the pre-trained representation space. During the unlearn-
ing phase, this configuration is kept identical across all methods
to ensure a unified representation space for fair comparison. All
unlearning methods, including baselines, are optimized using
the same training settings with a learning rate of1×10 −5, and
their objectives are applied to the speaker embeddingzpro-
duced by the auxiliary speaker head. For SAID, the margin is
set tom= 0. All models are trained for 25 epochs using the
AdamW [32] optimizer with a weight decay of 0.1.

4.2. Evaluation Metrics
We evaluate the proposed unlearning method on the three crite-
ria defined in Section 3.1.
Performance and generalizationTo measure the impact of
unlearning on speech recognition performance, we report the
Word Error Rate (WER) across multiple data partitions. For
utility preservation, we evaluate WER onDr,test, which consists
unseen utterances from retain speakers. This verifies that the
model maintains its transcription capability for identities that
should remain unaffected by the unlearning process. To as-
sess generalization, we additionally report WER onD f,test and
Dt. After unlearning,D f,test is treated as an unseen speaker
set. Thus, robust performance onD f,test indicates that forgot-
ten speakers are handled similarly to ordinary unseen speak-
ers rather than suffering unintended degradation in recognition
quality. Performance onD t further reflects robustness to com-
pletely unseen identities.
Membership Inference AnalysisTo quantify targeted identity
erasure at the representation level, we conduct a representation-
level MIA, following prior work [13, 22]. We train an exter-
nal binary classifier as an attacker to distinguish between train-
ing members and non-members using pooled hidden represen-
tations extracted from the encoder. Specifically, the attacker is
implemented as a logistic regression classifier operating on the
encoder representations. This simple linear classifier serves as a
probing model to measure whether membership signals remain
linearly separable in the latent space. The classifier outputs a
membership probability, indicating the confidence that a given
sample belongs to the training data. In our experimental setting,
the gold model represents ideal unlearning under the same data
distribution. Effective unlearning is therefore characterized by
the convergence toward the membership behavior of the gold
model.

5. Results
5.1. Main Results
Table 1 shows the performance of the proposed method and var-
ious unlearning baselines in terms of ASR performance (WER)
and privacy protection (MIA). These results highlight how dif-
ferent methods balance the three objectives defined in Sec-
tion 3.1. Regarding targeted identity erasure, SAID achieves
an MIA score of52.6%onD f,test, which is nearly identical
to the privacy level ofM gold. Since MIA was evaluated on
Df,test, composed of unseen utterances from forgotten speak-
ers, this result indicates that SAID successfully dissolves un-
derlying speaker-specific acoustic features rather than merely
erasing memorized training samples. In terms of utility, SAID
maintains robust performance onD r,test while showing a tar-
geted increase in WER onDf to closely align withM gold. This
suggests a more authentic forgetting process compared to base-
lines that show lower WERs onD f but exhibit higher privacy
risks. Furthermore, SAID satisfies generalization on the un-
seen contexts, as evidenced by its improved performance on the
unseen test setD t compared toM orig. While some baselines
achieve even lower WERs onDt, their suboptimal MIA scores
suggest a failure to meet the primary privacy requirements of
unlearning. These results position SAID as a more effective
and reliable solution for speaker-level machine unlearning.
5.2. Analysis
Layer-wise Analysis for Identity LocalizationTo identify the
optimal layer for speaker identity manipulation, we conduct a
layer-wise analysis on the HuBERT-base encoder fine-tuned for
ASR on the VCTK corpus. The attack is evaluated onD f,test
to distinguish between inherent acoustic discriminability and
identity memorization induced by training. As shown in Fig-
ure 2, bothM orig andM gold exhibit high classification accuracy
(>0.8) in early layers. This behavior is consistent with prior
observations in SSL speech models, where early layer repre-
sentations capture highly variant acoustic feature that naturally
separate speakers regardless of training exposure.
A critical transition occurs at the 6th encoder layer. At this
layer, the accuracy ofM gold, which has never been exposed
to the forget speakers, drops to near random chance of0.52.
In contrast,M orig maintains a significantly higher accuracy of
0.75. Such a substantial performance gap between the origi-
nal and gold models indicates that the separability cannot be
solely explained by raw acoustic differences. Instead, it reveals
that speaker-specific identity information of the forget speakers
is encoded in this intermediate layer due to training exposure.
While a notable performance gap persists across several subse-
quent layers, the difference between the two models eventually
diminishes in the final layers as representations become increas-
ingly specialized for linguistic transcription. Targeting the on-
set of such memorization, we select the 6th layer as the optimal
intervention point for speaker-level unlearning.
Layer-wise Effects of UnlearningAs shown in Figure 2, we
analyze the layer-wise MIA accuracy of the unlearned model
(MSAID) across the encoder hierarchy. In early encoder lay-
ers,M SAID shows slightly lower accuracy than bothM orig and
Mgold, indicating that the unlearning process actively disrupts
the acoustic signals associated with the forget speakers. This
proactive erasure weakens identity-related signals in the early
stages of representation, where identity information is typically
most variant. The more informative comparison appears at the
6th layer, the selected intervention point. At this layer,M SAID
closely follows the behavior ofM gold and substantially reduces
the gap previously observed betweenM orig andM gold. This
result indicates that the proposed identity dispersion effectively
neutralizes the speaker-specific signatures associated with the
forget speakers. While all models eventually converge toward
random-chance accuracy in the deeper layers as they prioritize
phonetic and linguistic content,M unlearn ensures that residual
identity signals are eliminated much earlier in the representa-
tion flow. This confirms that once the identity signatures are
dissolved at the targeted layer, the model maintains its privacy
state, effectively ensuring that no residual identity information
remains within the encoder.
6. Conclusion
In this work, we study speaker-level machine unlearning in
ASR. While ASR models are designed to recover linguistic con-
tent independently of speaker identity, prior study [5] shows that
speaker information still persist within their internal represen-
tations. Our layer-wise analysis confirms that SSL-based ASR
models retain speaker-specific traces in their latent representa-
tions, creating potential privacy risks. Motivated by this, we ex-
plore a feature-level unlearning strategy that targets these iden-
tity structures directly in the latent space. Experimental results
show that the influence of specific speakers can be removed
while preserving general transcription capability, providing a
promising direction toward privacy-aware speech recognition
systems. Beyond speaker identity, our framework can be ex-
