Probing and Mitigating Hallucinations in Speech-augmented Language
Models for Automatic Speech Recognition via Small Language Models
Bi-Cheng Yan, Jhih-Rong Guo, Fu-An Chao, Berlin Chen
National Taiwan Normal University, Taiwan
{80847001s, jhihrong, fuanchao, berlin}@ntnu.edu.tw
Abstract
The widespread success of large language models in natural
language processing has spurred the development of speech-
augmented language models (SLMs) to extend their reasoning
capabilities from textual inputs to spoken ones. Despite im-
pressive performance, SLMs remain susceptible to hallucina-
tions, posing challenges in practical utility. To this end, we first
probe hallucinations in SLMs for automatic speech recognition
(ASR) via the lens of causal mediation and behavioral analy-
sis. To mitigate fabricated ASR hypotheses, we propose Au-
dioSLM, a compact yet effective ASR framework built upon
a small language model, which leverages alignment cues and
cross-attention layers to synergistically bolster acoustic model-
ing and cross-modal interactions. Experiments on LibriSpeech
reveal that hallucinations arise from multi-head self-attention
modules with an excessive attention bias towards textual tokens.
AudioSLM considerably eases ASR hallucinations and outper-
forms some LLM-based ASR models.
Index Terms: speech-augmented language model, hallucina-
tion, cross-modal alignment, alignment information, automatic
speech recognition
1. Introduction
As witnessed by the impressive success of large language mod-
els (LLMs) in the natural language processing community, a
growing interest has surged in broadening LLM capabilities
to process and understand speech signals [1, 2]. By coupling
a speech encoder with a language model, speech-augmented
language models (SLMs) have demonstrated promising perfor-
mance in transducing complex acoustic patterns into contextu-
ally relevant outputs (e.g., transcriptions, captions, and intents),
serving as a more natural and direct interface for human-AI in-
teraction [3, 4]. However, the phenomenon of hallucinations
persists, as SLMs inadvertently generate content that diverges
from the spoken inputs, posing an arduous challenge to the reli-
ability and practical deployment of current SLMs.
Existing literature in natural language processing and com-
puter vision suggests that hallucinations primarily arise from
insufficient grounding on input modalities [5]. Early studies
reveal that hallucinated words often emerge as backbone lan-
guage models over-prioritize linguistic priors and rely heavily
on previously generated tokens, sidelining the information pro-
vided by visual and textual modalities [6]. To curb the over-
reliance on language priors, several methods have been pro-
posed, which broadly fall into two active strands of research.
The first line of research centers on post-processing strategies,
such as post-hoc corrections [7, 8] and specialized decoding al-
gorithms [9]. Another research direction aims to reduce the lex-
ical co-occurrence bias inherent in backbone language models

Despite significant strides in mitigating hallucinations for
vision-language and text-only models, this issue remains rela-
tively underexplored in SLMs. In response, this paper focuses
exclusively on their impacts on automatic speech recognition
(ASR), systematically investigating the underlying causes of
hallucinations in SLMs. As illustrated in Figure 1, we first
characterize hallucinated words as a specific subtype of inser-
tion error, referring to additional (or fabricated) tokens in the
ASR hypothesis that lack corresponding references after the
alignment process. Next, a series of analyses are conducted to
uncover the causal contributions of internal components within
SLM to fabricated token generation through the lens of attribu-
tion and intervention [13]. Finally, we propose AudioSLM, a
compact yet effective framework built upon a small language
model that mitigates ASR hallucinations through two com-
plementary strategies. Our method first leverages alignment
features from a Connectionist Temporal Classification (CTC)
module to provide reliable temporal cues for better acous-
tic modeling, and further enhances the backbone model with
cross-attention layers to facilitate fine-grained alignment be-
tween acoustic and textual modalities. Through causal medi-
ation analysis on the SLM components, we find that multi-head
self-attention (MHA) modules contribute more significantly to
hallucinations than multi-layer perceptron (MLP) modules. A
closer inspection of the attention weights on hallucinated tokens
reveals a pronounced tendency to over-prioritize textual context.

3. Mitigating Hallucination via AudioSLM
As shown in Figure 3, AudioSLM enhances temporal cues and
cross-modal alignment at both the input feature level and the
neural architecture level. At input feature level, we derive a se-
quence of alignment features from the CTC module and incor-
porate them into the SLM via a CTC-Gated module. At neural
architectural level, the Transformer blocks in the backbone lan-
guage model are augmented with cross-attention layers to bol-
ster the cross-modal alignment while preserving computational
efficiency.

4. Experiments
4.1. Experimental Settings
Dataset.We conducted ASR experiments on the LibriSpeech
corpus, a benchmark dataset for automatic speech recognition.
We used the train-clean-100 subset for training, while evaluat-
ing on the dev-clean/other and test-clean/other sets.
Implementation Details.The audio encoder in AudioSLM was
grafted from Whisper-large-v2, comprising 12 Transformer lay-
ers with 20 attention heads and a hidden dimension of 1280.
The connector module consisted of a 3-layer convolutional neu-
ral network with a kernel size of 5, achieving a subsampling
rate of 4. For the backbone model, we employed SmolLM-2-
135M [14], which consisted of 30 LLaMA blocks, each with
9 attention heads and a hidden size of 576. All models were
trained for 30 epochs using a cosine learning rate scheduler with
a maximum learning rate of 1e-4. During inference, the decoder
beam size was set to 5. Our source code is publicly available at
https://github.com/bicheng1225/AudioSLM.

4.2. Experimental Results
Causal Mediation Analysis of Hallucination in the SLM.
At the outset of experiments, we first compare the influence
scores of the MHA and MLP to Vanilla-SLM and AudioSLM
in Figure 4(a), and then quantify the contribution of the intro-
duced cross-attention (CA) layers to our model in Figure 4(b).
As shown in Figure 4(a), both the MHA and MLP modules
are critical for the generation of hallucinated words; however,
the removal of MHA modules results to a larger impact than
that of MLP modules. A consistent trend is observed in our
AudioSLM, where MHA modules continue to exert the most
profound influence, followed by the CA layers, both of which
surpass the impact of the MLP modules in Figure 4(b).
Analyzing Hallucination Patterns through Attention
Weights.Building on the causal mediation analysis, we inves-
tigate the distributions of attention weights for hallucinated and
non-hallucinated words to uncover distinct behavioral patterns.
Figure 5 illustrates the attention weights in the MHA layers
of the Vanilla-SLM, where weights assigned to audio and
text tokens are averaged across heads and normalized by their
respective sequence lengths. For non-hallucinated words, as
shown in Figure 5(a), attention weights exhibit an interleaving
pattern across modalities, with text tokens dominating at shal-
lower layers and audio tokens gaining prominence in deeper
layers. In contrast, from Figure 5(b), it is evident that the
attention weights for hallucinated words are overwhelmingly
skewed toward text tokens across all Transformer blocks, with
negligible attention allocated to audio ones. This result aligns
with the findings in computer vision [23, 10], where object
hallucinations are attributed to an over-reliance on language
priors, nearly setting aside visual information.

in [17, 27], suggesting that larger speech foundation models
are more susceptible to hallucinations due to their stronger
language priors.
5. Conclusion and Future Work
In this paper, we have put forward AudioSLM for ASR to mit-
igate hallucination. Through causal and behavioral analysis,
we identified that hallucinations primarily stem from attention
weights biased toward textual representations. By incorporating
alignment information and cross-attention layers, AudioSLM
effectively reduced HER while maintaining competitive ASR
performance. In future work, we plan to investigate training-
free methods to suppress hallucinations via decoding strategies
[23, 28, 29].
