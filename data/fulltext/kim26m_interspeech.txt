Abstract
Recent progress in generative models has made talking-head
deepfakes highly realistic and easy to create, jeopardizing public
trust. However, most audio-visual deepfake detectors rely on
lip–speech synchronization and are designed for static, frontal
videos, limiting their reliability in dynamic, in-the-wild record-
ings where the speaker moves and lip cues are degraded or miss-
ing. As an alternative, we propose a physics-aware detector for
dynamic speaking videos that leverages an acoustic constraint:
in real recordings, speech energy varies predictably with speaker-
to-camera distance, whereas deepfake generation can break this
coupling. Our method combines distance estimates from video
with distance-dependent acoustic measures from speech to de-
tect deepfakes. Experiments on in-the-wild benchmarks show
that the proposed physical cue is effective in dynamic scenarios,
and that it complements lip-sync-based detectors, with a simple
ensemble achieving consistent gains across datasets. Codes are
available athttps://byulharang.github.io/PADD/.

1. Introduction
Recent advances in deep generative models [1, 2, 3] have dra-
matically increased the realism and accessibility of synthetic
media, raising the risk of convincing deepfake videos in every-
day communication channels and public discourse [ 4, 5]. To
defense these threats, various detection methods have been de-
veloped for video forensics, ranging from detection of visual
artifacts [6, 7, 8] to verification of temporal and spatial consis-
tency [9, 10, 11]. In particular, manipulating a human speech
video is especially threatening since it can distort identity (visual
appearance) and intent (speech content), and may influence per-
ceived beliefs and decisions even with a short clip, exacerbating
misinformation and jeopardizing public trust. This highlights
the need for robust audio-visual deepfake detection.
Most existing audio-visual deepfake detection methods have
primarily exploited the strong correlation between speech and
lip motion, analyzing lip-speech synchronization to detect subtle
mismatches [15, 16, 17, 18, 19]. These approaches have shown
to be effective in videos where the speaker’s mouth is clearly
visible and unobstructed, such as news, interviews, podcasts, and
studio-like recordings [ 20, 21, 8, 22]. However, this reliance
on lip–speech synchronization limits practical deployment to
relatively constrained settings, such as static talking head videos
with frontal faces, stable lighting, and limited camera motion,
where the lip region remains consistently visible.

and easier to use, deepfakes are no longer limited to carefully cu-
rated static talking head clips; instead, they increasingly appear

and targets violations of the physical relationship between what
is seen (distance/motion) and what is heard (speech intensity) in
dynamic deepfake videos (refer to Fig. 1).

2. Method
This section presents our physics-aware deepfake detection
framework based onphysical alignmentbetween the speaker’s
visual distance and speech acoustics. We first introduce the
acoustic principle and distance-related measures used in our
method (Sec. 2.1). We then describe how we extract acoustic
measures and relative speaker distance, and how we convert
them into robust video-level features by distance-wise binning
and distributional statistics,e.g., variance and entropy (Sec. 2.2).
Finally, we build a lightweight multi-layer perceptron (MLP)
that maps these statistics to a deepfake score (Sec. 2.3).
2.1. Preliminaries
In real-world recordings, a speaker’s distance and speech signals
are coupled through acoustic propagation: as distance increases,
speech intensity decreases, and vice versa [ 25, 26]. This is
known as the inverse-square law. Based on this principle, we use
two acoustic measures, signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) and clarity at
50 ms (C50), to capture distance-dependent behavior and assess
physical consistency between spatial and acoustic signals.
Inverse-square law.In acoustics, the inverse-square law de-
scribes the physical relationship between sound intensity and the
source-receiver distance [23]. Assuming a source radiates power

2.2. Extracting Distance-dependent Acoustic Features
Our framework extracts physically meaningful features by jointly
using acoustic propagation and visual distance cues (see Fig. 2).
We derive acoustic measures (SNR andC50) from speech signals
and estimate the relative speaker distance from video frames.
These signals are then aggregated through distance-wise binning
and statistical modeling to obtain video-level features.
Acoustic measure extraction.We extract SNR and C50 from
the speech signal. To compute these, the speech component must
be separated from environmental noise. Thus, we employ SAM
Audio [14] with the text prompt “speech” to isolate the speech
signal from the input audio. Using the separated signals, we
compute SNR andC50 over temporal windows of 300 ms, which
covers typical syllable durations in speech [30] while capturing
most of reflections associated with room reverberation [31]. This
design enables stable estimation of the acoustic measures while
reducing interference from adjacent utterances.
Speaker distance estimation.We estimate the relative distance

between the speaker and the camera for each frame. Facial
landmarks are detected using RetinaFace [ 13] to localize the
speaker region. A dense depth map of the frame is estimated
using MiDaS [12]. We then obtain speaker distance by averaging
the depth values within the detected facial region, and its inverse
is used as a proxy for the speaker’s relative distanced spk.
Distance-wise binning.We use the speaker distance dspk to
group acoustic measures into distance bins. Specifically, a dis-
tance range within each video is partitioned into the bins, and
acoustic measures are averaged within each bin. This reduces
fluctuations in vocal intensity and phonetic content that may
violate the constant-power assumption of the physical model,
allowing the acoustic measures to follow an attenuation trend:
E[{SNR,C 50}|d spk]∝−20 log 10(dspk).
Statistical modeling of acoustic measures.Although the ex-
pected acoustic measures follow the physical attenuation trend,
instantaneous values of SNR and C50 are sensitive to speech
variability and environmental noise. Thus, we characterize the
distribution of acoustic measures within each depth bin using
variance and entropy, which capture the following relationships.
V ariance.When speech energy appears inconsistent with
the speaker’s visual distance, as often observed in deepfake
videos, acoustic measures within the same depth bin become
more dispersed, resulting in larger variance. In contrast, au-
thentic recordings show lower variance since acoustic prop-
agation remains physically consistent with speaker distance:
Var[x|d spk]Fake>Var[x|d spk]Real, wherex∈{SNR,C 50}.
Entropy.In deepfake videos, synthesized speech often ex-
hibits reduced variability in acoustic measures, resulting in lower
entropy [32]. In contrast, real-world videos are influenced by en-
vironmental noise and unconstrained recording conditions [33],
leading to higher entropy: H(x|d spk)Real >H(x|d spk)Fake.
We aggregate the bin-wise variance and entropy of acoustic
measures to obtain video-level statistics:
Ebin[Var(x|d spk)]Fake>E bin[Var(x|d spk)]Real
Ebin[H(x|d spk)]Real >E bin[H(x|d spk)]Fake
whereE bin denotes the expectation over depth bins.
2.3. Physics-aware Deepfake Detector
To capture the statistical behavior of acoustic signals across
distance bins, we extract features based on the variance and
entropy of SNR and C50. For each acoustic measure x∈
{SNR,C 50}, we compute the expected variance and expected
entropy across distance bins, denoted as Ebin[Var(x|d spk)]
and Ebin[H(x|dspk)], respectively. These statistics capture how
acoustic properties vary with respect to the estimated speaker
distance. Using these statistics, we define the following features:
Var SNR=Ebin[Var(SNR|d spk)],Var C50=Ebin[Var(C 50|dspk)],
HSNR=Ebin[H(SNR|dspk)],H C50=Ebin[H(C50|dspk)].

3. Experiments
In this section, we compare our physics-aware detector with
state-of-the-art lip-sync-based detectors. We first evaluate the
models on a curated dynamic subset, where physical cues are
more informative. Then, we report ROC–AUC scores of the
detectors on full set (i.e., both static and dynamic) of in-the-wild
benchmarks. We also present qualitative examples and ablation
studies to further analyze the method.
3.1. Experiment Setup
Competing methods.We compare against two state-of-the-art
lip-sync-based deepfake detectors, SpeechForensics [ 18] and
AuViRe [19]. Both methods focus on the alignment between
lip movements and speech, and adopt A V-HuBERT [34] as the
backbone encoder for audio-visual representation learning. They
have demonstrated strong detection performance on widely used
benchmark datasets that feature relatively static and constrained
human speaking settings [20, 21, 8, 22], often reporting ROC-
AUC scores above 0.95. However, their robustness under uncon-
strained real-world conditions remains less explored.
Datasets1.We use Deepfake-Eval-2024 (DF24) [ 24] and Au-
ViRe RealWorld (RW) [19] to evaluate deepfake detection un-
der unconstrained and dynamic conditions. Both datasets are
collected from media platforms and include both static and dy-
namic videos, although RW is predominantly static. They also
span diverse recording environments, including varying camera
motion, environmental noise, and editing artifacts. To ensure
reproducibility, we exclude samples with missing modalities or
invalid links. All models are trained on the DF24 training set
(fine-tuning existing detectors [18, 19] and training ours from
scratch) and evaluated on the DF24 test split (in-domain) and
the RW test set (cross-dataset). In addition, to analyze scenarios
where physical cues are most informative, we curate a dynamic
subset,DF-Dynamic, by manually selecting videos with notice-
able speaker motion from both test sets.

3.3. Ablation Studies
Impact of physical features.Table 3 reports the detection
performance when subsets of physical features are removed.
Removing the SNR-based statistics (Var SNR,H SNR) decreases
performance on DF24 (−0.0262 AUC), while removing theC50-
based statistics (Var C50,H C50) causes the largest degradation,
reducing the ROC-AUC on DF24 (−0.1169) and RW (−0.042).
We further analyze the contribution of the statistical descriptors.
Removing variance-related features ( Var SNR,Var C50) leads
to a noticeable drop, while removing entropy-based features
(HSNR,H C50) also degrades performance across datasets. Re-
moving the variance ratio featurervar results in an additional
performance decrease (−0.0193 AUC on DF24), indicating that
the relative variability between acoustic measures provides com-
plementary information. Overall, best performance is achieved
when all physical features are jointly utilized.
Effectiveness of universal sound separation.We evaluate
the impact of the sound separation model [ 14] by comparing
detection performance with and without SAM-audio (see Ta-
ble 4). Using raw audio yields ROC-AUC scores of 0.5945 on
DF24 and 0.5671 on RW, whereas applying SAM-audio im-
proves them to 0.7329 and 0.6510, respectively. This indicates
that separating speech from environmental noise enables more
reliable acoustic statistics for detection.

audio-visual deepfake detection in dynamic speaking videos.

ing speaker distance and speech energy. Our method estimates
speaker distance from video and extracts distance-dependent
