Abstract
This study presents an ensemble-based framework for classify-
ing Phonotraumatic (PVH) and Non-Phonotraumatic V ocal Hy-
perfunction (NPVH) using long-term neck-surface accelerome-
ter data. A key factor in enhancing performance was the imple-
mentation of temporal data segmentation focused on specific
periods of daily vocal activity. Models were developed using
the NeckVibe Challenge dataset, comprising 582 individuals,
including PVH/NPVH patients and matched healthy controls.
On the official challenge test set, our ensemble solution for
PVH detection achieved an AUC of 0.925, securing 1st place.
Furthermore, our NPVH model reached an AUC of 0.820, sig-
nificantly surpassing baseline and SOTA benchmarks. These
findings underscore the role of feature engineering and tempo-
ral optimization in advancing machine learning for ambulatory
healthcare. This work was part of the NeckVibe Challenge, or-
ganized by the V ocal Hyperfunction Clinical Research Center.

1. Introduction
V ocal hyperfunction (VH) is characterized by chronic patterns
of vocal abuse or misuse, resulting from excessive or poorly
regulated muscular effort within the phonatory system [1]. As
a foundational element in many common voice disorders, VH
was initially divided by Hillman et al. into adducted and nonad-
ducted forms [1], which have been more recently redefined as
phonotraumatic (PVH) and nonphonotraumatic (NPVH) vari-
ants [2].
Specifically, PVH involves the development of benign or-
ganic changes, such as vocal fold nodules and polyps [3]. Prior
research indicates that vocal fold nodules develop as an adap-
tive response to long-term phonatory stress, characterized by
repeated tissue damage and ongoing inflammation of the vocal
fold tissue [4, 5]. In contrast, NPVH manifests as persistent vo-
cal fatigue and impaired voice quality (dysphonia) despite the
absence of structural lesions or other underlying pathologies, a
condition frequently classified as primary muscle tension dys-
phonia [6]. NPVH may arise from a variety of heterogeneous
causes, including psychological factors such as stress and emo-
tional trauma, as well as physiological factors like chronic irri-
tation of the laryngeal and pharyngeal mucosa [7].
Recent research has increasingly leveraged machine learn-
ing (ML) to enhance the detection and monitoring of vocal
hyperfunction in naturalistic settings [3, 8, 9]. By employing
ambulatory neck-surface acceleration (NSA), which utilizes a
miniature accelerometer placed below the larynx, researchers
can noninvasively capture skin vibrations. These signals serve
as a robust proxy for glottal airflow and vocal fold dynamics
while circumventing the privacy concerns and environmental
noise sensitivity inherent in acoustic recordings [10, 11]. Ad-
vanced signal processing techniques, such as impedance-based
inverse filtering (IBIF), are often employed to derive aerody-
namic features from these acceleration signals [12, 9]. Super-
vised ML algorithms are trained on high-order summary statis-
tics of sound pressure level, fundamental frequency, and spec-
tral tilt to differentiate between vocally healthy individuals and
those with hyperfunctional disorders [3, 8]. These models have
demonstrated high diagnostic performance, with reported areas
under the ROC curve (AUC) reaching 0.82 for PVH and 0.78 for
NPVH detection, showing a potential for long-term clinical as-
sessment and real-time biofeedback interventions [12, 13, 14].
In our challenge work, we primarily focused on data pre-
processing to better capture variability in daily vocal behavior.
Rather than relying solely on full-day aggregated features, we
explored multiple strategies for partitioning each participant’s
continuous recordings into smaller segments. This allowed us
to investigate whether alternative temporal splits could reveal
additional discriminative patterns relevant for detecting both
phonotraumatic and nonphonotraumatic vocal hyperfunction.
By employing separate model architectures for each disor-
der, we achieved first-place in the NeckVibe challenge PVH
detection (Task 1) and outperformed current SOTA results
for NPVH (Task 2)..
2. Methodology
2.1. Dataset
The dataset used in this study was released as part of the Neck-
Vibe Challenge and consists of long-term ambulatory neck-
surface accelerometer (ACC) recordings collected during par-
ticipants’ daily lives. In total, the dataset includes 582 indi-
viduals: 213 patients diagnosed with phonotraumatic vocal hy-
perfunction (PVH) and 169 age-, sex-, and occupation-matched
healthy controls, as well as 116 patients with nonphonotrau-
matic vocal hyperfunction (NPVH) and 84 matched controls.
The ACC signals were processed into 50 ms frame-level time-
series features, including fundamental frequency (f0), ACC-
derived sound pressure level (SPL), cepstral peak prominence
(CPP), slope of harmonic amplitude decay of H1–H8 (spectral
tilt), difference in magnitude between first and second harmon-
ics (H1–H2), low-to-high spectral power ratio (L/H ratio), and
glottal airflow–based measures derived via impedance-based in-
verse filtering (IBIF). For a subset of participants, IBIF features
are unavailable due to data-collection limitations and were han-
dled accordingly in our analysis. In addition, behavioral masks
for voice activity and singing detection were provided, along
with demographic information (sex, anonymized subject ID,

2.2. Preprocessing
2.2.1. Preprocessing strategy 1: Ten Daily Segments
To model intra-day variability for PVH classification, acoustic
features were aggregated within ten partially overlapping time
windows spanning the full 24-hour cycle. These windows in-
cluded both shorter and longer segments (around 3 to 5 hours),
as well as a wrap-around night window to ensure complete tem-
poral coverage.

The use of overlapping windows reduces sensitivity to tem-
poral boundary effects, captures transitions between adjacent
intervals, and supports improved generalization across record-
ing days.
All statistics were computed using only voiced frames
within each time window. The analyzed acoustic features com-
prised Cepstral Peak Prominence (CPP), H1–H2, Spectral Tilt,
Low-to-High Frequency Energy Ratio (LH ratio), Acceleration
amplitude (dBcms2), and Sound Pressure Level (SPL).
For each acoustic feature and time window, five statistical
descriptors were extracted: mean, median, standard deviation,
10th percentile, and 90th percentile. In addition, the fraction
of voiced frames within each window was included as a sepa-
rate feature. This resulted in 6 × 10 × 5 = 300 window-based
statistical features and 10 voiced-fraction features, yielding 310
frame-based features per recording day.
IBIF features were computed over the full day using the
same statistical descriptors, yielding 8 × 5 = 40 additional fea-
tures. In total, 350 features were extracted per recording day.
2.2.2. Preprocessing strategy 2: Speech — Singing separation
Using the same set of acoustic features as in the first approach,
we aimed to capture distinct acoustic characteristics of different
vocalization types by aggregating features separately for voiced
speech and voiced singing. This separation recognizes that
speech and singing exhibit different phonatory behaviors and
may carry complementary diagnostic information for PVH clas-
sification. For each vocalization type, the same five statistical
descriptors (mean, median, standard deviation, 10th percentile,
and 90th percentile) were computed for each time window and
acoustic feature, effectively doubling the feature space. To man-
age the increased dimensionality, the number of daily time win-
dows was reduced from ten overlapping segments to four non-
overlapping intervals (06:00–10:00, 10:00–15:00, 15:00–20:00,
and 20:00–24:00).
2.2.3. Preprocessing strategy 3: NPVH Baseline improvements
The baseline was defined as the approach used by Cortés et al.
[12], which proposed working on 5-minute segments containing
at least 0.5 % voiced data. The segment duration and minimum
voiced-data threshold were treated as hyperparameters of the
method, and a wide range of other values were tested. For the
window, we tested values of (60, 150, 300, 600, 1800, 3600)
seconds, and for the threshold of voiced data following values
were chosen (0 %, 0.1 %, 0.5 %, 1 %, 5 %, 10 %, 20 %, 50 %).
Additionally, all voiced, speech-only, and singing-only frames
were evaluated; however, restricting the analysis to singing-only
frames resulted in insufficient data for many subjects. During
the experiments, optional aggregation was applied to combine
the data into a single sample per day or per subject, substantially
reducing the number of samples available for modeling. For
these aggregation procedures, the mean, 10th percentile, 90th
percentile, and standard deviation were calculated. Statistics
were computed using all provided features, except those with
a correlation coefficient greater than>0.9or those containing
missing values.
2.3. PVH Task - Model 1 (Boosted Trees)
For the PVH task, we employed a gradient boosting classi-
fier using XGBoost (version 3.1.3) with strong regularization to
promote generalization across subjects [15]. The pipeline uses
a 10 daily time-segment feature extraction strategy described in
Section 2.2.1. The model was configured with 1000 trees and
a low learning rate (0.03) to ensure stable convergence. Tree
depth was limited (max_depth= 6), and a large minimum child
weight (25) together with split regularization (γ= 0.2) were
used to prevent overfitting to subject-specific patterns. Addi-
tional stochasticity was introduced via row subsampling (0.6)
and feature subsampling (0.7). Both L1 (α= 0.7) and L2
(λ= 1.5) regularization were applied. The model optimized
the binary logistic objective with AUC as the evaluation metric.
All results are reported using Leave-One-Group-Out
(LOGO) cross-validation, where groups correspond to individ-
ual subjects. This ensures strict subject-level separation be-
tween training and validation data and prevents subject leakage.

2.4. PVH Task - Model 2 (Logistic Regression)
We also explored an alternative approach for PVH detection by
training a Logistic Regression classifier [16] on data prepro-
cessed as described in Section 2.2.2. To identify the most suit-
able model for this preprocessing strategy, we evaluated several
classification algorithms, including Support Vector Classifier
(SVC), Decision Tree, Random Forest, and XGBoost. Among
these, Logistic Regression achieved the highest performance
metrics and demonstrated the greatest capability to extract in-
formative patterns from the preprocessed data. The model was
implemented using the scikit-learn (version 1.8.0) [17] library
and trained with maximum iterations (1000), using the default
solver ‘lbfgs‘, L2 regularization withC= 1.0, tolerance of
1×10−4, and intercept fitting enabled. Consistent with our pre-
vious approach, it was trained using LOGO cross-validation to
ensure robust evaluation.
2.5. PVH Task - Ensemble
To leverage the complementary properties of the two ap-
proaches, an ensemble model was created by combining the
outputs of the XGBoost model (Section 2.3) and the Logistic
Regression model (Section 2.4). The final prediction probabil-
ity was calculated as the average of the probabilities produced
by the two models. No additional training was performed for
the ensemble. Evaluation was conducted using the same LOGO
cross-validation protocol to ensure direct comparability with the
individual models.
2.6. NPVH Task
Across all performed experiments, the original time window of
300 seconds, with at least 10 % speech and singing data present,
was found to consistently achieve the highest scores. It is impor-
tant to note that aggregating all the time windows for a patient
into a singular sample, and calculating additional statistics from
those metrics improved results.
For this configuration we incorporated XGBoost. The
model was configured with 500 trees and a low learning rate
(0.03) to ensure stable convergence. Tree depth was limited
(max depth= 4), and moderate minimum child weight (17) to-
gether with aggressive split regularization (γ= 7) was used
to prevent overfitting to subject-specific patterns. Additional
stochasticity was introduced via row subsampling (0.8) and fea-
ture subsampling (0.4). Both L1 (α= 0.2) and L2 (λ= 25)
regularization were applied to further control model complex-
ity. Class imbalance was addressed via positive class weighting
(8.5). The model optimized the binary logistic objective with
AUC as the evaluation metric.
3. Results
3.1. PVH Subject-Level Performance

ject. At this recording level, Model 1 achieved a ROC AUC of
0.819 and an accuracy of 0.76 on 3,278 recordings (class 0: n =
2,127; class 1: n = 1,151). To obtain subject-level classification,
we averaged the predicted probabilities across all days for each
subject. Aggregating predictions per subject under the same
LOGO protocol resulted in improved performance for Model 1,

4. Test set predictions
For the final evaluation in Task 1, we adopted an ensemble ap-
proach by averaging the prediction probabilities from XGBoost
(Sec. 2.3) and Logistic Regression (Sec. 2.4).This ensem-
ble strategy achieved an ROC AUC of 0.925, securing 1st
place among all challenge submissionsand significantly out-
performing the baseline of 0.82 AUC [12]. For Task 2, we uti-
lized the model and preprocessing strategy detailed in Section
2.6. Our best NPVH approach achieved an ROC AUC of 0.820
on the test set, placing 4th in the challenge and surpassing the
baseline performance of 0.78 AUC.
5. Discussion
The primary contribution of this work is the identification of an
optimal feature selection strategy and model optimization for
the binary classification of PVH. Our analysis reveals that low
variability in H1-H2 during the afternoon and evening serves
as a key predictor of pathology, as evidenced by the concen-
tration of blue positive SHAP values (Figure 4, SHapley Ad-
ditive exPlanations by Lundberg et al. [19]). On the other
hand, high variability during morning hours is a strong predictor
for the combined healthy control and NPVH group. Decreased
variation in H1-H2 has been linked to phonotrauma, reflecting
more abrupt glottal closure and increased laryngeal forces [20].
The predictive significance of the IBIF Closing Quotient (CQ)
further validates these results (Figure 4). Specifically, the red
SHAP values indicate that elevated CQ is a key indicator of
PVH, consistent with established links between CQ and glottal
closure pathologies [21]. Integrating singing data into the en-
semble models further enhanced classification accuracy. This
finding supports the hypothesis that the elevated vocal demands
of singing, specifically higher fundamental frequencies, render
the physiological markers of PVH more pronounced compared
to normal speech [22].
For NPVH classification, the preprocessing scenarios in-
volving daily segmentation (2.2.1) and speech-singing separa-
tion (2.2.2) were surprisingly less effective than for PVH. In-
stead, the best results were obtained by aggregating data into
5-minute segments. This suggests that NPVH is better identi-
fied by transient states, captured by 90th percentile statistics,
rather than by consistent patterns throughout the day. A pos-
sible explanation is that unlike PVH, where structural lesions
(like nodules) create a constant physiological footprint, NPVH
is often characterized by intermittent muscle tension. These
brief episodes of hyperfunction may be ’diluted’ when looking
at wide afternoon/evening windows, but they become statisti-
cally visible when analyzed in shorter, high-intensity intervals.
Finally, we evaluated several classic neural network archi-
tectures for the PVH classification task, including Multi-Layer
Perceptron (MLP), Convolutional Neural Network (CNN), and
Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) models with one to two lay-
ers [23, 24, 25]. Despite their low complexity, all models exhib-
ited substantial overfitting (results not shown in this article). We
experimented with various data aggregation methods and train-
ing on raw features; however, none of these approaches led to
improvement. We concluded that the limited number of distinct
subjects was the primary cause of this overfitting.

6. Conclusions
This study demonstrates that partitioning continuous ambu-
latory recordings into smaller daily segments significantly
improves the detection of both phonotraumatic and non-
phonotraumatic vocal hyperfunction. By accounting for these
daily variations in vocal dynamics, our machine learning mod-
els consistently outperformed established baselines in terms of
accuracy and generalization. Notably, our optimized ensemble
model achieved a 1st place in the NeckVibe Challenge PVH de-
tection task, reaching an ROC AUC of 0.925. These findings
underscore the importance of utilizing ensemble architectures
and temporal segmentation to develop robust, high-performance
diagnostic tools for ambulatory voice monitoring.
