Vowel Allophony Improves Maximum-Likelihood Classification
of Warlpiri Consonants

Abstract
Australian languages typically have a large number of place
contrasts and relatively few manner contrasts. We investigated
the acoustic cues that native speakers of Warlpiri, an Australian
language with stops in five places of articulation, could use to
distinguish them. Using a maximum-likelihood classifier, we
show that acoustic cues intrinsic to consonants are not sufficient
for this purpose. Adding information from consonant-extrinsic
acoustic cues in adjacent vowels improved consonant classifi-
cation for both CV and VC sequences. In both CVs and VCs,
consonant classification benefited the most from allophony in
formants at midpoints of adjacent vowels. We discuss the im-
plications of our results and the potential of using maximum-
likelihood classifiers for generating predictions based on proba-
bilistic models of speech perception for low resource languages.
Index Terms: Warlpiri, consonants, place of articulation,
acoustics, coarticulation, allophony, maximum-likelihood clas-

1. Introduction
Australian languages are well known for having a large num-
ber of place distinctions in their consonants, particularly for
stops [1, 2, 3, 4]. For instance, it is most common across the
world’s languages to have three place distinctions in stop conso-
nants: bilabial, dental/alveolar, and velar. However, Australian
languages typically have 4-6 place distinctions. These include
bilabial, dental, alveolar, retroflex, palatal and velar contrasts.
How do speakers of Australian languages distinguish a crowded
place continuum with the high accuracy [5] observed in native
listeners?
Cross-linguistically, many acoustic cues to stop place have
been identified. These include cues intrinsic to the stop itself:
stop duration [6]; spectral moments of the burst, i.e. center of
gravity, standard deviation, skewness and kurtosis [7, 8]; and
relative intensity of the stop burst with respect to the following
vowel [9, 10]. The locus of the second formant has also been
shown to be a primary descriptor of stop place of articulation
[11]. Additionally, cues extrinsic to the stop, in particular sec-
ond formant cues in adjacent vowels, are argued to be not only
helpful but sufficient to distinguish consonant place [12].
However, there is an extensive body of research showing
that the cues mentioned above are not equally effective in distin-
guishing the wider array of stop place distinctions in Australian
languages. For instance, F2 locus and F2 transition into the
vowel, often identified as a primary cue to stop place in other
languages, has been reported to be insufficient to distinguish

Yanyuwa and Yindjibarndi [13, 14]. Small differences in artic-

stop consonants, which Butcher and colleagues have argued is

signal consonant place over others [2, 1, 15, 4]. As a result,
coarticulatory influences of the vowel on the consonant have
been argued to be reduced, particularly in CV sequences.
1.1. Stop cues in Australian languages
Instead, based on extensive fieldwork, it has been argued that
distinguishing stop place in Australian languages requires a
more distributed constellation of acoustic cues. These include
many intrinsic cues like spectral tilt and skewness, which have
been shown to help differentiate both the velar and palatal in Pit-
jantjatjara [16] and velar and bilabial in several other Australian
languages [2]. Duration of stops, in particular voice onset time,
has also been reported to help identify place of articulation, dif-
ferentiating retroflex and alveolar consonants for 10 speakers of
several Australian languages [2]; apical coronals from laminals
in Wubuy [17]; and velars from retroflexes and alveolars, bilabi-
als from all coronals, and apicals from the palatals in Warlpiri
[18]. Additional stop-intrinsic cues include E-M/H, a measure
of spectral tilt quantifying the difference between spectral en-
ergy in high- and mid-band frequencies, which has been shown
to differentiate alveolars and retroflexes in some prosodic con-
texts in Wubuy [17]. Finally, another intrinsic cue, F2 locus,
has also been reported to distinguish bilabial and velar stops in
several Australian languages [1].
Extrinsic cues like vowel formants also distinguish stop
consonant place in Australian languages. For example, formant
transitions in the F2 and F3 of VC sequences have been shown
to vary between alveolars and retroflexes in Gupapuyngu, and
a lower F3 for the retroflex may cue this contrast in CVs in
careful speech [2]. Past work has also shown that alveolars and
retroflexes in Wubuy are differentiated by F1 and F2 transitions
in CV sequences and F3 transitions in VCs [19].
Overall, in Australian languages the cues that distinguish
place of articulation are varied and it is unclear how listeners
of a specific language integrate these cues to successfully dis-
tinguish native phoneme contrasts. Adding to the complexity is
evidence that specific acoustic cues to consonant place can vary
substantively in CV and VC sequences [1].
1.2. The current study
Our goal in this paper was to take a more systemic view of
consonant perception, and crucially demonstrate the utility of
maximum-likelihood classifiers as a tool to complement field-
work in under-resourced languages. To do so we investigated

how intrinsic and extrinsic cues jointly signal place of con-
striction in the Australian language Warlpiri. Warlpiri is a
Pama-Nyungan language spoken in central Australia by approx-
imately 2,600 speakers [20]. Phonologically, it is a prototypi-
cal Australian language, with stops at five places of articulation
without a phonemic voicing contrast: /p/, /t/, /ú/, /c/ and /k/. It
has three vowels, /i/, /a/ and /u/, which are contrasted in length.
To investigate how acoustic cues contribute to conso-
nant place identification in Warlpiri, we trained a maximum-
likelihood classifier over CV and VC sequences with inter-
vocalic Cs on various combinations of acoustic cues. A
maximum-likelihood classifier minimises categorisation error
rate over a test set balanced across categories (either CV or VC
sequences), presenting an upper bound on the performance of
an optimal listener under Bayesian models that have been found
to capture aspects of human speech perception [21, 22, 23].
That is, maximum-likelihood classifiers can provide a window
into what is possible for an optimal human listener over the en-
tire system of consonants.
In the baseline model, we used consonant-intrinsic cues
proposed in the literature to distinguish the place of articulation
for stop consonants. These include duration of the consonant
[2], burst spectral moments, and F2 onset locus [2, 17, 16], and
E-M/H [17]. All of these cues have been reported to be useful to
distinguish at least one pair of stop consonants in an Australian
language. We also used relative burst intensity, a cue that has
been identified as useful in other languages of the world. Our
results show that Warlpiri stops are quite confusable based on
the acoustic information in the consonant alone.
Next, we evaluated the relative contribution of two extrinsic
cues to reduce consonant place confusability in Warlpiri. The
two cues we assessed were formant transitions and allophonic
differences in neighboring vowel quality (provided by the esti-
mate of the formant values at the vowel midpoint). The utility
of formant transitions to signal differences in consonant place
has been widely attested in many different languages, includ-
ing those in Australia. The role of allophonic differences in the
vowel itself to signal differences in consonant place is relatively
understudied.
We know based on fieldwork in Bolivia, that subtle differ-
ences in consonant place, for example between velar and uvu-
lar consonants, is reinforced by Cochabamba Quechua speakers
with a difference in height of adjacent vowels [24]. Gallagher
also confirms that Cochabamba Quechua listeners use vowel
height in the surrounding vowels to distinguish the velar-uvular
place contrasts. Such facilitatory effects naturally emerge in
Bayesian models of speech perception [21, 22, 23] if perception
is optimised over sequences longer than a segment, allowing
listeners to exploit conditional dependence of phonetic distri-
butions on adjacent segments [25]. Consistent with predictions
of Bayesian models of speech perception, listeners have been
shown to use vowel quality cues to distinguish adjacent conso-
nants based on long-term exposure with their native language
[26] as well as based on short-term exposure in the lab [27].
We evaluated the relative contribution of the two extrinsic
cues to improve consonant place classification in Warlpiri by
augmenting the input to the maximum-likelihood classifier in
one of two ways. We either added the formant transition cues or
the vowel midpoint information to the baseline model with the
consonant-intrinsic cues. By comparing the relative improve-
ment in classification of consonants with the addition of each
of the two extrinsic cues, we sought to identify the cues that
listeners could exploit to effectively distinguish Warlpiri stop
consonants. A relative improvement in consonant classification
with the addition of either formant transitions or vowel midpoint
information or both would confirm whether these extrinsic cues
to distinguish consonant place in Warlpiri can be exploited by
listeners to distinguish place contrasts.
2. Methods
2.1. Corpus
Data were sourced from Carmel O’Shannessy’s field recordings
from DoReCo [28], which contains semi-spontaneous mono-
logic narratives from a structured story-telling elicitation task.
The data were forced aligned using MAUS [29], and bound-
aries were hand-corrected by the archive (see [30] for process-
ing pipeline). All audio files labelled as having “good” acoustic
quality in the archive were kept, resulting in an analysis of 14
female speakers aged 25-50.
Because we were interested in differences in classification
of consonants within CV versus VC sequences, we restricted
ourselves to consonants drawn from VCV sequences that could
be included in both VC and CV data sets. This resulted in an
initial data set of 5,081 tokens for both CVs and VCs.
Segments orthographically transcribed as retroflex flaps
were included in place of those transcribed as retroflex stops
/ú/. This was because a) there were very few (10 total) tokens
of /ú/ within VCVs, and b) the retroflex flap /ó/ and stop /ú/ have
been reported to neutralise in Warlpiri in intervocalic position
[18]. Thus, the five consonants included in the analysis were
/p/, /t/, /ó/, /c/, and /k/.
2.2. Acoustic cue extraction
We used Praat [31] to extract 31 acoustic cues from the corpus.
These cues were split into four groups labelled as follows:
•S:consonant-intrinsic cues including stop-internal (duration
and burst COG, standard deviation, skewness kurtosis, E-
H/M, and intensity) and formant locus cues (F1-F4 at con-
sonant/vowel boundary)
•T:formant transition cues (change in F1-F4 in the 25% of
vowel adjacent to consonant)
•M:vowel formant cues (F1-F4 at vowel midpoint)
The same set of 7 stop-internal cues (a subset of S) were
used for classification of both CV and VC sequences, while the
remaining 24 cues (formant locus cues within S as well as T
and M) were specific to either the preceding or following vowel.
Thus, each classifier used at most 19 cues.
Although not all phonemic stops in the corpus include a
burst due to lenition, it was not possible to manually check all
tokens for presence of a burst, which MAUS [29] does not anno-
tate. For consistency, extraction of spectral measures made use
of burst locations automatically identified using AutoVOT [32]
with its pretrained classifier. Spectral moments were extracted
following the approach of [33] from a 0.0256 ms window cen-
tered on these bursts and E-H/M was calculated using high and
low frequency bands of 3,500 Hz - 8,000 Hz and 1,250 Hz -
3,000 Hz respectively, following [17].
Not all acoustic cues could be extracted for all tokens due
to errors while running AutoVOT [32] or failure of Praat [31]
formant tracking to identify a formant. In the end, the data set
for the CV-classifier included 4,755 tokens with all CV-relevant
cues and the data set for the VC-classifier included 4,801 tokens
with all VC-relevant cues.

4. Discussion
To our knowledge, the current study represents the first use of
a probabilistic classification model to analyse how optimal use
of available acoustic cues may facilitate perception of conso-
nant place of articulation in an Australian language. Our re-
sults demonstrate the utility of using maximum-likelihood clas-
sifiers to investigate segment identification across the whole
system, and show that these methodologies can be applied to
low-resource languages using semi-spontaneous speech.
Using this approach we were able to classify both conso-
nants and vowels using a large number of cues that have been
reported to be useful in a variety of languages in Australia and
outside. Further, using model comparison we were able to eval-
uate the utility of specific cue combinations. Our results show
that provided only stop-intrinsic cues, the consonants of Walpiri
are quite confusable, as has been claimed broadly for Australian
languages [2]. However, the addition of extrinsic cues, specif-
ically from vowel midpoints, improves consonant classification
significantly. We found this pattern of results for both CV and
VC sequences. Further, we found that despite vowel allophony,
there was no drop in classification accuracy of vowels.
We speculate that such vowel allophony is more likely to
be useful to distinguish subtle place contrasts in languages with
smaller inventories. As such, Warlpiri with three vowels is an
ideal test case. Whether we see differences in the utility of
vowel allophony to distinguish consonant place contingent on
the size of the inventory, predicted by McGahay’s Iterated Con-
fusion Minimization model [25], we leave for future work.
One notable result was that the VC models performed
slightly worse than CV models when intrinsic cues were in-
cluded (all models that included S). Place identification of Cs
benefited more from information in the midpoint of the follow-
ing vowel. This is consistent with re-syllabification of the me-
dial C with the following V in VCV sequences, as predicted by
in-phase coupling within Articulatory Phonology [36, 37] or the
Maximal Onset Principle within Optimality Theory [38]. Alter-
natively, it may simply reflect greater influence of the following
vowel on C release bursts due to closer temporal proximity.
Although not presented here, we can use our maximum-
likelihood classifier to generate confusion matrices based on
acoustic overlap using any possible cue combination. Future
work exploring the confusability of consonant pairs using more
targeted combinations of acoustic cues may provide a bench-
mark to arbitrate between proposals of which cue combinations
are most informative for native listeners. For instance, analy-
sis not reported here shows that duration is particularly helpful
among S (stop-internal) cues for distinguishing retroflex conso-
nants, consistent with their realization as flaps with short du-
ration. Future work might similarly apply more fine-grained
analysis of individual cues to other consonants.
