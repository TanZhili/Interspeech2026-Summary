Abstract 
This paper proposes a speaker-independent speech synthesis 
method that generates speech waveforms from real -time MRI 
(rtMRI) videos. The proposed model uses EfficientNetV2 for 
per-frame feature extraction, E -Branchformer for temporal 
modeling, and BigVGAN -v2 for speech synthesis. Speaker 
characteristics are acquired from rtMRI videos through cross -
modal training with speaker embeddings, enabling speaker -
independent synthesis from rtMRI videos alone. Experiments 
using the USC 75-Speaker Speech MRI Database demonstrate 
that the proposed method achieves favorable linguistic accuracy 
for read speech in a speaker-independent setting. F0 correlation 
analysis indicates that relative prosodic patterns are captured to 
a reasonable extent, as expected from visual arti culatory 
information, though absolute F0 estimation remains 
challenging. Speaker similarity results show that although 
within-gender speaker discrimination proved difficult, speaker 
identity is reproduced to a reasonable degree. 
Index Terms : speech synthesis, speaker -independent, real -
time MRI, articulatory movement 
1. Introduction 
In recent years, several methods have been proposed for speech 
synthesis using articulatory data recorded by real -time MRI 
(rtMRI) [1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7 , 8 ]. Unlike text -to-speech (TTS) 
systems, these methods consider actual articulatory movements 
and provide a more realistic approach to speech synthesis. 
When combined with text -to-MRI [9] or speech -to-MRI [10] 
technologies, this approach holds promise for applications in 
computer-assisted pronunciation training (CAPT) systems [11] 
and rehabilitation for individuals with speech disorders. 
Among the various methods for recording articulatory 
movements, rtMRI [12, 13, 14] records the midsagittal plane of 
the upper airway using fast MRI, offering a significant 
advantage over other approaches in that it captures most of the 
vocal organs, including the soft palate and larynx, at relatively 
high spatial resolution. Although rtMRI has drawbacks such as 
challenging recording conditions and relatively low temporal 
resolution, speech synthesis methods using rtMRI videos or 
extracted vocal tract contours as input have shown promising 
results [ 1, 2, 3, 4, 5 ]. These methods have evol ved from 
estimating only vocal tract shape parameters [6, 7, 8] to directly 
estimating speech including fundamental frequency (F0). 
Although high-quality speech synthesis from rtMRI videos has 
been achieved, conventional methods typically employ 
speaker-dependent modeling and cannot synthesize speech 
from unseen spea kers. Recently, a TTS -based approach has 
been proposed that demonstrates reasonable performance on 
unseen speaker data [ 4]. However, in this approach, duration, 
prosody, and speaker characterist ics are generated by the TTS 
mechanism rather than being derived from the rtMRI videos. 
To realize speaker -independent speech synthesis that 
leverages articulatory information from rtMRI videos, it is 
necessary to robustly extract linguistic information, prosody, 
and speaker characteristics. Considering that F0 can be obtained 
from rtMRI videos in a speaker- dependent manner [15, 16], it 
is reasonable to expect that relative prosody can be estimated 
from rtMRI videos. Furthermore, since the entire vocal tract is 
captured in the midsagittal plane, it can be anticipated that some 
speaker characteristics can also be extracted from rtMRI videos. 
In this study, we propose a model consisting of EfficientNetV2 
[17] and E -Branchformer [18 ] to estimate  mel-spectrograms 
from rtMRI videos, and synthesize speech waveforms using the 
BigVGAN-v2 vocoder [19]. During training, we use speaker 
embeddings to guide the model in acquiring speaker-relevant 
information from rtMRI videos through cross -modal training, 
enabling speaker-independent synthesis from unseen speakers 
at inference without requiring reference speech. We conducted 
experiments using the USC 75- Speaker Speech MRI Database 
[20], which is significantly larger than conventional datasets 
and offers high temporal resolution. We evaluate the proposed 
method on unseen speakers in terms of linguistic accuracy using 
word error rate (WER) and character error rate (CER), as well 
as prosody and speaker characteristics. 
2. Related work 
2.1. Speech synthesis from rtMRI videos 
Early approaches to speech synthesis from rtMRI videos 
focused on estimating acoustic features representing vocal tract 
shape, such as MGC -LSP and mel -cepstrum, while supplying 
fundamental frequency and other source -related parameters 
independently to signal -processing-based vocoders [6, 7, 8]. 
This was because the low spatiotemporal resolution of rtMRI 
prevents direct recording of vocal fold vibrations. With the 
advancement of deep learning, several methods have since been 
proposed that estimate complete a coustic parameters or even 
speech waveforms directly from rtMRI videos [1, 2, 3, 4, 5]. 
Yu et al. proposed a method that uses a 3DCNN -BiLSTM 
to estimate mel -spectrograms from rtMRI videos, which are 
then converted to speech using a WaveGlow vocoder [1]. Their 
model estimates the mel -spectrum corresponding to each 
segment from 13 consecutive frames of rtMRI video, making it 
the first approach to synthesize speech from rtMRI videos alone. 
While the method successfully reproduced broad spectral 
shapes, the  authors noted that further improvements were 
necessary to achieve satisfactory speech quality. 
In our previous work, we proposed an ENv2 -BiLSTM 
model leveraging EfficientNetV2 for powerful image feature

Wu et al. proposed an approach that bypasses intermediate 
acoustic representations by synthesizing speech directly from 
vocal tract contours extracted from MRI images [3]. The 
method extracts 170 vocal tract contour points from each MRI 
frame semiautomatically, selects the 115 most relevant to 
speech production, and feeds them into an autoregressive HiFi-
CAR vocoder [ 21]. By  directly inputting this lightweight 
representation into a neural vocoder, the method eliminates the 
need for intermediate representati ons such as spectrograms, 
achieving high computational efficiency and speech fidelity. 
All of the above methods rely on speaker-dependent 
modeling and thus cannot synthesize speech from unseen 
speakers. In contrast, Shah et al. proposed an approach allowing 
application to unseen speakers [4]. Their method uses AV -
HuBERT [22, 2 3] to infer phoneme sequences from rtMRI 
videos, which are then passed to a VITS-based TTS system [24] 
for speech synthesis. Although the method demonstrates 
reasonable performance on unseen speakers, the TTS -based 
synthesis pipeline does not directly reflect speaker 
characteristics, duration, or prosodic features that could 
otherwise be extracted from th e rtMRI videos.  More recently, 
Li et al. proposed a conditional variational autoencoder for 
speaker-independent speech synthesis from rtMRI videos [25], 
without explicitly modeling or evaluating the reproduction of 
speaker characteristics or prosody. 
2.2. F0 estimation from rtMRI articulatory data 
Motivated by findings from our speech synthesis work [2], we 
investigated whether F0 can be directly estimated from vocal 
tract configuration captured in rtMRI [15, 1 6]. Using a 
relatively high -resolution rtMRI dataset [2 6, 2 7], we 
constructed a model that connects an EfficientNetV2 encoder 
to an F0 estimation head and demonstrated that F0 can be 
estimated with sufficient accuracy from a single MRI frame. 
Furthermore, using optical flow analysis, we visualized 
articulatory move ments associated with F0 chang es and 
confirmed that vocal tract shape variations correlated with F0 
are observable in rtMRI images across multiple speakers. These 
results suggest that prosodic information in the form of relative 
F0 changes can be extracted from rtMRI videos even in 
speaker-independent settings. 
3. Proposed method 
We propose an approach in which speaker characteristics are 
acquired from rtMRI videos through cross-modal training with 
speaker embeddings, enabling speaker-independent synthesis at 
inference without requiring reference speech. An overview of 
the proposed model is shown in Figure 1. 
Per-frame image features are extracted from the rtMRI 
video sequence using EfficientNetV2-B0 [17], which proved 
effective in the speech synthesis method [2], and temporal 
dependencies are modeled using E -Branchformer Base [18], 
which demonstrates strong performance in speech tasks with 
stable training. EfficientNetV2 is used up to its global average 
pooling layer, and an input projection consisting of a Linear 
layer and Layer Normalization is applied to align its output 
dimension with the feature dimension of E -Branchformer. The 
resulting feature sequence is conditioned on speaker 
information via Feature -wise Linear Modulation (FiLM) [28], 
which applies a channel -wise affine transformation to 
incorporate conditional information into the representation. The 
scale and shift parameters γ and β are shared across the time 
dimension, applying uniform modulation to the entire sequence. 
A skip connection from the E-Branchformer output is added to 
the modulated features, which are then projected to the mel -
spectrogram dimension via a Linear layer. Speech waveforms 
are synthesized from the estimated mel -spectrograms using 
BigVGAN-v2 [ 19], a high-quality neural vocoder with 
robustness to unseen speakers. As the mel -spectrogram frame 
period is matched to the rtMRI video frame rate, the upsampling 
factors of BigVGAN-v2 are adjusted accordingly. 
To achieve speaker -independent synthesis, the model is 
trained using two parallel paths. In the first path, an X -vector 
[29] extracted from reference speech is projected through an 
MLP block to produce an audio-derived speaker representation. 
X-vectors are pre -trained on speaker verification using the 
VoxCeleb1/2 datasets  [30] and kept frozen during training, 
encoding a wide range of speaker characteristics beneficial for 
speech synthesis [31]. In the second path, the E -Branchformer 
output sequence is aggre gated over the time dimension via 
attention pooling, where attention weights are computed by a 
network consisting of a Linear layer, a Tanh activation, and a 
final Linear layer. The resulting pooled representation is then 
passed through an MLP block to yield an MRI-derived speaker 
representation. Both representations are independently fed into 
a shared FiLM generator, which itself consists of an MLP block, 
to produce the modulation parameters for their respective paths. 
Here, each MLP block comprises two repetitions of a Linear 
layer and GELU activation followed by a final Linear layer. The 
MRI-derived speaker representation is trained to align with the 
audio-derived representation via a cosine similarity loss, with 
the audio-derived side fixed through a stop-gradient operation:

4. Experimental setup 
4.1. Dataset 
We conducted experiments using the USC 75-Speaker Speech 
MRI Database [20], a large -scale English rtMRI corpus 
comprising read and spontaneous speech from 75 speakers (35 
male, 40 female), with approximately 17 minutes of data per 
speaker. The rtMRI videos were acquired at a frame rate of 
83.28 fps with a spatial resolution of 84×84 pixels. The audio 
was denoised using an adaptive filter and sampled at 20 kHz. 
For each speaker, we used approximately 10 minutes of read 
speech (including 3.5 minutes of nonce  syllables) and 5.5 
minutes of spontaneous speech, excluding non-speech posture 
recordings (e.g., yawning and swallowing). 
4.2. Preprocessing 
Although the audio was denoised using an adaptive filtering 
method [32], residual MRI scanner noise and reverberation 
artifacts remained due to the challenging acoustic environment 
inside the MRI scanner.  We therefore applied CleanMel -L-
mask [33], a deep learning-based speech enhancement method, 
to further reduce these artifacts. As a mask -based speech 
enhancement method, it preserves greater fidelity to the original 
signal compared to generative approaches. 
To ensure sufficient audio quality, we selected a subset of 
speakers for our experiments. Two speakers (sub58 and sub75) 
were excluded due to near -silent recordings. An additional 22 
speakers were excluded for insufficient audio quality, identified 
by inconsistent voice characteristics across sessions using 
speaker embeddings trained to capture speaker-specific texture 
information [34 ]. S peakers for whom the median Euclidean 
distance of session-level embeddings from their geometric 
centroid exceeded 1.25 were removed. This yielded 51 speakers 
in total (24 male, 27 female), who were randomly split into 
training, validation, and test sets of 43, 4, and 4 speakers, 
respectively, with stratification by gender. 
Audio was downsampled to 15,989 Hz, which is 192 times 
the rtMRI frame rate, so that the mel-spectrogram frame period

aligns with the video frame interval.  Mel-spectrograms were 
computed with a frame length of four rtMRI frames and a frame 
shift of one frame, yielding 80-dimensional log-scale features, 
which were then standardized over the training set.  F0 and 
aperiodicity were extracted using Harvest [35] and D4C [36], 
respectively, both from the WORLD vocoder [37]. Since 
Harvest is designed to extract F0 broadly, we restricted the F0 
correlation loss to frames with a mean aperiodicity index of 0.5 
or below to focus training on high-confidence voiced frames. 
4.3. Training and inference 
The model was trained to estimate mel-spectrograms from 384-
frame rtMRI segments (approximately 4.6 seconds) together 
with the corresponding speaker embedding. The loss weights in 
Eq. (3) were set  to 𝜆𝜆𝑀𝑀𝑀𝑀𝑀𝑀 = 0.4, 𝜆𝜆𝑐𝑐𝑐𝑐𝑐𝑐 = 0.1, and 𝜆𝜆𝐹𝐹0 = 0.1. 
AdamW was used as the optimizer with a learning rate of 5e-4, 
warmed up linearly for 50 epochs from 1e-6 and then annealed 
with cosine scheduling back to 1e -6 over 500 epochs in total.  
For data augmentation, a random affine transformation 
comprising translation (±10%), scaling (±10%), and rotation 
(±15°) was applied uniformly to all frames within each segment. 
Training was conducted on a single NVIDIA RTX PRO 6000 
GPU and converged in approximately 4 hours. The source code 
is available at https://github.com/y-otn/m2s-code. 
At inference, the model processes each recording session as 
a single input sequence, typically spanning approximately 
2,500 frames (around 30 s). In the proposed speaker-
independent setting, speaker characteristics are derived solely 
from the rtMRI video, without any reference speech. For 
comparison, we also evaluate an audio -based condition using 
an X -vector averaged over recordings of the target speaker 
excluding the current session to prevent information leakage. 
BigVGAN-v2 was pre-trained on the LibriTTS corpus [38] 
and fine -tuned using mel -spectrograms estimated from the 
training speakers' rtMRI videos. The upsampling factors of the 
generator were set to (4, 3, 2, 2, 2, 2), yielding a total 
upsampling ratio of 192. 
4.4. Evaluation metrics 
Linguistic accuracy was assessed using differential WER 
(dWER) and differential CER (dCER) [39], which use 
automatic speech recognition (ASR) outputs on the original 
speech as ground-truth references, since the dataset does not 
provide verbatim transcriptions. ASR was performed with 
NVIDIA Parakeet TDT 0.6B V2 1, and normalized transcripts 
were used for scoring. 
F0 reproduction was evaluated using F0 RMSE and Pearson 
correlation coefficient. We also report the correlation restricted 
to high-periodicity frames, consistent with the voiced frame 
selection criterion used in the F0 correlation loss during training. 
Speaker similarity was assessed using Speaker Encoder 
Cosine Similarity (SECS) [40, 41], computed as the cosine 
similarity between X -vector embeddings extracted from the 
synthesized and original speech.

method performs comparably to the a udio path  across all 
speakers, demonstrating that linguistic content is preserved 
even when speaker characteristics are derived solely from the 
rtMRI video. While favorable results were obtained for sub20 
and sub55, with dWER  around 8% and dCER  around 4%,

some speakers remain challenging for the proposed system. 
Table 2 presents a breakdown of rtMRI path results by speech 
type. Reading speech yielded the lowest error rates, with dWER 
ranging from 4.5% to 11.1% and dCER from 1.8% to 5.6% 
across test speakers. Spontaneous speech showed higher error 
rates, which we attribute to the presence of filled pauses and 
reduced articulatory precision compared to read speech, though 
the results f or sub20 and sub55 suggest that the proposed 
method can generalize to spontaneous utterances beyond the 
read speech domain. Nonce syllables yielded the highest dWER 
and dCER values, which we attribute primarily to the limited 
accuracy of ASR systems on non-lexical utterances. 
Table 3 presents F0 RMSE, Pearson correlation coefficient 
for F0, and SECS for each condition. F0 RMSE is reported in 
Hz (with cents in parentheses). In addition to the correlation 
computed over all voiced frames, the correlation restricted to 
high-periodicity frames is shown in parentheses, consistent 
with the voiced frame selection criterion used in the F0 
correlation loss during training. The F0 RMSE increases 
progressively from copy synthesis to the audio path and further 
to the rtMRI path. The elevatio n from the a udio path to the 
rtMRI path reflects that vocal fold characteristics determining a 
speaker's mean F0 are not directly observable in rtMRI. 
Furthermore, since X -vectors encode mean pitch well but do 
not sufficiently encode pitch deviation [31], F0 dynamics tend 
to be averaged out even in the audio path . In contrast, the 
Pearson correlation coefficients indicate that relative prosodic 
patterns are captured to a reasonable extent. The rtMRI path 
achieves correlation values of 0.38–0.57 (0.62–0.76 for high-
periodicity frames), broadly comparable to those of the audio 
path, suggesting that the proposed method captures speaker-
independent relative F0 patterns from the rtMRI videos. 
 Speaker similarity as measured by SECS was 0.95–0.96 for 
the rtMRI path and 0.95–0.98 for the audio path. Table 4 shows 
the pairwise SECS matrix for the rtMRI path, where rows 
indicate the synthesized speaker and columns indicate the 
reference speaker; the diagonal entries correspond to the SECS 
values reported in Table 3. The diagonal entries are consistently 
high within each row, indicating that the proposed method 
reproduces speaker-specific characteristics from rtMRI alone. 
However, within-gender pairs (sub11/sub55 and sub20/sub22) 
show mutual similarity comparable to the diagonal, suggesting 
that speaker discrimination within the same gender remains 
limited. This is likely attributable to the limited spatial 
resolution of the rtMRI images and the restri ction to the mid -
sagittal plane, which cannot fully capture the individual 
acoustic properties of the vocal tract.  Synthesized speech 
samples and the speaker split used in our experiments are 
available at https://y-otn.github.io/m2s/. 
6. Conclusions 
 In this study, we proposed a speaker-independent speech 
synthesis method that estimates mel-spectrograms from rtMRI 
videos through cross-modal training with speaker embeddings. 
Experiments using the USC 75-Speaker Speech MRI Database 
demonstrated that the  proposed method achieves favorable 
linguistic accuracy for read speech in a speaker -independent 
setting. For prosody, the Pearson correlation coefficients for F0 
indicated that relative prosodic patterns are captured to a 
reasonable extent, though absolut e F0 estimation remains 
challenging. For speaker similarity, the proposed method 
reproduced speaker identity to a reasonable degree, though 
within-gender speaker discrimination proved difficult.  Both 
limitations are likely attributable to the inherent difficulty of 
capturing speaker-specific characteristics from the mid-sagittal 
rtMRI images, which do not directly reveal vocal fold 
characteristics or the detailed three -dimensional vocal tract 
geometry. Future work will focus on improving prosody and 
speaker similarity reproduction, as well as exploring 
multilingual modeling by incorporating rtMRI datasets in other 
languages [27, 42].
