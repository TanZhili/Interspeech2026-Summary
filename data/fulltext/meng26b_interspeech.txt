Abstract
Speaker recognition relies on substantial data to enhance model
performance, but the large-scale transmission of data by indi-
vidual organizations incurs privacy and economic costs. Feder-
ated learning addresses privacy concerns by avoiding direct data
transmission. However, due to inconsistent client data distribu-
tions, local models may learn redundant knowledge, leading to
degraded performance. In this work, we utilise the Fisher Infor-
mation Matrix to extract key dimensions from embeddings, en-
abling representation alignment between global and local mod-
els. By instructing local models to learn critical knowledge
from the global model, we mitigate the detrimental effects of
redundant knowledge, thereby enhancing overall model perfor-
mance.
Index Terms : Speaker Recognition, Federated Learning,
Fisher Information Matrix, Data Heterogeneity
1. Introduction
Speaker recognition [1] is a biometric technology that identi-
fies individuals by extracting personalised characteristics from
audio signals.Current systems are typically built by individ-
ual organisations using high-performance deep neural networks
trained centrally on proprietary datasets [2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7], mean-
ing that larger and higher-quality datasets lead to better perfor-
mance [8, 9]. However, rising privacy concerns make large-
scale raw data transmission not only costly but also privacy-
liable.
Federated learning [10] is a distributed machine learning
technique. Participants in federated learning typically comprise
one server and multiple clients. This training model enables
knowledge transfer without transmitting raw data, instead rely-
ing solely on the fusion of model parameters, thereby signifi-
cantly mitigating the risk of privacy breaches.
The current federated speaker recognition framework [11,
12] utilizes personal terminal devices as clients for federated
learning. This approach requires user authorization and is
limited by users’ finite computational power, making optimal
model performance challenging.
However, in real-world scenarios, different clients train
on different data distributions. Speaker audio within clients
may originate from different individuals, regions, and countries,
presenting what is known as the data heterogeneity problem
[13, 14, 15, 16, 17, 18, 19, 20]. Whilst server-side model ag-
gregation can mitigate the adverse effects of data heterogeneity

to a certain extent, the resulting global model inevitably retains
heterogeneous feature information from various clients. If such
redundant information is not filtered out, subsequent model up-
date processes will continue to be disrupted by it, thereby in-
creasing the risk of the model becoming trapped in local optima

To address the aforementioned issues, we enable the lo-
cal model to learn key knowledge from the global model. By
aligning the representations of the global and local models, we
mitigate the impact of data heterogeneity. Simultaneously, we
employ the Fisher Information Matrix to further filter informa-
tion, identifying feature dimensions within the global embed-
dings that contribute significantly to task discrimination. We
then constrain the embeddings learned by the local model to
align its key dimensions specifically toward these target dimen-
sions, thereby minimizing the impact of harmful information on
the model.

contained in observed data regarding the parameters of a model

to quantify the extent to which each parameter influences the
predictive behavior of the model.

2.2. Federated Learning
The participants in federated learning comprise one server G,
which possesses a global embedding extractorθG, and N clients
K, each holding a local embedding extractor θK and classifier
wK. The entire federated learning process comprises a local
training phase and a central aggregation phase. During the local
training phase, client K performs local training using its own
local dataset DK. Each training cycle consists of E epochs.
Subsequently, the local client uploads its model parameters θK
to the serverG. The server then performs aggregation, typically
weighting the model aggregation according to the proportion of
data contributed by each client, as illustrated in Equation 3.

on popular public speaker datasets V oxCeleb1 [33], V oxCeleb2
[34], and CN-Celeb1 [35]. We follow the work [23] and
uniformly partition the V oxCeleb dataset into K sub-datasets
based on speaker ID.

with a channel size of 1024 as the embedding extractor θ.
The input is the 80-dimensional log mel-spectrogram. The
speaker embedding dimension D is 512. The local loss

(m = 0.2,s = 30). The optimizer used was Adam with a batch
sizeB of 128. The initial learning rate is set to 0.001 with 0.97

4.2. Results and Analysis
Results on V oxCeleb1 and V oxCeleb2. We divided V ox-
Celeb1 and V oxCeleb2 into 4 sub-datasets based on speaker
ID for experimentation. The experimental results are shown in
Table 1. As seen in the table, our proposed method achieved
the best performance on the V ox-O test set. To further validate
the effectiveness of our proposed method, we tested the model
trained on V oxCeleb2 on V ox-E and V ox-H. The test results
demonstrate that our method achieves the best performance on
both test sets, proving its effectiveness even when confronted
with challenging and complex data.
Results on V oxCeleb1 and CN-Celeb1. We conducted feder-
ated learning using V oxCeleb1 and CN-Celeb1 as the client’s
private datasets, respectively, thereby simulating real-world
training scenarios. The results are presented in Table 2. As
demonstrated, our approach achieves favourable outcomes on
both datasets. Although the performance on CN-Celeb1 is
marginally inferior to FedFSS, our method maintains a signifi-
cant advantage over most competing approaches. Furthermore,
our approach avoids the transmission of Additional information
compared to FedFSS.
4.3. Parameter Sensitivity Analysis.
We conducted experiments with different hyperparameter val-
ues of scale parameter s, as illustrated in Figure 2. For the
EER, the first performance peak was observed when s was set
to 0.2. Subsequently, as the hyperparameter s varied, the EER
stabilised. For the minDCF, the metric performance peaks when
s is 0.9, though performance generally stabilises thereafter. The
results in the figure demonstrate that our method achieves com-
mendable performance regardless of the hyperparameter value
selected, thereby proving its robustness.

4.4. Ablation Study
In this section, we conducted ablation experiments to investi-
gate the role of FIM and feature selection (FS). The ablation
results are presented in Table 3. In the comparison experiments
without FIM for feature selection, we randomly selected di-
mensions at the same proportion as the FIM method to gen-
erate vD and vK. The experimental results demonstrate that
FIM can effectively identify and select important dimensions,
thereby significantly improving model performance. Simulta-
neously, we investigated the effect of dimension selection on
model performance. In experiments without dimension selec-
tion, we allowed the local embeddings to learn directly from the
global embeddings. The experimental results show that with-
out dimension selection, redundant knowledge is easily learned,
leading to a decline in model performance.
5. Conclusions
In this work, we employ federated learning to address pri-
vacy leakage arising from direct data transmission in speaker
recognition tasks. Resolving model degradation caused by data
heterogeneity through representations between global and lo-
cal models. We extract dimensions with higher Fisher val-
ues to mitigate the detrimental impact of redundant informa-
tion on model performance. Experiments demonstrate that our
approach is effective and robust, capable of handling complex
real-world scenarios.
