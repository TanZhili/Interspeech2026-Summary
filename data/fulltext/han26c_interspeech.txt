Exploring Hesitation as a Signal for Spoken Grammatical Error Correction

Abstract
Traditional spoken GEC pipelines treat disfluencies as noise
to be removed before correction. We challenge this assump-
tion for L2 learner speech, hypothesizing that disfluencies sig-
nal grammatical uncertainty and provide valuable cues for er-
ror correction. We propose a novel hesitation-aware approach
that preserves disfluency information via: (1) special marker to-
kens indicating hesitation positions and types, and (2) hesitation
type embeddings that encode disfluency context. Experiments
on the Speak & Improve Corpus 2025 show that our method
outperforms both rule-based disfluency removal (+2.05%p)
and human-annotated fluent transcription (+1.84%p) in F0.5,
with gains particularly pronounced near hesitation positions
(+2.78%p), demonstrating that disfluencies carry informative
signals for spoken GEC and should be leveraged rather than
discarded.

1. Introduction
Providing automated, high-quality grammatical feedback to
second-language (L2) learners is a central goal of Computer-
Assisted Language Learning systems. For spoken English, this
has been formalised as the Spoken Grammatical Error Correc-
tion and Feedback (SGECF) task, which requires a system to
identify grammatical errors in a learner’s speech and return cor-
rective feedback. The recent release of the Speak & Improve
(S&I) Corpus 2025 [1, 2] and the associated Speak & Improve
Challenge 2025 [3] have provided the first large-scale, pub-
licly available dataset annotated with both disfluency labels and
grammatical error corrections for L2 English, making rigorous
SGECF research more accessible than ever before.
The conventional pipeline for spoken GEC consists of three
cascaded modules: Automatic Speech Recognition (ASR), Dis-
fluency Detection (DD) and removal, and text-based Gram-
matical Error Correction (GEC) [4, 5]. Disfluency removal
is treated as a necessary preprocessing step that converts the
ASR transcript into a more written-like form, thereby enabling
GEC models—primarily trained on written corpora—to oper-
ate effectively [6, 7]. This design philosophy treats disfluencies
solely as noise to be eliminated before correction.
We challenge this assumption. A growing body of psy-
cholinguistic and computational evidence indicates that disflu-
encies are not random artefacts but are systematically linked
to moments of cognitive difficulty and linguistic uncertainty in
speech production [8]. In L2 learners specifically, such uncer-
tainty is often associated with grammatical uncertainty. Disflu-
encies and grammatical errors are known to co-occur at elevated
rates, particularly in lower-proficiency speakers [6]: a learner
who pauses or repeats a word is frequently doing so precisely
because they are uncertain about the grammatical construction
they are attempting. Removing disfluencies before feeding the
transcript to the GEC model thus discards a potentially powerful
contextual signal.
In this paper, we propose to leverage hesitation phenom-
ena as a positive signal for SGECF. Specifically, we intro-
duce five special tokens—[SP],[FP],[/FP],[REP], and
[/REP]—to mark silent pauses, filled pause regions, and rep-
etition regions respectively. Corresponding type embeddings
are also injected into the GEC model’s input representation, al-
lowing the model to attend to hesitation context during correc-
tion. We compare this approach against the standard disfluency-
removal baseline on the S&I Corpus 2025.
Experimental results demonstrate that our method outper-
forms both the rule-based DD baseline and the oracleFluent
baseline inF 0.5, confirming that hesitation markers serve as in-
formative signals rather than noise. Furthermore, the improve-
ment is particularly pronounced for errors near hesitation posi-
tions, providing direct evidence that disfluencies carry valuable
cues for grammatical error correction.
2. Related Work
2.1. Written Grammatical Error Correction
Written GEC has been extensively studied over the past decade,
driven by a series of shared tasks including CoNLL-2013/2014
[9, 10], BEA-2019 [11]. The field has progressed from
rule-based and statistical machine translation approaches to
sequence-to-sequence (Seq2Seq) neural models and sequence-
to-edit (Seq2Edit) taggers. GECToR [12] is a notable sequence-
labelling approach that achieves competitive performance with
high efficiency by predicting token-level transformations rather
than generating target text from scratch. Encoder-decoder mod-
els initialised from pre-trained language models such as BERT
[13] have further pushed performance on benchmarks [14].
Standardised evaluation is facilitated by ERRANT [15], an er-
ror annotation toolkit that classifies edits between original and
corrected sentences into linguistically motivated error types, en-
abling both overall F0.5 scoring and fine-grained error type
analysis. Each edit is labelled by an operation type—Missing
(M), Replacement (R), or Unnecessary (U)—combined with a
part-of-speech tag (e.g.,U:NOUNmarks an unnecessary noun
andM:PREPa missing preposition). While written GEC is
now mature, spoken GEC remains considerably more challeng-
ing and less explored.

2.2. Spoken Grammatical Error Correction
Spoken GEC (SGEC) faces unique challenges, including ASR-
induced error propagation, the confounding nature of disfluen-
cies, and data scarcity [6]. While early work focused on adapt-
ing written grammatical error detection (GED) systems to spo-
ken registers [6], conventional pipelines typically employ a cas-
cade (ASR→DD→GEC) that removes disfluent tokens be-
fore correction [4, 7]. Recent advances have explored joint op-
timization and end-to-end (E2E) architectures to mitigate error
propagation, with foundation models like Whisper [16] being
used to perform ASR, disfluency handling, and GEC simultane-
ously [17, 18]. To address data bottlenecks, synthetic augmen-
tation [19] and the release of theS&I Corpus 2025andSpeak &
Improve Challenge[1, 2, 3, 20] have provided essential bench-
marks and annotation methodologies for advancing integrated
SGEC research.
2.3. Disfluency Types in L2 Spoken Language
Disfluencies—including filled pauses, silent pauses, and
repetitions—are natural interruptions in speech [21]. In L2
acquisition, disfluency frequency and temporal pausing pat-
terns within reformulations are directly linked to proficiency
and grammatical repair processes [22], often co-occurring with
grammatical errors [6]. This work focuses on these three preva-
lent and computationally tractable disfluency types. We note
that our use of the term filled pause includes false starts and
incomplete words.
2.4. Disfluency as an Informative Signal
While conventionally treated as noise to be stripped for down-
stream tasks [23, 24], recent evidence suggests that disfluencies
provide critical linguistic signals. They anticipate production
difficulty and lexical surprisal [8], act as pragmatic cues rather
than mere interference [25], and improve diagnostic accuracy
when retained in transcripts [26]. Given that transformer mod-
els can implicitly capture disfluency structure [27], and that si-
lence features have been shown to improve disfluency detection
performance [28], we hypothesize that explicitly encoding dis-
fluency types can assist GEC models in locating and correcting
errors signaled by hesitation behavior in L2 speech.
3. Proposed Method
We propose a hesitation-aware approach to spoken GEC that
preserves disfluency information rather than discarding it (Fig-
ure 1). Our method consists of two components: (1) hesita-
tion marker injection, which inserts special tokens to explicitly
mark disfluency positions, and (2) hesitation type embedding,
which provides additional type-specific representations for to-
kens within disfluent regions.
3.1. Hesitation Marker Injection
Given a disfluent transcript with annotated hesitation phenom-
ena, we transform it into a hesitation-marked sequence using
special tokens. We define three disfluency categories and their
corresponding markers:
•Silent Pause (SP): Positions where the speaker paused
silently are marked with a single[SP]token.
•Filled Pause (FP): False starts and incomplete words are
wrapped with boundary tokens[FP]and[/FP].
•Repetition (REP): Consecutive repeated words are wrapped
with[REP]and[/REP].
For example, an utterance with a word repetition, a filled
pause, and a silent pause:
“I want want to (g-) goes [pause] there”
is transformed into:
“I[REP]want want[/REP]to[FP](g-)[/FP]goes[SP]there”
These five special tokens ([SP],[FP],[/FP],[REP],
[/REP]) are added to the model’s vocabulary, and their em-
beddings are learned during fine-tuning. This marking scheme
allows the model to identify both the location and type of each
hesitation phenomenon.

3.2. Hesitation Type Embedding
Beyond marker tokens, we introduce an auxiliary embedding
layer that assigns a learned hesitation type representation to
each input token. Formally, the input embedding for tokenx i is
computed as:
hi =e token(xi) +e type(ti)(1)
wheree token is the standard token embedding, ande type is a
learned embedding indexed by hesitation typet i∈{0,1,2,3}:
•t i = 0: Normal token (outside any hesitation region)
•t i = 1:[SP]token
•t i = 2: Token within[FP]...[/FP]region
•t i = 3: Token within[REP]...[/REP]region
This type embedding propagates hesitation context to all
tokens within a disfluent span, not just the boundary markers,
enabling the model to recognise that words inside these regions
may require special attention during correction.
3.3. Model Architecture
We adopt T5 [29] as our base encoder-decoder model due to its
strong performance on text-to-text tasks. The input is formatted
as‘‘gec: <marked transcript>’’, and the model is
trained to generate the grammatically corrected output. The five
hesitation marker tokens are added to the T5 tokeniser, and the
token embedding matrix is resized accordingly. We add a sep-
arate hesitation type embedding layer of dimensiond model with
four type vectors, initialised fromN(0,0.02). The type em-
beddings are summed with the token embeddings before being
passed to the encoder.
4. Experiments
4.1. Dataset
We conduct experiments on theS&I Corpus 2025. The cor-
pus provides three transcript types: (1) disfluent transcripts with
hesitation annotations (generated by human annotators who cor-
rected ASR outputs to accurately reflect exactly what the learner
said, including all language errors, hesitations, and false starts),
(2) fluent transcripts where disfluencies have been manually re-
moved, and (3) grammatically corrected transcripts.
We usemanual transcriptionsrather than ASR outputs to
isolate the effect of hesitation information from ASR recogni-
tion errors. This controlled setting differs from the S&I Chal-
lenge 2025 baseline [3], which evaluates a full cascade pipeline
(ASR→DD→GEC) on ASR outputs. Our goal is not to
benchmark end-to-end system performance, but to answer a
fundamental research question:does hesitation information aid
grammatical error correction?The higher absoluteF 0.5 scores
in our experiments reflect this difference in evaluation scope—
the absence of ASR error propagation—rather than a direct im-
provement over the challenge baseline.
4.2. Baselines and Systems
We compare three experimental conditions:
•DD: Applies rule-based disfluency removal to the disfluent
transcripts, simulating the DD→GEC portion of a conven-
tional cascade pipeline.
•Fluent: Uses the human-annotated fluent transcripts (disflu-
encies manually removed) as input. This represents an upper
bound for automatic DD.
•Proposed: Uses hesitation marker injection (Section 3.1)
with hesitation type embeddings (Section 3.2) on the disflu-
ent transcripts.
4.3. Implementation Details
All models are initialised fromt5-baseand fine-tuned on the
S&I Corpus 2025 training set. This differs from the S&I Chal-
lenge baseline, which trains on BEA-2019 [11], a written GEC
corpus. Training on in-domain spoken data allows the model to
learn patterns specific to L2 spoken English.
Models are fine-tuned for 10 epochs with early stopping
(patience=3) based on validation GLEU [30]. We use AdamW
[31] with learning rate1×10 −4, batch size 8, and gradient
accumulation over 4 steps. Maximum sequence length is set to
256 tokens for both source and target.
4.4. Evaluation
We evaluate using span-basedF 0.5 computed by ERRANT
[15], which weights precision twice as heavily as recall to re-
flect the importance of feedback accuracy in educational set-
tings. We directly process the STM transcription files: utter-
ance segments sharing the same ID are merged. The resulting
source–reference pairs are then evaluated with ERRANT.

where the speaker abandons an utterance mid-word and restarts,
often because they recognised a grammatical mistake. By pre-
serving these markers, our method retains the signal that “some-

attention on the surrounding tokens.

the” where one “the” should be deleted). The Fluent baseline
already captures much of this signal through removal, leaving
less room for additional improvement from explicit marking.

6. Conclusions
We proposed a hesitation-aware approach to spoken GEC that
treats disfluencies as informative signals rather than noise. By
injecting special tokens to mark hesitation positions and adding
type-specific embeddings, our method enables GEC models to
exploit the correlation between hesitation and grammatical un-
certainty. Experiments on the S&I Corpus 2025 show that pre-
serving hesitation information outperforms both rule-based dis-
fluency removal and oracle fluent transcription inF 0.5, with
gains concentrated near hesitation positions. These results con-
firm that disfluencies carry valuable cues for error correction in
L2 speech.
Scope and Limitations.Our experiments use manual tran-
scriptions to isolate hesitation effects from ASR errors, yielding
higher absolute scores than end-to-end pipelines evaluated on
ASR outputs. This controlled setting demonstrates the intrinsic
value of hesitation information for GEC—a finding that would
be obscured under ASR noise. However, realising these gains
in practice requires ASR systems that reliably output hesitation
annotations. Validating our approach with real ASR outputs,
and developing hesitation-aware ASR, are natural directions for
future work.
