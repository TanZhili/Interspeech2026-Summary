Robust Language Identification Using Semi-positive Contrastive Learning

Abstract
Conventionally, Spoken Language Identification is modeled as
a speech-based language identification task, ignoring the impor-
tance of text data. This work addresses the cross-corpora gen-
eralization issue for the low-resourced spoken language identi-
fication (LID) problem. We propose Semi-positive Contrastive
Learning (SpCL), a robust framework for LID that addresses
domain shifts without requiring explicit domain adaptation or
generalization. Our method uses a bi-modal audio-text en-
coder to map speech waveforms and phoneme-based text cap-
tions into a shared embedding space. By introducing a novel
semi-positive contrastive loss, SpCL distinguishes strong posi-
tive pairs (same language and domain) from semi-positive pairs
(same language, different domains) using a controlled weight-
ing scheme. We experimented on 12 Indian languages using
multi-domain datasets that demonstrate that SpCL outperforms
strong acoustic and phonetic baselines, achieving superior ac-
curacy on both seen and unseen test domains. Furthermore, we
conducted ablation studies that confirm the benefits of semi-
positive weighting and dynamic captions, with Whisper-based
audio encoders delivering the best robustness under domain
mismatch. 1

1. Introduction
Spoken Language Identification (LID) is the task of identify-
ing the language in a given speech utterance. It plays an im-
portant role in many downstream applications, including Auto-
matic Speech Recognition (ASR), speaker recognition and veri-
fication etc. Designing a good LID system is important because
errors at this stage will propagate to later stages, reducing the
overall performance of the final application.
LID systems often suffer from the domain shift problem,
where a model’s performance degrades when the test data
comes from a different distribution than the training data. This
also applies to large-scale, state-of-the-art models such as Whis-
per [1] and Wav2Vec2.0 [2]. This issue is more severe for low-
resource languages, which rely on small datasets with limited
variation. As a result, models overfit to domain-specific acous-
tic patterns such as recording conditions [3], [4].
To address this problem, researchers have used Domain
Adaptation (DA) or sometimes, Domain Generalization (DG).
Researchers have explored various signal processing and ar-
chitectural enhancements to extract domain-invariant features,
such as the Wavelet Scattering Transform (WST) [5] to capture

high-frequency details, or Flow-based Regularization [6] to fil-
ter environmental noise. Others have employed techniques like
V oice Conversion (VC) [7]. These methods aim to improve ro-
bustness by limiting domain-specific variation in the acoustic
signal.
Recently, there has been a shift toward using textual and
phonetic information as anchors for learning language features.
Here, ananchorrefers to a fixed linguistic signal such as text or
phoneme sequences that remains stable across speakers, chan-
nels, and domains, and serves as a reference point to guide
and constrain the learning of speech representations. Joint pre-
training frameworks like mSLAM [8], MuSeLI [9] achieve this
by combining speech-based w2v-BERT with text-based Span-
BERT to learn cross-lingual representations through connec-
tionist temporal classification (CTC) losses, while MAESTRO
[10] utilizes an RNN-Transducer framework to strictly align
speech and text sequences, ensuring the model represents spo-
ken and written language within the same embedding space.
This multimodal approach is more effective in zero-supervised
settings, as seen in Maestro-U [11], which leverages byte-level
text representations and language embeddings to train mod-
els using only unlabeled data. The paradigm has been further
refined through techniques like soft prompt tuning in CLEP-
DG [12], which uses learnable text prompts to simulate di-
verse acoustic environments, and feature splitting in SimM-
MDG [13], which decomposes data into modality-shared and
modality-specific components to separate core linguistic infor-
mation from domain-specific noise. However, while these mul-
timodal approaches improve representation, they often treat all
same-language samples identically, failing to explicitly model
the variance introduced by domain shifts.
In this research, we propose robust language identification
using Semi-positive Contrastive Learning (henceforth termed
SpCL.) Our approach integrates a bi-modal audio-text encoder
architecture to map acoustic and phonetic information into a
shared embedding space. A key component of our approach is
a novel semi-positive contrastive loss, which builds upon con-
cepts like Supervised Contrastive Learning [14], [15], [16] and
Proxy-based Learning [17], [18]. We introduce dynamic cap-
tions from phoneme sequences to increase structural variation
in the language representations. Moreover, we distinguish be-
tween strong “positive” pairs (same language/same domain) and
“semi-positive” pairs (same language/different domain) using a
control weightα. This encourages the model to cluster sam-
ples of the same language from diverse domains closely but not
identically, enabling effective generalization to unseen environ-
ments without requiring explicit domain adaptation.

2.3. Static and dynamic captions
We distinguishstaticanddynamiccaptions based on how the
text captionc i is constructed.
2641
A static caption is independent of the utterance content. For
an audio sample of languagel 1 from domaind 2, the caption
is: “this audio clip is in languagel 1 and domaind 2.” All ut-
terances from the same language–domain pair share the same
caption and thus the same anchor. Under semi-positive con-
trastive learning (Figure 2(b)), the anchor strongly pulls posi-
tives, weakly pulls semi-positives, and pushes negatives. Al-
though this relaxes binary constraints, samples are still clus-
tered around a few centers, reducing within-language variation.
This is consistent with findings that fixed class-level anchors or
proxy-based objectives reduce intra-class variance by collaps-
ing samples toward representative centers [18].
In contrast, a dynamic caption is derived from the utterance
itself, typically as a phoneme sequence [21]. Different utter-
ances of the same language produce different captions and text
embeddings, resulting in a more spread embedding space (Fig-
ure 2(c)). Each anchor pulls its positives and semi-positives
while pushing negatives, and these combined forces spread
samples across a wider space.
Geometrically, static captions pull samples toward a sin-
gle center, reducing variance but risking collapse and weaker
domain transfer. Dynamic captions distribute attraction across
multiple anchors. Semi-positive weights bring samples from
the same language but different domains closer without mak-
ing them identical, suppressing domain effects while preserving
phonetic structure.

analyze results in detail on an Indian language dataset, and addi-
tionally evaluate the performance over larger standard datasets.

3.2. Experiment Details
3.2.1. Proposed Models
The proposed SpCL contrastive framework is designed to align
high-capacity audio encoders with a text-based semantic space.
We utilise two distinct audio encoders to determine the impact
of pre-training objectives on domain robustness.
•SpCL w2v2: We utilize a phoneme-centric variant of
wav2vec 2.0 [21] to extract dense phonetic features (392×
time). To balance the preservation of universal phonetic rep-
resentations with the need for domain-specific flexibility, we
fine-tune the top four Transformer layers while keeping the
lower convolutional layers frozen.
•SpCL whisp: Given its weak supervision on vast multilin-
gual data [1], Whisper provides a robust linguistic prior. We
fine-tune the final two encoder blocks.
Both audio encoders are fine-tuned with the train data in
an end-to-end manner via the SpCL pipeline. The text encoder
employs RoBERTa, following the CLAP architecture [14]. This
encoder maps our static and dynamic captions (phoneme se-
quences) into the shared embedding space. All encoders are
followed by projection layers, as shown in Figure 1.
3.2.2. Baseline Models
To evaluate the effectiveness of semi-positive weighting and dy-
namic captions, we compare our system against four baseline
architectures:
•B1: MFCC-Conformer: A standard end-to-end LID base-
line using traditional MFCC features and a Conformer en-
coder [25]. This represents a purely acoustic approach with-
out phonetic or textual grounding.
•B2: ConfPhoneme: This model utilizes phoneme poste-
riors extracted from wav2vec2 phoneme [21] passed to a
conformer. It tests whether explicit phonetic labels, in the
absence of a contrastive framework, are sufficient to over-
come domain shift. A data augmented version of this model
(termed B2a) is also evaluated, to determine the effectiveness
of data augmentation in handling domain shift. Augmenta-
tion is done with noise and reverberation augmentation using
2642
MUSAN [26] and BUT RIR [27].
•B3: Explicit Domain-Adapted Baseline: To establish an
upper bound, we explicitly trained ConfPhoneme on the mis-
matchedytdataset using gradient reversal layer (GRL) [28].
This baseline measures how closely our SpCL approach can
approach the performance of unsupervised domain adapta-
tion (UDA.)
•B4: WhisperEncoder: We fine-tuned last 4 layers of the
Whisper-base encoder, pooled the outputs over time, and
passed them through two fully connected layers with 256 and
12 units, respectively. Fixed-length padding of 3,000 frames
was applied. The purpose of this baseline is to evaluate the
capability of the Whisper encoder.
3.3. Results and Discussion
Table 2 summarizes performance across seen and unseen test
sets. Across most settings, the SpCL models outperform the
baseline systems.
Phoneme-based baselines (B2) perform better than MFCC
on unseen data. This suggests that phoneme posteriors carry
more stable language cues. Unsupervised domain adaptation
(B3) and data augmentation (B2b) improve robustness on sig-
nificantly. The best performing baseline (B4) uses the Whis-
per encoder, drawing on it’s large-scale pre-training and fine-
tuning. We conclude that Whisper is a model that overcomes
domain shift to a large extent.
The proposed SpCL models achieve strong generalization
without explicit domain adaptation. SpCL w2v2, which aligns
wav2vec2 phoneme with text using contrastive learning, im-
proves accuracy on seen data. Its performance drops on In-
dicV oice unseen data, which suggests sensitivity to larger shifts
in domain and language distribution.
SpCL whisp achieves the best results across all test sets.
This indicates that as seen in B4, Whisper-based representa-
tions capture more domain-invariant acoustic cues. Alignment
with phonetic representations further strengthens this effect. All
considered models struggle with the IndicV oice dataset.

3.5. Ablation Studies
We ran experiments to analyze each component of the con-
trastive loss, focusing on static versus dynamic captions and
different semi-positive weightsα. We also evaluate standard
contrastive learning without semi-positive weights (α= 0). We
report ablation results on the EkStep and IndicV oice datasets in
Table 4.
In both caption settings, adding semi-positive labels im-
proves performance over standard contrastive learning across
most experiments. Whisper-based models perform better on all
splits. Even with static captions, Whisper shows strong unseen
results. Dynamic captions further improve performance, espe-
cially on IndicV oice, with gains across all weight settings. This
indicates that Whisper features handle domain shift well and
benefit from added text variation.
The semi-positive weight also influences performance.
Moderate values, such asα=0.3 andα=0.5, achieve higher un-
seen accuracy than hard positives alone. This trend is consis-
tent across encoders and caption strategies. Lower weights give
small gains, while very high weights show no consistent im-
provement. A weight of 0.7 gives the best unseen accuracy.
Overall, the results show that dynamic captions and semi-
positive contrastive learning complement each other and im-
prove SLID under domain mismatch.

4. Conclusion
The proposed SpCL method utilized information from the
shared text-audio embedding space, resulting in better cross-
domain performance. The use of dynamic captions along with
Whisper encoder, along with semi-positive contrastive learning
weights show tangible benefits. Unlike methods which use ex-
plicit domain adaptation, SpCL implicitly brings domain invari-
ance in its loss function. The current work uses only the audio
encoder for inference; future work will attempt this in the joint
embedding space.
