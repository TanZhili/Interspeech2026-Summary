Abstract
Previous descriptions of Assamese have reported a pattern of
vowel harmony in which a mid vowel is raised when followed
by a close vowel in the subsequent syllable. Articulatory studies
based on a single dialect have offered mixed findings, and sys-
tematic acoustic studies remain limited. This study provides an
acoustic analysis of /E/-raising in harmonic contexts across the
three major dialect regions of Assamese. The data were drawn
from 18 adult speakers (3 male and 3 female speakers per re-
gion), including both wordlist and connected speech recordings.
F1 and F2 measurements were analysed using linear mixed-
effects models with harmonic context, vowel duration, dialect,
speech style, and gender as predictors. The results show consis-
tent /E/-raising in harmonic contexts across dialects. Although
the harmony effect was evident across all dialects and speech
styles, its magnitude varied across dialect–style combinations,
particularly in vowel height (F1).

1. Introduction
1.1. V owel harmony
V owel harmony is a phonological process whereby vowels
come to share one or more features across intervening conso-
nants within a given domain, such as the word [1]. It is there-
fore a process by which vowels in adjacent syllables become
more like one another. Different features, relating to the acous-
tic and auditory properties of vowels, may be shared. In Turk-
ish, vowels within a word are typically all either front vowels
or back vowels; close vowels also align with preceding vowels
in whether they are rounded or unrounded [2]. In the Romance
language Lena Asturian, vowels may harmonise for height, for
example with (stressed) mid vowels/e, o/raising to close vow-
els[i, u]when preceding a suffix with a close vowel [3]. In
the Nilotic language Lopit, ‘Advanced Tongue Root’ ([+ATR])
vowels at the right edge of a word prompt preceding [-ATR]
vowels/I, E, U, O/to be realised as their [+ATR] counterparts
[i, e, u, o][4]. There are many language-specific ways that pro-
cesses described as vowel harmony can manifest, and while it
has been a topic of extensive research in the phonological liter-
ature, there has been less research examining the phonetic reali-
sation of vowels affected by harmony processes (though see e.g.
[5]), particularly beyond European languages that have been the
focus of many studies.

1.2. V owel system of Assamese
Assamese (Asamiya) is a major Indo-Aryan language spoken
primarily in the state of Assam in northeastern India. According
to the Census of India (2011), it is spoken by over 15 million
first-language speakers, with an additional 7 million reporting
Assamese as one of their primary languages. Figure 1 presents
the previously described phonemic inventory of vowel monoph-
thongs in ‘standard’ Assamese, typically associated with the
eastern districts of Assam [6] (see Figure 2 for major dialect
regions). The vowel system comprises eight monophthongs,
symmetrically distributed across the vowel space, with four
front and four back vowels distinguished by vowel height.
While most accounts assume this eight-way phonemic con-
trast, some studies propose alternative transcriptions or question
the presence of certain contrasts in both ‘standard’ Assamese
and regional dialects, particularly in the mid vowel space (e.g.,
[6, 7, 8, 9, 10]). Different vowel qualities have also been pos-
tulated such that the proposed height distinction between mid
vowels has been suggested to instead be a distinction based on
[+ATR/-ATR] ‘Advanced Tongue Root’ feature [9, 11].
Regional variation in Assamese is expected given the so-
ciolinguistic complexity of the region. However, systematic
phonological and phonetic evidence on vowel patterns across
regional dialects remains limited. A recent acoustic study of
three Assamese dialects, including the variety associated with
the ‘standard’, found little phonetic evidence for a clear phone-
mic contrast between /e/ and /E/, suggesting instead that /E/ may
function as the sole mid front vowel in contemporary Assamese
[12].

1.3. V owel harmony in Assamese
V owel harmony in Assamese has generally been viewed as a
regressive vowel raising process triggered by close vowels in a
following syllable ([6, 8, 13, 14, 15]), for example, close-mid
[e] in [beti] ‘daughter,’ but open-mid [E] in [bEta] ‘son’, with
an open rather than close vowel in the following syllable. Early
grammatical descriptions did not explicitly use the term ‘vowel
harmony’. Some studies noted systematic alternations consis-

terised these alternations as ‘harmonic mutation,’ whereby the
quality of a vowel is influenced by a following vowel, particu-
larly /i/ and /u/ [8]. Subsequent descriptive works [6, 15] did
not explicitly frame these alternations as vowel harmony, yet
they reported systematic neutralisation patterns consistent with
regressive raising. They argued that the eight-way vowel con-
trast is reduced in harmonic environments. In open syllables
followed by /i/ (and, according to some analyses, /u/), the con-
trasts /e,E/ and /o,O/ are described as neutralising to /e/ and
/o/ respectively, with the presence of the close vowel prompt-
ing the use of only close-mid and not open-mid vowel qualities
in preceding syllables. While this is typically characterised as
the spread of a vowel height feature, analyses which suggest
that a [+/-ATR] feature is relevant to the vowel quality contrasts
similarly propose that harmony in Assamese involves regressive
spread of a [+ATR] ‘Advanced Tongue Root’ feature from close
vowels /i/ and /u/ to preceding [-ATR] vowels/E, O/(with/e,
o/classed as [+ATR], and /a/ excluded from the process) [9].
A recurring observation across these accounts is that har-
mony is local and may be blocked when a nasal consonant or
a consonant cluster intervenes immediately before the trigger-
ing vowel [8, 9, 11]. However, empirical evidence for the con-
sistency and scope of this process remains mixed. An articula-
tory ultrasound imaging study based on speakers of the standard
variety tested the predictions of this feature-spreading account
[16]. The results, however, revealed substantial inter-speaker
variability. Some speakers showed patterns compatible with re-
gressive harmony, whereas others did not. None exhibited the
predicted effects uniformly across all contexts where harmony
was expected to apply or be blocked, and some speakers showed
no evidence of the harmony process at all. The authors note the
need for detailed acoustic analyses to shed further light on the
varying articulatory findings.

Despite recurring claims that Assamese exhibits regressive
vowel harmony, the phonetic and phonological status of the pro-
cess, therefore, remains unclear. Existing descriptions and for-
mal analyses, largely based on the standard variety, treat vowel
harmony as a systematic, categorical, and locally constrained
feature-spreading process. However, empirical evidence sup-
porting the consistency and scope of these claims remains lim-
ited. The mid front vowel space is particularly relevant in this
regard. The phonemic status of the mid front vowels proposed
to participate in vowel harmony is not uniformly described
across dialects [12]. Moreover, while some accounts charac-
terise vowel harmony primarily as vowel height mutation [8],
others analyse the process in terms of neutralisation [6, 15] or
[+ATR] feature spreading [9]. This divergence in interpretation
raises questions about whether the process involves categorical
vowel raising and whether such a pattern is realised consistently
across dialects and speech styles, given that there is lack of sys-
tematic vowel raising in harmonic contexts even across speakers
of the ‘standard’ Assamese [16].
2. Research aim
The present study addresses a gap in our understanding of As-
samese vowel harmony across three major dialect regions. By
comparing the acoustic realisation of /E/ in proposed harmonic
contexts (before /i/) with non-harmonic contexts (before /a/) in
CVCV words (i.e., /CECi/ vs /CECa/), the study models vowel
height (F1) and backness (F2) across dialect, speech style, and
gender. In doing so, it seeks to establish the phonetic evidence
for mid front vowel harmony, and whether its implementation
is uniform across dialects.
3. Methods
3.1. Speaker details
Data for this study were drawn from the Corpus of Contempo-
rary Assamese (COCAS), an Assamese speech corpus recorded
between 2023 and 2025 by the first author [12]. Speech record-
ings of 18 native Assamese adult speakers (age 50-65 years)
were selected from the database. The speakers were from three
semi-urban locations across Assam with 3 male and 3 female
speakers per location. The locations are Sorbhog, Bihaguri, and
Gogamukh which fall within the three major dialect regions of
Western Assamese, Central Assamese, and Eastern Assamese
dialects respectively (see Figure 2).
3.2. Material and procedure
Two sets of words were elicited in a wordlist elicitation task.
The words are/bEki/,/sEki/,/bEka/,/sEka/, where the first
two words represent the harmonic context (/CECi/) and the latter
two represent the non-harmonic context (/CECa/). The speakers
read each of the word three times in a carrier sentence /moi
X buli kalo/ ‘I said X’ with a pause of 5-10 seconds after ev-
ery utterance. In addition to the wordlist elicitation task, the
same speakers participated in a one-on-one conversation, which
continued atleast 30 mins. Bisyllabic /CECi/ and /CECa/ words
were isolated from the connected speech data, where the sur-
rounding consonants are either obstruents or sibilants, or both.
3.3. Phonetic & Statistical analysis
ELAN Version 6.9 (2024) was used for the transcription and an-
notation of both wordlist and connected speech data [17]. For
forced alignment, Montreal Forced Aligner was used through
customizing the pretrained English MFA acoustic model v3.1.0
to accommodate the Assamese phone representations, followed
by manual correction [18]. A total of 371 tokens were consid-
ered for the study: 212 from the wordlist data,and 159 from the
connected speech data. Table 1 presents the summary of the
number of tokens used in the study.
Acoustic measurements of first and second formant fre-
quencies (F1 and F2) were extracted with Fast Track [19] at
five temporal points (10%, 30%, 50%, 70%, 90%) in the vowel;
however, only midpoints (at 50%) are considered for this study.
V owel duration was also extracted. To ensure comparability

across speakers, formant measurements were normalized us-
ing the Nearey individual log-mean method (Nearey1). This
method was chosen following the recommendations in sev-
eral comparative studies of vowel normalization techniques [20,
21]. These studies have shown that speaker-intrinsic, formant-
intrinsic, and vowel-extrinsic normalization procedures, such as
Nearey1, perform well with sociolinguistic data. The Nearey1
normalized frequencies were scaled back to Hz.
All statistical analyses were done in R [22]. Thelme4pack-
age [23] was used to conduct linear mixed-effects modelling
and theemmeanspackage [24] was used to conduct pairwise
comparisons. The Akaike information criterion (AIC) was used
to assess model suitability, with lower values indicating a better
model.
4. Results
4.1. Descriptive statistics
Table 1 presents the descriptive statistics, summarising the
means and standard deviations of normalised F1 and F2 values
(in Hz) for the target vowels in /CECa/ and /CECi/ words across
styles and dialect regions. Across all dialect regions and styles,
/CECi/ words show consistently lower F1 values in the mid vow-
els than /CECa/ words, indicating a relatively higher vowel qual-
ity in harmonic context. Likewise, /CECi/ words exhibit higher
F2 values in the target vowels than /CECa/ words, reflecting a
more fronted articulation in the harmonic context. Some stylis-
tic differences are also observable. The wordlist data generally
shows lower F1 values and higher F2 values compared to the
connected speech data, suggesting a more peripheral articula-
tion under careful speech conditions. In contrast, connected
speech displays greater variability, particularly in F2, as re-
flected in larger standard deviations. Some regional differences
are also observed in the raw means although these are smaller
in magnitude than the harmonic environments and stylistic con-
trasts. Furthermore, vowel durations (not shown) are gener-
ally longer in the wordlist style than in connected speech style,
while differences between /CECi/ and /CECa/ words within each
style are comparatively small. The descriptive statistics there-
fore suggest systematic differences associated with harmonic
context, speech style and vowel duration, with more modest di-
alectal variation. These descriptive patterns are evaluated us-
ing mixed-effects models to assess the contribution of harmonic
context, vowel duration, speech style, dialect region, and gen-
der, and their interactions in the following section.

4.2. Linear mixed effect model analysis
To evaluate the patterns observed in the descriptive statistics,
linear mixed-effects models were fitted separately for F1 and F2
using thelme4package in R. Fixed effects included harmonic
environments (/CECa/ vs /CECi/), vowel duration, speech style
(wordlist vs connected speech), dialect regions (Sorbhog, Bih-
aguri, Gogamukh), and speakers’ gender. Random intercepts
were included for speaker and word to account for repeated
observations. Model comparison was conducted using likeli-
hood ratio tests based on maximum likelihood estimation. For
both F1 and F2, four models were compared sequentially: 1) an
intercept-only model, 2) a main-effects model, 3) an interaction
model that included all two-way interactions among harmonic
contexts, speech style, and dialect region, and 4) a model in-
cluding the three-way interaction between harmonic contexts,
speech style, and dialect region. Gender and vowel duration
were retained as additive predictors in all models except for the
intercept-only model.
For F1, the main-effects model significantly improved
model fit relative to the intercept-only modelχ 2(6) =
72.46,p < .001. Adding two-way interactions did not signifi-
cantly improve model fit(χ 2(5) = 11.93,p=.036). How-
ever, the inclusion of the three-way interaction between har-
monic environments, speech style, and dialect region signifi-
cantly enhanced model fit(χ 2(2) = 11.30,p=.004). The
final model for F1 therefore retained the three-way interaction.
This indicates that the effect of harmonic environments on F1
varies jointly across dialect region and style. Likewise, for
F2, the main-effects model significantly improved fit over the
intercept-only model(χ 2(6) = 61.49,p < .001). In con-
trast to F1, adding two-way interactions significantly improved
model fit(χ 2(5) = 21.54,p=.001), indicating interaction ef-
fects among harmonic environments, speech style, and dialect
region. However, the addition of the three-way interaction did
not significantly improve model fit(χ 2(2) = 2.80,p=.247).
The final F2 model therefore retained all two-way interactions
but excluded the three-way term. Additional models includ-
ing interactions between vowel duration, harmonic context, and
speech style were also evaluated. These did not significantly
improve model fit, indicating that the observed harmonic and
stylistic patterns cannot be attributed solely to style-related dif-
ferences in vowel duration.
These model results indicate that the difference in mid
vowel realisation in /CECi/ compared to /CECa/ words is clearly
attested in both height (F1) and frontness (F2). However, the in-
teraction pattern differs across acoustic dimensions. While F1
exhibits a three-way interaction among harmonic environments,
speech style, and dialect region, F2 shows significant pairwise
interactions but no higher-order interaction. This asymmetry
suggests that the phonetic realisation of the mid front vowel /E/
is more structurally conditioned in the height dimension than in
the frontness dimension. Additionally, vowel duration and gen-
der could account for some variation in the acoustic realisation
of the target vowel.

in harmonic contexts across the speech community. Although
the magnitude of the harmony effect varied somewhat across di-

5. Discussion & Conclusion
The present study investigated the acoustic evidence for As-
samese mid front vowel harmony across regional dialects by
comparing realisations of/E/in harmonic (/CECi/) and non-
harmonic (/CECa/) contexts [9, 15, 16]. The results provide
clear acoustic evidence that the quality of the mid front vowel
differs systematically across these harmonic contexts. In par-
ticular, the vowel/E/in the /CECa/–/CECi/ words shows sig-
nificant and consistent differentiation in both vowel height (F1)
and frontness (F2), indicating that the presence of a following

that characterise Assamese mid vowel harmony as a local and
regressive vowel raising process [15, 9, 11], while providing
quantitative support for the phenomenon. At the same time, re-
sults show that the pattern is not realised uniformly across con-
texts. For F1, the significant three-way interaction among lexi-
cal set, style, and regional dialect indicates that the magnitude
of the harmony effect varies across dialect–style combinations.
Although significant in all dialects and speech styles, no consis-
tent overall stylistic pattern was observed. In contrast, F2 shows
significant two-way interactions but no higher-order interaction.
The frontness difference remains highly consistent across di-
alects and speech styles, while stylistic effects were compara-
tively limited and reached significance only in Bihaguri. V owel
duration and gender contributed to variation in both acoustic
dimensions, although neither altered the overall patterns of har-
mony. The asymmetry between F1 and F2 suggests that vowel
height plays a stronger role in harmonic patterns than frontness.
Further work will offer more detailed insights into the extent to
which raising reflects a categorical phonological process or gra-
dient phonetic coarticulation, or some combination. The present
study provides clear phonetic evidence for mid front vowel har-
mony across the three major dialect regions of Assamese, open-
ing the door to future studies of ways that linguistic and social
factors interact with vowel realisation in Assamese.
