Abstract 
Phonetic analysis of little-studied languages, particularly those 
with complex segmental inventories or typologically rare 
features, often requires extensive manual verification of 
acoustic measurements. In the absence of established reference 
formant values, automatic extraction frequently produces 
systematic errors that must be visually inspected and corrected. 
Most large-scale scripts process datasets without interactive 
feedback, limiting transparency and reproducibility when 
manual intervention is necessary. This study pre sents 
NewAppVoice, a reproducible workflow integrating multiple 
formant-calculation algorithms with synchronized visualization 
of signals and measurements. Unlike fully automated pipelines, 
the software combines algorithmic processing with structured 
manual verification, improving reliability while ensuring 
transparency. The software and derived applications are freely 
available as open access tools supporting reproducible research 
on under-documented languages. 
Index Terms: phonetic software, acoustic analysis, formant 
analysis, voice quality measurement, reproducible research, 
documentary phonetics 
1. Introduction 
This study introduces NewAppVoice, a reproducible workflow 
that integrates multiple formant-calculation algorithms with 
synchronized visualization of acoustic signals and 
measurements. The application was developed for phonetic 
research on under-documented languages with complex 
segmental inventories and typologically rare features, 
particularly varieties of Qiang and lesser-described Tibetic 
languages within the Sino-Tibetan family. These languages 
exhibit properties such as vowel uvularization (Qiang) and  
phonation and tonal contrasts (Tibetic), which complicate 
reliable automatic formant extraction. Our primary focus is on 
acoustic measures used to characterize phonation (voice 
quality), resonance, and spectral structure. These include: (a) 
formant frequencies (F1–F4); (b) H1–H2; (c) harmonics-to-
noise ratio (HNR); (d) cepstral peak prominence (CPP) and (e) 
fundamental frequency (fo) (e.g., [1], [2]). Although established 
tools for extracting these parameters are widely available, most 
notably Praat [3] and VoiceSauce [4], their automatic 
procedures frequently yield systematic errors when applied to 
our target languages. Moreover, in the absence of established 
reference values against which measurements can be evaluated, 
such errors cannot be detected automatically and must instead 
be identified through careful visual inspection and manual 
correction. This need for systematic and integrated verification 
motivated the development of NewAppVoice. 
NewAppVoice provides a unified interface that implements 
and compares multiple acoustic measurement algorithms while 
maintaining continuous visual access to the underlying signal. 
By enabling cross-algorithm comparison and direct validation 
of extracted parameters, the workflow enhances both reliability 
and reproducibility. Originally developed in MATLAB App 
Designer and incorporating Praat-based routines, the software 
has since been compiled into standalone executables for macOS 
and Windows, with no MATLAB license required. The article 
proceeds in two stages. First, NewAppVoice is situated within 
the broader landscape of existing acoustic analysis tools. 
Second, the application itself is presented, with attention to its 
architecture and core functionalities. 
2. Existing Acoustic Analysis Tools and 
the Position of NewAppVoice  
NewAppVoice was developed in response to limitations 
encountered when applying existing tools to under-documented 
languages such as Qiang and Tibetic varieties. To clarify its 
contribution, we briefly review the current ecosystem of 
phonetic software. A wide range of programs supports acoustic 
measurement in phonetics, including Praat, VoiceLab [5], 
VoiceSauce, WaveSurfer [6], WinPitch [7], and XKL [8]. 
These tools provide standard measurements such as 
fundamental frequency (fo), formants, intensity, and voice-
quality metrics (e.g., HNR, CPP), as well as spectral analyses 
including spectrograms, FFT spectra, and LPC-based methods. 
However, they differ substantially in usability, the integration 
of visualization and measurement, and the balance between 
manual control and automation. Programs such as Praat and 
WaveSurfer combine rich visualizations with scripting for 
batch processing. While powerful, they require careful 
parameter adjustment and technical expertise. Tools such as 
VoiceSauce and VoiceLab emphasize automated extraction, 
particularly of voice quality parameters, but provide more 
limited interactive visual feedback. WinPitch and XKL 
prioritize interactive visual inspection of prosodic and spectral 
features while retaining automated functions. Although most 
programs allow data export, workflows are not always 
standardized or easily reproducible. The field has increasingly 
favored automated analysis in response to growing corpora. Yet 
automation often reduces continuous visual oversight during 
measurement, increasing the risk of systematic errors when 
default settings are poorly suited to specific datasets, speakers, 
or typologically unusual segmental configurations. This 
concern is particularly acute for under-documented languages, 
where reliable formant estimation frequently requires iterative 
parameter adjustment and routine visual validation. Fully 
manual workflows, while precise, are difficult to scale.  
NewAppVoice was designed to bridge this divide. It integrates 
automated extraction with continuou s, synchronized 
visualization of signal and measurements, enabling direct 
comparison of multiple algorithms within a single interface. 
This architecture supports systematic verification and 
correction of extracted values, thereby enhancing reliability and 
reproducibility, capabilities not jointly available in existing 
workflows for our target languages.

3. NewAppVoice Overview 
NewAppVoice operates on .wav files accompanied by 
annotated .TextGrid files. After accessing a designated folder, 
the user selects the audio file to be analyzed. The software 
automatically reads the corresponding TextGrid file and 
supports annotation-based segmentation, allowing the analysis 
to be restricted to user-selected tiers and labeled intervals. This 
enables long recordings to be processed selectively, without 
requiring analysis of the entire recording. Users can also define 
the number of measurement points within each interval. These 
points are displayed directly on the acoustic signal in a distinct 
color, ensuring transparent alignment between annotations and 
extracted measurements. The signal can be played back within 
the interface, facilitating auditory verification alongside visual 
inspection. Figure 1 presents a screenshot of the NewAppVoice 
working window, illustrating the analysis of the Baima Tibetan 
word /dzɑ̀/ ‘moon’, produced by a male speaker. The interface 
integrates file selection, parameter settings, visualization, 
measurement control, and results output within a single 
environment.
 
 
Figure 1: Screenshot of the NewAppVoice interface with the .wav file /dzɑ̀/ ‘moon’ (Baima Tibetan). 
 
At the top center of the interface, the acoustic waveform is 
displayed, with measurement intervals highlighted. Directly 
below, the corresponding spectrogram is shown, with 
superimposed tracking lines for fo and formants. This vertical 
alignment of waveform, spectrogram, and tracking curves 
enables immediate visual verification of automatic detection. 
On the left, the control panel provides access to file selection, 
mono/stereo settings, and the definition of  the number of 
measurement points. Users can acti vate specific acoustic 
parameters-such as fo, formants, harmonics, intensity (RMS, 
dB), spectrum, HNR, and CPP-via checkboxes. The same panel 
also allows selection of tiers and labeled segments from the 
associated TextGrid file. Additional parameter panels permit 
fine-grained adjustment of analysis settings, including 
minimum and maximum fo thresholds, the number and 
frequency range of detectable formants, and harmonic analysis 
settings. These controls allow users to tailor detection 
algorithms to individual speakers or datasets. The central 
“Measures” panel overlays multiple acoustic trajectories within 
the selected interval, including fo, formants (F1–F4), intensity, 
and CPP. Displaying these measures simultaneously facilitates 
cross-validation and rapid identification of tracking 
inconsistencies. In the lower-left panel, spectral and LPC-based 
analyses are presented. The spectrum view displays harmonic 
structure, while the LPC analysis provides an additional 
visualization of formant peak regions (F1-F4). Together, these 
complementary representations support detailed inspection of 
both source-related (harmonic) and filter-related (formant) 
properties. On the right, a results table lists the selected tiers, 
zones, files, and segments. This table allows users to review, 
select, and save measurement outputs and to adjust selections 
where necessary. The integration of waveform, spectrogram, 
multi-parameter trajectories, spectral/LPC displays, and 
tabulated results enables efficient detection of common errors, 
such as incorrect formant tracking, harmonic misidentification, 
or fo tracking failures. By combining multiple algorithms for fo 
and formant detection within a synchronized and visually 
controlled environment, NewAppVoice supports systematic 
verification and reproducible acoustic analysis. 
3.1. Pitch 
NewAppVoice integrates three fo detection algorithms:  
 
● STRAIGHT ([9]; here labeled fo Straight), 
● the Subharmonic-to-Harmonic Ratio method (SHR; [10]; fo 
SHR), and 
● Praat’s autocorrelation algorithm ([3]; fo Praat). 
 
For comparability, all three operate with identical window 
length, step size, and analysis parameters. fo estimation is 
particularly sensitive to language-specific phonation patterns 
and recording conditions (e.g., background noise), which may 
result in tracking errors. To mitigate this, NewAppVoice 
displays the outputs of all three algorithms simultaneously in 
the central “Measures” panel (see Figure 1). While many 
acoustic analysis tools implement multiple fo detection 
algorithms, they rarely allow their outputs to be visualized 
concurrently within a synchronized interface. A key innovation 
of NewAppVoice lies precisely in this integrated visualization: 
users can inspect and compare fo traces across algorithms in 
direct alignment with the acoustic signal and with one another. 
This facilitates immediate cross-algorithm evaluation and

supports informed assessment of robustness within a given 
recording context. Algorithm-specific parameters can be 
adjusted in the dedicated “F0” settings panel, including 
minimum and maximum pitch thresholds. The resulting fo 
contours are displayed together in the central visualization 
window: fo Straight appears as a solid line, fo Praat as dashed 
lines, and fo SHR as dotted lines (see Figure 2). This 
synchronized display provides direct visual feedback on pitch 
tracking performance, facilitating rapid detection of octave 
jumps, voicing errors, or algorithm-specific artifacts. For the 
SHR method, the raw algorithm is implemented without 
smoothing or noise filtering. This deliberate choice preserves 
sensitivity to fine-grained fo variation that might otherwise be 
attenuated through post-processing, thereby supporting detailed 
phonetic inspection. Figure 2 presents an example of fo tracking 
for the Baima Tibetan word /dzɑ̀/ ‘moon’. 
 
 
Figure 2: fo window with the different fo algorithms, the solid 
line corresponds to the fo Straight, the dashed line to the fo 
Praat, the dotted line to the SHR fo. These fo plots are

Figure 2 shows that the solid line ( fo Straight) and the 
dashed line (fo Praat) yield closely aligned and largely 
continuous contours across the duration of the word. By 
contrast, the dotted line ( fo SHR) displays several 
discontinuities, reflecting the greater sensitivity of the 
unsmoothed SHR algorithm to local signal irregularities. 
These interruptions arise because the SHR implementation 
does not apply additional constraints such as minimum voicing 
thresholds, silence detection, or periodicity filtering. 
Consequently, the contour remains highly responsive to short-
term fluctuations in the signal. If smoothing and voicing 
constraints comparable to those used in Praat or STRAIGHT 
were introduced, the SHR trace would appear more continuous. 
The decision to preserve the raw output is deliberate: it enables 
users to inspect fine-grained pitch variation and to compare 
algorithm-specific behavior transparently within the same 
analytical framework. 
3.2. Formant Frequencies 
Following the same principle applied to fo, NewAppVoice 
integrates two complementary methods for formant estimation 
in order to facilitate cross -validation. For each segment, 
formants F1–F4 are calculated together with their bandwidths. 
To enhance readability, each formant is assigned a distinct 
color: F1 (red), F2 (green), F3 (purple), and F4 (yellow). 
The first method, labeled STRAIGHT, is displayed in the 
central measurement window as color-coded points (see Figure 
3). This approach relies on LPC -based analysis, with the 
analysis window duration determined by the fo Straight 
estimate. The number of displayed formant points corresponds 
to the user-defined number of measurement intervals. For 
example, if three intervals are specified within a vowel, three 
measurement points per formant are shown. The number of 
formant measurement points is user-adjustable via the “N° 
Measures” field in the main interface. 
The second method is based on Praat’s LPC 
implementation using the Burg algorithm. These formants are 
visualized as dotted tracking lines on the spectrogram and are 
also represented in the central measurement window using the 
same color scheme. Key parameters-including the maximum 
number of detectable formants, maximum formant frequency, 
frame shift, and window length (in ms)-can be adjusted in the 
settings panel. 
For both methods, the LPC analysis window spans three fo 
periods to ensure stable and comparable formant estimation. 
The simultaneous display of point-based (STRAIGHT) and 
continuous (Praat/Burg) tracking facilitates rapid identification 
of discrepancies and potential tracking errors. 
Figure 3 illustrates formant tracking in the Baima Tibetan 
word /dzɑ̀/ ‘moon’. The spectrogram displayed is a wideband 
spectrogram, computed using a 1024-point Hamming window.

3.2.1. LPC 
The LPC analysis window (Figure 5) is derived from the fo 
Straight estimate. For each voiced segment, an analysis frame 
of approximately 1.5 pitch periods, centered within the 
segment, is extracted and Hamming-windowed prior to LPC 
estimation. The spectral envelope is computed using 
autocorrelation-based LPC with a user-specified model order 
(here: order 12). To ensure smooth and high -resolution 
visualization, the envelope is evaluated on a 2048 -point 
frequency grid. No additional pre-emphasis or FFT windowing 
is applied. 
In the display, the solid curve represents the LPC spectral 
envelope, while dashed vertical lines mark the formant 
frequencies corresponding to the poles of the LPC model. Both 
derive from the same analysis. When a vowel is divided into 
multiple temporal segments, each segment is analyze d 
independently, producing separate spectral envelopes and sets 
of formant estimates. 
LPC analysis models the vocal tract as an all-pole filter and 
generates a smoothed spectral envelope in which resonances 
appear as poles. The number of poles is determined by the 
selected model order; LPC does not intrinsically “detect” 
formants but estimates them according to the chosen 
parameters. An order that is too low may miss relevant 
resonances, whereas an excessively high order may introduce 
spurious peaks. For this reason, visual inspection of both the 
LPC envelope and the underlying (unsmoothed) spectrum is 
essential to assess whether detected poles correspond to actual 
spectral maxima. Figure 5 illustrates this principle.

discrepancy underscores the importance of comparing the LPC-
derived envelope with the unsmoothed spectrum (see Figure 7), 
which clearly reveals two distinct peaks between 3000 and 4000 
Hz, in addition to the peak at 4861 Hz.

3.2.2. Spectrum 
The LPC analysis window also displays the raw magnitude 
spectrum of the selected signal segment (see Figure 7). This 
spectrum is computed by applying a discrete Fourier transform 
(FFT) to a Hamming-windowed segment of approximately 1.5 
pitch periods. The FFT length corresponds to the segment 
length, and the spectrum is plotted in decibels.

The raw spectrum also serves as a reference for evaluating 
LPC results. By comparing spectral peaks in the unsmoothed 
FFT spectrum with the LPC-derived spectral envelope, users 
can assess whether the detected poles correspond to actual 
resonances in the signal.

3.3. Energy 
Energy is displayed in the central window as an intensity 
contour, expressed either in dB RMS or in linear RMS units 
(see Figures 8–9). The logarithmic dB RMS scale approximates 
human loudness perception, while linear RMS reflects the 
signal’s physical energy directly. In both cases, the intensity 
curve is plotted alongside the fo contour to facilitate comparison 
between amplitude and pitch dynamics across the segment.
