Abstract 
Real-world Lombard effects—vocal adjustments in noisy 
environments—are overlooked in patients with voice disorders. 
We analyzed ambulatory data from 24 patients with 
phonotraumatic and non-phonotraumatic vocal hyperfunction 
(PVH and NPVH) and 18 healthy controls (2–4 days; 
≥ 10 h/day), synchronizing equivalent ambient noise levels 
(Leq) with voice measures: SPL (sound level), F0 (pitch), CPP 
(cepstral peak prominence), and H1H2 (first minus second 
harmonic magnitude). We fit models predicting each voice 
measure from ambient noise (Leq mean, SD). With higher Leq, 
SPL, F0, and CPP rose and H1H2 fell; CPP showed the 
strongest correlation (r = 0.36–0.53). Nonlinear models fit best 
(max test r² = 0.32 for CPP). PVH had steeper SPL slope (0.65 
dB/dB Leq mean; 0.86 dB/dB Leq SD) and larger CPP slope 
(0.18 dB/dB Leq mean) than NPVH. Real-world Lombard 
effects in patients were subtype-specific, with CPP capturing 
voice-quality effects beyond SPL and F0. 
Index Terms: speech acoustics, Lombard effect, ambient 
noise, voice disorders, ambulatory monitoring 
1. Introduction 
Voice production adapts to the acoustic environment [1, 2]. As 
ambient noise increases, speakers often respond to maintain 
intelligibility by raising vocal intensity and adjusting other 
aspects of speech, such as pitch [3], voice quality [4, 5], spectral 
energy [6], speaking rate [7], phonation time [8], and 
articulation [9]; these patterns are termed Lombard effects [1]. 
These adjustments in everyday settings may affect vocal load 
and elicit voice disorder symptoms  [10]. Most Lombard 
evidence comes from controlled laboratory studies that play 
back noise during scripted speaking tasks [3, 8, 11-15]. These 
studies found that Lombard responses emerge around 46–57 
dBA [16, 17], with a 1 dB noise increase associated with 
increases of 0.3–1 dB in sound pressure level (SPL) [8, 11-15] 
and 1 Hz in fundamental frequency (F0) [3]. However, 
laboratory conditions do not capture the temporal variability of 
daily-life ambient noise [18, 19], leaving uncertainty about how 
well these findings generalize to everyday communication 
contexts and speaking behaviors. 
Studying the Lombard effect in daily life is important 
because real-world communication rarely occurs in the quiet, 
stable conditions typical of laboratory studies [20]. People 
speak while commuting, working, and moving through 
environments where noise levels can dynamically change [2, 
20]. Lombard adaptation may involve not only loudness and 
pitch changes but also systematic shifts in voice quality that 
may be clinically relevant [4]. Vocal changes are especially 
important to understand in common voice disorders associated 
with phonotraumatic and non -phonotraumatic vocal 
hyperfunction (VH), where impaired voice production may 
alter noise-driven phonatory adjustments, either increasing 
vocal tissue loading (phonotraumatic VH; PVH) or reinforcing 
inefficient voice use (non-phonotraumatic VH; NPVH) [21]. 
High voice use in noisy environments is linked to vocal 
symptoms and is cited as a risk for voice disorders [22-26]. 
A few studies with vocally healthy individuals have 
characterized Lombard-related vocal features in everyday 
environments using ambulatory voice and noise monitoring, 
pairing a neck-surface accelerometer (ACC) for voice measures 
with a microphone (MIC) for ambient noise levels [17, 18, 27-
31]. Linear-regression analyses showed large between-speaker 
variability, with SPL–noise correlations of 0.07–0.87 and F0–
noise correlations of 0.11–0.78 [29]. Systematic ambient noise–
related changes in voice quality during day-long monitoring 
have been reported: CPP increased (0.19 dB/dB) and H1H2 
decreased (−0.27 dB/dB) with increases in environmental noise 
level [4]. Together, these findings suggest that daily -life 
loudness increases may either support or compromise vocal 
function, with potential implications for VH risk. 
However, prior work has rarely studied individuals with 
voice disorders who may show maladaptive responses in 
acoustically demanding environments. One such study reported 
higher daily-life noise exposure in patients with PVH than those 
with NPVH and healthy speakers. SPL and F0 increased across 
noise bins (≤ 55, 55–70, > 70 dBA) for all groups but with no 
between-group differences in Lombard patterns [21]. That 
study was limited to reporting daylong averages of voice 
SPL/F0 and environmental noise. The current work expands 
upon the past work by employing continuous vocal-function 
characterization with new voice measures that may capture 
disorder-specific Lombard-related phonatory adjustments. 
2. Methods 
2.1. Study participants 
We collected real-world ambulatory recordings from 14 
patients with PVH (14 females; bilateral vocal fold nodules; 29 
monitored days; ages 30–63 years), 10 patients with NPVH (8 
females, 2 males; primary muscle tension dysphonia; 26 
monitored days; ages 19–44 years), and 18 vocally healthy 
controls (17 females, 1 male; 53 monitored days; ages 19–55 
years). The study was approved by the Mass General Brigham 
Institutional Review Board. All participants completed an 
initial laboratory visit at the Massachusetts General Hospital 
Voice Center to provide informed consent. During the visit, 
controls underwent laryngeal endoscopic screening to confirm 
healthy vocal folds. Patient diagnoses were based on laryngeal 
stroboscopy, aerodynamic assessment, acoustic analysis, and 
auditory-perceptual ratings of voice and speech [32].

2.2. Wearable voice and noise monitoring system 
Figure 1 outlines the study workflow and analysis. In lab, each 
participant received two devices with instructions and training 
on setup and wear. The voice-monitor device contained medical 
tape, a neck-worn accelerometer (ACC) connected to a 
smartphone; the environmental sound level device consisted of 
a shoulder-mounted microphone (MIC) paired with a noise 
dosimeter. A representative wearing both devices is shown in 
Figure 2. We used a uniaxial ACC to capture anterior neck-
surface vibrations [18, 31]. The ACC was secured with medical 
tape on the anterior neck above the clavicle and below the 
Adam’s apple. Each ACC unit was calibrated to units of cm/s2. 
The ACC was connected by a cable to a smartphone. Signal 
acquisition used sigma-delta modulation at 11025 Hz and 16-
bit resolution [33]. Because the wearable MIC was processed to 
capture ambient noise, we used an ACC–SPL mapping to 
estimate vocal SPL. To map ACC amplitude to acoustic voice 
SPL, a daily ACC–SPL relationship was derived by participants 
using an external microphone. With the ACC in place, 
participants sustained /a/ while gradually reducing intensity; the 
microphone was positioned 15 cm from the participant’s lips. 
A linear regression between ACC level (dB) and microphone 
level was obtained to apply to ambulatory ACC data [33].

The second device monitored noise level using a Spark 
705P noise dosimeter (sound level meter, SLM, in Figure 2) 
with a microphone (flat 20 Hz–20 kHz frequency response; 1 
Hz sampling). The MIC was fitted with a windscreen and 
mounted on a shoulder near the ear to stabilize placement and 
capture approximate sound levels sensed by the auditory 
system. Participants then completed 2–4 full-day monitoring 
sessions (≥ 10 h/day). Participants maintained usual routines, 
wearing devices throughout their waking day, except during 
situations likely to dislodge or damage the devices. 
Standardized reattachment instructions were provided, and 
daily placement was verified by signal-quality checks. 
2.3. Data processing and feature extraction 
Environmental noise was represented by equivalent sound level 
(Leq) in A-weighted decibel units (dBA) computed from the 
Spark microphone signal (raw MIC signals were not recorded) 
[34]. Environmental level was computed from the microphone 
in 1-s, non-overlapping frames to form a continuous Leq series. 
Because the microphone captured participants’ vocal sound 
level, Leq samples during detected speech activity (ACC-based 
voice activity detection) were excluded; microphone-based Leq 
was retained only during non-speech periods to represent 
ambient noise exposure. Microphone and ACC time axes were 
aligned using a MATLAB procedure: for each day, we selected 
the offset that maximized the cross-correlation between the 
microphone-derived Leq and ACC-based SPL trajectories 
during a reading passage [35] and three sustained vowels at the 
beginning of monitoring to yield day-specific alignment. 
We then extracted voice measures from the ACC signal that 
was segmented into non-overlapping 50 ms frames, and a 
MATLAB voicing detector identified voiced versus unvoiced 
frames [33]. For voiced frames, we computed vocal SPL, F0, 
CPP, and H1H2 from the ACC signal [4, 36]; SPL and F0 index 
voice level and pitch, whereas CPP and H1H2 reflect signal 
periodicity and glottal closure patterns, respectively [33, 37].  
Summary statistics of the MIC and ACC signal measures 
were computed across 3-minute, non-overlapping windows to 
enable the computation of time-varying, daily-life Lombard 
effects. Windows with less than 0.5 % voicing time were 
discarded to allow for adequate statistical analysis. Within each 
3-minute window, we summarized voice SPL, F0, CPP, and 
H1H2 using nine summary statistics (mean, median, SD, 
5th/95th percentiles, skewness, kurtosis, min, and max ). 
Ambient Leq was summarized using two statistics (mean and 
SD). Ambient-noise exposure time (non-speech periods with 
valid Leq) was computed per window in minutes and percent. 
We thus compiled a window-level dataset (one row per analysis 
window) indexed by speaker, diagnosis, date, and time, 
containing voice and noise summary features. 
2.4. Predictive modeling of vocal behavior given 
ambient noise levels 
We first quantified in-field Lombard behavior within each 
group using Pearson correlations between Leq mean and each 
voice feature, consistent with prior work [4, 29]. Leq mean  
(rather than Leq SD) was used as it represented the window’s 
overall noise exposure. This also enabled direct comparison 
with prior work [21, 38]. Those studies stratified ambient levels 
into three Leq ranges (≤ 55, 55–70, > 70 dBA) and computed 
group-wise mean voice measures within each range. For each 
voice measure category, we chose one summary statistic by 
ranking their Pearson correlations with Leq mean. Although F0 
max had the highest correlation with Leq mean, we used F0 
95th percentile because the maximum is sensitive to outliers. 
Final voice statistics were CPP mean, SPL median, F0 95th 
percentile, and H1H2 median. 
Then, using regression models, we predicted these voice 
statistics from the two ambient noise statistics (Leq mean, Leq 
SD). Models were trained separately by speaker group and 
predicted voice statistic. We compared four regression models: 
linear regression, second -order polynomial Ridge/Lasso 
(Poly2-RidgeCV/LassoCV), and gradient-boosted regression 
trees (GBR). Models were selected by 5-fold, cross-validated r² 
(KFold with shuffling; out -of-fold predictions) and then 
evaluated on a fixed 80/20 train-test split (number of train/test 
windows: 4927/1232 controls, 2562/641 NPVH, 2911/728 
PVH). We evaluated 48 candidates (3 speaker groups × 4 voice 
statistics × 4 model families) and reported 12 final models (one

3. Results 
We use shorthand names for the outcomes: Leq = Leq mean, 
CPP = mean CPP, SPL = median SPL, F0 = 95th-percentile F0, 
and H1H2 = median H1H2. Noise exposure differed across 
groups: PVH spent the largest fraction of time in noisy 
environments (> 70 dBA; 26.1% [24.7–27.6]), followed by 
controls (21.5% [20.5–22.6]) and NPVH (19.4% [18.1–20.9]). 
Figure 3 shows mean (±95% CI) voice measures across Leq 
bins (≤ 55, 55–70, > 70 dBA). Across groups, SPL, F0, and 
CPP increased with increasing ambient noise, whereas H1H2 
decreased. Across noise bins, CPP was highest in PVH and 
showed clearer separation than SPL or F0. NPVH was least 
CPP-responsive, with CPP in noisy conditions only similar to 
PVH in moderate noise. For SPL, NPVH and controls tracked 
closely, whereas PVH was slightly higher up to 70 dBA and 
then showed a larger increase in > 70 dBA, yielding clearer 
separation. For F0, controls showed the strongest increase; 
PVH and NPVH changed less.

4. Discussion and conclusions 
Prior Lombard work has focused on average-noise-level effects 
on SPL and F0 with linear noise–voice mapping in healthy 
speakers. Only one ambulatory study evaluated Lombard 
effects in patients with voice disorders [21], with noise level 
discretized into three bins (soft, moderate, loud) instead of 
continuous noise level modeling. Further, only SPL and F0 
measures were computed, missing critical vocal function 
characterization potentially needed to capture phonatory 
behavior in voice disorders. To address these gaps, we 
quantified Lombard behavior during daily-life monitoring by 
computing summary statistics in 3 -minute windows and 
aggregating them into hourly/day-level measures of SPL, F0, 
and vocal function measures (CPP, H1H2) in VH. We also used 
the 3-minute windows directly for model training. We then 
tested nonlinear noise–voice patterns using both noise mean 
and variability.  
We showed a consistent daily-life pattern: as ambient noise 
increased, vocal SPL, F0, and CPP increased, while H1H2 
decreased, indicating a multi-dimensional Lombard response 
consistent with expected voice physiology due to increasing 
vocal loudness [40, 41]. Accordingly, focusing on SPL or F0 
alone, as in prior work [21, 29, 30], can miss systematic voice-
quality shifts that track noise exposure. CPP showed the 
strongest noise coupling across groups (max r = 0.53 vs. 0.39 
for SPL) and higher predictability from noise than SPL (max 
test r² = 0.32 for CPP vs. 0.18 for SPL). H1H2 also showed a 
consistent negative correlation with noise (−0.30), capturing 
complementary spectral changes. In contrast, F0 was the 
weakest Lombard marker. 
Modeling Lombard behavior requires more than average 
noise level. Whereas Leq mean captured noise–voice 
associations, as in prior work [4, 21, 29, 30], noise variability 
has been less examined. Leq SD captured within-day noise 
fluctuations and added complementary predictive information, 
with stronger link to SPL and F0 than Leq mean in all groups. 
This indicates that noise variability is important for capturing 
real-world Lombard demand–response patterns. Moreover, our 
nonlinear machine learning models can reveal Lombard 
patterns that linear models or simple correlations may miss. The 
present study is among the earliest efforts to model in -field 
Lombard behavior using nonlinear mappings from noise 
characteristics. Nusseck et al. used stepwise linear regression to 
predict mean voice SPL during classroom teaching (r² = 0.52–
0.62). Noise–voice coupling was modest and highly 
individualized (mean r = 0.25; range 0.08–0.84), with stronger 
contributions from non-noise covariates (e.g., class size, class 
subject) [30]. Prior ambulatory work similarly reported wide 
between-subject ranges (e.g., r = −0.15–0.87 for SPL and 0.11–
0.78 for F0) [4, 29]. These results suggest that noise alone 
provides an incomplete linear account. Linear regression (even 
with two predictors) was not selected for any of the 12 final 
models, supporting the use of nonlinear models (e.g., GBR) to 
capture variability in daily-life noise-to-voice relationships. 
Patients with PVH experienced the highest noise exposure, 
indicating greater vocal loading demands, consistent with [21]. 
However, subtype differences were not observed using SPL and 
F0 across three noise levels [21]. In this study, adding vocal 
function measures better captured disorder-specific phonatory 
adjustments. Group differences emerged, particularly for CPP. 
CPP was similar between NPVH and controls at low noise but 
became increasingly discriminative at higher noise levels due 
to different CPP slopes. This effect was strongest when 
modeling noise variability (CPP slope [dB/dB Leq SD]: 0.08 
for NPVH vs. 0.35 for controls). Likewise, SPL in PVH 
diverged more from NPVH at higher noise levels due to steeper 
slopes (0.65 vs. 0.41 dB/dB Leq mean). Also, F0 increases with 
noise were reduced in the PVH and NPVH groups relative to 
the healthy control group, indicating reduced pitch-based 
Lombard flexibility in daily life. 
Limitations include age differences across groups . 
Predictive power of the voice features from noise mean and 
variability was modest; however, limited predictability may 
still reflect physiologically meaningful weaker noise sensitivity 
across groups. Adding contextual/acoustic predictors (e.g., 
environment, talker–listener distance, noise spectrum) may 
better capture in-field Lombard behavior. Future work could 
model higher-order voice statistics (e.g., SD, skewness, 
kurtosis) and test models that combine all voice features to 
improve noise prediction (i.e., reversing the mapping). 
In conclusion, the PVH group showed a larger Lombard 
effect than NPVH, with steeper SPL and CPP slopes, consistent 
with elevated vocal load and potential phonotrauma risk [36, 
42]. NPVH showed a more limited profile, relying mainly on 
loudness, which may relate to auditory–motor impairments [43,
