Hard Positive-targeted Training for Robust Audio Deepfake Detection under
Neural Codec Processing

Abstract
Neural audio codecs are increasingly used in speech pipelines,
enabling high-quality compression at low bitrates. However,
neural codec (NC) processing may impair audio deepfake de-
tection (ADD) by distorting discriminative cues and introduc-
ing artifacts that may obscure spoofing traces. Our embedding
analysis indicates that the robustness drop is driven mainly by
bonafide-side errors: NC-processed bonafide speech shifts to-
ward the spoof region more than NC-processed spoof shifts to-
ward bonafide. To mitigate this effect, we propose a training
strategy that (i) introduces an auxiliary loss to regularize fre-
quently misclassified NC-processed bonafide samples and (ii)
uses a mini-batch construction scheme that repeatedly presents
boundary-adjacent NC-processed bonafide–spoof pairs during
optimization. Experiments using equal error rate (EER) and ac-
curacy demonstrate improved robustness under NC conditions
while maintaining spoof detection performance.
Index Terms: Audio Deepfake Detection, Neural Audio Codec
1. Introduction
Recent advances in artificial intelligence have rapidly improved
the fidelity of audio deepfakes [1, 2], making synthetic speech
increasingly difficult to distinguish from genuine speech. In re-
sponse, the research community has organized multiple bench-
marking challenges [3, 4] and has continued to advance audio
deepfake detection (ADD) models. As deepfake audio can be
generated at scale and deployed in real-world scenarios, im-
proving ADD performance remains an important objective.
In parallel, deep-learning-based neural speech codecs
have emerged as practical alternatives to traditional signal-
processing-based codecs. By leveraging neural networks
throughout the encoding and decoding pipeline, NC enables
low-bitrate compression while preserving perceptual quality
[5, 6, 7, 8].
Recent work has shown that NC-processing can substan-
tially degrade the performance of ADD systems [9]. Prior work
notes that NC-processing can introduce artifacts similar to those
produced by vocoders commonly used in text-to-speech (TTS)
and voice conversion (VC), which may cause NC-processed
bonafide speech to resemble spoofed samples [10]. Conse-
quently, bonafide speech passed through NC compression may
exhibit spoof-like traces, which can elevate false alarm rates and
undermine robustness in NC-processed conditions.
In this work, we aim to improve ADD performance on NC-
processed audio that is frequently misclassified by existing sys-
tems. Unlike generic discriminative training, we explicitly tar-
get boundary-adjacent cases—NC-processed bonafide speech

exposes such hard positive configurations during training, while
incorporating NC-processed audio into the training data. As a
result, our approach improves robustness under NC-processing,
leading to lower EER and fewer false alarms on NC-processed
bonafide speech.
2. Motivation Evaluation
In this work, we follow the ASVspoof labeling protocol, where
spoofdenotes utterances generated by TTS and VC algorithms
[11]. Accordingly, synthesized or converted samples are labeled
asspoof, whereas genuine recordings are labeled asbonafide.
Here, NC-processing refers to reconstructing an input wave-
form by passing it through an NC encoder and decoder (NC re-
construction), rather than synthesizing new speech. Since NC-
processing reconstructs the same utterance, we keep the original
label unchanged after NC-processing.
We first visualize embedding distributions (Figure 1) using
t-SNE [12] on embeddings extracted from an SSL-Conformer
[13] model trained on ASVspoof2019 Logical Access (LA19)
[14], following the evaluation dataset setup in Section 4.2.2.
Figure 1a shows embeddings of the original bonafide and
spoof samples. Figure 1b additionally includes NC-processed
bonafide samples, while Figure 1c includes NC-processed
spoof samples. The visualization indicates that NC-processed
bonafide embeddings substantially overlap with the spoof re-
gion, whereas NC-processed spoof embeddings largely remain
within the original spoof cluster. Overall, the t-SNE plots sug-
gest reduced separability under NC-processing: spoof embed-
dings appear relatively stable, whereas bonafide embeddings
exhibit a more pronounced shift, leading to increased overlap
between the two classes.
Motivated by the trend visualized in Figure 1, we quan-
tify the effect and examine whether it generalizes across de-
tectors by evaluating two state-of-the-art ADD models, SSL-
Conformer and SSL-AASIST [15], under both the original and
NC-processed conditions. We report class-wise accuracy for
bonafide and spoof samples to assess whether the trend ob-
served in Figure 1 is reflected in measurable performance.

3. Methodology
In this section, we propose a training strategy to enhance ADD
robustness to NC-processing. The core idea is to widen the em-
bedding gap between NC-processed bonafide and spoof sam-
ples, which directly addresses the overlap region created by
NC artifacts. Accordingly, inspired by proactive batch sam-
pling [16], we design mini-batches that deliberately contain
boundary-adjacent NC-processed bonafide–spoof pairs that are
prone to confusion. We then jointly optimize the classification
loss with auxiliary objectives that promote separation in the em-
bedding space.
3.1. Batch construction
For effective training, we construct each mini-batch around a
clean bonafideanchor. Given an anchor sample, we include
NC-processed bonafide samples as positives (N positive) and
spoof samples as negatives (Nnegative).N negative contains both
clean spoof and NC-processed spoof samples.
In our experiments, each mini-batch consists of one
clean bonafide anchor, two NC-processed bonafide positives
(Npositive = 2; each processed by a different codec), and three
spoof negatives (Nnegative = 3). Among the three spoof sam-
ples, two are clean spoof and one is NC-processed spoof.

4. Evaluation
4.1. Neural codec processing
In our experiments, we generate NC-processed datasets using
four neural speech codecs: BigCodec 1, EnCodec2 [6], Speech-
Tokenizer3, and FunCodec4 [7].
BigCodec uses sequential modeling with vector quantiza-
tion for neural audio compression. EnCodec is a streaming
neural audio codec with quantized latents trained with adver-
sarial and multi-scale spectral losses. SpeechTokenizer uses an
encoder–decoder with residual vector quantization to derive dis-
crete speech tokens. FunCodec provides frequency-domain NC
models with reduced computational cost.

4.2. Models and datasets
4.2.1. Models
To assess performance variations under NC-processing, we
adopt two widely used state-of-the-art ADD models, SSL-
Conformer and SSL-AASIST, as baseline detectors. Both mod-
els use wav2vec 2.0 XLS-R [19] as the front-end feature ex-
tractor to obtain self-supervised speech representations from
raw audio. SSL-Conformer employs a Conformer [20] back-
end that integrates Transformer-based self-attention [21] with
convolutional modules, whereas SSL-AASIST uses the AA-
SIST [22] back-end, which leverages graph-based modeling for
spoofing detection.
We report EER and accuracy as evaluation metrics. EER
is defined at the operating point where the false acceptance rate
equals the false rejection rate.
4.2.2. Datasets
We use ASVspoof2019 Logical Access (LA19) as the baseline
training dataset. In addition, we create NC-processed versions
of LA19 by applying BigCodec and SpeechTokenizer, and in-
clude these NC-processed samples during training.
To evaluate generalization under diverse and realistic con-
ditions, we use multiple external datasets. We randomly sam-
ple 5,000 bonafide utterances from each of VCTK [23], Lib-
riSpeech [24], and V oxCeleb [25] to form bonafide evaluation
sets. Likewise, we randomly sample 5,000 spoof utterances
from ASVspoof2019 LA eval [14], ASVspoof2021 DF eval [3],
and the In-the-Wild dataset [26] to construct spoof evaluation
sets.
For evaluation, we further generate NC-processed versions
of each selected set using multiple codecs. Specifically, we ap-
ply BigCodec and SpeechTokenizer, which are also used during
training, as well as EnCodec and FunCodec, which are not used
during training, and evaluate performance under each NC con-
dition. This protocol incorporates variability in speaker char-
acteristics, recording conditions, and data collection environ-
ments.
4.3. Results
Table 2 summarizes EER for SSL-Conformer and SSL-
AASIST under two training settings. The baseline is trained
only on LA19, whereas the augmentation additionally includes
BigCodec- and SpeechTokenizer-processed training samples
while keeping the same training objective. We evaluate perfor-

First, the augmentation baseline yields only marginal im-
provements over the baseline. In terms of TOTAL EER,
SSL-Conformer improves from 24.36% to 23.11%, and SSL-
AASIST improves from 29.01% to 28.71%. Moreover, the
gains are not consistent across conditions. Under Ori. &
Seen NC, the EER of SSL-Conformer increases from 21.21%
to 23.69%, and SSL-AASIST also increases from 24.59% to
29.25%. Similarly, Table 3 shows that while performance under
the NC conditions included during training improves slightly,
performance on the original condition drops. Overall, these re-
sults suggest that augmentation alone is insufficient to reliably
improve robustness under NC-processing.

These results suggest that enforcing separation between NC-
processed bonafide and spoof during training is more effective
than conventional augmentation for improving robustness under
NC-processed conditions.
4.4. Ablation Study
Table 4 presents an ablation study where we disable each com-
ponent of the proposed objective in turn. Specifically, we fix the
margin configuration to(0.3,0.2), which yields the best perfor-
mance in Table 2, and retrain SSL-Conformer (i) without the
triplet loss and (ii) without the guidance loss. Both ablated vari-
ants yield higher EER than the full objective, suggesting that
jointly optimizing the triplet loss and the guidance loss is more
effective for improving robustness under NC-processing.

5. Discussion
The results suggest that robustness degradation under NC-
processing is closely tied to boundary-adjacent regions in the
embedding space, where NC-processed bonafide samples can
drift toward spoof. Simple augmentation does not necessar-
ily resolve this ambiguity because it does not explicitly shape
the embedding geometry around these hard cases. In con-
trast, the proposed training objective is designed to address such
confusable configurations by enforcing separation constraints
within mini-batches, which helps explain the observed robust-
ness gains.
Several questions remain for future work. The effectiveness
of hard-positive-based training is likely to depend on how hard
positives are selected—specifically, which samples are most
informative for separating NC-processed bonafide from spoof
negatives. Extending the evaluation to a broader range of NCs
and configurations, together with fine-grained error analysis and
margin/weight sensitivity studies, will help clarify when and
why the proposed strategy is most beneficial.
6. Conclusion
In this study, we analyzed robustness degradation in ADD under
NC-processing, with a particular focus on bonafide-side errors.
We proposed a NC-aware training strategy that incorporates
NC-processed audio and explicitly enlarges the embedding gap
between NC-processed bonafide and spoof samples that are fre-
quently confused. Experimental results using both EER and
accuracy verify the effectiveness of the proposed method. The
improvements are primarily driven by reduced bonafide-side er-
rors under NC-processed conditions, while maintaining spoof
detection performance. In addition, an ablation study confirms
that the key components of the proposed objective provide com-
plementary benefits, and that combining them yields the most
reliable performance gains.
