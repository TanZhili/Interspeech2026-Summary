A Dual-Stream Discrete Neural Codec with Fixed-Length Global Speaker
Tokens and Dynamic Frame Rates for Low-Bitrate Speech Tokenization

Abstract
Discrete speech tokens are an effective interface for speech lan-
guage models (SLMs), yet many codecs are inefficient due to
high token rates and mix speaker characteristics with content-
related variation, increasing the modeling burden for down-
stream SLMs. We propose a low-bitrate dual-stream discrete
codec comprising (i) a time-varying token stream from a sin-
gle codebook and (ii) a small set of fixed-length global speaker
tokens. To reduce redundancy, we introduce similarity-based
dynamic frame aggregation that merges consecutive frames and
enables controllable token rates at inference via a single thresh-
old. An adaptive deaggregation module restores the sequence to
the base frame rate for waveform reconstruction. Experiments
demonstrate strong intelligibility and audio quality at low bi-
trates, with improved bitrate–quality trade-offs over fixed-rate
baselines while maintaining speaker similarity.
Index Terms: Speech codecs, low-bitrate coding, speech con-
version, dynamic frame rate, speech generation
1. Introduction
Neural audio codecs (NACs) have become a core component in
speech language model-based text-to-speech systems, bridging
continuous waveforms and discrete sequence modeling [1–4].
Early neural codecs such as SoundStream [5–8] rely on resid-
ual vector quantization (RVQ) [9] to achieve high-fidelity re-
construction. However, RVQ typically produces multiple code
streams, which increases the generation burden for autoregres-
sive SLMs and introduces complex design.
To simplify complex modeling design and reduce genera-
tion latency for SLM, recent single-codebook codecs [10–13]
compress speech into a single discrete token stream, thereby re-
ducing the inference burden for speech language models and
simplifying downstream generation pipelines. However, a com-
mon limitation of these methods is feature entanglement: lin-
guistic content and speaker-related acoustic characteristics are
often encoded in the same token sequence. Consequently,
downstream SLMs must model both semantic and speaker-
dependent variations jointly, which increases modeling diffi-
culty.
To address this issue, a complementary line of research
has focused on disentangled speech tokenization. [4, 14, 15]
promote the factorization of semantic and acoustic information
within the vector quantization process. Semantic representa-
tions are typically derived from distilled student semantic en-
coder or pretrained self-supervised learning(SSL) speech en-
coder, while acoustic information is encoded by acoustic en-
coder and RVQ.

Parallel to research on semantic-acoustic disentanglement,
timbre–semantic decoupling codec such as [16–18] has be-
come particularly prominent, as it pushes speech LMs to fo-
cus on time-varying features and timbre can be modeled after
LM generation through a flow-matching decoder [19]. TiCodec
uses time-invariant continuous speaker embeddings, whereas
BiCodec [2] further quantizes speaker timbre into a fixed-
length sequence of global tokens using finite scalar quantiza-
tion (FSQ) [20], and feeds these global tokens directly into the
speech language model [2].
Orthogonal to codec factorization and inspired by the unsu-
pervised semantic unit discovery at syllable-level [21, 22], dy-
namic frame-rate NACs [23, 24] are proposed to further re-
duce token rates by adaptively merging semantically similar
frames. The core motivation for dynamic-rate codecs is that in
fixed-frame-rate (FFR) codecs, slowly varying regions such as
long vowels or silence often consume disproportionately many
frames despite carrying limited additional semantic informa-
tion [24]. In contrast, dynamic frame-rate modeling allocates
fewer tokens to temporally redundant regions while preserving
finer temporal resolution for rapidly changing speech content.
In this work, we propose DySTCodec a low-bitrate
Dynamic dual-StreamToken Codec that reduces the effective
semantic token rate via dynamic frame aggregation while re-
taining controllable speaker conditioning with a compact set
of global speaker tokens. The method most closely related
to ours is BiCodec. However, DySTCodec differs from it in
three important aspects: (1) timbre perturbation before seman-
tic feature extraction, which suppresses speaker leakage ; (2)
dynamic frame aggregation, which removes temporally redun-
dant semantic frames and reduces the effective token rate; and
(3) an adaptive deaggregation module with lightweight refine-
ment, which restores the base frame rate and mitigates boundary
artifacts.
2. Methods
2.1. Overall
The architecture of DySTCodec shown in Figure 1 consists of
six major parts: the global encoding pathway, the semantic en-
coding pathway, a fusion prenet, a postnet, an adaptive deaggre-
gation module, and a waveform decoder. The global encoding
pathway is composed of three components: a speaker encoder, a
query-based token aggregation module, and finite scalar quanti-
zation (FSQ). The semantic encoding pathway includes a frozen
Wav2Vec2.0 encoder, a dynamic frame aggregation module, a
semantic encoder, and a vector quantization (VQ) module. It
should be mentioned that we use the termSemantic Encoding

2.2. Global Encoding Pathway
The global encoding pathway is designed to capture time-
invariant speaker attributes from the original (unperturbed)
waveform. We first compute an 80-bin mel-spectrogram and
feed it into an ECAPA-TDNN encoder 1, producing a variable-
length sequence of speaker-aware hidden features. To obtain
a fixed-length representation suitable for discrete conditioning,
we apply a query token aggregator:M= 32learnable queries
attend to the encoder outputs via cross-attention and summa-
rize the variable-length sequence intoMcontinuous global to-
kens. In parallel, the same pathway also yields a pooled global
speaker embedding for vector-level conditioning.

2.3. Semantic Encoding Pathway
The semantic encoding pathway is designed to suppress
speaker timbre while preserving the time-varying linguistic and
prosodic information. To reduce speaker leakage, we apply a
timbre perturbation algorithm [25] to the original waveform be-
fore feature extraction. Specifically, the input waveform is first
time-stretched with a scaling factorβto shift pitch and formant
characteristics, and its duration is then restored using a pitch-
preserving tempo operation. This produces a perturbed sig-
nal that largely preserves content and prosody while weakening
speaker-dependent timbre cues. During training,βis uniformly
sampled from[0.8,1.2], following LSCodec [18].

Following [23, 26, 27], we further refine the aggregated se-

transition smoothness. Local attention is adopted for better

8192. The quantizer is initialized with k-means and updated
with EMA during training.
2.4. Fusion Prenet and Postnet
We use a vector-level speaker-conditioning mechanism in the
fusion prenet. Specifically, the quantized global token sequence
is converted into a compact global speaker embedding, which is
then injected into the prenet through adaptive layer normaliza-
tion.
Architecturally, the fusion prenet follows the same back-
bone design as the semantic encoder. It takes the quantized
semantic token sequence as the main input and incorporates
speaker information through vector-level conditioning. Given
the quantized global token sequenceG∈R M×d , we concate-
nate theMtokens along the feature dimension to form a flat-
tened representation ˜G∈R M d. A learnable linear projection
then maps ˜Gto a compact speaker embeddingD∈R d. This
embedding is used to modulate the hidden activations of the fu-
sion prenet via adaptive layer normalization, thereby providing
global speaker information in a fixed-dimensional form.
The postnet is attached after adaptive frame deaggregation
to provide auxiliary semantic supervision. It adopts the same

2.5. Adaptive Frame Deaggregation
Because the semantic sequence is dynamically aggregated, the
fused features produced by the fusion prenet are defined on the
aggregated segment sequence rather than the original frame-
level timeline (50 Hz) required for waveform reconstruction. To
resolve this mismatch, the adaptive frame deaggregation mod-
ule restores a frame-aligned latent sequence using the same seg-
ment assignments obtained during dynamic frame aggregation.
Specifically, letY= [y 1,...,y K]denote the fused la-
tent sequence on the aggregated timeline, where each latent
yk corresponds to a segmentS k. Deaggregation expands each
segment-level latent back to all original frames belonging to
that segment, yielding a restored frame-level sequence ˆY=
[ˆy1,..., ˆyT ], where
ˆyt =y k, t∈S k.(3)
In other words, all frames within the same merged segment
share the same latent representation after deaggregation. To
alleviate boundary discontinuities introduced by segment-wise
expansion, we further apply a lightweight non-causal bottleneck
transformer to the restored 50 Hz sequence. This module refines
local transitions, incorporates short-range context, and smooths
artifacts near segment boundaries, thereby improving waveform
continuity and perceptual naturalness before waveform decod-
ing.
2.6. Waveform Decoder
The waveform decoder reconstructs the time-domain signal
from the deaggregated and refined latent features. We use
a convolutional neural vocoder with progressive transposed-
convolution upsampling. Concretely, the decoder starts with a
1D convolutional projection, followed by a stack of four up-
sampling stages with stride factors[8,5,4,2]. Each stage con-
sists of a transposed convolution and a lightweight residual re-
finement block (with multiple dilated residual units) to improve
local waveform modeling after upsampling. A final1D convo-
lution followed bytanhmaps the hidden representation to the
waveform amplitude.
The overall upsampling factor is8×5×4×2 = 320,
which matches the frame-hop setting used in our system (i.e.,
50 Hz latent/frame sequence to 16 kHz waveform samples).
In implementation, we use weight-normalized convolutions and
LeakyReLU activations throughout the decoder.
2.7. Loss Construction
We train DySTCodec with a joint objective that combines
waveform reconstruction, semantic feature supervision, vector
quantization regularization, speaker consistency, and adversar-
ial learning. The generator loss is defined as
L=λ melLmel +λ featdistillLfeatdistill +λ vqLvq
+λ advLadv +λ fmLfm +λ spkLspk. (4)
Here,L mel is a multi-scale mel-spectrogram reconstruction
loss,L featdistill is an MSE feature distillation loss between
the postnet predictions and frozen Wav2Vec2.0 features,L vq
is the semantic quantization loss, andL spk is anL 1 loss be-
tween teacher and student speaker embeddings. The seman-
tic codebook is updated with EMA, so we do not use an ad-
ditional codebook-update loss. We further employ a multi-
scale STFT discriminator and a multi-period discriminator to
improve perceptual quality. In practice, we setλ mel = 15,
λfeatdistill = 1.0,λ vq = 1.0,λ adv = 2.0, andλ fm = 1.0.
2.8. Experiments Setup
We train DySTCodec on the 16 kHz LibriSpeech corpus (about
1,000 hours) [28] using the train-clean-100, train-clean-360,
and train-other-500 splits. We test our model and baselines on
the test-clean set. Our model is trained for 300 epochs with a
batch size of 36 on 8 H800 GPUs. During training, each ut-
terance is paired with a prompt segment obtained by randomly
cropping a contiguous span whose duration is uniformly sam-
pled in the range[1/3,1/2]of the full utterance. Optimization
is performed with AdamW, withβ 1 = 0.8andβ 2 = 0.9.
Letr s denote the effective semantic token rate after dy-
namic aggregation, and let|C s|denote the semantic codebook
size. The semantic-stream bitrate is defined as
Rsem =r s log2|Cs|.(5)
In our system,|C s|= 8192, so each semantic token carries
log2|Cs|= 13bits. For the fixed-frame-rate (FFR) variant,
the semantic token rate isr s = 50tokens/s, corresponding to
the 50 Hz frame rate, which givesR sem = 50×13 = 650
bps. For a fair comparison, we report thetotalbitrate by adding
the amortized global-token overhead to the semantic-stream bi-
trate, i.e.,R tot≈R sem+R glob. On LibriSpeech test-clean, the
global stream contributes approximately 52 bps when amortized
by the average utterance duration, resulting inR tot≈702bps
(0.70kbps) for the 50 Hz semantic stream. We apply the
same total-bitrate accounting to BiCodec by adding its amor-
tized global-token overhead, computed under the same conven-
tion.
For the dynamic-frame-rate (DFR) variant,rs is reduced by
adaptive frame merging controlled by the thresholdτ. Specif-
ically, we computer s as the number of semantic tokens after
aggregation divided by the utterance duration in seconds. On
LibriSpeech test-clean, we observe thatτ= 0.90yields an av-
erage semantic token rate of approximately40tokens/s, while
τ= 0.84yields approximately25tokens/s.
We assess DySTCodec from two complementary perspec-
tives: speech reconstruction and voice conversion results. For
reconstruction, evaluation is conducted on the LibriSpeech test-
clean set. We report UTMOS [29], STOI [30], PESQ (wide-
band) [31], and speaker embedding cosine similarity (SECS).
For voice conversion, we randomly sample 200 utterances from
the LibriSpeech test-clean set as source speech and select six
speakers from VCTK [32] as target speakers. All models are
evaluated under a cross-dataset voice conversion setting, con-
verting LibriSpeech test-clean sources to VCTK target speak-
ers.

and adaptive deaggregation modules improve compression ef-

Ablations on training techniques.Table 3 shows that
the full setting achieves the best overall balance (WER 6.12,
STOI 0.90, PESQ 2.48, SECS 0.85). For timbre perturba-
tion, both a wider range (β∈[0.7,1.3]) and a narrower range

bottleneck refinement after deaggregation is critical for smooth-
ing boundary artifacts and preserving perceptual quality.
3. Conclusion
DySTCodec combines similarity-based dynamic frame aggre-
gation and adaptive deaggregation with lightweight refinement
to improve token efficiency while preserving reconstruction
quality. Experiments show strong low-bitrate reconstruction
and cross-dataset voice conversion performance, and ablations
confirm the importance of timbre perturbation for reducing
speaker leakage and refinement for mitigating boundary arti-
facts.
