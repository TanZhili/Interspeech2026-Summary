Abstract
Automatic forced alignment is standard in phonetic research,
but the minimum data needed for reliable acoustic measure-
ments remains unclear. This study examines how token quan-
tity affects alignment reliability using the MFA on the TIMIT
corpus, with manual phoneme boundaries as the gold stan-
dard. Focusing on vowels and three acoustic features (Dura-
tion, F1, F2) token subsets were incrementally sampled and
mixed-effects models fitted at each step to evaluate automatic
versus manual measurements. Results show 85% of vowel-
feature combinations improve significantly with increasing to-
kens, with F1 showing the most consistent gains. Most vow-
els stabilise around 50% of available tokens, though variability
exists: Duration stabilises earliest (≈730 tokens) while F2 re-
quires the most data (≈2,335 tokens). These findings offer em-
pirically grounded guidelines for minimum token requirements,
with practical implications for large-scale sociophonetic studies
and low-resource language research.

1. Introduction
Automatic forced alignment has become a core tool in socio-
phonetic research, enabling large-scale analysis of speech with-
out the need for labor-intensive manual segmentation [1, 2, 3, 4,
5]. Tools such as the Montreal Forced Aligner (MFA) [6] and
similar systems (e.g., [3, 7, 8, 9, 10]) have demonstrated robust
performance across a variety of languages and datasets, allow-
ing researchers to efficiently align speech at the phonemic level
and extract acoustic measurements such as vowel formants and
durations [6, 11]. Despite their demonstrated reliability, prior
research has consistently highlighted variability in aligner per-
formance depending on factors such as the acoustic model used,
recording quality, and speech style [12, 13]. These tools are
routinely employed to investigate patterns of sound variation
within speech communities, serving as a foundation for studies
on vowel shifts, phonetic change, and sociolinguistic variation
[14, 15, 16, 17]. While the overall accuracy of forced aligners is
well-documented, a critical gap remains: there is limited empir-
ical evidence regarding how much automatically aligned data is
necessary to produce acoustic distributions and patterns that re-
liably reflect those obtained from manually segmented speech.
In this sense, we address the gap on what theminimum viable
datasetobtained from uncorrected forced alignment outputs is
to ensure that observed phonetic patterns are representative of
the speech community under study.

Establishing this threshold is particularly important in con-
texts where manually segmented data are limited or unavailable,
e.g., studies of low-resource languages or large-scale speech
analyses. Identifying an empirically derived number of tokens
required to achieve reliable acoustic measurements would pro-
vide researchers with practical guidelines for data collection
and analysis, ensuring that their conclusions are both statisti-
cally and phonetically sound. By determining the point at which
additional automatically segmented tokens no longer meaning-
fully improve alignment reliability on acoustic measurements,
researchers can optimise their study designs while reducing
time and resource costs. The present study addresses this gap
by empirically examining how dataset size affects the reliabil-
ity of automatic forced alignment in sociophonetic research.
Rather than focusing solely on alignment accuracy in isolation,
we evaluate whether and when automatically aligned data pro-
vide stable and reliable phonetic patterns comparable to those
derived from manual segmentation. By identifying the point at
which increases in automatically aligned data no longer mean-
ingfully improve phonetic outcomes, this study provides empir-
ical insight into the minimum dataset requirements for robust
and replicable sociophonetic analysis using forced alignment.
2. Aim of the Study
The aim of this study is to empirically examine the relationship
between the number of tokens used in automatic forced align-
ment and the reliability of the resulting acoustic measurements.
Traditional approaches to evaluating forced alignment perfor-
mance have typically focused on temporal precision, measuring
time differences and boundary displacements/overlap between
manual and automatic alignments at specific points within seg-
ments, such as the start, end, or midpoint [18, 19, 20]. While
such metrics are valuable for assessing alignment accuracy in
absolute terms, they do not directly address whether the acous-
tic patterns extracted from automatically aligned data are suffi-
ciently preserved for reliable phonetic analysis.
Therefore, the present study moves beyond boundary-based
evaluation and instead focuses on the acoustic measurements
themselves: segmental duration, first formant frequency (F1),
and second formant frequency (F2). We adopt the premise
that some degree of difference between manual and automatic
alignments is inevitable. However, the critical question is not
whether such differences exist, but rather whether the data de-
rived from automatic alignment remains reliable enough to pre-
serve the phonetic patterns that would be observed with manual
alignment. This represents an important conceptual shift from
prior alignment validation studies, which have largely treated
alignment quality as a binary question of accuracy. Instead, we
approach alignment reliability as a question of practical suffi-

3. Methodology
3.1. Speech Dataset
The present study uses the TIMIT corpus, a widely used speech
corpus in English that provides manually annotated phoneme
boundaries, which serve as the gold standard for this analysis
[21]. TIMIT includes phonetically rich sentences spoken by
multiple speakers from different regions, providing controlled
and variable data suitable for testing alignment accuracy. For
the purposes of this study, only vowel segments were extracted.
The final data consisted of more than 55,000 individual tokens,
with a mean count of 3,673 per vowel (SD=2,600), where UH
had the lowest count (n=532) and IY the most (n=8,552).
3.2. Montreal Forced Aligner
Automatic segmentation was performed using theMontreal
Forced Aligner(MFA) [6], which employs pre-trained acous-
tic models to align speech at the phonemic level. MFA has
been widely adopted in linguistic and sociophonetic studies due
to its high accuracy and compatibility with multiple languages
[6, 22, 23, 20]. We used version3.0.5and the English pretrained
acoustic modelv2.0.0and dictionaryUS.v2.0.0. The aligner
was run on the TIMIT speech data without additional train-
ing, simulating typical use cases in research where pre-existing
models are applied to new datasets.
3.2.1. Acoustic Features
The acoustic features were selected on the basis of their estab-
lished use in sociophonetic research, ensuring both analytical
validity and cross-study comparability. For each vowel, includ-
ing both English monophthongs and diphthongs as labeled in
the ARPA dictionary, three primary acoustic features were anal-
ysed. Segmental duration (in milliseconds) was calculated as
the temporal difference between the start and end boundaries of
each vowel, as determined by either manual or automatic seg-
mentation. In addition, F1 and F2 were measured at the tem-
poral midpoint of each vowel segment to avoid coarticulatory
effects on each segment. Formant values were extracted us-
ing Praat [24] and these acoustic measurements form the basis
for comparing manually and automatically segmented data and
for evaluating the reliability of forced alignment across vary-
ing numbers of tokens. Although diphthongs are often analysed
at multiple time points, typically between 20% and 80% of the
vowel duration, to capture spectral dynamics, this study adopts
midpoint measurements for all vowels to ensure methodological
consistency across vowel categories.
3.2.2. Statistical Measurements
All analyses were conducted in the R Statistical Software [25].
The core assessment was to examine whether increasing the
number of tokens led to a stabilisation of alignment differences,
and whether the token count required for stabilisation varied
across vowel categories. To examine how alignment differ-
ences changed as additional data were added, analyses were
conducted along incremental token trajectories. For each vowel,
subsets of tokens were randomly sampled starting at 200 tokens
and increased in steps of 50 tokens up to the maximum avail-
able. Each sampling step was repeated ten times at each incre-
ment to reduce variability due to random token selection. For
each increment and repetition, a linear mixed-effects model was
fitted using thelmerTestR package [26]. The dependent vari-
able was the acoustic measurement of interest, with alignment
source (manual vs automatic) entered as a fixed effect. Random
intercepts for speaker and word were included to account for
repeated measurements within speakers and lexical items.
From these model outputs, improvement trajectories were
constructed by averaging the absolute source-difference esti-
mates across repetitions at each token increment. To quantify
the effect of increasing token counts on alignment accuracy,
a set of sequential metrics was developed and applied across
three analytical stages. In the first stage, we assessed whether
the differences between manual and automatic measurements
significantly decreased as the number of tokens increased. For
this, we examined the trajectories of absolute differences be-
tween manual and automatic measurements across token incre-
ments. Improvement was quantified using the delta-based met-
ric, with significance assessed viaKendall’s taufor monotonic
trends [27, 28, 29] and associated p-values. A trajectory was
considered reliably improving whentau<0 andp≤0.05, in-
dicating a significant reduction in absolute differences as more
tokens were added.
In the second stage, we examined whether an inflection
point existed in the trajectory of absolute differences across to-
ken increments, similar to methods used in [30, 31]. This point
represents where improvements begin to plateau, indicating
that additional tokens produce progressively smaller gains in
alignment accuracy. The stabilisation point was identified as
the earliest token increment for which the distance to the final
estimate remained below a predefined toleranceεfor at leastk
consecutive increments:

In the third and final stage, we evaluated the position of the

identified whether the stabilisation point was reached at or be-
fore 50% of the maximum available tokens. Cases where sta-
bilisation occurred at or before this threshold were considered
favorable, as they indicate that relatively smaller amounts of
data can produce reliable alignment estimates. In contrast, sta-
bilisation points occurring beyond 50% were flagged as requir-
ing excessive data, suggesting that substantial token counts are
needed before alignment accuracy becomes practically stable.
This criterion allows us to systematically identify which vowels
or acoustic features achieve reliable automatic alignment with
minimal data requirements versus those that demand more ex-

4.1. The Improvement as a Function of Token Count
All vowels analysed showed significant improvement in align-
ment reliability as a function of increasing token counts. This
pattern held across the majority of vowel-feature combinations,
with 85% of cases (33/39) demonstrating statistically signifi-
cant improvement as more tokens were added. In the remain-
ing 15% (6), specific combinations did not show this improve-
ment: three cases involved Duration measurements (AO, EH,
OY), and three F2 measurements (AW, AY , OY). Only F1 con-
sistently showed significant improvement across all vowels as
more tokens were added. This widespread pattern of improve-
ment provides strong evidence that increasing the number of
automatically aligned tokens enhances the reliability of acous-
tic measurements, bringing them closer to gold-standard man-
ual annotations. In addition, while the magnitude of improve-
ment varied across vowels, the majority of observed trends were
statistically significant, confirming that larger token counts gen-
erally provide more reliable estimates of phonetic features.
4.2. Stabilisation of Alignment Overlap (Tokens and Per-
centages)
Examination of stabilisation points revealed substantial vari-
ability both across vowels and across acoustic features. When
averaging stabilisation points by vowel across acoustic fea-
tures, AW and AO showed the highest threshold of stabilisation
points, at 86% and 82% respectively, of the maximum avail-
able tokens. All other vowels showed varied patterns, with IH
achieving stabilisation earliest at 31%. In terms of absolute
token counts, AW, and IH required the fewest tokens to sta-
bilise (≈1,000 tokens), whereas AH and AE required the most
(≈2,000 tokens).
When comparing stabilisation patterns by acoustic feature
(See Figure 2), Duration measurements stabilised earliest and
with the fewest tokens (24%,≈730 tokens). F1 stabilised later,
at 36% (≈1,088 tokens), while F2 required the most data, stabil-
ising at 56% (≈2,335 tokens). These differences show the vari-

5. Discussion
Our results demonstrate that for the majority of vowel-feature
combinations (85%), alignment reliability improves signifi-
cantly as token counts increase, with measurements converging
toward those obtained from manual annotation. This conver-
gence supports the premise that while some degree of differ-
ence between manual and automatic alignments is inevitable,
these differences do not necessarily compromise the phonetic
validity of the extracted measurements. An important finding is
that the trajectories of improvement followed predictable pat-
terns, with most vowels and features reaching a stabilisation
point beyond which additional tokens provided not meaning dif-
ferences. This empirical demonstration of stabilisation is cen-
tral to our methodological contribution: it provides researchers
with a principled basis for determining when their datasets are
sufficiently large to support reliable analysis, rather than relying
on arbitrary or convention-based sample size decisions.
The variability observed across vowels and features is it-
self theoretically informative. The fact that F1 consistently im-
proved with additional tokens, while certain Duration and F2
measurements did not, suggests that alignment challenges are
not uniform across the acoustic space. Duration measurements
stabilised earliest and with the fewest tokens, reflecting their
relative robustness to boundary placement errors. In contrast,
F2 required substantially more data, indicating that capturing
reliable estimates of tongue advancement is more sensitive to
alignment variability than measuring vowel height or tempo-
ral extent. This has direct implications for study design: so-
ciophonetic research focused on fronting or backing patterns
should prioritise larger sample sizes, whereas studies centered
on vowel duration or height may achieve adequate reliability
with smaller datasets.
The use of the 50% threshold to distinguish early from late
stabilisation revealed that most vowels and features achieve re-
liability well before exhausting the available data. This suggests
that automatic alignment is not only feasible but efficient for the
majority of cases. However, the vowels that stabilised later, par-
ticularly AO, AW and OY , highlight the importance of segment-
specific validation. These vowels may present particular chal-
lenges for automatic alignment due to their acoustic characteris-
tics or lower frequency in the dataset, and researchers targeting
such segments should verify that their token counts exceed the
identified stabilisation thresholds.
6. Conclusions
This study advances our understanding of automatic forced
alignment by establishing that reliability is not solely a func-
tion of boundary accuracy, but depends critically on the quantity
of data analysed and the acoustic features under investigation.
The trajectory-based framework developed here demonstrates
that alignment sufficiency can be empirically determined rather
than assumed, offering researchers an informed method for val-
idating their datasets before proceeding to phonetic analysis.
By identifying the token counts at which acoustic measure-
ments stabilise, we provide a foundation for more transparent
and replicable research practices. The implications extend be-
yond methodological rigor. For researchers working with abun-
dant data, our findings enable more efficient workflows by iden-
tifying when further data collection offers diminishing analyti-
cal returns. For those working with low-resource languages or
understudied varieties, these empirically grounded thresholds
offer a pathway to scale up acoustic analysis while maintain-
ing confidence in measurement reliability. This is particularly
relevant as the field increasingly turns to automatic methods to
address larger and more diverse speech corpora.
Finally, it is important to acknowledge that findings are de-
rived from the TIMIT corpus, a controlled, high-quality, care-
fully produced speech dataset. The generalisability of our re-
sults to spontaneous speech, conversational data, or recordings
with variable acoustic quality remains an open question. Fac-
tors such as co-articulation, micro-prosodic phenomena, speech
style, background noise, language variety, and the specific
acoustic model used by the forced aligner are all likely to influ-
ence both the magnitude of alignment differences and the token
counts required for stabilisation. Consequently, the thresholds
reported here should be understood as empirically informed
guidelines rather than universal requirements.
