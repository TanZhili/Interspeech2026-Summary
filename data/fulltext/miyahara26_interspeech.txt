Abstract
Stuttering event detection (SED) is expected to quantify fre-
quencies with types of stuttering to support speech therapy.
Various stuttering symptoms are categorized into five common
disfluency types, but some types exhibit large acoustic varia-
tion across individuals and languages. Recent advances in SED
have been driven by the growth of stuttering speech corpora,
but SED research remains challenging for no- or low-resource
languages. We focus on the “self-attention weights of tempo-
ral acoustic vector sequence” (SAWF) as a feature for language
transferable SED. SAWF is considered robust to linguistic dif-
ferences as it extracts repetitions and prolongations of similar
acoustic properties. Experimental results of zero-shot cross-
lingual evaluation using stuttering speech corpora in three lan-
guages showed F1 scores reaching 77-98% of those in a mono-
lingual setting. Moreover, SAWF-based SED achieved com-
parable performance to a state-of-the-art model for language-
dependent disfluency types.

1. Introduction
Stuttering is a speech disorder characterized by disfluencies.
Although its symptoms exhibit considerable variability, they
have been categorized across languages into five common dis-
fluency types: sound repetition, word repetition, prolongation
of sounds, blocks, and interjections. In the diagnosis of stutter-
ing severity, such as in the SSI-4 [1], a primary index, the mean
interval between stuttering events requires the manual transcrip-
tion of at least 200 syllables. Since manual transcription is labo-
rious, stuttering event detection (SED) technology is expected
to automate this task. Furthermore, the SED technology is ex-
pected to contribute to the development of an ASR system that
is easier to use for persons who stutter (PWS) to use [2].
Recently, the SED technologies have been actively studied.
Advances have been strongly supported by new releases of in-
creasingly larger stuttering speech corpora. The first was Lea
et al.’s release of SEP-28k English stuttering speech corpus [3],
which contained ten times as much data as any previously re-
leased dataset, followed by its partitioning into training, vali-
dation, and test sets [4]. Subsequently, the KSoF German stut-
tering speech corpus [5] was released. Other releases include
the AS-70 Mandarin Chinese stuttering speech corpus [6], and
Boli [7], a corpus encompassing six official languages spoken
in India, including English.
Before the releases of these corpora, research primarily fo-
cused on CNN-based architectures [8, 9]. As the new cor-
pora became increasingly available, the focus shifted to mod-
els [10, 11, 12] based on the multi-layer Transformer models
such as wav2vec 2.0 [13] and Whisper [14], and more recently
to a model [15] based on the Conformer [16]. The field has
further evolved toward integrating these speech representations
with large language models [17, 18].
However, such data-hungry approaches cannot be directly
applied to SED for no- or low-resource languages, where
progress has been limited. Some researchers have begun to fo-
cus on cross-lingual SED model training [10, 11, 19] and zero-
shot cross-lingual evaluation [10, 19, 20]. The latter assesses
whether learned acoustic representations can be transferred to
unknown languages. Bayerl et al. [10] proposed a model that
combines wav2vec 2.0 with an attention mechanism, using the
final output of wav2vec 2.0 as both key and value and its tem-
poral mean as the query. They performed cross-corpus learning
and cross-lingual evaluation using stuttering corpora in English
and German. Subsequently, Changawala et al. [11] introduced
”Whister,” in which the wav2vec 2.0 backbone was substituted
with Whisper to leverage its robust multilingual representations
learned with large-scale weak supervision. They verified strong
cross-lingual performance on English and German datasets by
using features aggregated from all encoder layers, not just the
final output. Haas et al. [19] demonstrated that the addition of
a Mandarin stutter speech corpus to train a wav2vec 2.0-based
SED model, modified from a previous study [10], improved per-
formance not only between linguistically similar English and
German, but also when Chinese was included. Singh et al. [20]
demonstrated remarkable generalization in this setting. They
created a stuttering symptom vector database based on an En-
glish stuttering corpus, and then integrated it into a stuttering
detection model as a retrieval-augmented classifier (RAC) [21]
named ”StutterFuse.” They achieved state-of-the-art zero-shot
performance in German SED.
To develop a universal SED model that reduces linguis-
tic dependencies in feature extraction for stuttering, we focus
on the ”self-attention weights of temporal acoustic vector se-
quence” (SAWF) previously proposed by Miyahara et al. [22].
SAWF efficiently extracts repetitions and prolongations of sim-
ilar sounds or words, which are characteristics of stuttering
events, with less regard for their acoustic properties, by calcu-
lating the inner product of two acoustic vectors for all combi-
nations of different time points. SAWF is considered less de-
pendent on language-specific acoustic properties of stuttering,
so this study investigates its cross-lingual capabilities. We ex-
tend the SAWF models on the basis of not only the conventional
wav2vec2.0 but also the Whisper encoder. One SAWF model
evaluated in this study demonstrates superior cross-lingual per-
formance compared to that of the model by Bayerl et al. [10],
which was reported to perform well across multiple stuttering
corpora. The SAWF model shows competitive results against
the current SOTA model, StutterFuse.

Here, SED is performed as an image recognition task on
the two-dimensional SAWF. When SAWF has multiple lay-
ers, SED is performed based on the multi-layer SAWF. The
classification model is VGG-19 [23] with SAWF as its input.
The VGG-19 comprises sixteen convolutional layers and three
fully connected layers, capturing features from local to global
scales through alternating convolutions and pooling operations.
Its structure is based on the original SAWF work [22]. Dur-
ing training, only the classification model is trained, while the
Transformer-based model used for the SAWF extraction re-
mains frozen.
2.2. Cross-lingual adaptation
The zero-shot cross-lingual evaluation is carried out in two
steps: training a model on a high-resource stuttering speech cor-
pus and then evaluating it on a low-resource stuttering speech
corpus of an unknown language. Figure 1 shows a conceptual
diagram of this evaluation.
In addition to the original wav2vec 2.0 used previously
[22], we introduced wav2vec 2.0 xlsr-53 to support a variety
of languages. However, while covering various languages, the
xlsr-53 model has a disadvantage: it is not fine-tuned on ASR
tasks. We assume that discriminating between sound repetition
and word repetition requires linguistic knowledge of vocabu-
lary, which can be acquired through fine-tuning on ASR tasks.
Therefore, we applied SAWF to the Whisper encoder, which
was trained on ASR and speech translation tasks. The Whis-
per encoder comprises multiple Transformer encoder layers, en-
abling it to generate SAWF in a manner similar to wav2vec 2.0.
Although SAWF efficiently captures repetitions and contin-
uations of similar acoustic patterns, with less regard for their
acoustic properties, the SAWF-based SED model may learn
language-specific dependencies when trained on a stuttering
speech corpus in that language. Hence, this study evaluates not
only models trained on a single language but also those trained
on multiple languages.

3. Experiments
We evaluated the zero-shot transfer capability of the SAWF-
based models across various combinations of different lan-
guages.
3.1. Datasets
The experiment used three stuttering speech corpora, each
of which was labeled with a set of five common disfluency
classes: blocks, interjections, prolongations, sound repetitions,
and word repetitions, along with a normal class for speech with-
out disfluencies. Each corpus was partitioned into training,
development, and test sets. The following subsections report
the total number of samples and the sample counts in the split
datasets (training/development/test) for each corpus. All parti-
tions were speaker-open to ensure that the monolingual evalua-
tion was speaker-independent. In addition, they were balanced
with respect to symptom distribution and speaker gender.
3.1.1. SEP-28k
SEP-28k comprises 28,177 (15,213/6,402/6,562) three-second
audio clips extracted from stuttering-themed podcasts, totaling
approximately 23 hours. These clips were automatically seg-
mented from spontaneous dialogues involving two to three par-
ticipants per session, comprising approximately 500 English-
speaking persons who stutter (PWSs).
3.1.2. AS-70
AS-70 consists of 41,953 (27,469/4,710/9,774) variable-length
audio clips, totaling approximately 48.8 hours, derived from
spontaneous speech and 200 voice commands read aloud us-
ing voice command devices. These clips were extracted from
conversations between 70 Mandarin Chinese-speaking PWSs.
3.1.3. Kassel State of Fluency (KSoF)
KSoF consists of 5,597 (3,474/870/1,253) three-second audio
clips, totaling approximately 4.6 hours. They were automat-
ically extracted from recordings of spontaneous speech, tele-
phone conversations, and read speech. These clips were col-
lected from 37 German-speaking PWSs undergoing stuttering
treatment with fluency-shaping therapy.

3.2. Experimental setup
We conducted two experiments: monolingual and cross-lingual
SEDs. Both experiments employed non-exclusive multi-label
detection.
The first experiment was a monolingual evaluation task us-
ing a single stuttering-speech corpus for both training and eval-
uation. This setup estimated the upper bound of performance
by the next cross-lingual evaluation for each test set.
The second experiment was a cross-lingual evaluation task
using different stuttering speech corpora in different languages
for training and evaluation. The model was trained with no
data in the language of the test set. This zero-shot evalua-
tion covered five language combinations: Mandarin to English
(Cmn→En), English to Mandarin (En→Cmn), Mandarin to
German (Cmn→De), English to German (En→De), and Man-
darin and English to German (Cmn+En→De).
Performance was measured using F1 scores for six labels:
the five disfluency types and the no-disfluencies class.
3.2.1. Evaluation models
As a baseline, we reimplemented a model from previous stud-
ies [10, 6] and evaluated it on all three stuttering speech cor-
pora. The multi-task learning performed in the original study
was excluded from our experiments to control the conditions,
since this aspect was not examined in the original AS-70 paper.
The SAWF models were based on three different acoustic
features. Model 1: wav2vec 2.0 encoders fine-tuned on source-
language ASR tasks 1,2 , Model 2: a wav2vec 2.0 model pre-
trained by self-supervised learning on multilingual data cov-
ering the three target languages 3, and Model 3: the Whisper
encoder 4. All of these SAWF models used 24 Transformer en-
coder layers, which remained frozen during training.
The image recognition architecture was based on VGG-19,
where the kernel size for all convolutional layers was set to
5×5with a padding width of 3. A flatten layer and tw sub-
sequent linear layers of the standard VGG-19 were substituted
with a global average pooling layer. This modification reduced

4. Discussion
Model 2 fell behind Model 3 in F1 scores for repetitions. This
could be attributed to the self-supervised learning mechanism
of wav2vec 2.0. During this self-supervised learning, the model
learned acoustic patterns as quantized representations via a
codebook rather than phonemes or words. This lack of explicit
linguistic units likely limited sensitivity to repetitions, since the
model was not fine-tuned on an ASR task.
Model 3 also showed limitations, particularly for interjec-
tions. This weakness was probably due to the original train-
ing process of Whisper, where text standardization involves the
exclusion of fillers from ground-truth transcripts. Such prepro-
cessing prevented the model from capturing the acoustic or lin-
guistic nuances of interjections.
Interlingual differences persisted in the performance gap
between language pairs. Although SAWF mitigated these
differences, the (En→De) transfer continued to outperform
(Cmn→De) across several classes. This disparity was note-
worthy because the AS-70 (Cmn) corpus had a larger sample
size than SEP-28k (En); nevertheless, this quantitative advan-
tage did not fully overcome the linguistic distance. However,
this linguistic gap was effectively compensated in the multi-
source (Cmn+En→De) setting. Unlike single-source configu-
rations, the multi-source approach achieved over 40% perfor-
mance across all classes with Model 3. This result suggests
that training on multiple corpora not only increases sample size
but also enables the model to capture cross-lingual nuances that
SAWF alone cannot fully bridge, thus enhancing overall robust-
ness.
5. Conclusion
This study verified that self-attention weight features (SAWF)
are effective in reducing linguistic dependency in stuttering
event detection. Through zero-shot cross-lingual experiments
using Mandarin, English, and German stuttering speech cor-
pora, we verified that SAWF can capture language-independent
structural patterns.
Our experiments confirmed that SAWF captured language-
independent structural patterns, significantly outperforming
the conventional learning-based baseline across all categories.
While the state-of-the-art (SOTA) StutterFuse maintained an
advantage in acoustic-dominant symptoms, the SAWF model
exhibited superior performance in linguistically dependent
classes, namely interjection and word repetition. Most notably,
the SAWF model showed an F1 score exceeding the SOTA
model by 20 points in word repetition (0.40 vs. 0.20), high-
lighting its unique effectiveness in capturing linguistic struc-
tures across different languages.
