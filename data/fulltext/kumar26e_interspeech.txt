Abstract
Text-to-Speech (TTS) systems have improved significantly, but
most work focuses on high-resource languages, leaving Indian
languages less explored. V oice-cloning models trained on Indic
data often suffer from word-dropping errors and slow inference,
making deployment difficult. We propose a lightweight speaker
adaptation pipeline that uses only a 10-second reference sam-
ple. Synthetic speech generated from a voice-cloning model is
filtered through a four-stage quality-control pipeline and used to
fine-tune a FastSpeech2 model with dual-site speaker condition-
ing and a cosine consistency loss. Phoneme unification through
the Common Label Set (CLS) enables the adapted voice to syn-
thesize speech across Hindi, Marathi, Tamil, and Telugu with-
out additional recordings. The resulting system produces de-
terministic outputs, runs 53×faster than the baseline, and im-
proves intelligibility and naturalness while preserving speaker
characteristics in cross-lingual synthesis.
Index Terms: text-to-speech, speaker adaptation, multilingual,
Indian languages, synthetic data, cross-lingual synthesis
1. Introduction
Text-to-Speech (TTS) technology has progressed rapidly, with
recent models achieving near-human naturalness in speech gen-
eration [1] and incorporating emotional expressiveness [2]. Ad-
vances in personalized TTS, especially in voice cloning, have
enabled high-fidelity adaptation to a target speaker’s voice [3].
However, most of these advancements have primarily focused
on high-resource languages such as English and Mandarin, ben-
efiting from abundant datasets and research resources.
For Indian languages, progress has been more gradual. The
Indic TTS system presented in [4], based on Tacotron 2 [5] and
x-vectors [6], demonstrated strong performance in synthesiz-
ing Indian language speech but did not address conversational
prosody. The work by [7] explored the adaptation of reading-
style TTS models to conversational speech using cross-lingual
data, maintaining speaker identity.
Efforts to build robust TTS systems for a billion Indian
users have explored various acoustic models, vocoders, and
training paradigms [8]. A subsequent system covering 13 In-
dian languages, built on FastSpeech2 [9], demonstrated supe-
rior MOS scores. Inspired by multilingual frameworks such
as YourTTS [10], LIMMITS’24 [11] proposed a multispeaker,
multilingual TTS model for Indian languages. Building on this,
RASA [12] introduced expressiveness and emotion, while Par-
lerTTS [13] further extended this capability with prompt-based
expressive speech generation. In parallel, [14] demonstrated the
feasibility of synthesizing intelligible and high-quality speech
for unseen languages without any adaptation data, leveraging a
shared CLS [15] phone set.
V oice-cloning model [16] finetuned on Indian language IN-
F5 [17] has also achieved impressive performance in generating
natural and expressive speech across Indian languages, largely
through fine-tuning on extensive multilingual corpora. Despite
achieving high speaker similarity, these models often exhibit
word-dropping errors that lead to elevated WER. Furthermore,
IN-F5’s dependency on massive datasets and computational
overhead limits its scalability for rapid or low-resource deploy-
ment. Its auto-regressive decoding architecture contributes to
slow inference and makes it vulnerable to attention-related fail-
ures, such as phoneme repetition or omission.
In this work, we propose a lightweight three-stage speaker
adaptation pipeline for multilingual Indic TTS. A short refer-
ence sample is used to generate synthetic speech with a voice-
cloning TTS, which is then refined through a multi-stage filter-
ing process to remove word omissions, prosodic irregularities,
and statistical outliers. The non-autoregressive FastSpeech2
model is fine-tuned on the cleaned corpus with structured
speaker conditioning, enabling deterministic synthesis free of
word-dropping errors. Cross-lingual synthesis across unseen
languages is enabled through CLS-based phoneme unification.
The main contributions of this work are:
1. A multilingual FastSpeech2 base model with dual-site
ECAPA-TDNN speaker conditioning and a cosine consis-
tency loss, pretrained across four Indian languages with
CLS-based phoneme unification for cross-lingual synthesis.
2. A quality-controlled speaker adaptation pipeline that con-
verts synthetic voice-cloned speech into a reliable fine-
tuning corpus using phoneme-level CER filtering, pitch and
duration checks, and log-likelihood pruning.
3. Demonstrated cross-lingual voice adaptation from a single
10-second reference, with consistent gains over the voice-
cloning baseline on intelligibility, naturalness, and inference
efficiency across Hindi, Marathi, Tamil, and Telugu.
2. Proposed Approach
Our approach consists of three stages: (1) pretraining a multi-
speaker base model with dual-site speaker conditioning, (2)
synthetic data generation with quality filtering, and (3) speaker-
adaptive fine-tuning on the target speaker.

Dual-Site Speaker Conditioning
We extract 192-dimensional speaker embeddings using an
ECAPA-TDNN model [21], fine-tuned on IndicV oices-R [22]
Hindi speakers. These embeddings are used to condition the
acoustic model at two different stages.
Standard single-site conditioning injects a single global em-
bedding into the network, requiring the same representation to
encode both prosodic and spectral attributes. To alleviate this
bottleneck, we introduce dual-site conditioning.
Speaker information is injected at two locations:
(1) Prosodic Conditioning:
hpros =h+W pross(1)
This modulation occurs before the variance adapter, allow-
ing the speaker embedding to influence duration, pitch, and en-
ergy prediction.
(2) Acoustic Conditioning:
zacou =z+W acous(2)
This conditioning is applied to the length-regulated features
before decoding, influencing spectral generation.
Here,W pros andW acou are independent learned projec-
tions. This separation enables specialization between temporal-
prosodic and spectral-acoustic attributes and improves cross-
lingual speaker consistency.

2.2. Stage 2: Synthetic Data Generation and Filtering
We use a 10-second reference audio sample of a male Hindi
speaker sourced from PIB India 1. To ensure broad phone-
mic coverage, a Hindi text corpus spanning all 78 CLS
phonemes [15] is constructed and synthesized using IN-F5 [17].
This produces approximately two hours of speaker-consistent

synthetic speech with controlled acoustic conditions. This one-
time offline adaptation generates∼2,400 utterances via IN-F5
in∼50 minutes on an A6000 GPU.
To mitigate word-dropping and prosodic instability inherent
to IN-F5, we apply a multi-stage filtering pipeline:
1.Phoneme-level CER filtering.A data2vec-based ASR
model trained on Indian languages [23] transcribes syn-
thesized speech. Both reference and hypothesis texts are
CLS-phonemized, and phoneme-level Character Error Rate
(CER) is computed. Utterances with CER>10%are dis-
carded to remove samples with phoneme-level errors and
word omissions.
2.Pitch filtering.Utterances with abnormal global pitch val-
ues or excessive intra-utterance variation are removed to
eliminate unstable prosodic patterns. This process is fully
automatic and speaker-independent, utilizing thresholds de-
rived from corpus statistics without manual tuning.
3.Duration filtering.Samples shorter than 2 seconds are ex-
cluded to avoid truncated or phonetically insufficient utter-
ances.
4.Log-likelihood pruning.Frame-level alignment scores are
used to compute mean log-likelihood per utterance, and
samples falling more than two standard deviations below the
mean are discarded to remove statistically anomalous align-
ments.
This process yields a phonemically aligned, acoustically
stable corpus suitable for reliable duration modeling and cross-
lingual synthesis.
2.3. Stage 3: Target Speaker Fine-Tuning
The pretrained base model is fine-tuned on the filtered synthetic
corpus, adapting the speaker conditioning layers to the target
speaker’s voice characteristics while preserving the multilingual
phoneme knowledge acquired in Stage 1. No real recordings of
the target speaker beyond the 10-second reference are used at
any point in the pipeline. This teacher-guided, metric-driven
fine-tuning serves as a non-autoregressive analogue to prefer-
ence alignment paradigms [24].
The cleaned synthetic corpus is used to fine-tune a Fast-
Speech2 model [18] with HiFi-GAN vocoding [19]. All text
is phonemized using the Common Label Set (CLS) [15, 20],
enabling a unified phonemic representation across Indo-Aryan
and Dravidian languages.
3. Experimental Setup
3.1. Datasets
Stage 1: Pretraining:The base model is trained on approxi-
mately 119 hours of speech covering multiple speakers across
Hindi, Marathi, Tamil, and Telugu, drawn from IndicV oices-
R [22] and the story subset of the IndicTTS corpus [25].
Stage 3: Fine-tuning:The synthetic adaptation dataset consists
of 2,339 utterances generated using IN-F5, of which 2,088 utter-
ances (2.34 hours) remain after the filtering pipeline described
in Stage 2. The resulting corpus exhibits negligible phoneme-
level errors and stable alignments.

3.2. Text Processing and Alignment
All text is CLS phonemized [15, 20]. Phoneme-level dura-
tions for FastSpeech2 supervision are obtained via Kaldi-based
forced alignment [26].
3.3. Model Architecture and Training
Architecture.All models are implemented using the ESPnet2
framework [27]. The FastSpeech2 model uses 4-layer Con-
former [28] encoder and decoder blocks, 2 attention heads, and
d= 384, totalling approximately 70M parameters. Audio is
at 48 kHz with 160-bin mel spectrograms (n fft = 8192, hop=
1024), and waveforms are reconstructed by HiFi-GAN V1 [19].
Stage 1: Multi-speaker pretraining.The model learns to dis-
entangle linguistic content from speaker identity across four
languages. Utterance-level ECAPA-TDNN embeddings are
averaged per speaker to form stable conditioning prototypes.
Training uses Adam with a Noam schedule (4000 warmup
steps), batch size 20, FP16, on 2 NVIDIA A6000 GPUs
(≈16 hours).
Stage 2: Target speaker fine-tuning.The pretrained model is
fine-tuned on the synthetic corpus, adapting the speaker condi-
tioning layers to the target voice while preserving the multilin-
gual phoneme knowledge from Stage 1. Fine-tuning runs for 50
epochs atlr = 10 −4, batch size 16, on a single A6000 GPU.
4. Evaluation Metrics and Results
4.1. Objective Test
We evaluate on 50 Hindi sentences (adaptation language) and
30 sentences each in Marathi, Tamil, and Telugu (cross-lingual
transfer). We compare three systems:IN-F5, the zero-shot
baseline;Base FS2, our pretrained multi-speaker model used
directly without fine-tuning; andOurs, the fine-tuned speaker-
adapted model. Metrics are: (i)WERvia a data2vec ASR
model [23]; (ii)SECS, ECAPA-TDNN cosine similarity against
the 10-second Hindi reference; (iii) each sentence is synthe-
sized 10 times and we reportσ F0 (std. of per-utterance mean
F0 across repetitions, in Hz) andσsyl (std. of syllable rate across
repetitions, in syl/s) as measures of output determinism; and
(iv)MI, mean inference time in s/utt.
•Intelligibility.Our system achieves 18.6% relative WER
reduction on Hindi and 21.8% average reduction on cross-
lingual synthesis over IN-F5. Base FS2 already outperforms
IN-F5 on WER due to its non-autoregressive architecture,
which eliminates attention-related word omissions. Fine-
tuning on the quality-controlled synthetic corpus further re-
duces WER by 14–16% relative over Base FS2, confirming
the additional benefit of CER-filtered training data.

gram in a single feed-forward pass, whereas IN-F5 iterates an

head is similarly asymmetric: 16 hours on a single A6000 vs.
∼3 days on 32 H100 GPUs.

4.3. Summary
Base FS2 surpasses IN-F5 on WER, determinism, and natural-
ness through its non-autoregressive architecture alone. Fine-
tuning on the quality-controlled corpus then improves SECS
from 0.78–0.81 to 0.87–0.88 and reduces WER by a further 14–
16%, reaching 3.82–4.14 MOS across four languages. Against
IN-F5, our system achieves 18–22% WER reduction, 96–98%
speaker similarity, fully deterministic output, and 53×faster in-
ference at 4.7×lower parameter count. All the evaluation audio
samples are available here 2.
5. Conclusion
This work presented a lightweight speaker adaptation pipeline
for multilingual Indic TTS requiring only a 10-second reference
sample. A FastSpeech2 model with dual-site ECAPA-TDNN
speaker conditioning is first pretrained on multi-speaker Indic
data. Synthetic speech from IN-F5 is then filtered through a
four-stage quality-control pipeline and used for speaker adap-
tation with a cosine consistency loss. This enables the sys-
tem to generate intelligible and natural speech while preserving
speaker characteristics in cross-lingual settings. The final model
is compact (286 MB,∼71M parameters) with a shared 78-token
CLS vocabulary across major Indian languages, making it suit-
able for low-resource deployment. Evaluations confirm consis-
tent gains over IN-F5 on intelligibility, naturalness, and infer-
ence speed at 53×lower latency, while maintaining competitive
speaker similarity with minimal adaptation data. While current
claims are scoped to the demonstrated speaker, multi-speaker
validation remains a natural next step. Future work will extend
to 22 Indian languages, explore zero-shot cloning, and compare
single-site versus dual-site conditioning to isolate their contri-
butions.
