Readability Does Not Predict Speech Recognition Errors: Contrasting Human
and Machine Perception

Abstract
This study challenges the historical link between the readability
of a text and its intelligibility by automatic speech recognition
(ASR) systems. While textual complexity has a significant im-
pact on human comprehension, its effect on modern end-to-end
architectures remains unclear. We present a controlled exper-
imental pipeline using the CLEAR corpus, speech synthesis,
and acoustic degradations (babble noise, reverberation). Our
results demonstrate a near-perfect decoupling between readabil-
ity, measured by the Global Readability Index, and intelligibil-
ity, measured by the Word Error Rate. This orthogonality per-
sists under different acoustic degradations and is validated on
natural speech. The absence of a “cognitive load” on the model
during the transcription process confirms that it is now com-
pletely independent of textual complexity.
Index Terms: Automatic Speech Recognition, Readability, In-
telligibility, End-to-End Models, Listenability
1. Introduction
Human speech comprehension is a multidimensional process.
When faced with degraded acoustic environments, humans use
top-down cognitive mechanisms to maintain comprehension.
Under such conditions, the readability of a message becomes
a key compensatory factor: a simple sentence is significantly
easier to decode in noise than a structurally complex one [1, 2].
In other words, when accounting for cognitive load, the read-
ability of a text impacts its intelligibility to a human listener. In
the past, Automatic Speech Recognition (ASR) systems showed
a similar behavior. The performance of older architectures was
correlated with the structural complexity of the source text, af-
fecting its readability. Highly complex texts, characterized by
rare vocabulary or convoluted syntax, generated high perplex-
ity and thus a higher Word Error Rate (WER) [3, 4]. However,
large, self-supervised, and End-to-End (E2E) architectures have
established new baselines for acoustic-to-lexical mapping [5].
This raises a fundamental question: do modern ASR systems
still have a “cognitive load” similar to humans when processing
structurally complex language, or have they decoupled acous-
tic transcription from linguistic comprehension? Investigating
this relationship “in real-world conditions” presents a major
methodological challenge: human speakers naturally adapt their
elocution through hyper-articulation, pauses, or reduced speech
rate when reading difficult texts [6]. To strictly isolate textual
complexity from speaker-induced acoustic variations, we intro-
duce a controlled Text-To-Speech (TTS) experimental pipeline
based on the CLEAR corpus [7]. We expose various architec-
tures, ranging from Hidden Markov Model, Gaussian Mixture
Model (HMM-GMM) [8] and Whisper [5], to texts of varying
readability under controlled noise and reverberation. This ap-
proach allows us to precisely measure the impact of text diffi-
culty on machine error.
In this article, we challenge the historical paradigm that
links text readability to the “intelligibility” of ASR. By using a
readability index, we seek to determine whether the readability
of a text inherently degrades automatic transcription, even un-
der difficult acoustic conditions. Through a comparative study
of several ASR architectures, different controlled noise and re-
verberation conditions, and internal model measurements, our
study highlights the divergence between human cognitive load
and machine processing. Section 2 details our methodology by
presenting our experimental pipeline. In section 3, we present
our main evaluation, the impact of textual readability on human
perception compared to machine errors. We also validate our
observations using natural speech, then attempt to approach the
cognitive functioning of ASR models. Finally, section 4 ad-
dresses the remaining questions raised by these results and lays
the foundations for future work.
2. Methodology
2.1. Pipeline
As mentioned in our introduction, human speakers naturally
modify their speech when dealing with complex texts. In order
to isolate the impact of textual complexity from these acoustic
variations, we designed a controlled experimental pipeline (see
figure 1). Within the framework of a text corpus, a TTS system
first processes the source transcriptions. This step serves as an
acoustic control variable: it ensures that speech rate, prosody,
and articulatory accuracy remain perfectly constant, regardless
of the complexity of the text. The audio is then subjected to con-
trolled acoustic degradation (additive noise or reverberation) be-
fore being transmitted to the ASR models. In the case of an au-
dio corpus, the TTS part is naturally skipped to proceed directly
to this stage. This design allows us to observe the behavior of
the ASR architecture when confronted with complex syntax in
clean and degraded conditions, without the confounding factor
of human articulation.
2.2. Datasets
2.2.1. CLEAR Corpus
For this study, we used the CLEAR (CommonLit Ease of Read-
ability) corpus [7], which contains 4,718 text excerpts of vary-
ing degrees of complexity (informative or literary in nature).
While the excerpts are relatively short (172 words on average),
they offer a wide range of readability levels. This corpus repre-
sents the main dataset for our study. The corpus also provides
a human-annotated readability score for each text: the Bradley-
Terry Coefficient of Readability (BT).

2.2.2. LibriSpeech
In order to ensure that our results are also valid outside the con-
trolled environment of speech synthesis, we carried out a “real-
world conditions” validation using natural human speech. To do
this, we selected the train-clean-100 subset of the LibriSpeech
corpus [9], whose total duration approximately corresponds to
that of our audio dataset generated from the CLEAR corpus.
Because the standard LibriSpeech excerpts are relatively short
(15 seconds on average), we concatenated consecutive audio
segments from the same speaker. This process resulted in a fi-
nal set of 6,983 natural speech files with an average duration of
about one minute, again to match the temporal characteristics
of our generated audio files.

intelligibility without the subjectivity and variability inherent in

(WER) as our objective indicator [18]. The WER is defined as
the minimum edit distance between the reference transcript and

from pairwise comparison analysis [19]. Unlike traditional
readability scales based on text characteristics (sentence length,
number of syllables), the Bradley-Terry model estimates the la-

on relative human judgment. This approach estimates readabil-
ity through judgments of text simplicity. Concretely, annotators

2.3.4. Cognitive Effort: Uncertainty Metrics
The WER captures the final transcription error, but it does not
reflect the internal uncertainty of the model or its hesitations
during the decoding process [21]. To quantify this “cognitive
effort,” we extract the average predictive entropy of all tokens
generated for each utterance [22]. The entropy represents the
degree of uncertainty of the model when generating text. It
quantifies the extent to which the model hesitates between a
multitude of possible words instead of being sure of its tran-
scription. In addition, we calculate the compression ratio (ratio
between the length of the generated text and its compressed size
using zlib [5]) to determine whether models excessively trun-
cate or simplify complex syntactic structures when faced with
high perplexity. These measures allow us to evaluate the inter-
nal effort and confidence of the model when faced with different
levels of readability.
2.4. Speech Synthesis and ASR Architectures
To strictly isolate textual complexity, the source transcripts from
the CLEAR corpus were synthesized using the Amazon Polly
Standard engine (voice “Joanna”)1, providing a baseline of per-
fectly controlled, neutral elocution. Having established this
standardized acoustic baseline, we evaluated a diverse spectrum
of ASR architectures to demonstrate that the decoupling be-
tween readability and intelligibility appears to be a recent phe-
nomenon specific to certain architectures. For our traditional

2.5. Noise and Reverberation
To simulate challenging acoustic environments, we subjected
our audio files to controlled degradations. First, we introduced
additive babble noise (cafeteria environment) sourced from the
DEMAND dataset [28]. To ensure noise variability and pre-
vent models from adapting to a static pattern, a noise window
of matching duration was randomly sampled from a continuous
25-minute track for each utterance. This background noise was
applied at three distinct signal-to-noise ratios: 0dB, 10dB, and
20dB. At the same time, to assess the impact of spatial distor-
tion in a realistic accessibility use case, we applied simulated
reverberation using a room impulse response from the Mills Art
Museum, obtained from the OpenAIR database [29]. This re-
verberation was evaluated at two different levels (0.1 and 0.9)
to observe the different effects of slight and extreme reverbera-
tions. This gives us several datasets: a “clean” one with audio
files that haven’t been altered at all, and one for each level of au-
dio degradation (three levels of noise and two levels of reverb).
3. Results
3.1. GRI Validation
Before evaluating the machine’s performance, we validated the
GRI against human perception and assessed its robustness in
ASR transcription. Pearson correlation analysis between the
GRI and BT easiness scores revealed a strong inverse corre-
lation (r=−0.56,p <0.001). This confirms that as textual
complexity (GRI) increases, perceived ease by humans mechan-
ically decreases. Furthermore, the robustness of the measure
was confirmed by comparing the scores of the original texts
with those of the generated transcripts. The near-perfect cor-
relation (r= 0.97,p<0.001) ensures that transcription errors
do not significantly influence the measurement of the original
readability of the input.
3.2. Contrasting Human and Machine Perception
3.2.1. Evaluation of Different Models
Figure 2 (top) illustrates the WER of different ASR archi-
tectures evaluated against the readability (GRI) under optimal
acoustic conditions. The key observation is that the decoupling
between textual complexity and transcription error strongly de-
pends on the architecture. While modern end-to-end sequence-
to-sequence models (Whisper Tiny and Medium,r≤0.03) ex-
hibit near-perfect orthogonality, CTC-based Wav2Vec2 or V osk
models show sensitivity to textual complexity (r= 0.24and
r= 0.14). It is interesting to note that the HMM-GMM model
(PocketSphinx) also shows a low correlation (r= 0.05). But
this should not be misinterpreted as architectural robustness.
Rather, it reflects a high “error threshold,”, a state in which

3.2.2. Robustness under Noise and Reverberation
To ensure that these observations were not justified by overly
clean audio, we subjected Whisper Tiny (chosen deliberately
because it is the Whisper model most prone to errors) to acous-
tic degradation. Although the additive background noise and
simulated reverberation logically degrade the WER, they do not
restore the link with readability. As shown in figure 3 (top),
even with a severe signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) of 0 dB, the cor-
relation between Whisper’s WER and GRI remains completely
flat (r= 0.06). In contrast, the model’s alignment with human-
perceived difficulty remains remarkably stable, hovering around
r=−0.30across all noise and reverberation levels. So while
acoustic degradations impact overall performance, they do not
alter the relationship between text readability and machine tran-
scription performance: the ASR remains “immune” to textual
complexity even when struggling with the signal.
3.2.3. “Real-world” Validation
We also had to ensure that the orthogonality observed was not
only related to our controlled acoustic environment. To do this,
we carried out a “real-world” validation using natural human
speech from the LibriSpeech corpus (see section 2.2.2 for more
details). The results obtained on natural speech corroborate

3.3. ASR “Effort” Measurement
The stability of WER at different levels of readability suggests
no impact on the final result, but does not exclude the existence
of latent “cognitive effort” during the decoding process. In or-
der to determine whether modern ASR models are subject to an
artificial cognitive load similar to that experienced by humans,
we analyzed two intrinsic decoding measures: average predic-
tive entropy and final compression ratio.
•Predictive Entropy. Analysis of predictive entropy shows a
complete lack of correlation with GRI (r=−0.01). This re-
sult indicates that the model does not exhibit increased “hes-
itation” or statistical uncertainty when generating tokens for
complex syntactic structures compared to simpler structures.
•Final Compression Ratio. Similarly, the final compression
rate remains stable across the entire range of textual difficulty
(r=−0.08). This stability proves that the ASR system does
not resort to “simplification” or truncation of complex sen-
tences, a behavior sometimes observed in human summaries
or older lossy compression systems, but instead maintains the
same level of structural fidelity regardless of the readability
of the input.
These internal measures clearly confirm the absence of an
“artificial cognitive load” resulting from the readability level of
the text to be transcribed. The ASR architecture processes com-
plex syntax with the same level of statistical confidence and
structural accuracy as simple sentences. This further supports
our conclusion that contemporary E2E systems have reached
a stage where acoustic transcription is entirely independent of
textual complexity.
4. Discussion
After presenting these results, we can only observe the fol-
lowing: while modern E2E architectures have achieved near-
perfect orthogonality with textual complexity, they keep a sig-
nificant and constant correlation with human-perceived diffi-
culty, even when models are pushed to their limits under condi-
tions of acoustic degradation. Furthermore, given our results in
section 3.3, readability is not a factor that increases machine
uncertainty. This raises the following question: if readabil-
ity has been completely decoupled from model performance,
what non-structural elements continue to cause this correlation
with human judgment? Future studies will need to determine
whether dimensions other than readability, which may be re-
lated to human perception but are not strictly syntactic, are re-
sponsible for this residual correlation. Identifying the causes
of the perceived difficulty that still affects modern ASR will
be a crucial step in further research. Beyond the immediate
conclusions of this study, the observed decoupling between in-
telligibility and readability represents a significant advance in
research on the concept of “listenability” [32, 33]. Listenability
is defined as the overall optimization of a speech to maximize
information transmission while minimizing the cognitive load
imposed on the listener. To achieve such optimization, several
components must be improved simultaneously, such as the read-
ability of the speech script, the acoustic clarity of the signal, its
intelligibility, etc. By demonstrating the orthogonality between
textual complexity and the performance of modern ASR archi-
tectures, we confirm the independence of these modules. This
independence is a major asset for the design of a listenability
model. It validates a modular approach, where the readability of
a script can be optimized upstream without the risk of affecting
the downstream ASR system. As a result, this decoupling sig-
nificantly reduces combinatorial complexity and makes it easier
to design “listenable” systems without having to worry about
the impact of certain core modules on others. This paves the
way for many digital accessibility applications [34]. For exam-
ple, by designing audio guides in museums that are adapted for
people with intellectual disabilities.
5. Conclusion
Our work demonstrates that modern E2E ASR architectures
have achieved near-perfect orthogonality with textual complex-
ity. Unlike human listeners, the performance of these models
is not affected by the readability of the message, even in the
presence of noise or reverberation. The absence of artificial
“cognitive load,” confirmed by analysis of predictive entropy
and compression rate, validates the independence of acoustic
transcription from linguistic structure. This decoupling allows
for a modular approach to “listenability,” facilitating the devel-
opment of digital accessibility applications without the risk of
interference between text optimization and transcription mod-
ules. Future research will now need to identify the remaining
non-structural perceptual factors in order to definitively bridge
the gap between human and machine perception.
