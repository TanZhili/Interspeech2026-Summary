Abstract
While multimodal contrastive models like CLIP and CLAP suc-
cessfully learn shared representations, their embeddings typi-
cally exhibit a persistentmodality gap. We propose addressing
this separation through a purely geometric approach: chang-
ing the embedding domain from the unit sphere,S d−1, to the
probability simplex,∆ d−1. Although both manifolds share the
same intrinsic dimension, the simplex acts as a structural prior;
its nonnegative, unit-mass constraints serve as a cross-modal
calibration that forces both modalities to ground themselves in
the same coordinate system. By attaching lightweight softmax
adapters and using Total Variation similarity, we reduce the cen-
troidℓ 2 gap by 97–99% across five multimodal retrieval bench-
marks. This confirms that simplicial geometry induces genuine
semantic alignment, matching or exceeding the retrieval perfor-
mance of standard unconstrained models. 1
Index Terms: retrieval, multimodality, modality gap
1. Introduction
Contrastive multimodal models embed diverse data streams,
such as text and audio [1, 2] or text and images [3, 4], into
a shared latent space. Despite objectives designed to align
matched pairs, these models often converge to representa-
tions in which modality-wise embedding distributions remain
systematically offset, a phenomenon known as themodality
gap[5]. The practical consequences of this gap remain debated.
Several studies report that large modality gaps can miscalibrate
cross-modal similarity, degrading retrieval and distorting zero-
shot decision boundaries [6, 7]. At the same time, a nonzero gap
may encode modality-specific structure, and indiscriminately
collapsing it can perturb within-modality geometry and harm
downstream performance [5, 8, 7]. Driven by this tension, a
range of strategies have been proposed to manage cross-modal
separation, including modifying the contrastive objective [9],
architectural fusion via shared attention layers [10, 11], adver-
sarial alignment with gradient reversal [12], and alternative em-
beddings motivated by hierarchical structures [8, 13].
We take a geometric view of the modality gap and show that
output geometry alonecan close it dramatically, without mod-
ifying the pretrained backbone and with fewer than 1% train-
able parameters. Our key move is to replace the default spher-
ical embedding domainS d−1 with a simplex-constrained one,

Concretely, we freeze LAION-CLAP [2] for audio–text

DPR)[14, 15], changing only the scoring function: cosine simi-

tance for simplex outputs produced by a softmax projection.
To isolate the effect of geometry from architectural changes,
we evaluate matched-capacity baselines. We start from frozen
backbones and train lightweight Linear and MLP adapters with
Euclidean outputs, and we also train Linear/MLP adapters of
the same architecture whose outputs are followed by a soft-
max projection to the simplex. To disentangle output geome-
try from shared cross-attention, we use an Euclidean variant of
ReTreever [16] (we call itXAttn) that keeps the same learned-
query cross-attention but outputs cosine-scored Euclidean em-
beddings, and compare it to ReTreever’s simplex outputs [16].
Finally, to contrast coarse-to-fine strategies across geometries,
we include MRL as an Euclidean baseline [17] with Retreever.
Our contributions are as follows: (i) We propose simplex-
constrained adapters for multimodal retrieval and show that they
reduce the centroidℓ 2 modality gap by 97–99% across five
audio–text and image–text benchmarks while matching or im-
proving NDCG@10. (ii) We develop a geometry-adjusted gap
measure, RELGAP, which normalizes centroid separation by
intra-modality spread, enabling fair comparison of modality gap
across Euclidean and simplicial embedding domains. (iii) Us-
ing matched-capacity and matched-form baselines, we isolate
the effect of output geometry and show that simplex constraints
(rather than additional architectural capacity) drive gap closure.
(iv) We analyze coarse-to-fine behavior and dimensional scal-
ing, showing that Euclidean gaps grow with representation di-
mension whereas simplex gaps remain small across resolutions.
2. Related Work
Modality gap.Liang et al. [5] systematically characterized the
modality gap in CLIP and connected it to geometric effects such
as the cone effect and initialization-induced asymmetries. Sub-
sequent work has refined both the diagnosis and the proposed
mechanisms: Fahim et al. [9] formalize a contrastive view of
the gap, Schrodi et al. [7] relate it to information imbalance and
compositional failures, and Yaras et al. [18] further links initial-
ization and optimization dynamics to persistent separation. Em-
pirically, the gap has been associated with miscalibrated cross-

modal similarities that can hurt retrieval [19], distort zero-shot
decision boundaries [6], and hinder compositional generaliza-

Managing or reducing cross-modal separation.Prior
approaches intervene either in thetraining objectiveor the
architecture. On the objective side, methods modify con-
trastive learning to explicitly encourage alignment or reweight
supervision [9], use adversarial signals such as gradient re-
versal to suppress modality-specific features [12], or propose
structured/group-wise alignment strategies [20]. On the archi-
tectural side, fusion models introduce shared cross-modal pro-
cessing, e.g., shared transformer layers or attention blocks, as
in UNITER [11], FLA V A [10], V ATT [21], and BLIP-2 [22].
A complementary line adds lightweight adapters or projection
heads on top of frozen backbones (e.g., CLIP-Adapter [23] and
parameter-efficient tuning methods such as LoRA [24, 25]),
aiming to improve alignment with minimal trainable parame-
ters. Finally, hyperbolic spaces have been explored to better
capture hierarchical structure [8, 13].
Simplex and distributional representations.Simplex-
valued representations naturally model nonnegative mixture
weights, with roots in compositional data analysis [26] and
topic models such as LDA [27]. In deep learning, sim-
plex/Dirichlet parameterizations appear in uncertainty model-
ing [28] and in neural collapse, where late-stage features exhibit
simplex-like structure [29]. Most relevant here, ReTreever uses
softmax-normalized coarse-to-fine simplex embeddings for re-
trieval and is competitive with Euclidean multi-resolution base-
lines such as MRL [16, 17].

4. Experimental Setup
Datasets.We evaluate on five cross-modal retrieval bench-
marks spanning audio–text and image–text alignment. For
audio–text, we use Clotho [30], AudioCaps [31], and Sound-
Descs [32]. For image–text, we use MS-COCO Captions [33]
and Flickr30k [34]. All datasets consist of paired items with
bidirectional retrieval (text↔audio or text↔image).
Backbones and adapters.For audio–text experiments
we freeze LAION-CLAP [35], and for image–text we freeze
FLA V A [10]. On top of each frozen backbone we evaluate seven
configurations: the frozen encoder without any adapter; Linear
or MLP adapters with Euclidean outputs scored by cosine simi-
larity; Linear/MLP adapters followed by a softmax projection to
the simplex and scored with a TV-based measure; XAttn (shared
cross-attention with Euclidean outputs and cosine similarity);
and ReTreever (shared cross-attention with multi-resolution
simplex outputs) [16]. For coarse-to-fine analysis (§5.3), we
additionally compare MRL [17] (multi-resolution Euclidean)
against ReTreever. All adapters are trained for 200K steps with
AdamW (lr= 4×10 −4), batch size 64, and a learnable tem-
peratureτ, totaling fewer than 1% trainable parameters.
Metrics.We measure the modality gap using centroidℓ 2,
silhouette [36], and a geometry-adjusted relative gap metric.

the mean nearest-modality distance. To make gaps compara-
ble across Euclidean and simplex outputs, we report RELGAP,

5. Results
5.1. Simplex projection collapses the gap while preserving
retrieval
Tables 1–2 and Figure 2 summarize results across five bench-
marks (audio–text and image–text). Across all datasets, switch-
ing an otherwise identical adapter from Euclidean output to a
softmax projection onto the simplex collapses the modality gap
by two orders of magnitude, without sacrificing retrieval.
On frozen backbones, centroidℓ 2 gaps are large (0.36–0.81
across datasets). Adding a softmax to the same Linear/MLP
adapter reduces centroidℓ 2 to 0.005–0.020, corresponding to
a 97–99% reduction. For example, COCO drops from 0.810
(frozen) to 0.009 (MLP+Sfx), and Flickr30k drops from 0.731
(frozen) to 0.007 (MLP+Sfx). Figure 2 shows the same pattern
geometrically: softmax (simplex) methods cluster at low gap
while remaining competitive in average NDCG@10.
Because the absolute distance scale differs across output
domains, we also report RELGAP(Eq. 1), which normalizes
centroid separation by intra-modality spread. Simplex meth-
ods consistently achieve the lowest RELGAP, indicating that
cross-modal alignment improves beyond any uniform rescal-
ing of distances. For instance, on SoundDescs, RELGAPdrops
from 0.521 (frozen) to 0.179 (MLP+Sfx) and to 0.097 (Re-
Treever), and on AudioCaps it drops from 0.380 (frozen) to
0.180 (MLP+Sfx) and to 0.113 (ReTreever).
Finally, gap reduction does not trade off with retrieval qual-
ity. On Clotho, Lin.+Sfx improves T2A NDCG@10 from 0.259
to 0.382, and on SoundDescs, ReTreever achieves the best re-
trieval scores (0.535/0.542) while maintaining a very small gap.
Overall, simplex constraints remove modality-specific distribu-
tional offsets while preserving (and sometimes improving) the
neighborhood structure relevant for retrieval.

5.2. Separating cross-attention from output geometry
To separate architectural effects from output geometry, we com-
pare Euclidean and simplex models that share cross-attention.
XAttn and ReTreever use similar shared cross-attention mech-
anism, but XAttn outputs Euclidean embeddings (cosine)
whereas ReTreever outputs simplex embeddings (softmax, TV-
based scoring).
Across datasets, XAttn retains substantial gaps compara-
ble to other Euclidean baselines, while ReTreever achieves dra-
matically smaller centroid gaps and RELGAP. For example,
on SoundDescs, XAttn has centroidℓ 2 = 0.309and REL-
GAP= 0.331, whereas ReTreever reaches0.012and0.097.
On Flickr30k, XAttn has centroidℓ 2 = 0.527and RELGAP=
0.563, whereas ReTreever reaches0.005and0.129. Figure 4
shows the same trend on SoundDescs and AudioCaps: methods
with Euclidean outputs (including large cross-attention models
such as W A VE-7B [37]) retain large relative gaps, while sim-
plex outputs consistently reduce them. This isolates output ge-
ometry as the dominant factor in gap closure, beyond any effect
introduced by shared attention layers.
5.3. Coarse-to-fine scaling: Euclidean gaps grow with di-
mension, simplex gaps stay small
Figure 3 compares MRL (Euclidean coarse-to-fine) and Re-
Treever (simplex coarse-to-fine) across representation dimen-
sions. MRL exhibits increasing modality gaps as resolu-
tion/dimension grows, while ReTreever maintains small gaps
across resolutions. On AudioCaps, MRL’s centroid gap grows
from 0.032 at 2-d to 0.534 at 256-d (17×), whereas ReTreever
remains below 0.06 throughout.
5.4. Learned simplex embeddings lie in the sparse regime
Distance behavior on the simplex depends on the density of
the probability vectors (Sec. 3.3). We therefore estimate the
Dirichlet concentrationˆαfrom learned embeddings (Figure 1b).
On SoundDescs,ˆαdecreases from∼18atd= 2to∼0.06at
d= 1024, falling belowα= 1byd= 16. This places high-
dimensional ReTreever embeddings in the sparse regime, where
baseline distances plateau rather than contract (Figure 1a). De-
spite this, the modality gap remains very small and continues
to reamin very low across resolutions (Figure 3), and RELGAP
remains lowest for simplex methods. This supports the interpre-
tation that gap closure reflects improved cross-modal alignment
rather than a purely geometric change in baseline distance scale.
6. Conclusions
We presented a geometric approach to reducing the modality
gap by projecting embeddings onto the probability simplex. Al-
thoughSd−1 and∆d−1 have the same intrinsic dimension, sim-
plex constraints (nonnegativity and unit mass) impose a shared
coordinate system that acts as cross-modal calibration. Across 5
benchmarks, simplex projections reduce the centroidℓ 2 gap by
97–99% while matching or improving retrieval, with RELGAP
improving as well. Comparisons to Euclidean cross-attention
baselines (XAttn and W A VE-7B) indicate that shared cross-
attention by itself does not reliably eliminate the gap, and that
the simplex projection plays a central role in the reductions we
observe. Finally, coarse-to-fine analysis confirms the expected
scaling behavior across resolutions and supports that gap reduc-
tion reflects alignment rather than a distance-scale artifact. Sim-
plex embeddings also provide a convenient compositional and
interpretable representation, and enable information-theoretic
similarity measures (e.g., JS, Hellinger, KL) as alternatives to
cosine distance.
