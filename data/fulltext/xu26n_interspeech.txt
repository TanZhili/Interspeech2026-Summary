Abstract
The Cookie Theft picture description task is widely used to
assess cognitive–linguistic abilities. Analyzing how speakers
progress through the 23 content information units (CIUs) in the
picture provides insight into the informational relevance and ef-
ficiency of their descriptions. Although prior CIU-based studies
have shown effectiveness in distinguishing cognitively impaired
speakers, they largely rely on manual annotation and focus on
spatial CIU distributions, leaving temporal narrative dynam-
ics underexplored. We propose an automated framework for
identifying CIUs from picture descriptions and modeling transi-
tions between CIUs as a temporal graph. Graph-based features
are extracted to characterize narrative organization and tempo-
ral dynamics, and a visualization is designed to illustrate how
speakers traverse picture content over time. The framework
extends CIU-based analysis beyond spatio-semantic represen-
tations and offers a scalable approach for assessing cognitive
abilities.
Index Terms: picture description, cookie theft, cognitive im-
pairment, content information units, graph analysis
1. Introduction
The picture description task is a well-established tool for assess-
ing cognitive and language abilities [1]. Its simplicity of imple-
mentation and ease of administration make it particularly suit-
able for the early assessment of cognitive impairment [2, 3, 4].
One of the commonly used stimuli for this task is the Cookie
Theft picture [5], which depicts a mother washing dishes while
being unaware of an overflowing sink, as a boy climbs onto a
stool to steal cookies from a jar, and a girl stands nearby reach-
ing her arm toward him. A list of 23 elements (known as con-
tent information units, CIUs) representing the most informative
subjects, objects, actions, and locations in the picture have been
identified [6, 7]. Analysis of these CIUs provides insight into
the informativeness and relevance of the description, thereby
offering indicators of the speaker’s cognitive status.
For example, several studies report that cognitively im-
paired speakers produce fewer CIUs and exhibit reduced effi-
ciency compared with cognitively normal speakers [3, 8]. En-
coding CIUs and their relative spatial locations into a graph
yields a spatio-semantic representation that captures speakers’
visuospatial, attentional, and organizational abilities. Features
derived from this representation have been shown to effectively
distinguish cognitively unimpaired speakers from those with

mild cognitive impairment (MCI) or Alzheimer’s dementia [9],
to characterize spatial processing deficits in Huntington’s dis-
ease [10], and to detect primary progressive aphasia [11].
Despite prior successes, current CIU-based cognitive sta-
tus evaluation still leaves substantial room for further develop-
ment. For one thing, traditional CIU extraction relies heavily
on manual annotation, which is labor-intensive and difficult to
scale. To address this limitation, we proposed an automated,
training-free method that maps transcripts to CIUs using an
expert-curated dictionary [12]. However, the restricted vocab-
ulary of this dictionary-based approach limits its robustness to
unseen words. Moreover, by operating largely at the word level,
it does not model contextual relationships or capture sentence-
level semantics during CIU extraction, which constrains its ef-
fectiveness on linguistically diverse or structurally complex in-
puts. Another research gap is that prior studies have largely fo-
cused on the spatial organization of CIUs, such as inferring vi-
suospatial processing abilities from transition patterns between
CIUs located in different regions of a picture, while the tem-
poral dynamics underlying CIU transitions remain compara-
tively underexplored. As documented in the clinical literature,
individuals with cognitive impairment due to Alzheimer’s dis-
ease (AD) may exhibit a phenomenon commonly referred to as
“empty speech”, characterized by circumlocutions, nonspecific
language, and reduced efficiency in conveying ideas [13, 14].
When examined through the lens of CIU temporal dynamics,
such speech patterns may manifest as prolonged transitions be-
tween CIUs, repeated reliance on a limited subset of CIUs, or
alternation between brief bursts of informative content and ex-
tended periods of tangential or drifting speech.
In this study, we propose an automated framework for iden-
tifying CIUs in picture descriptions and modeling transitions
between CIUs as a temporal graph. We extract a set of graph-
based features to quantitatively characterize speakers’ narrative
organization and temporal dynamics, and we design a visu-
alization that provides an intuitive depiction of how speakers
progress through the picture content over time. Overall, this
work extends CIU-based analysis beyond spatial representa-
tions and contributes to the development of a scalable, clinically
interpretable framework that supports clinicians in the assess-
ment of cognitive–linguistic abilities.

2.1. Datasets
The data used in this study include the Pitt Corpus from Demen-
tiaBank [18], the Wisconsin Registry for Alzheimer’s Preven-
tion (WRAP) [19], and the Wisconsin Alzheimer’s Disease Re-
search Center (W-ADRC) clinical core [20]. Across all datasets,
speech recordings were collected during the Cookie Theft pic-
ture description task. Manual transcripts were stored in CHAT
format [21], with CIUs annotated at the sentence level. Table 1
lists the 23 CIUs [6, 7] considered in this study.

The Pitt Corpus and the WRAP dataset were combined to
train the BERT model for CIU identification, resulting in a total

dataset were divided into two groups: those with a diagnosis

diagnosed with “MCI” or “Dementia” were put into the Clin-
ical group. Table 2 summarizes the demographics and Mini-
Mental State Examination (MMSE) scores of the two groups.
The MMSE [22] is a widely used measure of cognitive func-
tion, with scores ranging from 0 to the best performance of 30.
2.2. BERT-based CIU identification
A pre-trained BERT-base-uncased model [23] was fine-tuned to
identify CIUs in each input sentence. We chose this model for
its widespread use [24, 25] in cognitive status estimation and
its efficient, deployment-friendly scale. The model was trained
to perform a 23-class multi-label classification task. During

For each transcript, sentence-level CIU identification re-
sults were concatenated to form a transcript-level CIU se-
quence and compared against manually annotated CIU se-
quences. Since manual CIU annotations are specified at the
sentence level and multiple CIUs may occur concurrently, iden-
tified CIUs were reordered within each sentence to best align
with the manual annotations prior to evaluation. Matches, in-
sertions, deletions, and substitutions were then computed. True
positives (TP) were defined as matches, false negatives (FN) as
the sum of deletions and substitutions, and false positives (FP)
as the sum of insertions and substitutions. Precision, recall, and
F1 score were subsequently calculated to assess performance.
2.3. LRP-based CIU localization
After obtaining the CIU identification results, a LRP–based
method [17] was applied to interpret the model’s decision-
making logic. This method defines relevance scores at the
output layer and propagates them backward through the self-
attention layers in BERT, thereby quantifying the relative con-
tribution of each input word to the model’s classification deci-
sions. Words receiving the highest relevance scores were con-
sidered as the locations of the corresponding identified CIUs.
Specifically, for CIUs corresponding to subjects, places, or
objects (as listed in Table 1), the top three input words with the
highest relevance scores were extracted and encoded into em-
beddings using a Sentence Transformer [27]. The word exhibit-
ing the highest cosine similarity to the identified CIU was se-
lected as the CIU location, and its onset time stamp provided by
WhisperX was retained for subsequent graph construction. For
CIUs corresponding to actions, the three words with the highest
relevance scores were treated as anchors. Three candidate text
spans were generated by extracting subsequences bounded by
each pair of anchors (i.e., anchors 1–2, 1–3, and 2–3). These
spans were encoded into embeddings, and the span exhibiting
the highest cosine similarity to the identified CIU was selected
as the CIU location. The onset time of the first word in the se-

lected span was recorded for downstream graph construction.
The accuracy of CIU localization was manually evaluated by
trained research personnel in accordance with the CIU coding
manual obtained from the developers of the WRAP dataset.
2.4. Temporal graph construction and feature extraction
Using the identified CIU sequences and their onset times, we
constructed a temporal graph for each sample. As illustrated in
Figure 2, each node represents a CIU mentioned in the narra-
tive, while directed edges encode temporal transitions between
CIUs. The time interval between consecutive CIUs is incor-
porated as the edge weight. Revisiting a previously mentioned
CIU results in an edge returning to an existing node, forming
cycles in the graph. The graph is intended to serve as an in-
tuitive visualization of speakers’ progression through the pic-
ture, thereby offering clinically relevant insights to support the
screening of cognitive impairment.
To quantitatively characterize speakers’ organizational abil-
ity and temporal dynamics, a set of graph features were also ex-
tracted, as listed in Table 3. The graph features were selected
based on prior literature [9] and informed by the structural prop-
erties of the proposed temporal graph, which is a directed, time-
ordered graph induced by a single traversal path through the
nodes. As longer narratives inherently produce larger graphs
with more nodes, edges, and traversal steps, narrative length ef-
fects were mitigated by normalizing the number of nodes, walk
length, and number of cycles by word count. Degree central-
ization was introduced to assess whether a narrative is concen-
trated on a limited set of CIUs, with higher values indicating
greater concentration. In addition, inspired by speech process-
ing, where “jitter” is computed as the short-term variability of
the fundamental period to quantify local temporal instability in
vocal fold vibration, we quantified the local variability of CIU
transition intervals using relative edge jitter, defined as the mean
absolute difference between consecutive edge weights, normal-
ized by the mean of edge weights.
Two well-established speech markers [28, 29, 30, 31] of
cognitive decline were included for comparison with the pro-
posed graph-based features to assess their relative effectiveness.
Unfilled pause rate was calculated as the ratio of the total dura-
tion of unfilled pauses (silent intervals longer than 300 ms) to
the total duration of the description, and speech rate was com-
puted as words per minute. During statistical analysis, features
from both groups of the W-ADRC dataset were adjusted for age,
gender, and education. Welch’st-test was then used to assess
the discriminative ability of the features between groups, and
effect sizes were quantified using Hedge’sg. Multiple compar-
isons were controlled using the Bonferroni correction.

Table 5 presents an evaluation of the extracted features’
ability to discriminate between the Normal and Clinical groups
in the W-ADRC dataset. Several phenomena can be observed.
First, the Clinical group produced significantly shorter descrip-
tions than the Normal group, accompanied by an increased un-
filled pause rate and a reduced speech rate. These findings are
consistent with prior literature [28, 29, 30, 31, 32, 33], and
collectively indicate declines in discourse productivity, speech
fluency, and speech tempo. Second, the graphs constructed
from clinical speakers’ narratives contained significantly fewer
unique nodes, with a large effect size of 0.80. This finding
aligns with prior work on spatio-semantic graph analysis [9]
and linguistic studies [8], suggesting that cognitively impaired
speakers convey less semantic information in narratives. Al-
though only small effect sizes were observed for normalized
node count and degree centralization, their trends, together with
the significant reduction in the number of unique nodes, imply
that clinical speakers tend to produce narratives that focus on
limited regions of the picture rather than covering a broader
range of content. These speakers also required significantly
more time to progress through the content, as reflected by longer
walk lengths, which may be attributable to slowed information
retrieval and impaired cognitive–linguistic processing [3].
One finding that contrasts with prior literature [9] is the
larger normalized cycle count observed in the Normal group.

After reviewing samples with more cycles, we observed distinct
behaviors between cognitively normal and cognitively impaired
speakers. Cognitively normal speakers tend to pause briefly af-
ter completing a full traversal of the picture and then provide ad-
ditional clarifications. While producing cycles, they often cover
CIUs that were previously omitted, while the overall narrative
still concludes quickly, maintaining high efficiency (as reflected
by shorter walk length and lower degree centralization). In con-
trast, impaired speakers may exhibit revisit midway through the
narrative (e.g., checking whether they have already mentioned
the cookie jar). During the revisits, they tend to return to CIUs
that have already been covered and repeatedly elaborate on the
same CIU, substantially prolonging the narrative without intro-
ducing additional information. Based on these observations, we
argue that the increased cycle counts observed in cognitively
normal speakers are more likely attributable to purposeful clar-
ification rather than pathological perseveration.
The temporal dynamics of the narratives were quantified us-
ing the mean edge weight, the standard deviation (std) of edge
weights, and relative edge jitter. As shown in Table 5, signifi-
cant differences were observed between the Normal and Clini-
cal groups, with effect sizes comparable to that of the unfilled
pause rate. The Clinical group exhibited a longer mean edge
weight, indicating a generally slower progression through CIUs
and aligning with our previous finding of increased normalized
walk length. A larger std edge weight reflects increased global
variability in the time they used to transition between CIUs, sug-
gesting an uneven narrative pacing across the description. In
addition, the Clinical group demonstrated higher relative edge
jitter, which suggests a pronounced local, short-term instability
between consecutive CIU transitions, possibly driven by pro-
longed pauses or empty speech. Together, these features reveal
disruptions not only in the overall pacing of information deliv-
ery but also in local control of narrative progression, providing
clinically meaningful evidence of cognitive impairment.
4. Conclusion and future works
In this study, we propose an automated framework to identify
CIUs from picture description recordings and construct graph
representations that capture narrative organization and tempo-
ral dynamics. These graphs provide an intuitive visualization of
temporal narrative structure, yielding clinically meaningful in-
sights that may support early screening of cognitive decline. By
extracting temporal graph features, our approach extends exist-
ing spatio-semantic CIU analyses with novel, sensitive markers
of cognitive–linguistic impairment. Major areas for further re-
finement include adapting WhisperX to clinical speech for more
precise utterance segmentation, along with enhancing the sensi-
tivity of CIU localization, especially for CIUs where the present
framework demonstrates comparatively lower sensitivity. Our
future work will advance the accuracy of the framework and
create a clinically viable visualization interface.
