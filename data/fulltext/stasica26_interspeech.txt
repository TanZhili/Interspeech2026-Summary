Abstract
Speech Emotion Recognition (SER) has been broadly studied
to assist automatic triage in Emergency Call Centers (ECC),
based on the assumption that emotion reflects medical urgency.
However, this has not been tested on real data using clinically
grounded priority labels. Using 250 calls of a French ECC an-
notated in 4 priority levels by dispatchers and metadata (age,
sex, speaker role), we compare a categorical SER (discrete emo-
tions) and a dimensional SER (arousal, valence), integrating the
outputs into ordinal logistic regression models. Results show
that categorical emotions do not improve prediction once meta-
data are included. Dimensional emotions show significant inter-
actions between arousal, valence, and speaker role, but effects
are speaker-dependent. Overall, metadata outperform emotion,
provide stronger predictive power than emotion, questioning the
utility of emotion-only approaches for emergency triage.

1. Introduction
1.1. Context
Emergency call centers (ECC), such as France’s SAMU (Ser-
vice d’Aide M´edicale Urgente), play a critical role in urgent care
by performing rapid telephone triage to determine the urgency
of situations and allocate appropriate resources. In France
alone, SAMU centers handle 32 million calls annually, with
call volumes increasing each year, placing growing pressure on
an already strained system [1].Urgencyis defined as a condi-
tion requiring immediate intervention; often defined as threats
to airway, breathing, circulation, or consciousness, such as car-
diac arrest, severe respiratory distress, or major trauma [2].
The determination of urgency relies on quick assessment under
challenging conditions including the absence of physical exam-
ination, variable communication quality, and significant time
pressure [3, 4]. Adding to these challenges, dispatchers face
overwhelming workloads with multiple simultaneous calls and
queues, while trying to make accurate prioritization to ensure
the most critical cases receive immediate attention.
1.2. Previous work
In this context, voice analysis has been proposed as a promis-
ing non-invasive source of immediate information. Prosodic,

Across these works, emotion classification accuracy ranges
widely, from 45% on real data using transformer-based mod-
els [12] to 94.9% on simulated corpora using convolutional neu-
ral networks [10]. Models evaluated on both simulated and real
calls typically perform well on acted data but show marked
drops on naturalistic ECC recordings (e.g., 63% on IEMO-
CAP [17] vs 45.9% on real ECC data [12] using a transformer-
based model), highlighting a substantial corpus mismatch be-
tween training conditions and the proposed real-world deploy-
ment. Moreover, the predominant reliance on emotion classifi-
cation accuracy to evaluate the models may be misleading for
triage, as correctly identifying emotion categories does not nec-
essarily translate into clinical utility. Finally, the studies don’t
specify for which speakers their models are expected to be valid,
implicitly treating callers as a homogeneous group, regardless
of whether they are patients themselves or third-party callers.
Most of the systems presented in these papers model emo-
tion using categorical representations. Six studies rely on
closely related sets of emotion (e.g., anger, fear, sadness, neu-
tral, joy) [7, 8, 12, 14, 15, 13], four of which use real ECC
data. Other works focus on restricted distinctions (e.g., fear vs.
non-fear) [18] or introduce additional categories such as stress
or disgust [10, 16]. Across these works, priority is inferred di-
rectly from emotion, with intense negative emotions interpreted
as high priority, neutral states as intermediate priority, and posi-
tive emotions as low priority. However, these priority mappings
are assumed rather than clinically justified. Only one prior study
adopts a dimensional representation of emotion using valence
and arousal on both simulated data and a French fire station cor-
pus [6]. While that study combines emotional dimensions with
metadata to predict severity, its priority labels are not aligned
with established emergency medical practice, as they focus pri-
marily on observable somatic injuries. The label definition of
these studies illustrates a broader limitation in the literature: ur-
gency categories are often loosely defined and not grounded in
professional ECC prioritization schemes, thereby constraining
the clinical interpretability of reported findings.

1.3. Objectives
This study uses real calls from the SAMU54 to directly assess
whether emotions conveyed through speech correlate with pri-
oritization levels annotated by professional dispatchers, with
the aim of empirically assessing the validity of emotion-based
triage approaches.
2. Dataset
2.1. Priority labels
In the SAMU system, callers first get in contact with a dis-
patcher who collects basic information (name, location, age)
and main symptoms. The dispatcher then assigns a priority level
indicating the urgency of the situation and the order in which
calls should be handled by physicians afterwards. The physi-
cian evaluates the most appropriate response based on clinical
assessment and available resources. This final course of action
may range from simple medical advice or recommendations to
consult a physician independently, to dispatching a medicalized
emergency team. The priority categories annotated by dispatch-
ers, ranging from less to more urgent, are defined as follow1:
•P3: no emergency identified; call can be closed prior to med-
ical triage without risk;
•P2 SNP (Unscheduled Care): non–life-threatening situation
suitable for general care;
•P2 AMU (Emergency Medical Assistance): non–life-
threatening emergency but still requiring urgent care;
•P1: potentially life-threatening situation requiring priority
medical assessment;
•P0: immediate life-threatening emergency.
2.2. Metadata
The dataset comprises emergency calls from the SAMU54
recorded between January 1, 2024, and December 31, 2024.
From this corpus, we construct a controlled subset for analysis
by randomly selecting 50 audio recordings per priority level,
resulting in a balanced dataset of 250 calls. Since our focus
is on call prioritization (i.e., before the caller gets to be heard
by a physician), we only analyze the initial calls between the
dispatcher and the caller, excluding subsequent conversations
with emergency physicians. In cases where multiple callers are
present in a recording, we retain the patient’s speech if present,
else we select the speaker who spoke the most during the call.
We annotate the speaker’s relationship to the patient into four
categories: the patient themselves, a family member, another
healthcare professional present with the patient at the time of
the call, or someone else (e.g., a colleague or bystander who
witnessed an accident). The demographics of the patients, the
distribution of speaker types and the duration of the processed
audio files are summarized in Table 1.

3. Method
3.1. Audio preprocessing
We perform speaker diarization using the pre-trained DiariZen2
model [19], followed by manual verification and correction of
annotations in Praat [20]. We include complete verbal utter-
ances, short vocalizations, and non-linguistic vocal behaviors
(e.g., mumbling, laughter) from the speaker, but exclude over-
lapping speech segments as well as segments from the dis-
patcher and other speakers. All speech segments belonging to
the main speaker are concatenated into a single audio file [21].
3.2. Speech emotion recognition models
SER models trained on French speech were required for this
study. However, the SER proposed in the French ECC liter-
ature are not available, preventing direct reuse. We therefore
rely on two publicly available SER models trained on conversa-
tional French datasets: a categorical and a dimensional model.
This choice allows us to evaluate the two main emotion rep-
resentations discussed in the literature, while considering their
respective advantages and limitations [22].
The categorical emotion recognition model,
Lajavaness3 [23], is a five-class Wav2Vec classifier
[24] trained on real-life French conversational speech. It is
designed to provide emotion recognition solutions in real-world
settings such as call centers. The model outputs a single
categorical emotion (pleased, relaxed, neutral, sad, or tense),
along with a confidence score.
The dimensional emotion recognition model,
SpeechDimEmo4, is also trained on French datasets.
We use pretrained models estimating frame-level arousal 5 and
valence6. In line with usual practices in affective computing
[25], we aggregated frame-level estimation by computing their
median for each recording.
3.3. Statistical models
To examine the relationship between emotion and priority level,
we employed ordinal logistic regression with a proportional
odds model [26], treating priority labels as an ordered categori-
cal outcome (P3 = lowest, P0 = highest priority).

4. Results
4.1. Comparison of the SER approaches
Multinomial logistic regression revealed no significant effects
of valence or arousal on categorical emotion labels relative to
the reference (Neutral), suggesting a limited alignment between
dimensional and categorical emotion representations. This re-
sult is consistent with prior work arguing that categorical and
dimensional models capture partly distinct aspects of affective
expression rather than being directly interchangeable [22, 28].

The remaining emotion categories show comparatively limited

5. Discussion
Categorical emotion.Categorical emotion did not significantly
improve priority prediction once metadata were considered.
This contrasts with previous studies where categorical emotion
is often used as a central predictor, suggesting that categorical
labels alone provide limited added value for triage.
Dimensional emotion.Important differences in severity def-
inition limit direct comparison with the previous study using
dimensional emotion for triaging [6]. In their first study, Abi
et al. define a low severity level (i.e., small wounds, minor or
no medical condition) and a high severity level (i.e., severely
wounded resulting in a dangerous medical condition), without
defining precisely what they consider being a “medical condi-
tion” [29]. However, from their examples, the focus seems to be
on observable somatic injury, also grouping deceased cases with
highly injured ones. In contrast, French ECC practice classifies
deceased patients as low priority (P3), as non urgent medical
intervention is required. Moreover, emergencies involving psy-
chological distress (e.g., suicidal ideation) may require imme-
diate intervention despite the absence of physical injury. These
labels, not defined with professionals, are likely to introduce
systematic biases that affect how model performance is inter-
preted in a clinical context. More broadly, this highlights the
need for collaboration with clinicians, as alignment with real-
world practice is essential for developing computational tools
that meaningfully support healthcare decision-making.
Association of arousal and lower priority.Contrary to previ-
ous emotion-based triage studies, which associate intense neg-
ative emotions with higher urgency, our results link higher
arousal (e.g., tense, nervous, excited, alert) with lower priority
levels. Stratified analyses indicate that this pattern is speaker-
dependent: family callers exhibit the strongest negative associ-
ation between arousal and priority, whereas arousal has weaker
or non-significant effects for other speaker types. However, it
is worth noting that patients are never the speakers in P0-level
emergencies, as they are unconscious or experiencing cardiac
arrest, which can bias our results. We tested a logistic regres-
sion excluding P0 calls but arousal and valence remained non-
significant, with only their interaction showing a weak effect.
This reinforces the caution needed when interpreting the ob-
served associations. These findings suggest that high arousal
in emergency calls may reflect emotional distress or agitation
of the caller that does not necessarily correspond to medical
urgency for the patient. Replication on larger datasets is re-
quired to determine whether this inverse relationship persists or
whether it reflects limitations of the present data.
Metadata.Across both emotion modeling approaches, meta-
data variables remain consistently significant. Age is positively
associated with higher priority, which aligns with clinical ex-
pectations [30], but this effect might also be influenced by the
structure of our dataset: many calls involving children are clas-
sified as low priority, as parents often contact ECC for advice re-
garding minor issues. Sex also emerges as a significant predic-
tor, with male patients more frequently associated with higher
priority calls, potentially reflecting known epidemiological dif-
ferences such as higher cardiovascular risk [31]. Overall, these
findings suggest that basic demographic and contextual infor-
mation may provide more robust signals for triage than emotion
alone, highlighting the need to carefully assess the added clini-
cal value of emotion-based triage in emergency call settings.
6. Conclusion
Thus study evaluates the role of automatic emotion recogni-
tion for priority estimation in a real French emergency call cen-
ter context. Using ordinal logistic regression and AIC-based
model selection, we show that categorical emotion does not pro-
vide additional explanatory power beyond demographic meta-
data and speaker role. Dimensional emotion modeling reveals
statistically significant effects involving interaction of valence
and arousal as well as arousal and speaker role. Yet these ef-
fects remain speaker-dependent, showing significance only for
family members and other callers. Our findings suggest that
emotion, when used in isolation, is a weak proxy for medical ur-
gency, and that patient metadata remains a more reliable indica-
tor as a first proxy for triage decisions. These results, highlight
the need for clinically grounded modeling strategies in larger-
scale studies before deploying emotion-based decision support
tools in emergency call centers.

7. Ethical statement
This study relies on audio recordings from the SAMU54. All
