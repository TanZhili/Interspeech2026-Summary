A Study on Unconstrained Broadcast Audio

Abstract
When processing raw broadcast audio for self-supervised
learning (SSL) pretraining, initial filtering decisions include
whether to retain only speech and restrict to the target language.
While common in prior work, these choices rely on heuristics
without systematic evaluation. We present a controlled compar-
ison of 4 filtering strategies of increasing selectivity on Finnish
broadcast data: no filtering, energy-based voice activity detec-
tion (V AD), neural V AD, and neural V AD with language identi-
fication (LID). Energy-based V AD consistently degrades repre-
sentation quality compared to no filtering. Neural V AD substan-
tially improves ASR, especially when combined with LID (up
to 12.7% absolute WER reduction using 42% of the data), but
increasingly selective filtering degrades general audio under-
standing by up to 9 percentage points. We show that no single
strategy is universally optimal and quantify trade-offs between
preprocessing cost, downstream accuracy, and generalizability.
Index Terms: audio data filtering, self-supervised learning,
speech foundation models
1. Introduction
Raw broadcast audio provides a rich and abundant source of
unlabeled speech data for self-supervised learning (SSL). How-
ever, due to continuous capture, the content is inherently noisy,
diverse, and often multilingual, unlike clean, curated datasets
commonly used in SSL research, such as LibriSpeech [1]. Com-
bined with limited metadata, this poses significant challenges
for effective pretraining data selection for SSL models.
While increasing pretraining data generally improves per-
formance [2, 3], prior work has also shown that diversity and
quality are important [4], with multi-domain datasets outper-
forming single-domain ones even when total training data size
is held constant. However, relying solely on such datasets lim-
its scalability to real-world scenarios, where large-scale audio
collections are predominantly noisy and unstructured.
Broadcast archives represent a realistic alternative for SSL
pretraining, but effectively leveraging these resources requires
preprocessing and filtering. [5] shows that monolingual founda-
tion models outperform multilingual alternatives on target lan-
guage tasks, highlighting the importance of selecting relevant
target language content from multilingual data. Several stud-
ies have explored monolingual SSL pretraining using broad-
cast audio, primarily focusing on evaluation frameworks and
benchmarks [6, 7], or model capabilities [8, 9]. However, when
processing raw broadcast audio, the initial filtering decisions
are whether to retain only speech segments (via voice activity
detection, V AD) and whether to restrict to the target language
(via language identification, LID). These choices appear in prior
work, yet often rely on heuristics. For instance, [10] prioritized
energy-based V AD over neural alternatives to reduce computa-
tional cost and avoid potential bias against unseen languages,
without empirical comparison of their effects on pretraining.
While these filtering methods are individually well-established,
their relative effects on SSL pretraining quality remain unex-
plored, particularly for unconstrained, real-world audio. More-
over, most existing filtering studies focus on labeled or pseudo-
labeled datasets for supervised fine-tuning [11–13], rather than
unlabeled audio for SSL pretraining.
In this work, we address this gap by isolating the effect
of pretraining data filtering on SSL representations through a
controlled evaluation on real-world broadcast archives. Using
large-scale Finnish TV data, which represents challenges com-
mon to real-world archives globally, including multilingual con-
tent, diverse acoustic events, and minimal metadata, we com-
pare four pipelines of increasing selectivity and computational
cost: raw segmentation (no filtering), energy-based V AD, neu-
ral V AD, and neural V AD with audio-based LID. Across diverse
datasets and evaluation protocols, our main findings are:
1. Contrary to common heuristics, energy-based V AD consis-
tently degrades SSL representation quality compared to no
filtering.
2. Neural V AD substantially improves downstream ASR, with
further gains when combined with language filtering (up to
12.7% absolute WER reduction) using only 42% of the data.
3. These ASR gains come at a cost: beyond substantial pre-
processing overhead, increasingly selective filtering reduces
general audio understanding, with classification accuracy
drops of up to 9 percentage points.
2. Data
The pretraining data consist of archived recordings from the
over 5-Mhour Radio and Television Archive of the Finnish Na-
tional Audiovisual Institute (KA VI). We received fixed time
slots from AlfaTV , a TV channel offering a mix of talk and cur-
rent affairs shows, lifestyle content, music, nature, and sports
programs, with predominantly Finnish-speaking material total-
ing about 20,400 hours of audio.
Naturally, these real-world data include a substantial
amount of non-speech content, such as noise, silence, and var-
ious acoustic events. While the primary language is Finnish,
some segments contain speech in other languages. This acous-
tic and linguistic variability can affect the quality of SSL speech
representations. To investigate its impact on downstream per-
formance, we experiment with different filtering techniques to
selectively retain specific types of content.

2.1. Data filtering methods
For the baseline setup, no filtering was applied. Audio record-
ings were segmented into fixed 30-second chunks, regardless of
acoustic content. This approach is expected to expose the SSL
model to a diverse spectrum of real-world acoustic conditions
within the same training example, including both speech and
non-speech events.
Next, we applied energy-based voice activity detection (E-
V AD), selecting speech segments by applying energy thresh-
olds. However, E-V AD relies solely on signal energy and there-
fore may select other types of audio events along with the actual
speech. For this method, we used the Auditok toolkit 1.
To distinguish speech from non-speech more effectively
and filter out other acoustic events, we employed a neural
network-based voice activity detection model (N-V AD) using
the pyannote.audio toolkit [14, 15]. This approach allows for
more accurate detection of speech turn boundaries by utiliz-
ing pretrained deep acoustic features learned directly from raw
waveforms, capturing task-relevant frequency patterns without
requiring hand-crafted features.
Finally, we ran the segments filtered by the N-V AD model
through a separate audio-based language identification (A-LID)
system to exclude parts potentially from non-target languages.
This step is intended to enable the SSL model to focus more
precisely on speech patterns in the target language during pre-
training. We used the ECAPA-TDNN Spoken Language Iden-
tification model2, trained on 6,628 hours of speech across 107
languages, including Finnish. Due to the lack of gold-standard
language labels for our domain (Finnish TV broadcasts), we
cannot directly evaluate the LID model’s accuracy on these data.
Instead, we assess its effectiveness indirectly by comparing the
downstream performance of SSL models pretrained with and
without this additional filtering step.
A crucial practical consideration in audio data filtering is
preprocessing time, especially when working with large-scale
materials. To evaluate the computational efficiency of each
technique, we measured the real-time factor (RTF). Specifically,
we randomly selected 100 audio samples (approx. 100 hours in
total), applied each filtering method, and computed the mean
and standard deviation of the RTF across samples. To isolate
the filtering procedure, data loading time was excluded from
the measurements. For theRaw(no filtering) setup, we mea-
sured only the time required to segment the audio array into
fixed-length chunks. For theN-VAD + A-LIDsetup, the to-
tal preprocessing time included both N-V AD processing of the
full audio sample and subsequent LID inference on the result-
ing speech-only segments. For filtering approaches involving
neural networks (N-VADandN-VAD + A-LID), the RTF was
measured using a single AMD MI250X GPU.
Table 1 summarizes the pretraining data and filtering ap-
proaches used, along with their corresponding RTF measure-
ments.E-VADadds only a negligible computational overhead
compared to simple segmentation into fixed-length chunks,
while removing 13.5% of the original content. In contrast, neu-
ral network-based filtering incurs considerably higher computa-
tional costs but provides more selective filtering. Finally, while
N-VADalone retains about 60% of the data, combining it with
A-LIDremoves more than half of the content, at the cost of fur-

3. Pretraining
We selected wav2vec 2.0 [16] as a fixed architecture across all
experiments to isolate the effect of data filtering from model
design. This framework remains widely used for developing
non-English speech foundation models [5–9, 17, 18], making
our findings directly relevant to practitioners in these settings.
Given the computational requirements of SSL pretraining, we
used theBasevariant (95M parameters).
All models were implemented in Fairseq [19]. To ensure
a fair comparison across filtering approaches, we pretrained all
models with identical hyperparameters. Each model was trained
on one of the four subsets described in Section 2.1. Pretraining
used a learning rate of 6e-4, a dynamic batch size of 3 minutes
of audio per GPU, and ran for 62K steps on 512 AMD MI250X
GPUs. We pretrained models in full precision to avoid gradient
overflow issues that can occur with mixed precision training [7],
ensuring stable and consistent parameter updates across setups.
4. Fine-tuned ASR evaluation
We evaluated ASR performance by fine-tuning our pretrained
models on Finnish subsets of three datasets: Common V oice
16.1 [20] (2.7h), FLEURS [21] (8.8h), and V oxPopuli [22] (22.6
h). We followed the standard fine-tuning procedure, randomly
initializing a single linear layer on top of the wav2vec 2.0 model
and optimizing with CTC loss while keeping the CNN feature
encoder frozen. Models were fine-tuned using HuggingFace
Transformers [23] for 80 epochs with a learning rate of 1e-4,
batch size of 12 per GPU across 8 AMD MI250X GPUs, and
evaluated using Word Error Rate (WER) on the corresponding
development and test sets.
Table 2 summarizes the results.N-VADconsistently outper-
forms both the baseline andE-VAD. Notably,E-VADdegrades
downstream ASR performance, suggesting simple energy-
based filtering yields suboptimal pretraining data points for
ASR.N-VAD + A-LIDachieves the best performance, with 4.8-
12.7% absolute WER improvement over the baseline (largest
gains on the smallest dataset, Common V oice) and 0.6-2.6%

6. Non-speech audio probing
While our data selection pipelines substantially improved
speech recognition, we next evaluated whether and to what ex-
tent these filtering decisions affect general audio understand-
ing capabilities. We used the ARCH benchmark [25], which
assesses pretrained models on various non-speech audio tasks.
Following the official protocol, each classification task involves
training a single-layer linear classifier for 200 epochs on frozen
SSL representations with fixed hyperparameters. Unlike ML-
SUPERB, which uses a weighted average of layers with learn-
able weights, ARCH uses only the final layer’s output, averaged
over time to produce sequence-level embeddings.
Specifically, we focused on two subsets of the benchmark:
(i)Acoustic Events, which includes ESC-50 [26], UrbanSound
8K (US8K) [27], FreeSound Dataset 50K (FSD50K) [28], and
Variably Intense V ocalizations of Affect and Emotion (VI-
V AE) [29] datasets; and (ii)Music, which comprises Free Mu-
sic Archive (FMA) [30], MagnaTagATune (MTT) [31], Instru-
ment Recognition in Musical Audio Signals (IRMAS) [32], and
Medley-solos-DB (MS-DB) [33].
The results of these probing experiments are shown in Ta-
ble 4. In contrast to ASR, the model pretrained on unfiltered
broadcast audio outperforms others across most non-speech
classification tasks (6/8).E-VADexhibits varying performance,
remaining competitive on US8K, VIV AE, and FMA, but pro-
viding lower scores on other datasets, particularly on IRMAS
instrument recognition (35.6% vs 45.7% for theRawbaseline).
N-VADachieves mid-tier results on most tasks, outperforming
others only on MS-DB melody and solo instrument classifica-
tion. Finally,N-VAD + A-LIDyields the worst results on 3 out
of 8 tasks and never achieves top performance on any dataset.
7. Discussion
Data filtering of real-world noisy broadcast audio data presents
multiple challenges and trade-offs beyond simple performance
metrics. Balancing data quality and quantity while maintaining
reasonable computational efficiency requires careful considera-
tion of the intended application and available resources.
Figure 1 shows the trade-off between filtering cost and ASR
performance across our pipelines, evaluated after fine-tuning
the pre-trained models. The substantial increase in preprocess-
ing time caused by neural network-based V AD is accompanied
by considerable improvements in downstream ASR. Despite re-
ducing the original data by around 40%, this step results in
WER reductions (WERR) of up to 10.1%, suggesting the com-
putational overhead is justified for speech recognition applica-
tions.

The performance of energy-based filtering highlights the
limitations of simplistic approaches. While appealing due
to minimal preprocessing overhead,E-VADunexpectedly de-
graded ASR performance despite removing presumably unin-
formative content such as silence. Since all filtering methods
produced variable-length segments (2-30 seconds), this degra-
dation likely stems fromE-VADcreating training samples with
inconsistent acoustic contexts for the wav2vec 2.0 contrastive
learning objective, rather than due to variable duration.E-VAD
generates segments containing only speech, only non-speech,
or mixed acoustic content, complicating the pretraining task
of distinguishing correct quantized representations from dis-
tractors sampled within the same sequence. Speech-only sam-
ples present a challenging task due to the acoustic similarity
between true representations and negative examples, whereas
non-speech segments involve different acoustic conditions of
varying complexity. Mixed-content utterances can range from
simple tasks, where acoustic events are easily distinguishable,
to more complex ones, depending on acoustic diversity within
each sample. This potentially forces the model to learn mul-
tiple distinct contrastive tasks of widely varying difficulty si-
multaneously. In contrast, raw splitting provides predomi-
nantly mixed-content segments, simplifying contrastive learn-
ing through higher acoustic diversity between target represen-
tations and distractors, while N-V AD-based methods enable the
model to focus specifically on learning within speech contexts.

while filtering out non-target languages, despite potential clas-

more than fourfold increase in computational cost compared to

In scenarios with more linguistically diverse audio streams, lan-
guage filtering may yield more substantial improvements on
downstream performance, even with larger fine-tuning datasets.
The ML-SUPERB frozen representation evaluation pro-
vides additional insights complementing the fine-tuning exper-
iments. These results validate our findings and demonstrate
that the improvements of neural V AD-based filtering stem from
enhanced representation quality rather than fine-tuning effects.
Similarly, the consistent degradation byE-VADacross both
evaluation protocols shows that this approach harms represen-
tation quality. However, some inconsistency between datasets
can be observed.
For Common V oice and FLEURS, including both the 10-
minute and 1-hour training setups, there is a notable perfor-
mance gap between simpler methods (Raw&E-VAD) and
neural-based ones (N-VAD&N-VAD + A-LID), with relatively
small performance differences within each category. How-
ever, V oxPopuli shows different patterns with larger perfor-
mance gaps between filtering methods and changed model rank-
ings. Specifically,N-VAD + A-LIDdegraded model perfor-
mance compared toN-VAD, contrasting with the consistentA-
LIDimprovements observed in fine-tuning experiments.
While ASR evaluation shows that neural V AD filtering im-
proves representation quality, with additional language filtering
yielding further gains in most scenarios, the ARCH benchmark
results reveal another dimension of trade-offs in data selection
strategies: selective pretraining data filtering comes at the cost
of general audio understanding capabilities.
In contrast to the ASR experiments, the ARCH benchmark
reveals the opposite pattern, with no filtering achieving top re-
sults across 6 out of 8 tasks. The most notable relative im-
provements can be observed on the highly complex FSD50K,
featuring a multi-label classification task on 200 diverse classes
representing sound events from physical sound sources and pro-
duction mechanisms.
E-VADshows mixed and inconsistent performance across
tasks, achieving the best results on VIV AE, ranking second-best
on 3 tasks, and performing worst on 3 tasks. The most notable
performance degradation occurs on IRMAS music instrument
recognition, where this method scores 35.6% mean average pre-
cision compared to 45.7% for the baseline. This inconsistent
pattern suggests that while degrading ASR performance, this
data selection approach provides questionable benefits for gen-
eral audio understanding tasks.
N-VADshows the most balanced performance, achieving
mid-tier results on most tasks while outperforming others on
MS-DB melody and solo instrument classification. It has vari-
able results relative toE-VADand smaller performance drops
relative to the baseline thanN-VAD + A-LID. With substantial
ASR gains and only moderate non-speech performance degra-
dation,N-VADrepresents a practical choice for noisy, mixed-
content broadcast data when both capabilities are needed in the
pre-trained model.
Finally,N-VAD + A-LID, the most restrictive data filtering,
yields the worst results on 3 out of 8 tasks and never achieves
top performance. This suggests that while providing the lowest
downstream ASR WERs, the resulting speech foundation model
is poorly suited for audio tasks beyond speech recognition.
8. Conclusions
Through a systematic evaluation of filtering strategies for SSL
pretraining on real-world broadcast archives, we show how ini-
tial filtering decisions fundamentally shape foundation model
performance across different tasks. Simple energy-based V AD
consistently degrades SSL representation quality compared to
no filtering, while neural-based V AD substantially improves
downstream ASR, especially when combined with LID, achiev-
