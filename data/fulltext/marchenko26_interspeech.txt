TIMBRE: Layer-Wise Cross-Lingual Speech Emotion Recognition
Across 49 Layers and 26 Corpora
Anatoly Marchenko1
1Independent Researcher, Russia
marchenkoanatoli@gmail.com
Abstract
We present the first large-scale layer-wise cross-lingual speech
emotion recognition analysis: 49 layers of wav2vec2-xls-r-1b
across 26 corpora, 23 languages and 14 families, a49×26×26
tensor of 31,850 experiments. Under linear probing of mean-
pooled representations, cross-corpus transfer peaks at layer 15
(31% depth, F1=0.392), the CRIS layer, and collapses by 24.6%
at layer 45. Typology is associated with the shape of the
layer profile: Romance languages show a U-shaped relative
advantage, and the tonal advantage grows with depth. On the
same26×26matrix with identical classifiers, mean-pooled
wav2vec2 outperforms 27-dim acoustic features by 41% (F1:
0.355 vs. 0.252); acoustic features retain 71% of this baseline at
a3.7×10 7-fold lower complexity and prevail on only 3 of 26
corpora. Per-corpus expressiveness and elicitation style, rather
than group or family membership, drive overall transferability.
Index Terms: speech emotion recognition, cross-lingual trans-
fer, wav2vec2, layer probing, typology
1. Introduction
As speech emotion recognition (SER) systems expand across
languages, it remains unclear at which level of representa-
tion emotional cues generalize across typologically diverse lan-
guages.
Self-supervised speech models such as wav2vec2 [1] have
improved SER performance across multiple benchmarks [2, 3].
Layer probing studies reveal that different depths encode differ-
ent linguistic properties [4], and cross-lingual SER benchmarks
like EmoBox [5] evaluate transfer across languages. However,
no prior work has combined these: probingevery layerof a
large multilingual model for emotion transfer acrossdozensof
languages.
We fill this gap with three contributions:
1. TheCRIS layer(Cross-lingual Representational Integration
Stratum): cross-corpus emotion transfer peaks at layer 15
(31% depth) of wav2vec2-xls-r-1b, then collapses at upper
layers, a49×26×26analysis comprising 31,850 exper-
iments, over 80×more cross-corpus probing runs than the
closest prior work [6] (4 corpora).
2.Typological layer profiles: Romance languages show a
U-shaped relative advantage across layers, while the tonal
advantage grows with depth, indicating that prosodic type
shapes the layer-wiseprofileof cross-lingual transfer.
3. Afeature comparison: on the same26×26matrix with
identical classifiers, mean-pooled wav2vec2 outperforms 27-
dimensional acoustic features by 41% (F1: 0.355 vs. 0.252);
acoustic features retain 71% of this baseline at3.7×10 7-fold
lower complexity, prevailing on only 3 of 26 corpora.
2. Related Work
Cross-lingual SER.EmoBox [5] evaluated 10 pretrained mod-
els across 32 corpora but used only the last transformer layer
and did not perform layer-wise probing. Wagner et al. [2]
showed that transformer models close the valence gap but did
not evaluate cross-lingual transfer per layer. Han et al. [6] con-
ducted cross-lingual layer probing for SER but on only 4 cor-
pora.
Layer probing.Pasad et al. [4] probed wav2vec2 layers for
ASR and phonetic tasks, finding task-dependent optimal layers.
Scheidwasser-Clow et al. [7] probed multiple SSL models for
SER across 7 corpora (6 languages), finding that middle lay-
ers capture the most emotional information, but evaluated only
within-corpus performance, not cross-corpus transfer. Pepino et
al. [3] first applied wav2vec2 to SER with learned layer weight-
ing on 2 corpora.
Acoustic features.GeMAPS [8] standardized handcrafted
features for affective computing. Perturbation measures (jitter,
shimmer, HNR) are assumed universal emotion correlates [9],
but cross-linguistic ablation evidence is sparse.
Prosodic typology.Tonal languages use F0 lexically, con-
straining its availability for emotion [10]. This predicts that
tonal speakers rely on alternative acoustic channels, a predic-
tion our data examines directly.
3. Method
3.1. Corpora
We use 26 corpora spanning 23 languages from 14 fam-
ilies, grouped into four language groups (one prosodic,
three genetic): tonal (Mandarin, Cantonese, Thai, Viet-
namese; 4 corpora), Romance (Portuguese-BR, French×2,
Italian, Spanish; 5), Germanic (English, German×2; 3),
Slavic (Polish, Russian×2; 3), plus 11 others including Tur-
kic, Indo-Aryan, Semitic, Uralic, Dravidian, and Iranian lan-
guages. All labels are harmonized to 4 core emotions (an-
gry, happy, sad, neutral) with a maximum of 500 utter-
ances per class. Corpora used: ASED [11], CaFE [12],
CA VES [13], DUSHA [14], EmoDB [15], emoUERJ [16],
Emozionalmente [17], ESD (Chinese subset) [18], Esto-
nian EEKK [19], Hi,KIA [20], JVNV [21], Kannada SER [22],
KazEmoTTS [23], MDER [24], MESD [25], nEMO [26],
Oreau [27], PA VOQUE [28], RA VDESS [29], RESD [30],
ShEMO [31], SUBESCO [32], Thai-SER [33], TurEV-DB [34],
URDU [35], ViSEC [36]. A full corpus-metadata table (per-
corpus elicitation style, size, and label set) together with li-
censes is provided with the publicly released code and data.1

3.2. Representations
Acoustic features.We extract 27 features in 6 groups using
Parselmouth [37] and librosa [38]: perturbation (jitter, shimmer,
HNR), frequency (mean F0, F0 SD, voiced ratio), MFCC (co-
efficients 0–12), spectral (centroid, bandwidth, rolloff, ZCR),
formants (F1–F3), and energy (RMS). Feature normalization is
experiment-specific (Sec. 3.3); no test-corpus statistics are used
in any cross-corpus experiment.
wav2vec2 embeddings.We use wav2vec2-xls-r-1b [39,
40], which has 48 transformer layers plus a CNN feature en-
coder (49 extraction points). For each utterance, we extract
the mean-pooled representation at every layer, yielding a 1280-
dimensional vector per layer.
3.3. Experiments
Experiment 1: Acoustic cross-corpus.For each ordered pair
(i,j)of 26 corpora, we train a logistic regression (L2,C=1.0)
on corpusiand evaluate on corpusj, using raw acoustic feature
values (no normalization). This produces a26×26transfer
matrix (650 off-diagonal pairs).
Experiment 2: wav2vec2 cross-corpus (mean-pool).
Same protocol as Exp. 1, but using wav2vec2 representations
mean-pooled across all 48 transformer layers.
Experiment 3: Layer-wise cross-corpus (flagship).For
each of the 49 layers independently, we repeat the cross-corpus
protocol of Exp. 1 with single-layer representations, z-scored
using training-corpus statistics only, yielding a49×26×26
transfer tensor: 31,850 cross-corpus experiments. We use lo-
gistic regression with fixed hyperparameters (C=1.0) following
EmoBox [5] conventions, avoiding per-pair tuning.
Experiment 4: Acoustic ablation.Per-corpus SVM clas-
sification (RBF, grid search overCandγ, 5-fold CV , features
z-scored within each training fold) with 6-group leave-one-out
ablation on 25 corpora (22 of the 26 above plus 3 additional;
the set differs owing to licensing and label-coverage constraints,
detailed in the released data).

4.2. Typological Layer Profiles
Figure 2 disaggregates the layer curve by language group. The
profiles diverge sharply.
Romance U-curve.Romance languages show above-
average transfer at early layers (advantage = within-group
mean minus grand average;+0.041at L0), dip below aver-
age at middle layers (−0.023at L15), then partially recover
(+0.004at L35). We interpret this as: early layers capture
phonetic/vowel-quality similarity within Romance; middle lay-
ers extract language-universal emotion features where Romance
loses its clustering advantage; upper layers re-encode language-
specific patterns where Romance reconverges.
Tonal late-peaking rise.Tonal languages (Mandarin, Can-
tonese, Thai, Vietnamese; 3 families, though 2 of 4 corpora
are Sino-Tibetan) show increasing within-type transfer: from
−0.004at L0 to+0.055at L15 to+0.075at L35. Because
F0 carries lexical load in tonal languages, reducing (though
not eliminating) its availability for emotion [10], this is con-
sistent with the model increasingly relying on non-F0 emotion
channels. The result is convergent representations despite ge-
netic distance (though tone itself spread areally among these
languages, which limits the genetic inference).

based contrasts are weak (within-group transfer is computed
over the 15 corpora of the four groups: 44 within vs. 166 be-
tween pairs): within-group transfer (0.410) is statistically in-
distinguishable from between-group transfer (0.425); a corpus-
clustered bootstrap of the difference gives a 95% CI of

4.3. Acoustic vs. wav2vec2
Table 1 compares cross-corpus transfer using acoustic features
(Exp. 1) and wav2vec2 mean-pooled embeddings (Exp. 2) on
the same 26 corpora.
Using identical classifiers (logistic regression,C=1.0),
mean-pooled wav2vec2 outperforms acoustic features by 41%
on average (F1: 0.355 vs. 0.252). The 27-dimensional features
retain 71.0% of this mean-pool baseline, at a3.7×10 7-fold
reduction in model complexity (the ratio of upstream encoder
parameters,∼10 9, to feature dimensionality, 27); against the
optimal layer 15 (F1=0.392), the ratio is 64.3%. Acoustic fea-
tures prevail on only 3 of 26 test corpora (TurEV-DB, Hi,KIA,
MESD), with margins of at most 0.025; the first two are among
the weakest wav2vec2 transfer targets. Handcrafted features
are no substitute for SSL representations, but they offer an in-
terpretable near-zero-cost baseline at roughly two thirds of the
cross-corpus quality.
4.4. Supporting Findings
MFCC dominance.In the ablation study (Exp. 4, 25 corpora),
MFCC is universally the most important feature group (mean
∆F1=−0.097), with perturbation showing language-dependent
utility: Romance corpora average∆Pert=−0.033(all 5 neg-
ative) vs. non-Romance−0.007(Mann-Whitneyp <0.001;
d=−2.7,n=5 vs. 20).
Expressiveness predicts transfer.At layer 15,
EmoDB [15] (German, theatrical) achieves the highest
incoming transfer (0.640), while DUSHA [14] (Russian,
crowd-sourced) is among the lowest (0.269; only the small
Estonian EEKK corpus scores lower, 0.209). Thai→EmoDB
transfer reaches 0.562: tonal-language training data transfers
well to Germanic test sets, and the expressiveness of the
elicitation (theatrical vs. subdued) appears to matter more than
language similarity.
5. Discussion
Our layer-wise analysis reveals a clear depth-dependent struc-
ture in cross-lingual emotion encoding. The CRIS layer at
L15 occupies a representational “sweet spot”: deep enough to
abstract past language-specific phonetics, but shallow enough
to avoid language-specific semantic commitments that impair
transfer. The 24.6% collapse at L45 confirms that upper layers
specialize for the pretraining language distribution.
The divergent typological profiles (Romance U-curve vs.
tonal late-peaking rise) point to language prosody as a factor in
how emotions are encoded across the network. Tonal languages,
with reduced F0 freedom for emotion, produce representations
that become increasingly transferable with depth. Romance lan-
guages cluster early (shared phonetic inventories) but disperse
at universal layers. We note that expressiveness (theatrical vs.
subdued elicitation) also drives transfer (Section 4.4) and may
partially confound the typological patterns, since all Romance
corpora use acted speech.
These findings have practical implications. For multilin-
gual SER deployment with frozen wav2vec2-xls-r-1b features,
layer-15 embeddings (or any layer in the L12–L18 plateau)
should be preferred over the conventional last-layer or all-layer
mean. Where compute or interpretability constraints rule out
SSL models, handcrafted acoustic features provide a transpar-
ent fallback at roughly two-thirds of mean-pooled wav2vec2
quality.
Limitations.(1) 25 of 26 corpora use acted, read, or crowd-
performed speech (only URDU is naturally occurring), so our
conclusions primarily concern acted-emotion transfer. (2) The
Romance U-curve rests onn=5 corpora (including two French),
and tonal onn=4 (two Sino-Tibetan); corpus size also co-varies
with group (four of the five Romance corpora are among the
smallest in the set, all four tonal corpora among the largest), so
these patterns should be treated as exploratory. (3) The layer
curve is broad-peaked (L12–L18 form a plateau); the L15 rec-
ommendation is a point estimate within this range. (4) We probe
only wav2vec2-xls-r-1b; the CRIS layer may shift in other SSL
models (HuBERT, WavLM). (5) Logistic regression may un-
derestimate layer capacity compared to fine-tuning. (6) Within-
language variation (EmoDB [15] vs. PA VOQUE [28]) can ex-
ceed between-language differences.
6. Conclusion
We present the first49×26×26layer-wise cross-lingual
SER analysis, establishing layer 15 of wav2vec2-xls-r-1b as the
CRIS layer (Cross-lingual Representational Integration Stra-
tum) for cross-lingual transfer of frozen mean-pooled repre-
sentations. Typological layer profiles (Romance U-curve, tonal
late-peaking rise) indicate that language group shapes the layer-
wise transfer profile, while per-corpus expressiveness and elic-
itation style, rather than group or family membership, drive
overall transferability. Acoustic features, at a3.7×10 7-fold
lower complexity, retain 71% of mean-pooled wav2vec2 trans-
fer quality but prevail on only 3 of 26 corpora. Code and corpus
metadata are publicly released.2
