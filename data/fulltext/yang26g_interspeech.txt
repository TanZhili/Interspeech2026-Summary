Pitch-Injected Residual Adapter for Tonal Language in Neural Audio Codec
Jie-Shiang Yang1, Ya-Tse Wu1, Chi-Chun Lee1,∗∗
1 Department of Electrical Engineering, National Tsing Hua University, Hsinchu, Taiwan

Abstract
Neural Audio Codecs (NACs) achieve high-fidelity reconstruc-
tion at low bitrates but remain insensitive to fundamental fre-
quency (F0) distortion—a critical limitation for tonal languages,
covering 40%+ of the world’s languages, where F0 contour
marks lexical distinctions. We propose the Pitch-Injected
Residual Adapter (PIRA), a lightweight plug-and-play mod-
ule that restores tonal information in frozen NACs through ex-
plicit F0 and voiced/unvoiced side-information injection. PIRA
processes quantized F0 through dilated convolutions to cap-
ture tone sandhi dependencies, supervised by a CREPE em-
bedding loss for pitch-specific gradients. A confidence network
gates injection per-frame, suppressing it for checked tones and
unvoiced segments. With 1.25M–1.65M trainable parameters,
PIRA reduces codec-induced Tone Error Rate (dTER) by 35.7%
on average (macro-averaged across five codecs, each averaged
over three tonal languages), while preserving English quality
and adding≤0.4 kbps overhead.
Index Terms: neural audio codec, tonal language, pitch restora-
tion, low-resource adaptation, F0 injection
1. Introduction
Neural Audio Codecs (NACs) have become foundational to
modern speech generation, voice conversion, and discrete tok-
enization for LLM-based speech synthesis [1, 2, 3, 4, 5]. These
models employ encoder-decoder architectures with Residual
Vector Quantization (RVQ) to achieve high compression while
maintaining perceptual quality, with recent single-codebook de-
signs operating below 1 kbps [6, 7]. However, these codecs are
predominantly trained on non-tonal language corpora [8] and
optimized for perceptual metrics—PESQ and STOI—that are
insensitive to fundamental frequency (F0) distortion [2], treat-
ing F0 as a secondary prosodic feature. Beyond F0, RVQ-
induced quantization has been shown to incur broader represen-
tational deficiencies: semantic content is degraded when codecs
are integrated with language models [9, 10], and pitch fidelity is
compromised even with multiple codebooks [11], leading to F0
smoothing, octave errors, and voicing confusion. While these
limitations are tolerable for non-tonal languages, they become
a critical failure mode when F0 contour serves a phonemic—
rather than merely prosodic—function.
Tonal languages, comprising over 40% of the world’s lan-
guages [12], employ F0 contour for lexical distinction: min-
imal pairs such as Mandarinm ¯a(‘mother’) vs.m ˇa(‘horse’)
are differentiated solely by tone, rendering F0 preservation crit-
ical for intelligibility. Many tonal languages further exhibit
tone sandhi—contextual tonal alternations spanning 3–5 syl-
lables [13, 14], introducing long-range F0 dependencies that

A natural response to this domain mismatch is to fine-tune
pretrained codecs on target tonal languages. However, end-to-
end fine-tuning risks catastrophic forgetting [15] of pretrained
knowledge and demands substantial data and compute—both
scarce for low-resource tonal languages. More fundamentally,
standard codec losses lack explicit pitch supervision: even with
sufficient resources, fine-tuning improves spectral adaptation
but provides limited F0 restoration—a limitation we empirically
confirm in Section 4.2. This structural deficiency motivates
plug-and-play adaptation: lightweight modules that inject pitch-
specific information directly into frozen codec latent spaces,
without modifying pretrained parameters. Such parameter-
efficient adapters are well-established in NLP [16, 17] and
speaker recognition [18], yet remain unapplied to tonal restora-
tion in NAC latent spaces.
The feasibility of explicit F0 conditioning has been demon-
strated in pitch-controllable synthesis [19, 20, 21, 22]. Cru-
cially, pretrained NACs already exhibit strong performance
across diverse downstream speech tasks [23], making frozen-
model adaptation particularly valuable: it preserves all pre-
trained capabilities while remaining fully removable for non-
tonal deployment. Concurrent work on semantic-rich tok-
enization [24] further highlights the gap between what stan-
dard perceptual losses optimize and what downstream speech
LMs require—yet none target the frozen-codec setting or ad-
dress phonemically contrastive F0. All existing methods require
full codec retraining and target prosody transfer in synthesis
pipelines rather than tonal restoration at inference time—and
therefore cannot serve as plug-and-play baselines on a frozen
codec, precluding direct comparison (Section 3.3). To the best
of our knowledge, no prior work addresses post-hoc recovery
of phonemically contrastive F0 in pre-trained NACs—the set-
ting where retraining is infeasible.
We propose the Pitch-Injected Residual Adapter (PIRA),
a lightweight parallel module that restores tonal informa-
tion in frozen NACs through explicit F0 and voiced/unvoiced
(UV) side-information injection. PIRA processes quantized F0
through a dilated convolutional network [25] with a large recep-
tive field to capture long-range tone sandhi dependencies, su-
pervised by a CREPE embedding loss [26] that provides pitch-
specific gradients absent from standard perceptual objectives. A
confidence network learns per-frame gating to suppress injec-
tion for checked tones and unvoiced segments where spectral
cues carry tonal distinction. With 1.25M–1.65M trainable pa-
rameters, PIRA reduces codec-induced Tone Error Rate (dTER)
by 35.7% on average across five state-of-the-art codecs and
three typologically diverse tonal languages. Crucially, PIRA is
fully removable: when serving non-tonal languages, the origi-
nal codec operates unchanged with zero quality degradation—a
property no fine-tuning approach can offer.

2. Methodology
2.1. System Overview
Rather than modifying pretrained codec parameters, PIRA op-
erates as a parallel side-module in thequantized latent space, in-
jecting pitch-aware residualsRweighted by learned per-frame
confidenceαto correct tonal distortion while preserving pre-
trained spectral reconstruction (Figure 1). Only the adapter pa-
rameters are trainable; both encoder and decoder remain frozen.
Given input audiox∈R T atf s = 16kHz, the frozen en-

2.2. Residual Pitch Injector with Dilated Convolutions
The Pitch InjectorΦprocesses concatenated F0/UV features
[ ˆf0,uv]∈R 2×Tz through a1×1input projection (D h = 256),
followed byL= 6dilated Conv1d layers with kernel sizek=
3and base-3 dilation ratesd i = 3i−1, i.e.,[1,3,9,27,81,243].
Withk=3, base-3 dilation avoids receptive field gaps while
yielding RF= 729frames vs. 127 for base-2. Each layer
applies residual connections with GELU activation; skip con-
nections sum outputs from all layers before the final projection
to(D,T z). The resulting receptive field covers 9.7 s at 75 Hz
(EnCodec)—sufficient to capture multi-syllable sandhi depen-
dencies across prosodic phrases.
2.3. Confidence-Weighted Fusion for Checked Tones
Naively injecting F0-based residuals uniformly risks corrupting
regions where pitch is not the primary tonal cue. Checked tones
in Cantonese, Hokkien, and Vietnamese are characterized by
abrupt glottal closures with minimal voicing [28]—e.g., Can-
tonese tone 1 (high-level) vs. tone 7 (high-entering) differ in
duration and glottal stop rather than F0 contour—so injecting
pitch residuals there corrupts spectral cues carrying tonal dis-
tinction.
To address this, we introduce aConfidence NetworkΨ
that learns per-frame gatingα∈[0,1](Eq. 1) to modulate
residual injection.Ψconsists of three Conv1d layers (kernel
size 3) mapping(D+1,T z)→(1,T z)via hidden dimensions
[64, 32] with GELU and sigmoid output. By conditioning on
zq (spectral context) anduv(voicing state), the network learns
α→1for voiced tonal regions andα→0for unvoiced seg-
ments (including checked tones and consonants) through end-
to-end optimization of the reconstruction objective, without ex-
plicit frame-level supervision.

3.3. Baselines and Implementation
We evaluate PIRA across five frozen pretrained NACs span-
ning diverse architectures: multi-codebook RVQ (EnCodec [2],
DAC [4], Mimi [36]) and single-codebook designs (WavTok-
enizer [37], BigCodec [6]). PIRA’s output projection dimen-
sion is adjusted to match each codec’s latent spaceD(see Ta-
ble 1); all other hyperparameters remain fixed. We compare
against three baselines:Pretrain(frozen codec without adap-
tation),Decoder FT(decoder-only fine-tuning), andFull FT
(joint encoder-decoder fine-tuning), all using identical training
splits. Training uses AdamW (lr10 −3, clip norm 1.0), 200
epochs with early stopping (patience 15), ReduceLROnPlateau,
batch size 64 on a single A100 GPU.1

4.3. Ablation and Efficiency
Table 3 isolates each component on EnCodec–Hokkien—the
configuration with the largest baseline degradation (F0-RMSE
117.9 cents) and the most complex tonology (7–8 tones, multi-
syllable sandhi), making component contributions most visi-
ble. Removing confidence gating (α= 1) causes the largest
VDE degradation (.144→.337), confirming gating is essential
for unvoiced and checked-tone regions, which has the richest
checked-tone inventory (tones 7/8/9). Removing the CREPE
loss (λc = 0) yields the largest F0-RMSE degradation (43.1
→69.7 cents) and GPE increase (.024→.082), confirming
that mel/STFT losses provide insufficient pitch-specific gradi-
ents. Increasing from 4-bit to 8-bit yields modest gains (dTER
.171→.162) at doubled F0 bitrate (0.30→0.60 kbps); 4-bit
achieves a favorable trade-off.
On a single A100 GPU (5 s audio, 50 runs), PIRA adds
only 0.92 ms (4.6% overhead) to the frozen EnCodec pipeline
(19.89 ms). The primary bottleneck is F0 extraction: WORLD
Harvest requires 1406.66 ms (Real-Time Factor (RTF) = 0.28).
However, 4-bit quantization maps F0 to 16 log-spaced bins each
spanning∼260 cents (log-spaced over∼3.46 octaves)—well
above the extraction noise of faster alternatives (<20 cents)—
suggesting lighter-weight extractors may substitute without de-
grading PIRA’s discrete input. With pre-extracted F0 caching,
end-to-end latency reduces to 20.84 ms (RTF = 0.004).
5. Conclusion
We presented PIRA, a plug-and-play adapter that restores tonal
accuracy in frozen neural audio codecs through explicit F0/UV
injection, reducing dTER by 35.7% across three tonal lan-
guages and five codecs with 1.25M–1.65M parameters. Our
findings yield two insights: first,tonal distortion in NACs is an
information-routing failure, not an intrinsic compression limi-
tation—F0 is recoverable from 4 bits per frame, but standard
perceptual losses lack pitch-specific gradients that the CREPE
embedding loss bridges. Second,adaptation and restoration
are complementary: combining PIRA with decoder fine-tuning
reduces dCER while preserving pitch restoration, confirming
orthogonal mechanisms. Current limitations—WORLD Har-
vest latency and F0/UV bitrate overhead—point to substituting
faster extractors, extending to streaming, and integration with
speech language models. Formal perceptual evaluation remains
important future validation.
