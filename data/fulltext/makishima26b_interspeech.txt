Unified Audio-Visual Modeling to Recognize Which Face Spoke When and
What in Scenarios with On- and Off-Screen Participants
Naoki Makishima, Suzuka Yamada, Taiga Yamane, Mana Ihori, Tanaka Tomohiro, Satoshi Suzuki,
Shota Orihashi, Ryo Masumura
NTT, Inc., Japan
naoki.makishima@ntt.com
Abstract
We propose an audio-visual modeling method that recognizes
which face spoke when and what from videos with or without
visible speakers. The conventional method represents informa-
tion about which face spoke when and what was spoken as a
single serialized token sequence and performs autoregressive
prediction. However, it assumes that all speakers are always
visible in the video, whereas in real-world environments, some
speakers may be occluded or outside the camera’s view. To
handle these cases, we introduce a dedicated token to indicate
the absence of a speaker in videos. We compared the proposed
method to the conventional method and the combined system
that separately estimates when and what is spoken, and which
face is speaking. The experimental results demonstrate that the
proposed method outperforms both methods when off-screen
speakers are included, while achieving almost the same perfor-
mance as the dedicated conventional method when all speakers
are on-screen.
Index Terms: speech recognition, audio-visual speech recog-
nition, active speaker detection
1. Introduction
Understanding multi-talker conversations in videos requires the
ability to recognize who is speaking, when they are speaking,
and what they are saying. This task involves not only recogniz-
ing the spoken content but also associating it with the correct in-
dividual if present in the visual scene. In this paper, we refer to
this task as audio-visual speaker-attributed speech recognition
(A VSASR). In multi-talker conversations, such as meetings, it
is common for several people to speak at the same time. As a
result, both audio and video often contain overlapping speech
and multiple faces moving simultaneously, making it challeng-
ing to accurately identify who is speaking and what each per-
son is saying. Research to date typically addresses these chal-
lenges independently: multi-talker audio-visual speech recogni-
tion (A VSR), which estimates what is being said by leveraging
both audio and visual information, and active speaker detection
(ASD), which determines who is speaking at any given moment
by processing audio and visual cues.
In the field of A VSR, while many studies have focused on
single-talker speech [1–5], recent studies have handled the chal-
lenge of multi-talker overlapped speech [6–8]. Most of these
studies on overlapping multi-talker speech utilize visual infor-
mation as a query to isolate the target speaker in a noisy audio
signal, leveraging the visual modality to transcribe the speech of
a specific individual. On the other hand, since multiple videos
corresponding to all the people present in the scene are ob-
tained, we must decide which face is the target speaker before
A VSR; i.e., ASD is required to distinguish the target speaker
from other non-speaking and speaking participants [9]. Re-
cently, several studies have addressed A VSASR, which jointly
conducts A VSR and ASD [10–12]. In [10], an attention layer is
introduced that computes an attention score over multiple face
tracks for each non-overlapping audio frame, to jointly estimate
which face spoke what. In [12], an A VSASR modeling method
was proposed to transcribe each utterance in overlapped speech
segments while simultaneously determining the corresponding
audio and video streams. These studies have succeeded in asso-
ciating transcriptions with the speaker’s video to estimate which
face spoke when and what.
However, a limitation of conventional methods is that they
assume the speaker in the audio is always present in the video.
In the real-world cocktail-party environments, there are cases
where a face is occluded by an obstacle [13–15] or where speak-
ers not visible on camera are talking with each other [16]. Fur-
thermore, due to privacy concerns, such as in business meet-
ings and in sales meetings with customers, it is often difficult
to obtain video of all the speakers, making it impractical to al-
ways have visual information for every participant in the au-
dio [17]. To address such situations, it is important to estimate
which speech segments in the overlapping speech correspond to
hidden speakers and determine which audio and video streams
can be associated. Although some studies on ASD and audio-
visual speaker diarization [18–20] have explored the off-screen
speakers by utilizing audio-visual synchronization [21], speaker
identity information [22], and i-vectors [23], to the best of our
knowledge, no prior research has investigated the A VSASR
modeling under these conditions.
In this paper, we propose a multi-talker A VSASR model-
ing method for overlapped speech with on- and off-screen par-
ticipants. The proposed method extends the modeling method
proposed in [12]. In [12], to associate who is speaking when,
and what is being said, video tokens are introduced; they are as-
signed to each transcript generated from the overlapped speech.
In this paper, we add a special video token to associate utter-
ances with off-screen speakers. This token is used when the
speaker corresponding to the audio is absent from the video,
thereby preventing forced, and thus erroneous, associations
with any candidate speaker in the video. Furthermore, in exper-
iments, the simulated training and evaluation datasets are ex-
panded from those of [12] to include on- and off-screen partici-
pants. We evaluate the proposed method in two settings: speech
containing only on-screen speakers and speech containing off-
screen speakers. For the off-screen setting, we build a baseline
that separately estimates when the utterance is spoken and when
each lip is moving to determine audio-visual association. The
experiments show that the proposed method outperforms the
conventional methods on metrics evaluating which face spoke
when and which face spoke what.

3. Proposed method
3.1. Strategy
The conventional methods assume that the target speakers are
included in the video; i.e.,V 1:I always includes the speaker
of the overlapped speechX. As discussed in Section 1, this
assumption is invalid in real-world scenarios. To address this
problem, we propose a multi-talker A VSASR for overlapped
speech with on- and off-screen participants. Figure 1 overviews
the proposed method. The proposed method introduces a spe-
cial token[None]representing that the speaker corresponding
to the transcript does not appear in the video. While conven-
tional methods can only handle cases where all speakers appear
in the video as shown in Fig. 1 (a), our proposed token enables
the model to be trained for various cases, such as when some
speakers are absent as shown in Fig. 1(b) or all speakers are
absent as shown in Fig. 1 (c).

4. Experiments
4.1. Dataset
We used the Lip Reading Sentences 3 (LRS3) dataset [28] for
our experiments. Although the sentences of LRS3 in the train-
val and test sets are clipped to 100 characters or 6 seconds, the
pre-train set includes longer videos, which leads to a gap be-
tween the training set and the others. Thus, we mixed the orig-
inal pre-train, train-val, and test sets and split them into new
pre-train, train-val, and test sets so that speakers did not overlap
across datasets, and the train-val and test sets included longer
audio as [12]. Since LRS3 consists of single-talker videos, we
created two-speaker and three-speaker simulated mixtures by
mixing the utterances from different speakers. When mixing the
audio signals, the original volume of each utterance was kept
unchanged, resulting in an average signal-to-interference ratio
of about 0 dB. The delay values applied to each utterance were
randomly chosen under the constraints presented in [26]. Since
the length of the created overlapped speech exceeds that of each
speaker’s video, we copied the nearest frame of the video to fill
the gap. For the memory constraint, we only used one in every
five frames, which resulted in 5 fps videos. We clipped96×96
regions around the mouth and converted them to gray scale. We
added Gaussian noise with a standard deviation of 0.05 to every
frame of the video. We used 80 log mel-scale filterbank coeffi-
cients as acoustic features, extracted using a 20-ms-long Hann
window with a 10-ms-long shift. We used 8K sentence pieces
as textual tokens.
4.2. Implementation
For the video encoder, we used MobileNetV3-Small [29]. The
operation of the speech encoder is described below. First, the
acoustic feature was passed to layers composed of two1×1
convolutions with1×1strides, two max pooling with a stride of
2, two3×3depthwise convolutions with1×1strides, and two
long-short term memory layers with outputs of 256 dimensions.
Next, we stacked eight Transformer encoder blocks, where the
number of heads in the multi-head attention was set to 4, the
dimensions of the output continuous representations were set
to 256, and the dimensions of the inner output in the position-
wise feed-forward networks were set to 1,024. The dimension
of the embedding in Eq. (7) was 256. We used a Transformer-
based model [30, 31] in this paper. For the Transformer en-
coder in Eq. (9) and the Transformer decoder in Eq. (10), we
stacked two-layer Transformer encoder blocks and two-layer
Transformer decoder blocks, respectively, where the settings
were the same as those of the speech encoder. For the activation
function, we used Swish [32].
4.3. Settings
We compared the following four methods: conventional multi-
talker ASR, conventional multi-talker A VSASR , a combined
system of conventional multi-talker A VSR and lip moving de-
tection (LMD), and the proposed method. For fair compari-
son, all models had the same architecture. The conventional
multi-talker A VSASR is based on [12], which estimates the se-
rialized transcriptions of multiple speakers, timestamp tokens,
and video tokens. Since[None]token is not used in multi-
talker A VSASR, it cannot handle the video token of the off-
screen speakers; thus this model was trained only with videos
of visible speaking and non-speaking participants. The conven-
tional multi-talker A VSR estimates the serialized transcriptions
of multiple speakers and timestamp tokens; i.e., the video token
uk is removed and the target token is given as (1). Since video
tokens are not estimated, the conventional multi-talker A VSR
can utilize videos containing off-screen speakers for training
in the same way as the proposed method. During training,
both multi-talker A VSR and the proposed method were assigned
videos following (13), where all patterns were set to be equally
probable and we setI= 3for allk.
As a naive approach to estimate which face is speaking
or none of the speakers in the videos are speaking, utilizing

utilizing a LMD model. The LMD model, which consists of
the video encoder described in Section 4.2, four Transformer-

defined as: the number of frames predicted as 1 by the LMD

videos and assigned the utterance to the video with the high-

it to an off-screen speaker. The models were pretrained follow-
ing [12]. Multi-talker A VSR and the proposed method were first
trained without off-screen speakers, and then trained with off-
screen speakers added, using the obtained parameters as initial
values. All models were optimized using RAdam [33].
We used the word error rate (WER), visual word error rate
(VWER), and visual time error rate (VTER) [12] to evaluate
the overall performance of the methods. In contrast to the orig-
inal WER that considers only what is spoken, VWER is a cri-
terion that evaluates the prediction performance of which face
spoke what. In VWER, every video has a transcription label,
and the predicted video token’s transcription becomes the label
for evaluating WER. In other words, when the predicted video
token is not correct, the predicted transcriptions are counted as
errors even when they match the transcriptions of another video.
VTER is a criterion that evaluates the prediction performance of
which face spoke when. In VTER, every video has an utterance-
level timestamp label, and the predicted video token’s times-
tamp label is used to calculate TER. TER was calculated as the
sum of missed speaker rate and false alarm rate [24]. When
comparing hypothesized boundaries to references, we used a
tolerance of±250ms. When calculating the original WER, we
compared hypotheses with references while accounting for ut-
terance order. We only evaluated textual tokens excluding the
special tokenO, the timestamp tokenT, and the video tokenU
when calculating WER and VWER.
4.4. Results
Table 1 shows the result. Note that WER is relatively high com-
pared to other studies using LRS3 because we used a differ-
ent test set that includes longer audio. Since the conventional
multi-talker A VSASR cannot handle off-screen speakers, we
showed its performance on off-screen speakers for reference.
We can see that the proposed method outperforms conventional
methods both in VWER and VTER when off-screen speakers
are included, while achieving almost the same performance as
the dedicated multi-talker A VSASR model when all speakers
are on-screen. Since the conventional multi-talker A VSASR as-
sumes that the target speakers are always included in the video,
its WER increases when off-screen speakers are included, be-
cause the model erroneously associates the speech of off-screen
speakers with visible participants. On the other hand, the pro-
posed method alleviates its increase, demonstrating that the pro-
posed token prevents those associations.
Since the difference between WER and VWER arises from
errors in speaker association, we can see that speaker asso-
ciation becomes more difficult when off-screen speakers are
included and the speech is composed of multiple speakers in
the proposed method. A possible reason is as follows. When
all speakers are on-screen, the model can simply associate the
video with the speech in the order of lip movement onset. On
the other hand, when the off-screen speakers are included, this
approach is invalid because the number of moving lips does not
necessarily match the number of utterances, and the model has
to estimate the speakers based on audio-visual synchronization,
making it challenging and resulting in a larger difference be-
tween WER and VWER. Appropriate modeling to address this
audio-visual synchronization remains as future work.
5. Conclusions
In this paper, we proposed an audio-visual modeling method
to recognize which face spoke when and what was said for
overlapped speech with on- and off-screen participants. Al-
though the conventional A VSASR method enables the predic-
tion of which face spoke when and what with a single model,
it assumes that the speakers are always present in the video.
This assumption does not hold in real-world environments, as
there are cases where some speakers are outside the camera’s
view. To address this limitation, we introduced a video to-
ken to associate utterances with off-screen speakers, which pre-
vents forced, and thus erroneous, associations with any candi-
date speaker in the video. We expanded the simulated training
and evaluation datasets from the conventional study to cover
real-world scenarios, which includes on- and off-screen partic-
ipants. Experiments showed that the proposed method outper-
forms conventional methods in both VWER and VTER when
off-screen speakers are included, while achieving almost the
