Abstract
We introduce phonological process inference, a structured
prediction task that explains deviations in child speech by
jointly recovering canonical phone sequences and phonological
process tags. The initial stage pretrains on phone recognition
without text conditioning. In the second stage, we fuse embed-
dings of an expected phone sequence with downsampled audio
tokens and train a single decoder to output canonical phones
with explicit process tags. A rule-based augmentation pipeline
injects process supervision at scale without manual labeling and
supports multilingual training by swapping language-specific
grapheme-to-phone (G2P) mappings. Experiments across mul-
tilingual child and clinical corpora show that joint modeling
improves tagging accuracy and reduces phone error rates com-
pared to ASR-based baselines, while producing interpretable
phone-level diagnostics suitable for screening and Computer-
Assisted Pronunciation Training (CAPT).
Index Terms: phone recognition, pronunciation assessment
1. Introduction
Language development is fundamental to social and academic
success, yet clinical assessment of children’s speech remains
time-intensive and difficult to scale. Screening typically re-
quires clinicians to listen, transcribe, and label phonological
patterns manually. Automatic speech recognition can produce
transcripts, but transcription alone does not provide phone-level
diagnostic feedback. We therefore represent both canonical pro-
nunciation and acoustic evidence as token sequences and train
a compact LLM to perform structured prediction over these to-
kens. The model consumes a fused stream of audio and ex-
pected pronunciation and predicts canonical phones augmented
with explicit process tags (e.g., fronting, backing, deletion).
Our work builds on several strands of prior research. First,
pronunciation assessment and CAPT have been widely stud-
ied, with surveys emphasizing phone-level feedback, robust-
ness, and interpretability as key challenges [1, 2, 3, 4]. Re-
lated work has explored articulatory one-class SVMs for low-
resource phoneme-level verification in disordered child speech
[5] and case-based reasoning for phonological-process predic-
tion in speech-therapy support [6]. Second, mispronuncia-
tion detection and diagnosis (MDD) systems have shifted from
Goodness of Pronunciation (GoP)-style pipelines toward end-
to-end sequence models that jointly localize and classify pro-
nunciation errors [7, 8, 9, 10]. Prompt-aware architectures
further improve non-autoregressive MDD by conditioning pre-
dictions on target pronunciation context [11]. Third, multi-
lingual and cross-lingual modeling emerged for low-resource
pronunciation assessment, where self-supervised and language-
adaptive encoders improve transfer across accents and lan-
guages [12]. Fourth, speech-LLM approaches show that large
language models can fuse acoustic evidence with linguistic pri-
ors for MDD [13], but most systems focus on L2 learner speech
and sentence-level scoring rather than explicit phonological-
process tagging. In parallel, multilingual G2P remains impor-
tant for scalable phone supervision across languages [14, 15].
This work makes three contributions: (i) We introduce
the task of phonological process inference, where a model
jointly predicts canonical phone sequences and phonologi-
cal process tags from speech. (ii) We propose PhonLLM, a
speech–language architecture that fuses acoustic evidence with
expected pronunciations to perform this structured prediction
task. (iii) We introduce a scalable rule-based augmentation
pipeline that injects phonological process supervision without
manual annotation. Unlike conventional MDD systems that
classify errors independently, our formulation models phono-
logical processes as transformations between expected and re-
alized pronunciations. PhonLLM jointly infers the target repre-
sentation and underlying processes.

3. Methodology
3.1. Phonological Process Inference
We define phonological process inference as follows: Given an
acoustic signala 1:T and an expected phone sequencex 1:N de-
rived from orthography, the goal is to predict a canonical phone
sequence augmented with phonological process tags that ex-
plain deviations between expected and realized speech. The
goal is to model the conditional distribution
p(y|a 1:T,x 1:N ),(1)
wherea 1:T denotes the acoustic signal,x 1:N the expected
phone sequence derived from orthography, andythe output se-
quence containing canonical phones and optional phonological
process tags such as fronting, backing, or deletion.
3.2. Architecture
Figure 1 illustrates the proposed setup. Orthography is con-
verted to phone sequences and fused with downsampled au-
dio embeddings. A compact LLM decoder predicts canonical
phones augmented with phonological process tags. We use a
two-stage training pipeline combining an audio encoder with
the LLM. Latent audio representations from wav2vec 2.0 [33]
(OmniASR [34], 300M) are projected to the LLM input space
and concatenated with phone embeddings before decoding with
LLaMA [35] (1B). The first stage pretrains cross-lingual phone
recognition to learn robust phone-level alignments. The sec-
ond stage trains on child speech with phonological process su-
pervision, jointly predicting canonical phones and process tags
(fronting, backing, deletion). The expected phone sequence is
used only in this stage. Phonological processes are modeled as
deviations between expected pronunciations derived from or-
thography and realized acoustic speech.
Formally, in the second stage, we condition on audio frames
a1:T and an expected phone sequencex 1:N . Conditioning on
the expected phone sequence provides linguistic context anal-
ogous to target prompts used in pronunciation assessment sys-
tems, enabling the model to detect deviations relative to the in-
tended pronunciation. The text pathway maps phone tokens to
embeddings asg 1:N =f text(x1:N ) 2 . In parallel, the audio
pathway concatenates everyrconsecutive frame embeddings
(withr= 5in our experiments) and projects them to the LLM
embedding space, i.e.,e 1:L =f audio(a1:T ) 3 . We concate-
nate the text and audio token streams and project them to a
shared space,h 1:L =W[g 1:L;e 1:L]. An autoregressive de-
coderf LLM 4 then predicts a target sequencey 1:L of canon-
ical phones with process tags. Training minimizes next-token
cross-entropy,L=− PL
l=1 logpθ(yl |y <l,h 1:L), with the
first stage usingy=phones and the second stage usingy=
phone+process tags. The model therefore performs structured
phonological inference, jointly recovering the canonical phone
sequence and the phonological process that explains deviations
in the observed speech signal.
3.3. Data Augmentation for Phonological Process Detection
Data augmentation 1 visualized in Figure 1 is a central
contribution that injects explicit phonological process super-
vision without requiring manual tagging. Canonical targets
are represented as simplified IPA phone sequences obtained
from grapheme-to-phoneme conversion from the orthographic
transcription using eSpeak 1, with suprasegmental diacritics re-
moved and phones collapsed to a reduced inventory for cross-
lingual consistency.
After the conversion, we apply process-specific rewrite
rules to create a pairedexpectedsequence that reflects a realis-
tic child-speech deviation while preserving the canonical target.
During training, we simulate child-speech deviations by mod-
ifying the expected sequence. At inference time, the expected
sequence corresponds to the canonical pronunciation the child
is asked to produce. The model is trained to output the canon-
ical phone stream augmented with a process tag at the affected
location, enabling it to learn alignment between expected and
realized speech, correction, and localization.
We define IPA-level mapping tables for velar fronting, coro-
nal backing, and deletion. For each word, we sample a pro-
cess type (orrecognition onlywithout the expected sequence)
according to the probabilities in Section 4.2, identify eligible

3.4. Metrics
We report metrics for both, process tagging and phone recogni-
tion. For tagging, we compute precision, recall, and F1 at the
process-label level, emphasizing recall since missed processes
provide no corrective feedback. We also report chance-level F1
based on class prevalence.
For phone recognition, we report Phone Error Rate (PER)
and Articulatory Weighted PER (AW-PER) which was ex-
plored in similar ways in various studies [36, 37, 38]. PER
counts substitutions, deletions, and insertions with unit cost,
while AW-PER weights substitutions by articulatory distance
norm(reference,hypothesis)∈[0,1]using PanPhon [39].
This reflects the clinical severity of near-neighbor confusions.

4.1. Baselines
We use three baseline settings for process tagging, all trained

4.2. Proposed PhonLLM
To enable efficient adaptation, we keep the audio encoder and
LLM weights frozen and train only low-rank adaptation (LoRA)
[41] layers (r=16,α=32) together with the audio projection
module. This significantly reduces the number of trainable
parameters while preserving the linguistic knowledge encoded
in the pretrained LLM. The model is trained in two stages.
First, we train for 10 epochs on cross-lingual phone recogni-
tion using next-token cross-entropy over canonical phone tar-
gets to learn robust acoustic–phonetic mappings. Second, we
fine-tune for 10 epochs on child speech with phonological pro-
cess supervision, where the target sequence interleaves canoni-
cal phones with process tags. The number of epochs was se-
lected based on validation convergence. To balance process
frequencies, augmentation rules are applied with probabilities
p(fronting) = 0.30,p(backing) = 0.60,p(deletion) = 0.12,
andp(recognition only) = 0.30. These values were set empir-
ically to compensate for differences in the number of eligible
phones per process: backing applies to a smaller set of targets
than fronting, deletion is applicable to any phone, requiring a
lower sampling rate. Together they yield approximately bal-
anced augmented example counts per class.
5. Results
5.1. Baselines
Table 2 compares three baselines averaged over all datasets and
processes. The in-context LLM baseline (B1) shows moderate
precision (43.8) with higher recall (65.1), yielding F1 50.2 and
indicating a bias toward over-predicting tags. Fine-tuning the
LLM (B2) substantially improves both precision and recall (to
94.3/85.1), producing the best overall F1 (89.0). This represents
an oracle upper bound, as B2 operates on perfect reference tran-
scripts rather than raw speech – a condition unavailable at infer-
ence time. PhonLLM closes most of this gap while operating
directly on acoustics, without access to ground-truth transcrip-
tions. The audio+text pipeline (B3) degrades tagging perfor-
mance (F1 48.5) relative to B2, consistent with error propaga-
tion from the XLSR-53 transcription stage. The XLSR-53 tran-
scription performance is listed in Table 3. The PER/AW-PER
(58.0/26.2) provides the phone-recognition baseline for down-
stream comparisons. Chance F1 is low (19.2), confirming that
improvements reflect learned tagging rather than class imbal-
ance. This highlights the advantage of direct speech-to-process
modeling compared to cascaded ASR pipelines.
5.2. Joint Phone Recognition and Process Tagging
Table 3 reports phonological process tagging and phone recog-
nition across datasets and languages. Overall, our setup
achieves strong tagging performance with an average F1 75.9,
well above the average chance F1 of 19.2. The corresponding
phone recognition errors remain substantially lower than the
XLSR-53 baseline (PhonLLM PER/AW-PER 23.5/12.6 com-
pared to XLSR-53 58.0/26.2). This suggests that joint process
tagging improves acoustic–phonetic alignment.
Process-Level TrendsFronting shows the highest and
most consistent performance, with F1 values in the 80s range
and the best scores on M ´asd´ottir (F1 90.1) and Cummings (F1
88.5). Backing is more challenging: German datasets show
moderate F1 (around 60), while English Cummings reaches
86.2, suggesting dataset-specific distributional effects and label
frequency differences. Deletion is consistently harder, with F1
between 66.6 and 74.9, reflecting the difficulty of distinguishing
deletions from alignment and coarticulation effects.
Dataset and Language EffectsAll German datasets tend
to yield slightly lower recall than English or Icelandic in sev-
eral processes, which reflects smaller per-process sample sizes.
English datasets show strong fronting and backing performance
but remain less robust on deletion. Icelandic M ´asd´ottir consis-
tently achieves high precision and recall for fronting and com-
petitive results for backing and deletion.

expected phone sequencex 1:N is omitted, the model predicts

This indicates that phonological process tagging is not learned
as an unconditional behavior from acoustics alone, but is trig-

expected pronunciations provide essential linguistic context for
structured phonological inference.
6. Analysis
6.1. Beyond Phone Error Rate
We analyze phone substitutions using an AW-PER confusion
map that aggregates articulatory distances across aligned sub-
stitutions. Figure 2 shows that most errors cluster among ar-
ticulatorily similar phones (e.g., place or voicing shifts), while
distant confusions are rare. The most frequent substitutions are
[i]↔[n]and[N]↔[D], highlighting systematic child-speech
error patterns beyond aggregate PER.
6.2. Age-Conditioned Performance
We stratify the M ´asd´ottir dataset into age cohorts (2–7 years)
to analyze developmental effects. Figure 3 shows a mono-
tonic decline in AW-PER from 18.83 (2y) to 9.54 (7y), indi-
cating improved phone recognition with age. Tagging preci-
sion remains high (78.25–85.19) while recall increases slightly
(68.22–72.16), yielding stable F1 around 74 with a peak of
78.13 (7y). Variance across cohorts likely reflects imbalanced
sample sizes (e.g.,≈12.1k reference phones at 3y vs.≈1.4k at
7y). Overall, phone recognition improves with age more than
process tagging, suggesting the need for age-balanced training
or targeted augmentation.

7. Conclusion
We introduced phonological process inference and demon-
strated that a lightweight speech–LLM architecture can jointly
recover canonical pronunciations and phonological process tags
from child speech. By fusing expected phone embeddings with
downsampled audio tokens and supervising with rule-based
process tags, the model produces interpretable phone-level di-
agnostics while maintaining strong recognition accuracy across
multilingual child and clinical corpora. The results indicate that
joint modeling improves both tagging performance and phone
error rates relative to ASR-based baselines, supporting its use
in screening and CAPT settings. Future work will extend the
process inventory to include common developmental processes
such as deaffrication, cluster reduction and stopping.
