Abstract
ASR bias research is largely English-focused and the mecha-
nisms underlying performance disparities remain poorly under-
stood. We address both gaps for Spanish and French. Using a
20-hour, gender-balanced corpus (the first for these languages
designed for dialect bias analysis, with country-level strati-
fication and manual transcripts preserving dialectal features),
we benchmark seven models and find non-uniform dispari-
ties: models favor European varieties in French, while Span-
ish reveals unexpected patterns with Dominican outperforming
Peninsular and with Chilean showing the highest error rates.
Lexical analysis links failures to morphosyntactic ”correction”
and regional discourse markers; acoustic analysis shows that
speaker embeddings encode dialect information; a diagnostic
fine-tuning experiment localizes these errors to the decoder. To-
gether, these results demonstrate that ASR errors systematically
reflect linguistic distance from training distributions.
Index Terms: speech recognition, dialectal bias, sociolinguistic
diversity
1. Introduction
Recent progress in Automatic Speech Recognition (ASR),
driven by Deep Learning and the transformer architecture from
NLP [1, 2], has made it a backbone of systems from virtual
assistants to multimodal large language models. As these tech-
nologies are deployed in critical domains (medical transcrip-
tions, legal proceedings, public services), their equitable perfor-
mance is a societal necessity [3]. Yet these models still harbor
systematic biases that reflect imbalances and limitations in their
training data [4, 5]. A very prominent manifestation isdialect
bias, where ASR performs significantly worse on non-standard
or low-resource dialects [6, 7]. African-American Vernacular
English (AA VE) is a stark example where even state-of-the-art
models transcribe it poorly, which can result in real-world con-
sequences [8, 9].
This paper extends efforts to combat such imbalances by
addressing two gaps in the existing research. First, prior work
has been quite English-centric, leaving global languages with
rich sociolinguistic diversity, like Spanish and French, under-
explored. Second, the explanations behind these disparities are
often unclear [10]. We perform an integrated analysis of ASR
bias for Spanish and French that links performance benchmarks
to linguistic root causes. We make two primary contributions:
1.Evaluation Corpus and Benchmarking:We develop a
pipeline for constructing dialect-balanced ASR evaluation
corpora and use it to build what is, to our knowledge, the
first manually transcribed evaluation corpus for Spanish and

demonstrate that ASR errors are systematically linked to the lin-

for Spanish and French ASR and a replicable framework for
analyzing dialectal bias in any language.
2. Related Work
Research has confirmed significant ASR performance gaps by
dialect, gender, and race, most notably the WER disparity be-
tween Black and White US speakers [6]. Yet efforts to build
balanced evaluation corpora remain English-centric and con-
centrated in the Global North [11, 12, 13]. Separately, probing
studies have shown that self-supervised models encode dialect-
relevant acoustic cues [14], but this work has progressed in iso-
lation from bias evaluation; it remains unclear how representa-
tional distances translate into transcription failures.
Mitigation strategies exist, including synthetic accent aug-
mentation [15, 16] and geographical routing [17, 18], but these
require either curated data or specialized model infrastructure.
Absent from this landscape is an integrated framework that ex-
tends evaluation beyond English to typologically diverse lan-
guages and connects observed performance gaps to their lin-
guistic root causes [19].
Multilingual resources such as Common V oice [20] offer
broad dialect coverage but elicit read speech from standard-
ized text prompts (typically sourced from Wikipedia), mean-
ing dialectal morphosyntax and regional discourse markers are
largely absent from reference transcripts by design. Recent
work has begun addressing this for German [21], though Span-

3. Data Collection Pipeline
A primary limitation in non-English dialect research is the lack
of high-quality, balanced evaluation corpora. To address this,
we developed a pipeline for constructing dialect-balanced ASR
datasets from podcast audio (Figure 1).
Stage 1: Podcast Discovery.We identified target dialect
regions for each language: Spain, Mexico, Argentina, Chile,
and the Dominican Republic for Spanish; France, Belgium,
Canada, Senegal, and Ivory Coast for French. These dialects
were selected to maximize phonological and lexical distinc-
tiveness while spanning major continental varieties and captur-
ing both high-resource (European) and lower-resource (African,
Caribbean) dialects. For each region, we searched Apple Pod-
casts for shows with hosts from that region, prioritizing inter-
view and conversational formats to maximize natural speech.
We note that podcast speech, while capturing naturalistic con-
versation, may not represent all registers or speaking contexts.
Stage 2: Filtering.Episodes were filtered to include only
those with good audio quality and minimal background music.
Stage 3: Preprocessing.Episodes were segmented into
5–30 s clips following standard ASR benchmarking protocols;
per-dialect mean durations span a narrow range (Spanish 15.6–
21.1 s; French 16.9–20.8 s) and do not track WER. Segments
were filtered for single-speaker content free of crosstalk or arti-
facts. Speaker metadata (gender, regional origin) was annotated
from podcast descriptions and verified by the authors, with a
minimum of three speakers per gender-dialect cell to mitigate
idiosyncratic speaker effects.
Stage 4: Transcription.The authors, fluent in the tar-
get languages, manually transcribed audio segments, preserv-
ing regional orthography and dialectal markers (e.g.,voseoverb
forms in Spanish) to ensure WER reflects model performance
on authentic dialectal speech rather than normalization mis-
match.
The resulting corpus (Table 1) comprises 1,223 minutes of
speech from 129 speakers across 10 dialects, balanced for gen-
der. Each dialect contains approximately 120 minutes of audio
with at least three speakers per gender-dialect cell.
Ethical considerations.All audio derives from publicly
distributed podcasts intended for broad consumption, follow-
ing established practice for constructing speech corpora from
publicly available recordings [22]. To respect redistribution
constraints, the corpus is released through a gated repository
with request-based access (https://doi.org/10.5281/
zenodo.20575155).
4. ASR Performance Benchmarks
We evaluated seven state-of-the-art ASR models on our corpus,
reporting Word Error Rate (WER) across all ten dialects (Ta-
ble 2). Whisper v3 achieved the lowest WER on most dialects,
though Whisper v2 slightly outperformed on Dominican Span-
ish and on the female subsets of French-France and Canada.
Dialect-Specific Disparities.The results challenge the hy-
pothesis that population size predicts performance. Dialects
from smaller populations like the Dominican Republic signif-
icantly outperformed “canonical” varieties like Mexican and
Peninsular Spanish, suggesting training data quality and expo-
sure to regional variation may matter more than raw speaker
counts [23]. A Kruskal-Wallis H test on segment-level WERs
confirmed significant performance variance across dialects for
both Spanish (H= 179.24,p < .001) and French (H=
63.18,p < .001). Post-hoc pairwise comparisons (Dunn’s
test, Bonferroni-corrected) revealed three significantly distinct
clusters for Spanish: Argentina and Dominican Republic (low-
est WER), Mexico and Spain (intermediate), and Chile (high-
est). For French, European varieties (France, Belgium) formed
a single cluster that significantly outperformed Canadian and
African varieties, which did not differ. Chilean Spanish exhib-
ited the highest error rates, a pattern we trace to its distinctive
acoustic and lexical profile in Section 5. French showed clear
continental bias: European dialects (France, Belgium) consis-
tently outperformed African and Canadian varieties, the largest
gap being between male speakers from France and the Ivory
Coast.
Gender-Specific Disparities.Gender gaps vary strongly
by dialect (Mann-Whitney U for within-dialect gender compar-
isons). In Spanish, male speakers exhibited significantly higher
WERs in Argentina, Chile, and Mexico (allp<.001), whereas
for the Dominican Republic, females had a higher WER (p=
.0025). In French, gender bias was most prominent in African
dialects: Senegalese and Ivorian French showed highly signif-
icant disparities (bothp < .001), with males exhibiting higher
error rates. European and Canadian French showed minimal
gender gaps (p>.05). This supports the argument that gender
bias in ASR is regionally conditioned rather than universal.

To explain the performance gaps documented above, we con-
duct lexical and acoustic analyses on Whisper v3 outputs.

5.1. Lexical Analysis: JSD Word Shifts
We employ JSD-based word shifts [24] to quantify how specific
tokens contribute to divergence between correctly transcribed
speech (PS) and the error corpus (P E), which is composed of
tokens involved in substitutions, deletions, and insertions. JSD
is defined as:
JSD(PE||PS) = 1

2 KLD(PS||M)(1)
whereP E andP S are token-level probability distributions and
M= 1
2 (PE +P S)is their mixture (mean) distribution. We
calculate each word’s contribution to total divergence for ev-
ery dialect-gender split, isolating terms disproportionately rep-
resented in ASR failures.
Spanish Findings.Manual inspection of top-ranked error
contributors revealed three primary patterns. First,morphosyn-
tactic correction: Chileanvoseoconjugations (pod ´ıs,ten ´ıs) ex-
hibit near-total failure rates (∼40 observed cases). Notably,
Whisper frequently substitutes the Rioplatense variantten ´esfor
Chileanten ´ıs, making it the #2 error contributor for Chilean
female speakers; the Peninsularsab ´eisappears at #6. This pat-
tern suggests “forced convergence”: the model detects devia-
tion from standardtuteobut defaults to higher-resource variants
rather than producing the intended Chilean form. Decoder prob-
ability analysis of Chileanvoseofailure instances confirms this
pattern: the correct suffix typically receives≤1%probability
while higher-resource alternatives dominate. In Argentina, the
Rioplatense formsten ´esandpod ´esremain top error contribu-
tors (ranks 16 and 13 for females) because the model “corrects”
to standard Spanish (tienes/puedes).
Second,regional vernacular: the model consistently fails
on regionalisms. Chilean errors are dominated byChilenismos
likecachai(#3 M),huevon(#6 M), andweon(#19 M). Domini-
can errors reflect Caribbean code-switching patterns and Angli-
cisms (coaching,net), characteristic of the region’s high lexical
borrowing [25].
Third,pragmatic markers: the fillerpuesis the #1 error in
Spain and #2 in Mexico for both genders, yet entirely absent
from Argentine and Dominican error lists, where speakers pre-
ferbueno[26]. This “pues divide” suggests that dialect-specific
discourse marker density systematically triggers segmentation
or language-modeling failures—mainly deletion-type errors.
French Findings.Three parallel patterns emerge. First,
discourse marker divergence: Canadian French exhibits the
clearest case of dialect-specific failure. The markerspuis,ben,
andsaisrank as the top three error contributors for male speak-
ers, withsaisandpuissimilarly dominating the female list.
These are foundational to Qu ´eb´ecois speech—particularlytu
sais(“you know”)—but appear at much lower frequencies in
European French. The model frequently deletes these tokens
entirely, directly inflating Canadian WER.
Second,vocabulary gaps and hallucinations: African
French varieties trigger a distinct pattern: when encountering
unfamiliar items, the model generates phonetically plausible
but nonexistent words. In Ivory Coast French,antelopebe-
comessolantilopeorlentilope; the surnamebinamais rendered
asmorbina; the namemvomosurfaces asmboma. For Sene-
galese French, regional terms likedaradjiandrapodjotrigger
similar hallucinations. These vocabularies, shaped by contact
with indigenous languages, fall largely outside model coverage.
Third,register mismatch: standard written French requires
the negative particlene(je ne sais pas), but spoken French fre-
quently elides it (j’sais pas) [27]. This creates systematic er-
rors across all dialects, most acute in France wherenandne
rank as top error contributors for both genders. This pattern
also appears elsewhere—Canada (#11/#12 M, #4/#6 F), Bel-
gium (#2/#3 F), Senegal (#6 F, #8 M). This suggests the lan-
guage model is biased toward formal written register.
5.2. Acoustic Analysis
The lexical analysis leaves some variance unexplained. Belgian
French achieves the lowest average WER (3.89%), showing no
systematic lexical error patterns—its errors are dominated by
nouns (pommeraie) or proper nouns (Courgault) rather than di-
alectal features. This suggests lexical divergence alone does not
fully account for performance variation [28]. We investigate
whether acoustic properties captured in speaker embeddings
encode dialect-distinguishing information, using two sources:
pyannote/embedding, a speaker embedding model inde-
pendent of Whisper, and Whisper’s Layer 16 encoder represen-
tations, where prior work on self-supervised speech models sug-
gests phonetic information is richest in the middle layers [29].
This approach parallels recent work showing that accent
embeddings capture dialect-distinguishing information useful

5.2.1. Experimental Setup
We conduct four analyses: (1)dialect classificationvia Logis-
tic Regression with Leave-One-Speaker-Out Cross-Validation
(LOSO-CV) on speaker embeddings; (2)global divergence,
measuring each speaker’s centroid cosine distance from the
global mean across all speakers in that language and correlating
with dialect-gender level WER (Pearson) to test whether acous-
tic distance predicts transcription accuracy; (3)anchor diver-
gence, measuring cosine distance from the highest-performing
demographic subset (Argentine Female for Spanish; Belgian
Male for French) and correlating with dialect-gender level WER
(Pearson); and (4)outlier analysisvia Cook’s Distance, flag-
ging speakers exceeding the4/Nthreshold.
5.2.2. Experiment Results
Both embedding sources reliably classify speakers by dialect-
gender cell (Spanish: 83–86%; French: 67–77%), well above
the 10% chance baseline, confirming that embeddings encode
dialect-distinguishing acoustic information. Acoustic distance
from the global mean correlates with dialect-gender level WER,
as does distance from the highest-performing variety (Table 3).
Both pyannote and Whisper’s encoder yield parallel patterns,
suggesting that the signal reflects genuine acoustic variation
rather than model-specific artifacts. Cook’s Distance confirms
these patterns are robust, not driven by outliers.
These correlations operate primarily at the dialect-gender
level: groups acoustically distant from the centroid exhibit
higher aggregate WER. Intra-dialect analyses were limited by
sample size (Spanish:n≥3per cell; French:n≥7per
cell) and showed inconsistent patterns, precluding strong claims
about speaker-level effects. We interpret these patterns as con-
verging evidence—not causal confirmation—that the acoustic
dimensions along which dialects diverge relate to the dimen-
sions along which WER varies.

that fine-tuning recovered specific features the baseline had sys-
tematically failed on: the Chilean particlepo, Canadian French
markerspuisandtu sais, and the French negative particlene
were correctly transcribed post-adaptation. However, perfor-
mance gaps across dialects persist even after fine-tuning, indi-
cating that LoRA adaptation on data of this scale is insufficient
to eliminate dialectal bias. More aggressive interventions, such
as dialectal pretraining or architectural modifications, are left
for future work.
6. Conclusion
We presented a mixed-methods analysis of dialectal ASR bias
for Spanish and French, benchmarking seven models on a 20-
hour, gender-balanced corpus across 10 dialects. Errors track
linguistic and acoustic distance from high-performing varieties:
lexical analysis tied them to specific features (voseocorrection,
nedeletion), acoustic analysis showed speaker embeddings en-
code the dimensions along which dialects vary, and a diag-
nostic fine-tuning experiment localized these errors to the de-
coder. Equitable ASR thus requires modeling the linguistic fea-
tures of dialectal variation; our corpus, pipeline, and diagnostic
framework offer a foundation for more robust models for under-
resourced varieties.
