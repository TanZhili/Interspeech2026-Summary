Abstract
Automated music captioning remains difficult for narrative-rich
works (e.g., opera) where instrumentation, affect, and struc-
ture evolve over time. Existing LLM-based captioners depend
on scarce paired audio–text data, and fine-tuning can overfit
to dataset-specific phrasing, limiting transfer and controllable
narration. We propose Music Artistic Captioning (MAC), a
training-free framework that generates long-form, grounded de-
scriptions by conditioning an instruction-following LLM on au-
tomatically extracted multi-scale audio evidence. MAC aggre-
gates frame-, segment-, and song-level descriptors, from low-
level acoustics (e.g., tempo) to predicted high-level semantic
cues (e.g., instrumentation), into constrained pseudo-evidence
for hierarchical captioning. To support user-preferred style,
MAC uses a one-time iterative prompt optimization to balance
narrative voice and evidence faithfulness. Experiments on an
opera corpus and caption benchmarks show consistent gains
over strong baselines.

1. Introduction
Music can tell stories: narrative-rich forms such as orchestral
suites and opera [1] convey evolving musical narratives (tension
and release, thematic development, structural transitions) even
without lyrics [2]. Most Music Automated Audio Captioning
(AAC) systems pair a pretrained music encoder with an LLM
and train on paired audio–text clips, butlong-form(i.e., multi-
minute tracks with evolving structure and affect, unlike stan-
dard 10–30 s clips) captioning remains challenging. Generating
a multi-sentence, coherent narrative for a minutes-long piece re-
quires integrating multi-scale cues [3], from low-level acoustics
(tempo, rhythm, timbre, dynamics) to higher-level perceptions
(affect, motifs, form), while staying consistent across sections.
Consequently, current pipelines often missartistically aligned
long-horizon captions: narratives that satisfy two properties at
once: (i) they stay grounded in audible structure and attributes;
and (ii) they read as coherent, program-note-like prose rather
than generic or hallucinated text. (Table 1).
Prior AAC for music largely follows two paradigms. (i)
Paired-supervision captioning/tag-to-textfor short, fixed-length
audio, typically encoder–decoder generation [4, 5], with data-
scarcity mitigations via pseudo-captions [6]. (ii)Retrieval-
augmented or instruction-tuned audio–LLM pipelinesthat con-
dition an LLM on music embeddings and/or retrieved text to
improve alignment [7, 8, 9]. Extensions add reasoning [10],
efficiency under limited supervision [11], and broader multi-

many systems remain tuned to short clips or factual retrieval
and struggle to sustain coherence and multi-layer understand-

with scarce paired data, captioners tend to overfit the phrasing

Training-free adaptation via in-context learning and struc-
tured prompting has shown that LLMs can follow task in-
structions without weight updates [16, 17, 18], and retrieval-
augmented generation can further ground outputs with external
text [19]. However, for long-form music AAC, grounding can-
not rely on external documents alone: captions must stay faith-
ful to time-varying audio cues, and naive prompting can halluci-
nate or drift when the input spans minutes and exhibits repeated
or subtle patterns. This motivates a lightweight, audio-grounded
alternative that reduces dependence on paired captions while ex-
plicitly anchoring long-horizon generation.
To address these limitations, we proposeMusic Artistic
Captioning(MAC), a modular,training-freeframework for the
task of producing a long-form, multi-sentence narrative that
is grounded in audible attributes and remains coherent across
the sections of a minutes-long piece; the LLM is prompted
off-the-shelf, unlike fine-tuned paired-supervised baselines.
MAC generates long-form music narratives by conditioning
an instruction-following LLM on a structured set of automat-
ically extracted audio descriptors (an evidence layer) that con-
strains what the caption may assert, without any task-specific
parameter updates. Concretely, MAC performs multi-scale seg-
mentation and extracts descriptors spanning low-level acous-
tics (e.g., tempo, onset rate, dynamics) and higher-level per-
ceived attributes (e.g., instruments, mood, multi-dimensional

3. Experiments and Results
We evaluate our MAC framework in two settings: (i)Music
Captioning Benchmark, using short-form datasets; and (ii)Mu-
sic Artistic Captioning, targeting narrative-rich, long-form mu-
sic such as opera.
Experimental Setup.We use MSAF [23] for functional seg-
mentation and MERT [24] for high-level semantic features
(genre, instruments, vocals, mood, emotion). Low-level acous-
tics (e.g., onset rate, RMS) are computed with standard MIR
tools (e.g., librosa), and tempo is estimated with BeatNet [29].
Captions are generated with GPT-OSS 20B [25] served via
vLLM (temperature=0.2 andmax tokens=2048 (with
seed=42). Experiments run on two RTX 3090 (24GB)
GPUs: one for vLLM inference and one for feature extrac-
tion/encoding.
Datasets.We evaluate MAC on five datasets covering bench-
mark and artistic captioning. For benchmarks, we use Music-
Caps [27] (20s, human captions), a 30s subset of MQAD [13]
(AI captions), and Song Describer [28] (longer audio with
brief human annotations), which differ notably in caption style.
For artistic captioning without paired ground truth, we use
the Wagner Ring Cycle opera corpus [1] and full-length MSD
tracks [13] to test narrative-rich and full-song settings. Statistics
are in Table 3.
Baselines.We compare MAC to open-source music–language
models: FUTGA [20], MusiLingo [8], MU-LLaMA [9],
Qwen-Audio [21], Qwen-Omni [22], and LP-MusicCaps [6].
Forartistic captioning, we use Qwen-Omni and FUTGA
as instruction-tuned baselines. For fairness (except LP-
MusicCaps), we evaluate each baseline with (i) a shared min-
imal prompt and (ii) its recommended prompt/template, report-
ing the better score in the main table (both settings in the supple-
ment). We also report an ablation that uses the same evidence
text as MAC but removes hierarchical context and IPO, iso-
lating gains from multi-scale context construction and prompt
optimization. We omit baselines without public weights (e.g.,
MMQAD [13], LLark [10], FUTGA-MIR [30]).
Evaluation Protocol.Forbenchmark captioningwith
references, we report BLEU, ROUGE-L, METEOR, and
BERTScore. Forartistic captioningwithout references, we
use three complementary checks that align with artistic ground-
ing, expressivity, and long-horizon consistency. (i)Seman-
tic alignment:we embed the audio and caption with MuQ-
MuLan [31] and report their cosine similarity. (ii)Emo-
tional alignment:we predict emotion from the caption us-
ing a BERT classifier fine-tuned on CARER [32], and com-
pare it to the audio-side emotion prediction (agreement score).
(iii)Art score:our no-reference metric that combinesfactual-
ity(caption mentions match pseudo-labels extracted from au-
dio, e.g., key/tags/instruments/vocals/tempo/meter),expressiv-
ity(caption specificity and lexical diversity), andcoherence
(adjacent-window caption consistency via embedding similar-
ity). Factuality uses soft synonym/number matching to reduce
lexical bias. For songswith captions{c s,i}Ns

4. Conclusion and Future Work
We presentMusic Artistic Captioning(MAC), a training-free
modular framework for long-form, artistically aligned music
captioning. MAC conditions an instruction-following LLM on
automatically extracted multi-scale audio evidence, verbalized
as interpretable pseudo-evidence and integrated via feature-
conditioned prompting. Iterative Prompt Optimization (IPO)
enables user-preferred narration while preserving evidence con-
sistency. Experiments on opera and standard benchmarks show
gains over strong baselines in automatic metrics and LLM-
judge evaluation, yielding more coherent and expressive long-
horizon captions.
Limitations and Future Work.Long-form artistic music cap-
tioning remains underexplored, with reliable evaluation as the
main open challenge. Our LLM-judge protocol is an indirect
proxy with limitations, while human studies are also subjec-
tive and costly. For no-reference analysis, Art may still carry
self-preference because MIR/MERT pseudo-labels are used in
both MAC generation and evaluation, although soft matching
reduces circular lexical bias. Future work should further study
this issue and develop hybrid measures combining automatic,
LLM-based, and human assessments.
