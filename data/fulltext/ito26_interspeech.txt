for Controllable Text-to-Speech Synthesis

Abstract
Controllable text-to-speech systems that use explicit prosodic
features enable fine-grained control, which is particularly use-
ful for emotional speech synthesis. However, specifying frame-
level details often imposes a heavy burden on users. To allevi-
ate this burden, we formulateprosody restorationas a unified
problem of recovering frame-level prosody from simplified and
easy-to-specify prosodic inputs. By expanding two prior tasks
with three novel ones, we address a total of five tasks. Together,
they support practical user workflows by enabling restoration
from prosody that is partial, coarse, or both. We also pro-
pose two diffusion-based prosody restorers: a supervised model
trained to reverse simulated degradations, and an unsupervised
model leveraging samplers for linear inverse problems. Ex-
periments across all five tasks show that our methods restore
prosody more accurately than non-diffusion baselines, preserv-
ing linguistically valid prosodic structures.
Index Terms: prosody restoration, text-to-speech, diffusion
model, inverse problem
1. Introduction
Controllable text-to-speech (TTS) models [1–3] conditioned
on explicit prosodic features including pitch, voiced/unvoiced
(V/UV) flags, and energy are particularly effective for expres-
sive speech synthesis. However, these systems often require
frame-level prosody. This requirement burdens users, as it is
difficult to specify prosody that achieves a desired style while
preserving linguistically valid patterns. This burden is amplified
in pitch-accent languages such as Japanese, where pitch patterns
that violate accentual regularities not only degrade naturalness
but can also reduce intelligibility [4–6]. To alleviate this bur-
den, recent works predict prosody from simplified representa-
tions such as partial prosody [7] or smoothed prosody [8]. Al-
though these approaches improve controllability, they focus on
specific use cases, highlighting the need for a unified framework
capable of handling diverse input patterns. Additionally, their
prediction targets are prosody averaged over phonemes, sacri-
ficing granular detail by discarding variation within phonemes.
In parallel, stochastic modeling of prosody using deep gen-
erative models has been widely explored. Several works [3, 9–
14] have sought to model pitch, energy, V/UV flags, or du-
ration by utilizing normalizing flows [15], diffusion models
(DMs) [16], and flow matching models [17, 18]. Also, DMs
have proven effective for restoration across various domains.
In addition to supervised models specializing in specific tasks
such as time series imputation [19] and audio upsampling [20],
general linear inverse problem solvers for pretrained DMs, in-
cluding denoising diffusion restoration models (DDRMs) [21]
and GibbsDDRM [22] have shown strong performance.

We formulateprosody restorationas a unified problem
of recovering frame-level prosody from simplified and easy-
to-specify representations, which we calldegradedprosody.
Beyond tasks explored in prior work [7, 8], namely restora-
tion from partial or smoothed prosody, we explore three novel
tasks: restoration from 1) piecewise-averaged prosody, 2) par-
tially missing smoothed prosody, and 3) partially missing aver-
aged prosody. Solving these tasks strongly supports practical
workflows; the first task enables users to specify low-temporal-
resolution prosody, while the latter two allow users to spec-
ify prosody partially and coarsely. Motivated by successes in
restoration using DMs and stochastic prosody modeling, we
propose two diffusion-based prosody restorers (DPRs) as uni-
fied solvers for all five tasks: a supervised DPR trained to re-
verse simulated degradations, and an unsupervised DPR trained
as a standard conditional DM on clean prosody, handling degra-
dations only at inference via samplers for linear inverse prob-
lems [21, 22]. Our main contributions are as follows:
• We formulateprosody restorationas a unified problem utiliz-
ing the framework of linear inverse problems.
• Beyond the two previously explored tasks, we introduce three
novel restoration tasks, which support practical workflows.
• We propose two DPRs, operating in supervised and unsuper-
vised settings, as unified solvers for these tasks.
• Experimental results on two emotional corpora across all five
tasks demonstrate that our DPRs effectively restore severely
degraded prosody, preserving linguistically valid structures.

3. Proposed Method
Fig. 2 (a) illustrates the overall system, coupling the proposed
prosody restorer (PR) with a prosody-controllable backbone
TTS model. The PR predicts the restored prosody ˆFfrom
text, speaker and style IDs, duration, the degraded inputF (deg),
and variant-specific degradation information. Subsequently, the
TTS model generates waveforms conditioned on the predicted
ˆF. We propose supervised and unsupervised diffusion-based
PRs (DPRs), detailed in Sect. 3.2 and 3.3, respectively. Both

DPRs share a unified architecture described in Sect. 3.1 and are
trained as continuous-time score-based DMs [16]. Since the PR
is trained independently, it can be flexibly integrated with any
prosody-controllable backbone TTS; our specific implementa-
tion is detailed in Sect. 4.1.
3.1. Architecture of DPR
The DPR consists of a context encoder (CE) and a score esti-
mator (SE). As illustrated in Fig. 2 (d), the CE transforms text,
speaker and style IDs, and duration into frame-level context fea-
turesC (cxt) of lengthl utt, utilizing a Transformer-based text en-
coder [24]. As shown in Fig. 2 (b) and (c), the SE takes time
t∈[0,1], noisy prosodyF t, and conditioning features includ-
ingC (cxt) to estimate the conditional score ofF t. The com-
position of the conditioning features varies between supervised
and unsupervised DPRs. For the SE, we adopt a 1D U-Net [25],
which is suited for our configuration where prosody and auxil-
iary information are concatenated along the channel dimension.
3.2. Supervised Approach
In this approach, the model is trained to restore prosody from
simulated degradations. At each training step, a task is sampled
from the set{Inp, Ref-S, Ref-A, InpRef-S, InpRef-A}. Then,
based on the selected task, degradation parameters are deter-
mined. Specifically, for Inp, InpRef-S, and InpRef-A, we sam-
ple a masking block sizel blk and a mask ratior. Similarly,
for Ref-S and InpRef-S, we sample a Gaussian kernel width
σG, whereas for Ref-A and InpRef-A, we select a mean win-
dow size ¯lwin to generate{l win,j}j. Simultaneously, we com-
pute a degradation embeddingC (deg). This embedding is de-
rived from a frame-level degradation ID tensor indicating the
degradation state for each prosodic feature per frame, taking
values in{0,1,2,3} 3×lutt. Here, the IDs0to3correspond
to masking, no degradation, smoothing, and averaging, respec-
tively. GivenF (deg),C (cxt), andC (deg), which compose the
conditioning features, the SE estimates the conditional score
∇p(Ft|F (deg),C (cxt),C (deg)). During inference, we employ
an ordinary differential equation solver.
3.3. Unsupervised Approach
As covering all degradation patterns via supervised training is
intractable, we further propose an unsupervised approach for
DPR. In this setting, we train the SE to estimate the context-
conditional score∇p(F t|C (cxt)). For inference, we employ
two samplers designed for linear inverse problems. The choice
of sampler depends on whether the task is non-blind or blind.
For non-blind tasks whereHis known, namely Inp, Ref-
A, and InpRef-A, we utilize the DDRM sampler [21]. At each
timestept, the clean prosodyF θ,t is predicted via Tweedie’s
formula using the estimated score and the current noisy state
Ft. Subsequently,F t is updated in a spectral space defined by

4. Experimental Evaluation
4.1. Experimental Setup
Dataset:We conducted experiments using two Japanese
emotional speech corpora: the in-house (IH) and JVNV
datasets [27]. The IH dataset contains 31 hours of speech from
four professional speakers, two males and two females, cover-
ing four styles:neutral,happy,angry, andsad. We reserved
eight utterances per speaker-style pair for validation and test-
ing, yielding 20,210 training, 128 validation, and 128 test ut-
terances. For the JVNV dataset, we extracted only verbal seg-
ments, assigning 1,423, 96, and 96 utterances to training, vali-
dation, and testing. We used internally annotated phoneme and
accent (PA) symbols as text inputs, where the accent symbols
indicate accent phrase boundaries and accent nuclei [28].
From 22.05 kHz waveforms, we computed 80-dimensional
log-mel spectrograms with a 256-sample hop as the acoustic
model target and vocoder input, and used the same frame rate
for all prosodic features. We obtained pitch and V/UV flags via
pYIN [29]. Pitch was log-normalized and linearly interpolated
in unvoiced regions. Energy was computed as the root mean
square of the log-mel spectrogram across frequency bins, fol-
lowed by normalization andµ-law companding withµ=2 14−1.
Baseline PRs:We prepared two baseline PRs,DetandCV AE,
based on a deterministic Transformer and a conditional varia-
tional autoencoder [30], respectively.Detis an extension of
the sketch-to-contour predictor in [8]. It first extracts condition-
ing features following Sect. 3.2, except thatF (deg) is linearly
quantized and embedded. A Transformer decoder then converts
these features into the prosody estimates.CV AEextends Det by
