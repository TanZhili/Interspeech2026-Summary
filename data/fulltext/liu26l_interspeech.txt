Abstract
Language model (LM)-based text-to-speech (TTS) systems typ-
ically rely on sampling-based decoding, which suffers from
stochastic instability. While beam search offers a maximization-
based alternative, it is limited in speech generation due to severe
degeneration (e.g., temporal collapse) and high latency. Our
analysis reveals that beam search cost is highly imbalanced
between search and evaluation stages. Motivated by this, we
propose Search-and-eValuatE beam decoding (SaVE-Beam),
which decouples hypothesis expansion from sequence scoring.
Specifically, a lightweight student model performs chunk-wise
beam construction, while the teacher LM retains exact evaluation.
Hard repetition constraints are introduced to prevent degener-
ate loops. Experiments show that SaVE-Beam achieves up to
5.1× speedup over conventional beam search without degrading
speech quality, and reduces WER by up to 50% compared to
sampling, enabling practical maximization-based decoding for
real-time LM-based TTS.
Index Terms: speech synthesis, beam search, decoding strategy
1. Introduction
Language model (LM)-based text-to-speech (TTS) systems have
recently achieved remarkable progress by modeling speech as
sequences of discrete codec tokens and generating them autore-
gressively [1, 2, 3, 4]. By inheriting the generative capabilities of
LM, these systems enable high-fidelity synthesis, strong speaker
consistency, and zero-shot generalization.
Despite these advances, decoding in LM-based TTS remains
fundamentally fragile. Unlike text tokens, where local token
errors may be semantically recoverable, speech tokens encode
tightly coupled phonetic and prosodic structures over long tem-
poral spans. A single erroneous token can disrupt pronunciation,
rhythm, or speaker characteristics, and due to strong temporal au-
tocorrelation, such errors are often irreversible and may rapidly
cascade [5]. Consequently, speech synthesis is highly sensitive
to decoding decisions.
In practice, most systems rely on sampling-based strategies
such as top-k [6] or nucleus sampling [7], typically with conser-
vative configurations to stabilize generation [8, 9, 10, 4], includ-
ing low temperatures, small sampling thresholds, or confidence-
based filtering [11, 12]. While sampling mitigates catastrophic
failures, it sacrifices determinism and may still produce tail-
sample errors. Maximization-based decoding, particularly beam
search [13], offers a principled alternative by explicitly maintain-
ing multiple high-probability hypotheses and reducing random
fluctuations. However, applying beam search to LM-based TTS
introduces two critical challenges: degeneration and latency.
First, due to the low information density and strong tempo-
ral redundancy of speech tokens, beam search tends to amplify

search incurs prohibitive inference cost. In autoregressive speech

at each step. As a result, conventional beam search becomes
impractical for real-time or low-latency TTS systems.
In this work, we revisit beam search from a computational
perspective. Our analysis reveals a pronounced imbalance: over
98% of decoding time is spent on beam expansion (search),
while sequence evaluation and selection account for only a small
fraction. Motivated by this asymmetry, we proposeSearch-
and-eValuatEbeam decoding (SaVE-Beam), a framework that
decouples hypothesis expansion from sequence scoring through
model specialization. Specifically, a lightweight student model,
obtained via self-distillation, is responsible for constructing local
beam trees, significantly reducing expansion cost. The original
teacher LM is retained for exact evaluation and final selection to
preserve generation quality. To prevent error accumulation intro-
duced by the weaker student, we further introduce chunk-wise
beam expansion, where student-led search and teacher-led eval-
uation alternate in short segments. In addition, we incorporate
hard repetition constraints during beam construction to explicitly
suppress degenerate loops, which we find to be crucial for stable
maximization-based speech decoding.
Unlike distill-to-decode approaches that replace the origi-
nal model during inference [15], SaVE-Beam does not transfer
final decoding responsibility to the student. Instead, it sepa-
rates search from evaluation, accelerating beam construction
while maintaining the original decoding objective. Experiments
demonstrate that SaVE-Beam accelerates conventional beam
search by up to 5.1× while achieving comparable or improved
speech quality. Compared to original sampling-based decoding,
it further reduces WER by up to 50% without sacrificing latency,
making maximization-based decoding practical for LM-based
TTS. Our contributions are summarized as follows:
• We analyze the computational bottleneck of beam search in
LM-based TTS and identify the severe imbalance between
search and evaluation stages.
• We propose SaVE-Beam, a decoupled search-and-evaluation
framework that accelerates beam search via model specializa-
tion and chunk-wise beam expansion.
• We provide comprehensive experiments showing that SaVE-
Beam achieves up to 5.1× speedup over standard beam search
with robust quality improvements.
• We identify and validate the critical role of hard repetition con-
straints in preventing temporal collapse under maximization-
based decoding for speech generation.

2. Method
2.1. Beam Search for LM-based TTS
Autoregressive decoding is central to language models and their
downstream generation tasks. Maximization-based decoding
aims to find the most probable token sequence and provides
stronger determinism than stochastic sampling, which is desir-
able in TTS for stability and faithfulness to the input text.
Beam search (BS) is a canonical maximization-based strat-
egy that can be decomposed into two stages:SearchandEval-
uation. At each step t, the search stage expands the current
beam setXt−1 of size B over the vocabulary V, producing
|V|×B candidates. The evaluation stage computes cumulative
log-probabilities and retains the top-B sequences. For any hy-

Compared to sampling-based decoding, BS explicitly pro-

2.2. Temporal Collapse
However, directly applying standard BS to LM-based TTS often
results in temporal collapse. Speech token sequences differ fun-
damentally from text: they are substantially longer and exhibit
strong temporal autocorrelation with low information density.
Speech signals also contain abundant low-energy segments, such
as pauses, inhalations, trailing phonation, and background noise,
which manifest as repeated or homogeneous tokens in the dis-
crete domain. Under uncertainty, the model tends to favor these
high-prior patterns [3].
Beam search further amplifies this tendency. By determinis-
tically selecting the highest-probability continuation at each step,
BS reinforces locally dominant acoustic regimes. Once decoding
enters a silence- or noise-dominated region, the self-reinforcing
feedback loop may lead to persistent token repetition, producing
prolonged silence or noise artifacts.
2.3. Latency Bottleneck Analysis
Recent work such as TRAD-BS [13] mitigates degeneration via
temporal repetition penalties and related refinements, enabling
BS for LM-based TTS. Nevertheless, a critical limitation re-
mains: prohibitive inference latency. In standard BS, both the
expansion that constructsCt and the scorings(·) in Eq. (3) are
computed by the same full model, leading to aB× overhead in
autoregressive decoding.
To quantitatively examine latency bottleneck, we reproduced
TRAD-BS on CosyV oice2 and evaluated it on the test-zh subset
of SeedTTS-Eval [16] with beam sizeB= 5 . Latency profiling
on an NVIDIA RTX 3090 GPU is summarized in Table 1. Com-
pared to sampling-based decoding, BS incurs a 4.86× slowdown
in the LM decoding stage and a 4.34× slowdown in end-to-end
generation, yielding a real-time factor (RTF) of 4.95. Such over-
head makes conventional BS impractical for latency-sensitive
TTS applications. Further analysis reveals that the bottleneck of
BS primarily lies in the search phase, which accounts for 98%
of total decoding time. This asymmetry suggests that search and
evaluation need not share the same model capacity.
2.4. SaVE-Beam
Inspired by the above imbalance, we propose SaVE-Beam, a
decoding framework that decouples hypothesis expansion from
sequence scoring via model specialization. Instead of executing
beam expansion with the full-scale LM, we delegate the search
phase to a lightweight student model, while preserving exact
evaluation with the original LM. The overall decoding pipeline
is illustrated in Figure 1. This separation significantly reduces
search cost without altering the decoding objective.
2.4.1. Model Specialization Strategy
In conventional beam search, each decoding step requires ex-
panding all B active beams, resulting in B forward passes
through the full-scale LM. For long speech token sequences,
thisB× overhead accumulates severely across autoregressive
steps.
SaVE-Beam mitigates this bottleneck by assigning beam
expansion to a compact student model distilled from the teacher
LM. Crucially, the student model is not intended to replace the
teacher for final decoding, but rather to specialize in constructing
high-quality beam candidates. Since beam search relies primarily
on relative token ranking rather than exact probability calibration,
the student model is optimized to preserve token ordering among
high-probability candidates.
Inspired by the training-time test paradigm in EAGLE-3
[17], we train the student model under self-referential autoregres-
sive conditions. During training, the student’s own predictions
are recursively fed back as inputs to simulate multi-step beam
expansion, exposing it to its prediction errors and improving
robustness under inference-time rollout.

2.4.2. Chunk-wise Beam Expansion
To balance efficiency and fidelity, we propose chunk-wise beam
expansion, alternating student expansion and teacher evaluation.
Due to error accumulation in long acoustic sequences, it would
degrade quality to let the student construct the entire beam tree.
Instead, SaVE-Beam limits student expansion to short chunks
of length L, after which the teacher evaluates and selects the
highest-scoring beam segment to update the prompt.
Search Stage.The student model constructs beam tree
within a local search chunk. To prevent temporal collapse dis-
cussed in Section 2.2, we enforce hard repetition constraints:
tokens within a recent windoww are masked, i.e., their probabil-
ities are set to zero and the remaining mass is renormalized. We
deliberately avoid soft penalties, e.g., probability suppression
[9, 13], which remain vulnerable to collapse under the student’s
weaker predictions. This constraint exploits speech token redun-
dancy to robustly suppress degenerate loops without degrading
synthesis quality. Finally, a dynamic pruning strategy retains
only the top-N nodes ranked bys(·) in Eq. (3) to enhance effi-
ciency.

Distinction from Speculative Decoding.While resembling
the draft-verify paradigm of speculative decoding, SaVE-Beam
differs fundamentally. Speculative decoding accelerates infer-
ence by maximizing token acceptance rates, offering limited
quality gains. Conversely, SaVE-Beam targets beam search la-
tency while strictly preserving its goal of finding high-probability
sequences. By decoupling search and evaluation with rigorous
constraints, it accelerates generation without compromising the
robustness of maximization-based decoding. Existing specula-
tive decoding for TTS adopts Medusa-like architectures [22, 23],
with no open-source implementation directly comparable to our
chunk-wise student–teacher framework.
3. Experiments
3.1. Datasets & Baseline
We use LibriTTS [ 24] and the premium subset of Wenet-
Speech4TTS [ 25] for training. For evaluation, we use the
SeedTTS-Eval benchmark [16]. CosyV oice 2 [26], a standard
LM-based TTS architecture, serves as the foundation model
for SaVE-Beam. We further reimplement TRAD-BS [ 13] on
CosyV oice 2 using the recommended configuration (beam size
B= 5 with temporal and beam-wise repetition penalties) to
establish a fair baseline.
3.2. Implementation Details
Experiments are conducted on 8 NVIDIA H800 GPUs for train-
ing and a single RTX 3090 for evaluation. We use CosyV oice2-
0.5B variant with 1–2 hidden layers as the student model, with
distillation temperatureτ= 2 . For chunk-wise beam expansion,
we set chunk lengthL= 5 and beam widthB= 8 . Dynamic
pruning retains top-N= 100 nodes. Hard repetition constraints
use window sizes w= 10 (search) and w= 15 (evaluation).
The LM filter keeps top-K= 5 candidates, with acceptance
thresholdα= 0.5.

3.3.2. Comparison with Baseline
As shown in Table 2, SaVE-Beam substantially improves speech
quality over the original sampling-based decoding strategy from
CosyV oice 2 on bothtest-enandtest-zh. W/CER are reduced
by 50% and 33%, respectively, with additional gains in MOS.
Meanwhile, the decoding speed remains comparable to the base-
line. Regarding SS, perceptual differences are minimal when SS
values are sufficiently high, for example above 0.7 [13].
Compared with TRAD-BS, which follows a more conven-
tional beam search paradigm, SaVE-Beam achieves more robust
and efficient quality improvements. It significantly reduces the
decoding latency of the LM module, achieving a speedup of
3.9× to 5.1×. This headroom allows a larger beam, contribut-
ing to the observed quality gains over full beam search. These
results collectively indicate the effectiveness and robustness of
SaVE-Beam when applying beam search to LM-based TTS.
3.3.3. Detailed Analysis
We further analyze thetest-zhsubset to examine computational
redistribution between search and evaluation, as well as the over-
all system RTF. As shown in Table 3, SaVE-Beam leads to a
noticeable reduction in the time proportion of the search stage
compared with TRAD-BS. This verifies that SaVE-Beam suc-
cessfully decouples the two stages and reallocates their compu-
tational responsibilities in a more efficient and balanced manner,
which leads to a significant improvement in overall decoding
efficiency. As a result, the system RTF approaches that of the
baseline, greatly enhancing the practicality of beam search in
LM-based TTS.
The comparison between the 1-layer (1h) and 2-layer (2h)
students further reveals a trade-off between model capacity and
decoding efficiency. Although both variants generate a compara-
ble number of effective tokens in the final decoding output, the
1h student model, due to its limited fitting capacity, produces
beam candidates that are more frequently rejected during evalua-
tion. This leads to additional chunk expansions and thus more
decoding steps overall. Consequently, the 1h variant does not
exhibit clear speed advantages despite its smaller model size.

Fortest-hard, TRAD-BS does not exhibit additional tempo-

this issue, resulting in degraded performance of TRAD-BS on
the hard dataset [30].

degradation. We conjecture that using a weaker student model

may help escape local optima. To validate this hypothesis, we
remove dynamic pruning to increase randomness, which leads to
a further improvement in CER ontest-hard(Table 4). This inter-
play between stochasticity and determinism suggests a promising
direction for mitigating hallucination in LM-based TTS.
4. Conclusion
In this work, we proposed SaVE-Beam, an efficient decoding
framework addressing the latency and degeneration bottlenecks
of beam search in LM-based TTS. By decoupling hypothesis
expansion from sequence evaluation, we shifted the computa-
tionally expensive search phase to a lightweight student model
via chunk-wise expansion. Combined with hard repetition con-
straints to prevent temporal collapse, SaVE-Beam achieves up to
a 5.1× speedup over conventional beam search while matching
the real-time factor of baseline systems. Crucially, it signifi-
cantly reduces generation errors compared to standard sampling
techniques without compromising speech naturalness. These
