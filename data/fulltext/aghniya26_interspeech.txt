for Speech Time-Scale Modification Benchmarking

Abstract
Despite extensive research on Speech Time-Scale Modification
(STSM), evaluation for Mandarin remains limited due to the
lack of naturally recorded, multi-speed parallel data. Exist-
ing studies rely on English corpora or artificially time-scaled
references, which fail to capture rate-dependent prosodic and
tonal characteristics. As a tonal language, Mandarin depends
on fine-grained pitch–timing coordination, making it sensitive
to time-scaling distortions. We introduce GRATS (Granular
Rate Adaptation for Temporal Scaling), a naturally recorded
parallel multi-speaker Mandarin dataset with 25 speakers and
five speaking rates (0.5×, 0.75×, 1.0×, 1.25×, 1.5×), allow-
ing comparison against authentic target-rate recordings. Bench-
mark results show trade-offs between intelligibility and percep-
tual quality, and degradation in temporal and pitch consistency
under extreme rates, highlighting the importance of tone-aware
and alignment-aware evaluation.
Index Terms: speech time-scale modification; mandarin
speech; prosody; lexical tone
1. Introduction
Speech Time-Scale Modification (STSM) [1, 2] modifies speak-
ing rate while preserving pitch and perceptual quality, with ap-
plications in playback, language learning, hearing assistance,
and data augmentation [3, 4, 5].
Existing STSM research has focused mainly on artifact
reduction and signal fidelity, especially under moderate rate
changes. Evaluation practices are often shaped by this signal-
processing perspective: system outputs are typically compared
against artificially time-scaled references using waveform-level
or intrusive metrics, implicitly assuming that artificial scaling
approximates natural rate variation.
However, this assumption is particularly problematic for
tonal languages such as Mandarin. Mandarin encodes lexical
meaning through fine-grained pitch–timing coordination [6, 7],
making it sensitive to time-scaling distortions. Artificial refer-
ences do not capture natural rate-dependent variations in artic-
ulation and prosody, potentially conflating waveform similarity
with authentic target-rate realization. Recent evidence further
suggests that natural and artificially time-altered speech is per-
ceptually non-equivalent [8]. As a result, it remains unclear
whether current STSM systems truly preserve tonal and tempo-
ral structure under realistic speaking-rate variation.
Despite significant algorithmic advances—including
Waveform-Similarity Overlap-Add (WSOLA) [1], Phase-
V ocoder [2], and neural duration or vocoder-based approaches

[9, 10, 11], evaluation resources remain largely English-centric
and waveform-focused. The lack of naturally recorded
multi-speed Mandarin data creates a methodological gap
between algorithmic development and linguistically grounded
evaluation.
To address this limitation, we introduce GRATS, a natu-
rally recorded parallel multi-speaker Mandarin corpus designed
for STSM benchmarking. Unlike artificial references obtained
by deterministically scaling a 1.0×recording, GRATS pro-
vides independently recorded target-rate utterances from the
same speaker and sentence. This design enables direct compar-
ison between STSM-generated outputs and authentic target-rate
recordings, allowing evaluation of whether systems approxi-
mate natural rate-dependent changes rather than only reproduce
a signal-level transformation.
Evaluation is conducted using complementary intelligibil-
ity, perceptual, temporal, and pitch-related metrics, including
Character Error Rate (CER) computed with a fixed Whisper
(medium, multilingual) Automatic Speech Recognition (ASR)
model [12], Perceptual Evaluation of Speech Quality (PESQ,
ITU-T P.862.2 wideband) [13], Short-Time Objective Intelli-
gibility (STOI) [14], Deep Noise Suppression Mean Opinion
Score (DNSMOS) [15], syllable-level duration Mean Absolute
Error (MAE), and fundamental frequency (F0) correlation com-
puted from WORLD-extracted F0 contours [16].
Contributions:
• We introduce GRATS (Granular Rate Adaptation for Tempo-
ral Scaling) dataset, which is, to our knowledge, the first par-
allel naturally recorded multi-speed Mandarin speech dataset
covering 25 speakers and five controlled speaking-rate condi-
tions.
• We propose a linguistically grounded benchmarking proto-
col that evaluates STSM systems against authentic target-rate
recordings rather than artificially time-scaled references.
• We provide comprehensive benchmark results across rep-
resentative classical and neural STSM systems using com-
plementary intelligibility, perceptual, temporal, and pitch-
related metrics.

Mandarin, publicly available corpora such as AISHELL-1 [19]

primarily designed for automatic speech recognition or hearing-

recordings of the same speaker-sentence pairs across multi-

rely on artificially time-scaled references or lack deterministic
source-target pairing, limiting their ability to assess how well
STSM systems approximate authentic rate-dependent prosodic
and temporal patterns.

GRATS dataset is designed to address this gap.
3. Dataset Design
Data Collection:The GRATS dataset consists of Mandarin
read speech recorded at five speaking-rate conditions: 0.5×,
0.75×, 1.0×, 1.25×, and 1.5×, covering slower and faster con-
ditions relative to the natural rate. All recordings are produced
directly at the target rates without artificial time-scaling.

The dataset includes 25 native Mandarin speakers (13 fe-
male, 12 male speakers), each recording the same 60 utterances
across all five speaking rates, resulting in 7,500 fully parallel ut-
terances and total duration 8.1 hours of speech. To ensure con-
sistent control of speaking rate, a karaoke-style visual prompt-
ing system highlights characters according to the target tempo.
Recordings are conducted in a low-noise environment, and ut-
terances with reading errors are re-recorded. Recordings are
stored as 16-bit PCM W A V (44.1 kHz, mono) using an Audio-
Technica ATR2500x-USB microphone. No post-processing,
normalization, silence trimming, or artificial time-scaling is ap-
plied. Figure 1 shows the recording software interface used dur-
ing data collection.
Dataset Characterization:We further compute transcript-
level and alignment-based statistics to characterize the corpus,
as summarized in Table 2. The prompts are controlled to have
the same length, while all Mandarin tone categories are repre-
sented in the corpus. The duration statistics also show a mono-
tonic decrease from slower to faster target rates, confirming that
the visual prompting protocol effectively controls speaking rate.
Dataset Organization:The dataset provides metadata for sys-
tematic loading and reproducible use, and its parallel speaker–
sentence structure enables direct comparison of the same utter-
ance across natural rate variations.

Evaluation setup:
All systems are evaluated in an inference-only setting using
official or public implementations and pretrained checkpoints,
without retraining or fine-tuning on GRATS.
For each speaker and utterance, the naturally recorded 1.0×
speech is used as input, and each system generates outputs at
four target rates (0.5×, 0.75×, 1.25×, 1.5×), which are com-
pared with the corresponding naturally recorded target-rate ut-
terances.
This protocol therefore evaluates the gap between artifi-
cial STSM-generated speech and independently recorded nat-
ural target-rate speech for the same speaker and sentence. Each
baseline is run with its official or public inference pipeline and
configuration summarized in Table 3, meaning outputs are first
generated at the method’s native sampling rate. For metric com-
putation only, both generated outputs and their corresponding
natural references are converted to 16 kHz mono to ensure con-
sistent scoring across systems.
Baseline STSM methods:We evaluate two classical baselines
(WSOLA [1] and Phase V ocoder [2]) and three neural base-
lines (TSMNet [9], ScalerGAN [10], and CLPCNet [11]). All
systems use official or public implementations and pretrained
checkpoints, as summarized in Table 3. Links to the evalu-
ated implementations and inference scripts are provided on the
project page.
Evaluation Metrics:We evaluate STSM performance using
complementary measures of intelligibility, perceptual quality,
temporal structure, and pitch consistency.

Objective and perceptual quality are evaluated using PESQ

natural target-rate recordings, and DNSMOS [15] computed as

To assess prosodic consistency, we compute syllable-level
duration MAE and F0 correlation, where F0 contours are ex-
tracted using WORLD (Harvest) [16], and alignment between
generated and reference speech is obtained using MFA [21].
These alignments enable duration comparison and alignment-
aware computation of Pearson correlation for F0 trajectories.
5. Experimental Results
Figure 3 shows performance trends across speaking rates, with
detailed results in Table 4. Overall, Mandarin STSM evaluation
is multi-dimensional: CER reflects ASR-based content preser-
vation; DNSMOS, PESQ, and STOI capture quality-related as-
pects; duration MAE measures temporal consistency; and F0
correlation reflects pitch-contour consistency relevant to Man-
darin lexical tones.
These metrics are therefore complementary. As shown in
Figure 3(a)–(d) and Table 4, Phase V ocoder achieves the lowest
CER across all rates, indicating stronger preservation of ASR-
recognizable linguistic content. However, neural methods such
as CLPCNet obtain higher DNSMOS and STOI scores, sug-
gesting better perceptual or spectral quality. This mismatch
shows that lower CER does not necessarily imply better per-
ceived quality or linguistic correctness.
Temporal and prosodic metrics reveal another failure mode.
As shown in Figure 3(e)–(f), Duration MAE follows a U-shaped
pattern, with larger errors at the extreme rates of 0.5×and 1.5×,
while F0 correlation decreases under faster and more extreme
speaking-rate conditions. These trends indicate that extreme
rate modification makes it harder for STSM systems to preserve
natural rhythmic structure and pitch–timing coordination, even
when the linguistic content remains recognizable.
Together, these results show that Mandarin STSM should
be interpreted using multiple metrics, since each metric captures
a different failure mode.
6. Discussion
The results show that performance drops more noticeably at ex-
treme speaking rates, which indicates that current STSM sys-
tems are not equally robust across rate conditions. Neural mod-
els such as CLPCNet and ScalerGAN perform competitively
near natural rates but degrade under larger time-scale modifi-
cations. In Mandarin, lexical meaning depends on accurate F0
trajectories aligned with syllable timing, so such instability may
affect both signal quality and tonal realization.
The use of naturally recorded multi-speed references also
changes how system performance should be interpreted. When
evaluation relies on artificially time-scaled signals, the task
mainly measures how accurately a system reproduces a deter-
ministic transformation of a normal-rate recording. In contrast,
natural target-rate recordings contain authentic variations in ar-
ticulation, rhythm, duration, and F0 movement. This setting
directly links artificial rate modification to naturally produced
target-rate speech by asking whether STSM outputs approach
authentic Mandarin speaking-rate realizations, rather than only
matching a signal-level time-scaling operation. Under this set-
ting, waveform-level similarity alone does not fully reflect per-
formance, especially for Mandarin where pitch–timing coordi-
nation is tied to lexical tone realization. Temporal and pitch-
related metrics, such as duration MAE and F0 correlation, there-
fore provide clearer insight into how well a system preserves
rhythmic structure and tonal consistency. This highlights the
importance of alignment-aware and tone-aware evaluation for
Mandarin STSM.
Based on these results, Mandarin STSM evaluation should
rely on several complementary metrics rather than a single
score. CER is useful for measuring linguistic content preser-
vation, but it does not fully capture perceptual quality or tonal
faithfulness. Similarly, DNSMOS, PESQ, and STOI reflect
quality-related aspects but may not reveal timing and pitch-
contour errors. Duration MAE and F0 correlation are therefore
important for identifying rate-dependent degradation in rhythm
and tonal consistency, especially at extreme speaking rates.
Although GRATS enables controlled and reproducible
benchmarking, it is limited to read speech from Taiwan Man-
darin speakers. In addition, objective metrics do not fully re-
place human perception, especially for naturalness, tonal faith-
fulness, speaker identity stability, and target-rate appropriate-
ness. Therefore, we consider subjective listening tests and
broader speaking-style coverage important directions for future
work.
7. Conclusion
We introduced GRATS, a naturally recorded parallel multi-
speaker Mandarin dataset for benchmarking time-scale mod-
ification under realistic speaking-rate variation. By provid-
ing authentic target-rate recordings, GRATS enables evaluation
against natural references rather than artificially time-scaled
signals, especially since realized speaking rates do not perfectly
match nominal targets even under controlled prompting. Exper-
iments across classical and neural baselines show that STSM
performance in Mandarin is inherently multi-dimensional, with
clear trade-offs between intelligibility, perceptual quality, tem-
poral alignment, and pitch consistency. Extreme speaking rates
increased duration errors and reduced F0 correlation, effects
that are less visible when artificial references are used for eval-
uation. The dataset and benchmarking scripts will be released
through a controlled-access request process on the project page.
Future work includes subjective listening tests and broader
speaking styles and dialects.
