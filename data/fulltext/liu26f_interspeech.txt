Abstract
Recent query-based open-vocabulary sound event detection
(SED) is promising beyond closed-set models. Yet most meth-
ods only use query vectors for retrieval without semantic under-
standing of events, thus limiting recognition of novel classes.
To tackle this, we propose a semantic-anchor-based method.
Specifically, we learn a set of semantic anchor vectors to serve
as semantic-level reference tokens, so arbitrary events can be
better understood by attending to the anchors. We also intro-
duce bidirectional-attention to enhance query-feature interac-
tion for more effective retrieval, and design tailored query aug-
mentation to improve robustness. Experiments on AudioSet-
Strong show superior accuracy in recognizing novel classes,
achieving 34.9 PSDS under the open-vocabulary setting and
surpassing prior open-vocabulary SED models. Moreover,
cross-dataset evaluation on DESED validates our method’s
strong generalization, and notably in zero-shot, we achieve 44.1
PSDS1, even surpassing DESED-supervised baseline.
Index Terms: sound event detection, open-vocabulary, seman-
tic anchor, audio-language models
1. Introduction
Sound event detection (SED) identifies overlapping sound event
categories and temporal boundaries in audio clips. SED has
been widely applied in smart homes [1] and intelligent driv-
ing [2]. Recently, SED has also attracted increasing attention
in audio and multimodal large language models (LLMs), where
SED is commonly used as a benchmark that enables LLMs to be
aware of surrounding acoustic events [3, 4, 5]. However, most
existing SED methods follow a closed-set assumption [6, 7, 8],
restricting detection to predefined classes and thereby strug-
gling to detect unseen events in real-world acoustic scenarios.
To overcome these limitations, open-vocabulary learning
has been introduced and further advanced. It was first estab-
lished in vision–language models (VLM), exemplified by CLIP
[9], which aligns visual and textual representations. In the au-
dio domain, MS-CLAP [10] pioneered large-scale audio–text
pretraining, and LAION-CLAP [11] further strengthened this
paradigm by scaling up training data. Building on these ad-
vances, several studies have explored applying open-vocabulary
learning to sound event detection (SED). [12] explicitly incor-
porates temporal modeling tailored for the localization task, but
depends only on computing the similarity between query (text)
and feature (audio), making it vulnerable to modality mismatch.
MGA-CLAP [13] introduces a modality-shared codebook to
alleviate modality mismatch, but still retains the dual-stream
CLAP architecture and clip-level contrastive training paradigm,

ulates the feature-decoding process with queries to enhance
query–feature interaction, but its understanding of events still
relies on the CLAP text encoder, heavily limiting generaliza-

localization and introduces dedicated cross-modal modules to

severely from cross-modal mismatch.
In this paper, inspired by the adoption of learnable class
embeddings as class-level reference tokens in vision models
[16], we propose a semantic-anchor-based framework for open-
vocabulary SED. Specifically, we learn semantic anchor vec-
tors to serve as semantic-level reference tokens, allowing event
query vectors to attend to these anchors via self-attention for
semantic-level event understanding. Additionally, motivated
by the effectiveness in CV [17, 18], we introduce bidirec-
tional cross-attention to further enhance query–feature interac-
tion while substantially improving cross-modal alignment be-
tween text queries and audio features. Finally, we design a novel
query augmentation strategy that leverages LLMs to enrich text-
form event descriptions, together with random sampling during
training, jointly improving robustness and generalization.
Extensive experiments are conducted to evaluate our
method under two settings: open-vocabulary detection and
cross-dataset detection. Specifically, in open-vocabulary ex-
periments on AudioSet-Strong, we more faithfully simulate
real-world open-vocabulary scenarios (Sec. 3.1). Our method
achieves 34.9 PSDS on novel classes, outperforming prior
open-vocabulary SED models, confirming the effectiveness of
our method in open-vocabulary scenarios. Additionally, in
cross-dataset evaluation on DESED, our method achieves a
zero-shot PSDS1 score of 44.1, even surpassing the DESED-
supervised baseline [19], sufficiently demonstrating the strong
generalization of our method.
2. Methodology

as semantic-level reference tokens, which helps the model in-
terpret novel events unseen during training as combinations
of these references and enables more precise open-vocabulary
sound event detection. As illustrated in Figure 1, we treat open-
vocabulary sound event detection as a query-based retrieval
task. The input consists of an audio clip to be analyzed and
event queries. Events can be provided as either textual descrip-
tions or audio examples containing the target event. An audio
encoder extracts features from the detected audio. A query en-
coder encodes text or audio queries into event query vectors,
which are then concatenated with a set of learnable semantic

2.1. Audio Encoder
The audio encoder is used to encode the input spectrogram into
a temporal feature sequence. The output audio features are de-
noted as F = [ f1, f2,..., fT ]∈ RT×D, where T denotes the
number of frames andD is the feature dimension. In this work,
we adopt two widely used pre-trained audio spectrogram Trans-
former models, PaSST [20] and HTS-AT [21], as the encoder.
2.2. Query Encoder
The query encoder is responsible for generating event query
vectors for the events of interest, accepting either text or au-
dio forms. Given N queried events, the query encoder outputs
N event query vectors, denoted as Q = [ q1, q2,..., qN ]∈
RN×D. Leveraging the cross-modal alignment capability of
CLAP, we adopt a pre-trained MGA-CLAP [13], which com-
prises a CLAP audio encoder and a CLAP text encoder.
For an audio-described event (i.e., multiple audio clips con-
taining the target event are available), the audio clips are fed into
the CLAP audio encoder, and the output features are average-
pooled along the temporal dimension to obtain the event query
vector. For text-described event, since CLAP is trained on
natural-language textual descriptions, we first employ a large
language model (LLM) to perform textual augmentation to
better exploit CLAP’s encoding capability. Specifically, for
each event, six detailed, event-tailored descriptions with diverse
styles are generated, and one description is randomly sampled
per training instance and fed into the CLAP text encoder as the
event query vector. Notably, this random sampling strategy en-
courages the model to focus on the underlying event seman-
tics rather than surface textual variations, thereby enhancing
the generalization and representational robustness of the learned
Semantic Anchor Vectors.
Most importantly, we introduce a set of learnable Seman-
tic Anchor Vectors as semantic-level reference tokens, thus fa-
cilitating the interpretation of novel events at inference time,
denoted as S = [ s1, s2,..., sK ] ∈ RK×D. Finally, we
concatenate the semantic anchor vectors with the event query
vectors to obtain the output of the query encoder, denoted as
[s1, s2,..., sK, q1, q2,..., qN ].
For efficiency, we pre-compute and store event query vec-
tors offline for all training classes in both text and audio modal-
ities. Additionally, we use the LLM for textual augmentation
only during training. At inference, each class is converted
into a natural-language sentence using the template "sound of
{class}" and then encoded by the CLAP text encoder.
2.3. Anchor-guided Bidirectional Decoder
The anchor-guided bidirectional decoder is stacked with two
bidirectional-attention decoder blocks. Each decoder block
consists of a query self-attention layer, an audio self-attention
layer, a query-to-audio cross-attention layer, an audio-to-query
cross-attention layer, and feedforward networks (FFN) applied
independently to the query and audio streams. Residual con-
nections and layer normalization are applied to all layers.
2.3.1. Query Self-Attention
Query self-attention allows each event query vector to focus
on anchors that are semantically related, thereby enabling the
model to better handle novel classes rather than struggling to
generalize. Notably, we apply a tailored attention mask in this
layer: event query vectors are mutually invisible, so each event
query vector is only allowed to attend to the semantic anchor
vectors. This design prevents cross-event information leakage
and forces the model to interpret each event through the an-

3. Experiments
3.1. Experiment settings
We conduct comprehensive evaluations of our method on two
benchmark datasets, AudioSet-Strong and DESED. Specifi-
cally, AudioSet-Strong is used for open-vocabulary evaluation
as well as ablation studies, while DESED is employed to assess
cross-dataset generalization.
AudioSet-Strong. AudioSet-Strong [24] is a large-scale
strongly labeled SED dataset with 407 sound event classes,
comprising 93,927 clips in the training set and 14,203 clips in
the evaluation set. Notably, we split all 407 events into 308
common classes and 99 rare classes based on their total dura-
tion in the training set, using a threshold of 6 minutes (shorter as
rare, longer as common). We adopt an open-vocabulary setting
as follows: the model is trained only on the common classes,
while evaluation is conducted on all classes. Since the rare
classes are unseen during training, performance on them bet-
ter reflects the practical open-vocabulary scenario, making the
results more convincing.
DESED. DESED [25] is a dataset specifically designed for
SED, consisting of 10 event classes. The training set contains
1578 weakly labeled clips, 3470 strongly labeled clips, 10000
synthetic strongly labeled clips, and 14412 unlabeled clips. The
validation set contains 1168 strongly labeled clips.
Evaluation Metrics. We evaluate performance using PSDS
[26]. Specifically, for experiments on AudioSet-Strong, we use
PSDS1 [19] but omit the class-variance penalty term follow-
ing [27, 28], as it is designed for more balanced datasets. We
report the overall PSDS and the rare-class PSDSr on AudioSet-
Strong, with PSDS r as the primary metric since it better re-
flects the model’s open-vocabulary capability. For experiments
on DESED, we use the standard PSDS1.
Implementation Details . We adopt two training settings
based on query modality: text and audio. For PaSST, we use
attention pooling to compress the output frequency dimension.
We train with 40K steps per epoch and a batch size of 32, freez-
ing the audio encoder for the first 20K steps. AdamW is used
with a learning rate of 1.5× 10−5 for the audio encoder and
2× 10−4 for the remaining components. We use 400 seman-
tic anchor vectors (set by ablations) with embedding dimension
D = 384 . For fine-tuning on DESED, we adopt the mean-
teacher [29] semi-supervised strategy, following [6]. A median

3.2. Results
3.2.1. Comparison with other methods under the open-
vocabulary setting.
We compare our method with other open-vocabulary SED
methods. For a fair comparison, since LAION-CLAP and
MGA-CLAP are not trained on AudioSet-Strong, we fine-tune
them on AudioSet-Strong; for FlexSED and DASM, we report
the results in their papers. As shown in Table 1, our method
consistently surpasses prior models, strongly validating our ef-
fectiveness. Models using audio-query achieve the best perfor-
mance, since audio queries share the same modality with the
input audio, without cross-modal alignment issues. However,
the text-query setting is more challenging due to the modality
mismatch between text queries and audio input, and even the
prior best model, DASM, suffers severely: with PaSST as the
backbone, DASM’s PSDS r drops from 32.7 (audio-query) to
only 23.3 (text-query), indicating an alarming 9.4 performance
gap. In contrast, our method alleviates this issue, reducing the
gap to only 2.9. Further with HTS-AT, we achieve a superior
PSDSr of 32.3, outperforming LAION-CLAP (+16.2), MGA-
CLAP (+9.4), FlexSED (+4.5), and DASM (+2.2). These re-
sults demonstrate that our effective textual augmentation and
rich cross-modal interaction induced by bidirectional-attention
effectively mitigate cross-modal mismatch.

3.2.3. Cross-dataset experiments on DESED.
To assess cross-dataset generalization, we evaluate models pre-
trained on AudioSet-Strong on the DESED dataset, as shown
in Table 3 (Query type ’None’ denotes closed-set models). In
the zero-shot setting, our method achieves an impressive zero-
shot PSDS1 score of 44.1, outperforming prior works and even
surpassing the DESED-supervised DCASE baseline, which di-
rectly highlights our strong generalization. We attribute this
advantage primarily to the semantic anchor: it provides a sta-
ble semantic-level reference, enabling reliable interpretation of
novel events even on unseen datasets. Additionally, the text-
query setting achieves better zero-shot results, likely because
the text modality is more generalizable, whereas the audio
modality suffers from larger domain shifts across datasets. For
a more comprehensive comparison, we also report fine-tuned
results on DESED. The results show that our method outper-
forms closed-set models trained on DESED and prior open-set
models, further validating the effectiveness of our method.
4. Conclusion
In this paper, we propose a semantic-anchor-based open-
vocabulary sound event detection framework. To enable more
accurate detection of arbitrary sound events, we present three
main contributions: introducing semantic anchor vectors as re-
liable semantic-level reference tokens, leveraging bidirectional-
attention to strengthen query–audio interaction, and incorpo-
rating a tailored textual augmentation strategy during training.
Extensive experiments on AudioSet and DESED demonstrate
strong robustness and generalization to novel events, validating
the effectiveness of the proposed method.
