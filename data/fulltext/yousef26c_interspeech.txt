Measuring Vocal Efficiency in Daily Life in Patients with Voice Disorders Using 
Wireless Accelerometer and Microphone Sensors

1 Center for Laryngeal Surgery and Voice Rehabilitation, Massachusetts General Hospital, USA

Abstract 
Voice disorders are linked to inefficient phonation, with vocal 
efficiency (VE) measured in clinic. We studied ecological vocal 
efficiency (EVE) by estimating daily-life VE as SPL/Ps 
(dB/dB) using a wireless dual-sensor voice monitor: subglottal 
pressure (Ps) predicted from a neck -surface accelerometer 
(ACC) and sound pressure level (SPL) from a microphone. 14 
females participated (7 with vocal fold nodules; 7 matched 
controls). In lab, VE was computed via a /p/-vowel sequence 
(loud-to-soft; 3 pitch levels). Participants completed 3 days of 
ambulatory monitoring (12 h/day), and EVE was computed. 
Patients showed lower EVE and reduced variability than 
controls in daily life (4.1 vs 4.7, effect size r = 0.67; SD 0.4 vs 
1.0, r = 0.91); variability metrics separated groups better in field 
than in lab. Patients spent significantly more time outside the 
EVE normative range than controls (92 vs 36%; p = 0.03; 
r = 0.81). Preliminary results support EVE as an ecological 
marker of vocal function. 
Index Terms: vocal efficiency, ambulatory voice monitoring, 
phonotraumatic vocal hyperfunction, neck -surface 
accelerometer, subglottal pressure 
1. Introduction 
Voice disorders are often characterized by inefficient voice use 
that can limit effective communication in everyday life [1]. A 
long-standing goal in clinical voice science is to quantify vocal 
efficiency (VE) in physiologically meaningful terms; for 
example, the subglottal pressure (Ps) component of VE is an 
essential aerodynamic measure for clinical voice assessment 
[2]. VE represents how effectively the laryngeal system 
converts aerodynamic power generated by the lungs into 
radiated acoustic power during speaking [3]. VE was 
traditionally quantified as the ratio between sound pressure 
level (SPL, referring to acoustic output) and a combination of 
glottal airflow (AFLOW) and Ps [2]. Because airflow estimates 
can be relatively variable compared to SPL and Ps, several 
studies have adopted a simplified VE formula that omits 
AFLOW, proposing the SPL/Ps ratio [4].  
Among VE formulations, the SPL/Ps ratio has shown 
clinical utility: it can distinguish patients with voice disorders 
from vocally healthy individuals [4-7]. For example, patients 
with glottal cancer have shown significantly lower VE than 
healthy individuals [8]. SPL/Ps is also sensitive to within -
patient treatment effects, with significant pre–post changes 
reported after surgery and/or voice therapy  [4, 7-10]; for 
instance, VE increased significantly after surgical removal of 
vocal fold lesions [4, 8]. Although VE metrics—particularly the 
SPL/Ps ratio—show promise for assessing voice disorders and 
treatment-related change, most supporting evidence comes 
from laboratory-based clinical protocols [7]. These protocols 
typically use simplified voice tasks (e.g., repeated /p/-vowel 
syllables at varying loudness) to empirically measure intraoral 
pressure and indirectly estimate Ps, alongside microphone -
derived SPL, to compute the VE ratio [4, 9]. Neither the in-lab 
environmental conditions nor the speaking tasks performed 
reflect the real-world voice use during natural communication. 
This limits our ability to characterize how efficiently patients 
with voice disorders use their voice in everyday life. 
Recent advances in ambulatory voice monitoring provide 
an opportunity to extend VE assessment beyond brief in-clinic 
snapshots to natural communication in daily life [11-18]. Prior 
work has established noninvasive methods to estimate Ps from 
anterior neck-surface vibrations recorded by an accelerometer 
(ACC). These methods use in-lab protocols to measure intraoral 
pressure and relate it to ACC amplitude, enabling speaker -
specific calibration from ACC features to Ps [19-21]. In daily 
life, ACC recordings are robust to environmental noise, 
preserve privacy, and can be processed efficiently for 
continuous monitoring, enabling ongoing noninvasive 
estimation of Ps [19-21]. When synchronized with a 
microphone-based estimate of SPL in the field, this framework 
offers continuous estimation of the SPL/Ps ratio during 
everyday voice use, providing an ecological measure of VE. 
Studying an ecological vocal efficiency (EVE) measure can 
help estimate VE during everyday activities to quantify 
clinically meaningful changes in vocal function and behavior. 
Using a state-of-the-art, dual-sensor, wireless voice monitor, 
we measure vocal SPL with a microphone and estimate Ps with 
an ACC, yielding EVE (SPL/Ps) during daily life. We 
investigate whether EVE differentiates individuals with voice 
disorders from vocally healthy controls; we hypothesize that 
patients will show lower mean EVE and reduced variability, 
consistent with inefficient and restricted vocal behavior in 
natural settings. We additionally performed standard in -lab 
protocols to compute conventional VE measures, enabling 
comparison between in-lab and daily life estimates. 
2. Methods 
2.1. Participants 
The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review 
Board at Mass General Brigham. Fourteen adult females 
participated: seven patients with a clinical diagnosis of vocal 
nodules (mean age 26.3 years, SD 6.4; range 18.4–29.1) and 
seven age-matched female healthy controls with confirmed 
vocally healthy status (mean age 25.0 years, SD 3.8; range 
18.9–39.6). All participants provided informed consent and 
were invited for an in-lab visit at Massachusetts General 
Hospital. During this visit, after reporting their medical history 
and completing multiple questionnaires, patients completed a 
comprehensive clinical assessment to verify their current 
diagnostic status, including an imaging examination of the 
larynx and vocal folds, an auditory-perceptual voice quality 
evaluation, and standard protocols for acoustic and 
aerodynamic voice assessments [2]. The control group was 
considered eligible if they reported no history of voice 
disorders. All controls were also examined using an endoscopic 
technique to confirm healthy vocal folds with no abnormalities.

2.2. Data collection 
Data were collected in two phases. First, participants completed 
a calibration session during their in-lab visit in a sound-treated 
booth to concurrently record three signals that were utilized in 
this paper: acoustic, intraoral pressure, and ACC. Participants 
wore the following equipment: 1) a head-mounted condenser 
microphone, 15 cm away from the lips, to capture vocal sound 
pressure level (SPL); 2) a pneumotachograph face mask 
(Glottal Enterprises, Syracuse, NY, USA) with an intraoral tube 
placed in the mouth and connected to a low-bandwidth air 
pressure sensor to estimate intraoral pressure; 3) the voice 
monitoring device including a miniature wireless, uniaxial 
ACC sensor—attached midway between the thyroid 
prominence and the top of the sternum using double -sided 
tape—to measure skin-surface vibration amplitude. The 
microphone and intraoral pressure signals were low-pass 
filtered at 8 kHz and then sampled at 20 kHz (16 -bit 
quantization), while the ACC signal was sampled at a rate of 
8,889 Hz (16-bit quantization). The microphone, pressure, and 
ACC signals were calibrated to pascals (Pa), centimeters of 
water (cm H2O), and vibration acceleration (cm/s2). 
After setting up each individual with the equipment, they 
were instructed to produce /pa/, /pi/, and /pu/ syllables, each 
repeated at a range of loudness from loud to soft; all the p-vowel 
sequences were repeated twice in three pitch conditions 
(comfortable, high, low) [2, 22]. The measured intraoral 
pressure during /p/ occlusion served as a proxy for Ps. Ignoring 
the beginning and ending utterances in the sequence, SPL from 
the microphone signal and RMS ACC amplitude from the ACC 
signal were computed for each vowel; the corresponding Ps was 
computed as an average of the peak intraoral-pressure plateau 
amplitudes immediately before and after each vowel segment. 
After converting SPL and Ps to dB units, we then computed an 
in-lab VE ratio [SPL (dB SPL) / Ps (dB re cm H2O)]; we only 
considered the comfortable pitch condition to mimic clinical 
examination. In addition, to predict Ps from the ACC signal in 
daily life, individual-specific regression models were built 
using the in-lab data to find the calibration factors predicting Ps 
from the ACC RMS: Ps = (slope) × ACC RMS + (intercept) 
[19, 21]; examples are shown in Figure 1.

Second, participants received a neckband voice monitoring 
kit for the daily life phase of data collection. Kit components 
(Figure 2) and wear instructions were provided to participants. 
Participants wore a wireless neckband voice monitor that 
included (i) the neck-surface ACC affixed at the midline of the 
anterior neck and (ii) a built-in miniature microphone mounted 
on the right side of the neckband. The ambulatory kit also 
included an Android smartphone, a backup ACC sensor, 
charger, and extra double-sided tape. The neckband streamed 
both ACC and microphone signals wirelessly to the 
smartphone, which recorded the synchronized data during 
monitoring for subsequent analysis. Participants completed 
three waking days of ambulatory monitoring (around 12 h/day) 
during their daily activities using the provided monitoring kit.

2.3. Signal processing and ecological vocal efficiency 
estimation 
Although the raw ACC signal was stored during ambulatory 
monitoring, it does not contain intelligible speech content. To 
further protect participant privacy, the built-in microphone was 
used only to compute vocal SPL, and the raw audio signal was 
not stored. Although lab and field SPL were measured with 
different microphones, both were calibrated to the same 15-cm 
SPL reference to reduce discrepancy. For ambulatory 
recordings, ACC RMS and SPL (dB SPL) were computed in 
approximately 50-ms non-overlapping frames. An ACC-based 
voice activity detection algorithm automatically classified 
frames as unvoiced, voiced speech, or voiced singing [23]; 
analyses were restricted to non-singing voiced speech frames. 
For retained voiced-speech frames, we extracted SPL from the 
aligned microphone signal and ACC RMS amplitude (cm/s2) 
from the ACC signal; we also computed voicing time as the 
total duration of these frames across the day.  
We then used ACC RMS to estimate subglottal pressure in 
the field (Ps; cm H 2O) using each participant’s in -lab 
calibration factors between ACC and Ps (e.g., Figure 1). This 
ACC–Ps calibration method was previously validated with 
average Ps errors of approximately 2 cm H₂O [19]. Finally, 
measured SPL and estimated Ps were converted to dB units and 
ecological vocal efficiency (EVE) was computed as: EVE = 
[SPL (dB SPL)] / [Ps (dB re cm H2O)]. EVE distributions were 
summarized separately for in-lab and daily life conditions by 
pooling all retained voiced -speech frames across each 
monitoring day within each group and computing the median, 
standard deviation (SD), interquartile range (IQR), 95th 
percentile (p95), skewness (skew), and kurtosis (kurt). 
For each patient, a personalized normative EVE range was 
defined from their matched control (mean ± 1 SD). Patient 
voiced-speech frames were labeled as low/typical/high relative 
to this range, and the percent voicing time above and below the 
range was computed; the same thresholds were applied to the 
matched control for paired comparison. The same normative-
range labeling and time-outside-range calculation was applied 
to the in-lab EVE measures to enable direct in-lab versus daily-
life comparisons. Statistical analyses were performed on per-
participant summaries, not frame-level observations, to 
preserve participant-level independence. Patient–control 
differences in both the distributional EVE metrics and the 
normative-range measures were tested using paired Wilcoxon 
signed-rank tests, and effect sizes were reported as Wilcoxon r.

3. Results  
Figure 3 provides an example of how the ambulatory system 
captures within-day dynamics for a patient in terms of SPL, Ps, 
and their ratio (EVE) during natural communication (top panel). 
Across 11.7 hours of monitoring, SPL and Ps show clear time-
varying patterns, with relatively steady periods interrupted by 
short bursts and step changes. Overall, SPL and Ps track each 
other for much of the day; for example, after 19:38 (7:38 PM), 
the two signals follow similar trajectories and EVE shows a 
comparable pattern. At other times, EVE shows sharper 
changes when SPL and Ps diverge. Around 13:48, EVE jumps 
because SPL increases while Ps remains relatively low. In 
contrast, between 13:48–15:28, Ps increases while SPL stays 
comparatively lower, producing a dip in EVE. The voicing-
percentage panel adds context by showing how long these 
behaviors persist. For example, later in the day (around 20:28), 
voicing time increases and EVE fluctuates, whereas earlier 
(around 14:38) voicing time is high but EVE remains lower, 
indicating sustained voicing during a less efficient period.  
Table 1 summarizes group-level EVE distribution statistics 
for controls (Ctrl.) and patients (Pt.) in lab and daily life, with 
p-values and effect sizes (r) for Ctrl–Pt comparisons. In both 
laboratory and daily-life settings, patients showed lower 
median EVE than controls, with significant separation in the 
laboratory (3.94 vs 4.83; p = 0.03) but not in daily life (4.07 vs 
4.68; p = 0.08). Patients showed a more restricted EVE range 
than controls in both settings. Dispersion differences were less 
clear in the laboratory, with SD significant (p = 0.047) and IQR 
not significant (p = 0.22). In daily life, both SD and IQR 
showed significant separation between patients and controls 
(p = 0.02) with a large effect size (0.91). 
Percentile-based summaries showed a similar upper-tail 
pattern (p95) in both settings, with controls reaching higher 
EVE values than patients (p = 0.05; r = 0.75). In contrast, the 
lower-tail statistic (p5) separated groups mainly in lab 
(p = 0.02; r = 0.91); in daily life, controls extended to even 
lower EVE values than in lab, reducing the p5 separation from 
patients. Skewness and kurtosis showed clearer group 
separation in the laboratory (r ≥ 0.67), but this separation was 
not significant in daily life. In addition, patients in daily life 
exhibited distributions closer to symmetric whereas controls 
remained right-skewed.

4. Discussion  
Prior work has shown that VE metrics—particularly SPL/Ps—
have the potential to distinguish voice disorders and track 
treatment effects [7]; but this evidence comes from brief,  
laboratory tasks. In this paper, we extend this contribution to 
daily life by studying an ecological VE measure, EVE, that 
continuously estimates SPL/Ps using a dual-sensor ambulatory 
system (microphone-derived SPL and ACC-estimated Ps) 
during everyday voice use. We evaluated the capability of EVE 
to distinguish patients with voice disorders from vocally 
healthy matched controls, and compared EVE derived from 
daily-life monitoring with standard in-lab VE measurements.  
Ambulatory monitoring reveals substantial within -day 
variability in vocal behavior, consistent with prior ambulatory 
studies showing that ACC-derived measures capture time-
varying patterns of voice use across daily activities [24-27]. 
Extending this perspective to vocal efficiency, results showed 
that the relationship between SPL and Ps changes over the day, 
producing time-varying EVE that is difficult to capture with 
brief laboratory tasks or a single average value. As in Figure 3, 
periods of elevated SPL are not always accompanied by 
proportional changes in Ps, leading to sustained shifts in EVE 
across the day. By integrating EVE with voicing percentage, 
ambulatory data also add a temporal dimension—showing not 
only whether efficiency is atypical, but for how long—thereby 
identifying extended periods when patients phonate under 
atypical VE patterns in daily life. 
In line with our hypothesis, patients tended to show lower 
EVE than controls. However, the median group difference was 
significant in the lab but not in daily life (Table 1). One likely 
reason is that daily-life recordings are much more variable and 
depend heavily on context (different speaking situations, 
loudness demands, and background noise). When we average 
the data, the variability can mask the group difference. This 
matches what others have reported in ambulatory voice studies: 
measures that often separate patients from controls in the lab 
(e.g., SPL, fundamental frequency, cepstral peak prominence) 
sometimes show little or no group difference when summarized 
over a week in daily life [25, 28]. Our results suggest that 
simply averaging may not be the best way to analyze ecological 
VE. Instead, daily-life data may be more informative for how 
variable the behavior is. In Table 1, variability measures (SD 
and IQR) separated groups more clearly in daily life than in the 
lab. Patients showed a more restricted EVE range in daily life, 
with tighter clustering and lower skewness (closer to 
symmetric), which was less evident in the lab. One possible 
reason is that lab tasks may prompt patients to push their voice, 
reaching ranges they may avoid in everyday communication. 
This restricted daily-life range is consistent with reduced vocal 
flexibility in vocal nodules, leaving less room to a djust 
efficiency during routine voice use. 
The lab–field differences became clearer when we 
examined each matched patient–control pair using a customized 
normative EVE range for each patient. Although this method 
may be sensitive to control variability, it reduced confounds 
such as age and sex. It captured not only how atypical a 
patient’s vocal efficiency is, but also for how long, by 
quantifying the percent of voicing time spent outside the typical 
EVE range. Compared with a single median, the pair -level 
analysis showed that patients spent significantly more time 
outside their normative EVE range (median = 92%), making 
group differences easier to interpret . Most patients also 
phonated below the typical range—an observation that was less 
clear from median values alone. This comparison also revealed 
heterogeneity: in 3 of 7 patients, the direction of deviation 
differed between lab and daily life, suggesting that in -lab 
behavior may not always reflect everyday voice use for some 
patients. These differences were more apparent for EVE than 
for SPL or Ps alone, supporting the added value of the ratio for 
capturing clinically meaningful vocal efficiency in daily life. 
This study is limited by the small sample size, which 
restricts generalizability. In daily life, ACC sensor movement 
might affect Ps estimates, and SPL may still vary with head 
orientation or clothing movement. Future work should validate 
EVE in larger, more diverse samples and across a broader range 
of voice disorders to establish clinically meaningful reference 
ranges. Longitudinal studies are also needed to test whether 
EVE tracks treatment-related change (e.g., phonosurgery, voice 
therapy) and to identify the most responsive EVE features.
