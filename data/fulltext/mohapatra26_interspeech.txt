Influence of Vocal Tract Curvature on Speech Acoustics: A Three-Dimensional
FEM Analysis

Abstract
V ocal tract curvature influences speech acoustics by shifting for-
mant frequencies and exciting higher-order transverse modes.
Nevertheless, many physics-based acoustic models approxi-
mate the vocal tract as a straight three-dimensional (3D) tube
with a varying cross-sectional area. Although wave propagation
in realistic vocal tract geometries has been studied previously,
the specific effects of tract curvature on resonance frequencies
remain largely unexplored. In this study, a 3D finite element
(FE) wave solver is employed to characterize the frequency re-
sponse of curved vocal tracts up to 14 kHz. The degree of cur-
vature is systematically varied for vocal tracts with both uni-
form and non-uniform cross-sectional areas. For uniform cross-
sections, curvature has a negligible effect on the frequency re-
sponse. In contrast, for non-uniform cross-sections, curvature
excites higher-order transverse modes at high frequencies and
produces noticeable formant shifts above 10 kHz.
Index Terms: speech production, vocal tract curvature, articu-
latory speech synthesis, finite element method
1. Introduction
The vocal tract cavity—extending from the larynx to the mouth
opening—has a complex three-dimensional (3D) tubular shape.
This intricate geometry plays a crucial role in shaping the
acoustic characteristics of the human voice by directly influ-
encing how sound waves propagate and resonate within the
upper airway cavity. The geometrical complexity of a vocal
tract stems from several factors, including its irregular cross-
sections, curved configuration, and additional side branches.
These geometric features are evident in its volumetric repre-
sentation, captured through various imaging modalities, such
as magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) [1, 2] and computed to-
mography (CT) [3]. While numerous studies have investigated
the acoustic effects of irregular cross-sectional shapes [4, 5],
side cavities [6, 7, 8], and lip radiation [9], the acoustic con-
sequences of vocal tract curvature remain largely unexplored.
Understanding the effects of a bent air column in curved vo-
cal tract configurations is important for the development of an
accurate speech production model.
Conventional vocal tract models either neglect tract cur-
vature entirely or incorporate it within realistic 3D vocal tract
shapes, making its acoustic influence difficult to isolate sys-
tematically. For example, one-dimensional (1D) [10] and two-
dimensional (2D) physics-based acoustic wave solvers [6, 11]
represent vocal tract geometry using a 1D area function de-
rived from volumetric imaging data [12, 13]. Since 1D solvers
describe wave propagation solely in the longitudinal direction,
they cannot model duct curvature. Furthermore, conventional
2D solvers employ a nonlinear area function scaling approach
to approximate the acoustic response of the corresponding 3D
tube. This scaling method applies to straight-tube representa-
tions, and its extension to curved tract geometries is not straight-
forward. Consequently, 1D and 2D models typically approx-
imate the vocal tract as a straight tube [4, 5], thereby failing
to approximate curvature-induced wave propagation and reso-
nance phenomena that may occur in realistic 3D geometries.
Alternatively, the acoustics response of realistic tracts has been
investigated using 3D numerical schemes [4, 14, 15]. In these
studies, the vocal tract geometry—including its natural curva-
ture and anatomical irregularities—is modeled directly using
volumetric data. Although such models inherently account for
tract curvature, they do not allow a systematic investigation of
curvature-specific acoustic effects. As a result, it is difficult to
distinguish spectral features arising from curvature from those
caused by other geometric and acoustic factors, including irreg-
ular cross-sectional shapes, side cavities, and mouth radiation.
A few studies have attempted to characterize curvature-
specific acoustic effects. Sondhi employed an analytical ap-
proach to derive the eigen frequencies (formants) of bent vo-
cal tracts with uniform rectangular cross-sections [16]. In that
study, the tract length was fixed at 17 cm, while tract cross-
sectional area and bending angle were systematically varied to
examine their effects on formant frequencies. Sondhi’s analy-
sis revealed a slight upward shift in the first two formants and a
downward shift in the third and fourth formants for bent tracts
compared to straight configurations. However, this analytical
method is limited to simplified geometries and cannot readily
accommodate bent tubes with arbitrary cross-sectional areas, as
in human vocal tracts. However, numerical studies of bent vocal
tracts [4, 17] yield formant shifts that differ from Sondhi’s an-
alytical solutions. Beyond airway acoustics, the effects of duct
curvature have also been investigated in the context of wood-
wind instrument modeling [18, 19], where curvature-induced
resonance shifts were found to be strongly frequency depen-
dent. However, these findings cannot be directly extended to
speech acoustics because of the substantially greater geometric
complexity of the vocal tracts.
In this paper, we present a systematic investigation of cur-
vature effects on vocal tract acoustics using the finite element
method (FEM). Compared to the regular domain discretization
schemes (e.g., FDTD [5], DWM [4] and TLM [20]), FEM al-
lows smooth representation of curved geometries through con-
forming mesh elements. Unlike previous work, we simulate
curved tract-like tubes with both uniform and spatially vary-
ing cross-sectional areas across a range of physiologically rel-
evant bending angles. We also alter the curvature intensity to
determine the effect of sharp bending in vocal tract acoustics.
For each tract geometry, we analyze the resulting transfer func-
tions and pressure (acoustic) field distributions to characterize

2. Method
2.1. Modeling Bent Vocal Tracts
We model bent vocal tracts with varying levels of geometri-
cal complexity to investigate curvature-induced acoustic effects.
As illustrated in Figure 1, the geometries are classified into
four categories based on two factors: cross-sectional area vari-
ation along the centerline and curvature intensity. The cross-
sectional area uniformity indicates whether the tube diameter
remains constant or varies along the centerline. By curvature
intensity, we refer to the rate of change of the tract center-
line curvature at a given point. For numerical modeling, we
discretize each vocal tract configuration into a sequence of44
cross-sections—equivalent to44cylindrical tube segments—
uniformly distributed along a centerline of fixed lengthL= 17
cm. This centerline length corresponds to the average vocal
tract length of an adult male speaker articulating the vowel [A]
[21]. By keeping the centerline length constant across all con-
figurations, any observed differences in the acoustic response
can be attributed solely to vocal tract curvature, rather than to
resonance shifts arising from length variations. Since vocal
tract curvature is governed by both postural and anatomical con-
straints, we systematically varied the bending angle (θ) across
60◦,90 ◦, and120 ◦ for each tract configuration. This range cap-
tures the transition of the upper airway from a predominantly
vertical to a more horizontal orientation.
We begin by analyzing bent tracts with uniform cross-
sections. In these geometries, the cross-sectional area remains
constant along the entire tract, as illustrated in Figs. 1(a) and (b).
Their geometric simplicity makes them an ideal starting point
for isolating the acoustic effects of the bent air column. Fur-
thermore, Sondhi derived an analytical solution of bent tracts
with uniform area functions [16]. Hence, they provide a valu-
able benchmark for comparison with numerical simulations.
In contrast, realistic vocal tracts have non-uniform cross-
sectional area distributions along the centerline, as shown in
Figs. 1(c) and (d). To represent such geometries, we use the
1D vocal tract area function for the vowel [A] reported by Story
[12]. The longitudinal area distribution is preserved, while the
tract length is fixed at 17 cm to ensure consistency between uni-
form and non-uniform configurations. To isolate the acoustic ef-
fects of tract curvature and avoid confounding influences from
cross-sectional shape, the present study is restricted to vocal
tracts with circular cross-sections [5]. Such configuration has
been commonly adopted in 1D [10], 2D [22], and 3D [9] vocal
tract models. The radial symmetry of circular cross-sections
eliminates higher-order acoustic modes associated with non-
circular geometries, such as tracts with elliptical or irregular
cross-sectional shapes [23].
To characterize the influence of curvature intensity, we eval-
uate the transfer functions of tract shapes with centerlines ex-
hibiting different bending angles. In this study, the curvature
intensity is defined asκ= 1/R 0, whereR 0 denotes the radius
of curvature. Based on curvature intensity, the models are clas-
sified into two types: low-curvature and high-curvature. The-
oretically, low-curvature tracts correspond to larger values of
R0, whereas high-curvature tracts are associated with smaller
R0. Low-curvature ducts are modeled by smoothly bending
straight cylindrical waveguides into circular arcs, as shown in
Figs. 1(a) and (c). With a fixed arc length (i.e., centerline length
L), the radius of curvature is determined byR 0 =L/θ. The
low-curvature tracts correspond toR 0 values between 8 cm and
16 cm. Sondhi’s analysis was limited to low-curvature bent vo-
cal tracts with circular-arc centerlines.
In realistic vocal tracts, however, anatomical posture pro-
duces more complex bending profiles, making such simplified
configurations unlikely. To better approximate realistic vocal
tract geometries, we also consider bent ducts with high curva-
ture, which are composed of cylindrical and toroidal segments.
In this configuration, only a portion of the waveguide is curved,
as illustrated in Figs. 1(b) and (d). The centerline lengthLis
partitioned into three parts: two straight cylindrical segments
of lengthsL 1 andL 3, and an intermediate toroidal segment of
lengthL 2 and radiusR 0. For these high-curvature tracts,R 0 is
fixed at 3 cm for the curved section, substantially smaller than
the radii used in the low-curvature cases.
This framework, which integrates tract shapes with varying
curvature, cross-sectional area functions, and geometric com-
plexity, provides a systematic basis for investigating the acous-
tic effects of vocal tract curvature.

Fully reflective wall boundaries produce unrealistic acous-
tic responses when simulating narrow air-filled ducts such as
the vocal tract. In practice, viscous and thermal diffusion near
the tract walls introduce energy losses that attenuate wave prop-
agation. Accordingly, wall losses are modeled using a Robin
boundary condition, which represents a semi-reflective bound-
ary. At the mouth termination, a homogeneous Dirichlet bound-
ary condition (p= 0) is imposed to represent an open-end radi-
ation condition. While this approximation neglects lip radiation
effects, it is appropriate for the present study, as the primary
objective is to examine the acoustic effect of tract curvature. A
Gaussian pulse is introduced as a source volume velocity at the
glottal end [14], and outgoing pressure samples are recorded us-
ing a virtual microphone placed3mm inside the tract exit over
a simulation duration of50ms.
2.3. Acoustic Response Analysis
For comparative analysis, the vocal tract transfer functions and
acoustic pressure distributions were evaluated for each tract
configuration. Specifically, the transfer functions of the bent
vocal tracts were compared with those of their straight counter-
parts to assess the effect of tract curvature. The transfer func-
tion was computed as the complex frequency-dependent ratio
of the acoustic pressure at the mouth (or radiated volume veloc-
ity) to the glottal volume velocity. This metric is widely used
to characterize vocal tract acoustics because it captures the for-
mant frequencies and their bandwidths. Accordingly, the trans-
fer function for each tract geometry was computed over the fre-
quency range up to 14 kHz.
To investigate the modal behavior underlying the observed
spectral features, acoustic pressure distributions were computed
at selected frequencies. A pressure distribution (pressure map)
provides a two-dimensional representation of the acoustic pres-
sure amplitude within the midsagittal plane of the vocal tract at a
given frequency, typically chosen in the vicinity of a resonance
(formant) or antiresonance. These maps enable visualization
of the spatial structure of the acoustic field and are therefore
used to identify the onset of higher-order modes in bent vocal
tracts. Accordingly, pressure amplitude distributions were com-
puted for the previously described vocal tract configurations at
selected frequencies. It is worth noting that pressure distribu-
tions are not presented for straight vocal tract geometries in this
study. As shown in prior work by Blandin et al. [24] and Moha-
patra et al. [23] (see Fig. 6), straight cylindrical vocal tracts do
not support the excitation of higher-order transverse modes, and
the acoustic field remains predominantly plane-wave in nature.

3.2. Pressure Distribution Analysis
Fig. 3 illustrates the pressure distributions inside bent tracts with
uniform and nonuniform cross-sectional areas for bending an-
gles of 60°, 90°, and 120°. It also highlights the acoustic ef-
fects associated with both low- and high-curvature configura-
tions. For the uniform cross-sectional cases, the vocal tract is
excited using a sinusoidal glottal volume velocity at the8th for-
mant frequency, obtained from the corresponding transfer func-
tions of the tracts. The resulting pressure distributions are dom-
inated by plane-wave propagation, with negligible variation in
the transverse direction. The pressure amplitude varies only
along the longitudinal direction. Even in the presence of bend-
ing, the pressure distribution pattern closely resembles that of a
one-dimensional standing wave.
In contrast, ducts with varying cross-sectional areas ex-
hibit a stronger influence of the bent air column. To extract
the pressure distribution, these geometries are excited using
their first antiresonance frequencies. V ocal tracts with low- and
high-curvature disrupt the predominantly plane-wave propaga-
tion and lead to the onset of higher-order modes, as evident in
Fig. 3. These acoustic features, however, are not simply a direct
consequence of using vocal tract area functions alone. For a
straight, cylindrical tube with nonuniform areas, the pressure
distribution does not show the change in pressure amplitude
along the transverse direction [23], even at higher frequencies.
By contrast, curved and geometrically complex ducts introduce
the perturbations necessary for higher-order modes to emerge
in the pressure distribution. This highlights the importance of
tract curvature in articulatory modeling.
4. Conclusion
This study highlights the role of vocal-tract curvature in shaping
the frequency response above8kHz, where the acoustic field
no longer follows a purely plane wave assumption. At these
frequencies, ducts with high-curvature and non-uniform cross-
sectional areas give rise to higher-order (transverse) modes.
Below8kHz, the influence of tract curvature is considerably
weaker, and the acoustic response remains largely dominated
by plane-wave propagation. In comparison, ducts with uniform
cross-sectional areas show a negligible influence of the bent air
column on their acoustic response. In future work, we plan
to validate these findings using 3D-printed physical vocal tract
models and to assess their perceptual implications.
