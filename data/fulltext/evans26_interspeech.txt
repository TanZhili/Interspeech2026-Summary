Mapping Acceptable Pronunciation Range for te reo M ¯aori through
Perceptual, Acoustic, and Marker Evaluative Data

Abstract
Pronunciation teaching assumes a canonical target. Yet speech
varies across speakers and generations, meaning targets are bet-
ter understood as ranges of acceptable pronunciation. For many
languages, including ones subject to major revitalisation efforts
like New Zealand’s te reo M ¯aori, these ranges are not docu-
mented. We developed a method triangulating acoustic mea-
sures, phonetician transcription, and fluent speaker evaluation
to map acceptable variation in M ¯aori vowels. Examining the
monophthongs /a u/ and diphthongs /ai ae au/, we found a clear
asymmetry: variants acceptable as monophthongs were pe-
nalised in diphthongs. This challenges the widespread view of
diphthongs as vowel sequences — if they were simply concate-
nations, acceptability would transfer across contexts. Our find-
ings suggest fluent speakers treat diphthongs as unit phonemes
with distinct acceptability boundaries, implying they should be
taught as distinct sounds rather than extension of monophthong
instruction.
Index Terms: pronunciation pedagogy, vowel production, ac-
ceptability judgements, te reo M¯aori
1. Introduction
Te reo M¯aori (henceforth M¯aori) is the indigenous language of
New Zealand. In a population of just over 5 million, M ¯aori is
only spoken ”fairly well” by 7.9% of the population [1, 2]. It is
a language undergoing revitalisation. From the 1950s through
to the 1980s, the number of proficient speakers of M ¯aori de-
clined sharply, placing the language in a highly endangered state
[3, Chapter 7]. Beginning in the mid-1980s, extensive revital-
isation initiatives were introduced, and these efforts have con-
tributed to a growing cohort of M ¯aori speakers who are also
fluent in English. All contemporary speakers of M¯aori are bilin-
gual. The interest in learning M ¯aori extends well beyond those
that identify as M¯aori (18% of the population [4]. Over 60% of
New Zealanders believed that it should be taught in schools in
2022 [1]. The Teaching Council of New Zealand requires that
teachers meet a standard in the use of M ¯aori to comply with
the Code of Professional Responsibility [5]. Amongst the adult
population demand to learn the language is strong too: There
are courses offered at the tertiary education level, with many
having long wait-lists [6].
In a 2024 study, Keegan [6] reflects on the ”paucity of re-
search” in teaching the pronunciation of M ¯aori. He observes
that much of the online resources give inaccurate or limited pho-
netic descriptions. Keegan argues that a knowledge of acoustic
phonetics would help both teachers and students gain insights
into language pronunciation. This has been demonstrated for
well-resourced languages such as English [7], which benefit
from extensive acoustic phonetic research. For M¯aori, however,
acoustic phonetic and perceptual studies remain limited. The
most in depth acoustic studies on M ¯aori vowels used a corpus
of 62 speakers of M¯aori, whose birth dates span 100 years, e.g.
[8, 9]. Recently, a perception study of vowel durations has been
completed [10]. Little has been done on the consonants other
than two acoustic analyses of /r/ [11, 12, 13, 14], all of which
derived data from only a small number of speakers.
An acoustic understanding of M ¯aori needs to be in-
formed by as many speakers as possible. Speech is in-
herently variable across speakers, contexts, and generations,
a fact well-established in phonetic and speech production
studies documenting segmental reduction, coarticulation, and
context-dependent realisation patterns [15]. Consequently pro-
nunciation ‘targets’ should take the form of arangeof accept-
able productions, reflecting the the variability in how sounds
may be articulated while remaining intelligible and culturally
appropriate. For global languages like English, these ranges are
well documented through large acoustic and perceptual studies.
However, such knowledge is limited for many languages, and
for M ¯aori, whilst anecdotes exist, there are no formal studies
distinguishing acceptable variation from speech error.
This paper presents some preliminary results from the first
large-scale study of acceptable variability in spoken M ¯aori. It
will present the methodology of the study, the bespoke FRED
(Formant Research for EDucation) platform we have developed,
and present a case study focusing on variability in the monoph-
thongs /a/ and /u/ and diphthongs /ai/, /ae/, and /au/. Before
that, however, it is necessary to provide more background on
what we know about the vowels in the case study.
1.1. The phonetics and phonology of M ¯aori vowels
While the five-vowel inventory of M¯aori is well established, re-
cent research suggests that the realisation of individual vowels
may be less stable than the phonemic system implies. The /u/
monophthong, for example, has attracted attention: Harlow de-
scribed /u/ as ”phonetically ...[0]” [16, p. 1]. A decade later, the
same author compared the production of /u/ to the consonant /w/
[3, p. 63]. The MAONZE project’s acoustic data help resolve
this apparent contradiction, by demonstrating that /u/ fronting
is a sound change in progress, with speakers born in the late
19th and early 20th century producing a more backed vowel
than speakers born in the 1970s–80s [17]. This diachronic shift
means that both descriptions may be accurate for their respec-
tive speaker cohorts, but it also means that L2 learners today
may be targeting a vowel whose ’correct’ realisation differs,
depending on which generation of speakers they are modelling.
Beyond /u/, additional evidence of vowel instability comes from
/e/, which appears to be raising [8, 17]. While there is no offi-
cial recognition of schwa production in M ¯aori, there is acous-

Diphthongs in M ¯aori are often described as sequences of
monophthongs [3, 19, 20], and this characterisation is reflected
in how they are often taught to learners [19]. However, re-
cent acoustic work has suggested that M ¯aori diphthongs may
be becoming distinct from simple monophthong combinations
[8], raising the question of whether this traditional description
remains adequate. If diphthongs are indeed monophthong se-
quences, we would expect each component vowel to behave
acoustically like its corresponding monophthong; if they are
emerging as unit phonemes, we might expect divergence. There
is some evidence to support the link between monophthongs
and diphthongs. Acoustic studies [8] have shown that the sec-
ond target of /au/ tracks the position of the monophthong /u/
across speaker generations: as /u/ has fronted over time, so
has the diphthong endpoint. This covariation suggests that the
diphthong and monophthong may share an underlying target.
The M¯aori vowel system also contains the diphthongs /ai/ and
/ae/, which share a common first element: the open vowel /a/,
also present in /au/. If diphthongs are indeed monophthong se-
quences, we would expect the /a/ component to vary in a similar
fashion across all three diphthongs, and to pattern with /a/ as a
monophthong. If, however, /a/ behaves differently depending
on which diphthong it appears in — or differs from its monoph-
thong counterpart — this would lend support to the view that
diphthongs function as phonemic units with their own articula-
tory targets, rather than as simple vowel concatenations. By
triangulating perceptual classifications and acoustic measure-
ments with fluent speaker evaluations, we can begin to map not
just the variation that exists, but how native speakers have inter-
nalised these targets. This has direct implications for whether
diphthongs warrant treatment as distinct phonemes in pronun-
ciation pedagogy. For effective pronunciation learning in lan-
guages subject to extensive revitalisation efforts such as M¯aori,
understanding these empirically grounded acceptable ranges is
essential, ensuring that learners are guided toward forms that are
accurate, culturally appropriate, and aligned with fluent-speaker
judgements.

2.2. Data Processing
Data processing consisted of a three-source framework. Per-
ceptual transcriptions were included to define the phonetic vari-
ability of the L2 speakers’ M ¯aori productions. Independently,
fluent M¯aori speakers provided evaluative judgement, establish-
ing a ground-truth acceptability at the syllable level. Finally,
acoustic measures established objective correlates of both the
perceptual observations and fluent-speaker evaluations.
Perceptual Analysis:A trained phonetician reviewed the
audio and alignment files using Praat. Any misaligned phoneme
boundaries were corrected, according to acoustic criteria, and
the default phoneme labels were updated to phonetic labels to
reflect the actual production of the phonemes.
Evaluative Assessment:The recorded pepeha files were
evaluated by a cohort of approximately 10 markers - all fluent
M¯aori speakers. Marks were given per syllable as either ”ac-
cepted” (full mark) or ”rejected” (zero mark). Syllables con-
sisted mostly of CV structures or V (where V includes both
long/short monophthongs and diphthongs), and there were three
CVV (vowel sequences) which were treated as one syllable for
marking purposes. More details about the marking process are
published elsewhere [21]. The markers were instructed to evalu-
ate on an ”acceptability” criterion, not a canonical target sound.
They were also instructed to be stricter on the vowels than the
consonants. The markers only assessed the recording at a seg-
mental level, not the prosody. To date there has been no inter-
rater reliability performed, but it is planned for the future.
Acoustic Analysis:The following acoustic features were
extracted from the data:
◦F0: The speaker data set did not include sex 3, information
required by Praat for setting appropriate formant ceiling pa-

2.3. Tool
To facilitate the integration of the three data sources described
above, a bespoke vowel visualisation tool was developed with
the assistance of AI. Dubbed ”FRED” (Formant Research for
EDucation), this tool provides a graphical interface which en-
ables the user to filter large spreadsheets containing acoustic
and categorisation data, and displays these results in highly
configurable standard F1/F2 plots, distribution plots, and time-
normalised formant trajectories. Crucially, this allowed our al-
lophonic classifications and acceptability judgements to be ex-
amined against acoustic evidence, which provided an objec-
tive basis for validating or challenging perceptual assessments,
and ensuring a consistent analytical framework across different
phonological contexts.

4. Discussion
Our results demonstrate that the same acoustic variant may be
tolerated in one phonological context but rejected in another.
Backed realisations of /0/ were highly acceptable as monoph-
thongs, yet the same backed quality in the second target of /50/
was penalised. This asymmetry challenges the widespread con-
ception of diphthongs as ’vowel sequences’.
The /5/ component of diphthongs tells a complementary
story. When produced as a monophthong, /5/ showed extensive
variation — 31% of tokens were produced as [@] — yet mark-
ers accepted both variants at near-identical rates ([5]: 97.8%;
[@]: 98.7%). This tolerance did not extend uniformly across
diphthong contexts, however. The onset of /5e/ was acousti-
cally indistinguishable from canonical [5] on both F1 and F2,
while the onset of /50/ was produced at a height matching [@]
rather than [5], though further back. These patterns suggest that
the /5/ component is not a single stable vowel recycled across
contexts, but is shaped by its diphthong environment.
The /5i/ and /5e/ diphthongs provide additional evi-
dence, this time regarding merger. Markers accepted cross-
substitutions in both directions at near-ceiling rates (all [5e]
tokens produced for /5i/ were accepted; 93.3% of [5i] tokens
produced for /5e/ were accepted), and tokens produced as the
”wrong” diphthong were acoustically indistinguishable from
canonical productions of that surface form. This is consistent
with an ongoing merger of these two diphthongs, which is it-
self evidence that speakers process these vowels as holistic units
rather than discriminating their component elements. Such re-
sults have implications for descriptions of M ¯aori which still
treat them as distinct phonemes [23].
These findings suggest that fluent speakers have inter-
nalised diphthongs as separate phonemes with their own accept-
ability boundaries, rather than as sequences of independently-
evaluated monophthongs. The acoustic covariation [8], where
diphthong endpoints tracked monophthong positions across
generations, does not translate to perceptual equivalence.
Speakers appear to have distinct targets for /0/ and for /50/, even
if those targets have shifted in parallel.
The value of our triangulation approach is most visible
where sources diverge. Acoustically similar productions re-
ceived different marker judgements depending on context sug-
gesting that fluent speaker judgements encode something be-
yond raw acoustic distance from a target — potentially includ-
ing expectations shaped by phonological structure.
Whether similar patterns hold for the remaining vowels of
M¯aori remains to be tested. If fluent speakers’ tolerance of
phoneme variation is materially affected by phonological con-
text, these findings have direct implications for pronunciation
assessment in M ¯aori, and support assertions that diphthongs
should be approached as individual sounds [6], rather than as
afterthoughts to monophthong instruction, see for example [23,
p. 16].
The study has many implications for speech technology
for M ¯aori. The research team is developing a computer-aided
pronunciation training (CAPT) platform for M ¯aori. Standard
CAPT and GOP 4-based scoring systems typically evaluate de-
viations from a canonical phoneme sequence [24, 25, 26, 27,
28, 29, 30, 31]. Many CAPT systems ”implicitly assume a na-
tive norm”, comparing language learners pronunciation against
L1 productions [24]. Establishing acceptable variation requires
first that errors need to be systematically identified, and acous-
tically defined. It has been observed that if researchers do not
have extensive training in phonetics, automating pronunciation
feedback can be susceptible to misuse [32]. This makes it even
more essential to analyse and establish the acceptable range of
pronunciation as the first step to designing pronunciation learn-
ing technology for the language.
5. Conclusion
This analysis highlights the importance of empirically mapping
acceptable phonetic variation in languages subject to extensive
revitalisation efforts, where canonical targets may be contested
and learner productions highly variable. Our triangulation of
acoustic data, phonetician knowledge, and fluent speaker in-
tuition suggests that no single source suffices: acoustic sim-
ilarity does not guarantee perceptual equivalence, and expert
phonetic labelling does not always predict fluent speaker accep-
tance. This represents a first step toward systematically charac-
terising the boundaries of acceptable variation in modern M¯aori
pronunciation. M
