Abstract
Modern emotional text-to-speech (TTS) models typically rely
on utterance-level conditioning. However, natural emotional
speech requires fine-grained variations across words to achieve
nuanced, human-like expressiveness. To address this, we intro-
duce emotion residual vectors (ERVs), which capture neutral-
to-emotional deviations in word-aligned self-supervised speech
embeddings as an annotation-free cue for local prosody. To
stabilize intensity control, we compress ERVs into a low-
dimensional bottleneck space and train a RoBERTa-based pre-
dictor to infer the projected vectors from text and emotion. Dur-
ing inference, the predicted vectors are converted into additive
hidden-state offsets and injected into a neutral-conditioned TTS
model, enabling continuous word-level intensity control. Ex-
perimental results show that our method improves local emo-
tional controllability while preserving naturalness compared to
controllable emotional TTS baselines.

word-level emotion control
1. Introduction
Emotional expressiveness is important for the perceived natural-
ness of text-to-speech (TTS). In prior work on emotional TTS
[1–6], utterance-level emotion conditioning was commonly
adopted due to its implementation simplicity and straightfor-
ward controllability. However, when the conditioning is applied
only at the utterance level, the model can misallocate word-level
prosodic prominence, emphasizing non-target words while at-
tenuating the intended focus word. Consequently, subsequent
studies have investigated fine-grained conditioning schemes
[7–9] that parameterize emotional intensity as a scalar con-
trol variable and apply it through word-aligned conditioning,
thereby allowing per-word adjustment of emotion strength.
Representative methods include EmoQ-TTS [7], which en-
ables local intensity control by deriving proxy intensity lev-
els from the embedding-space distance to a neutral reference
and conditioning on quantized codes during phoneme-aligned
synthesis. HED-TTS [8] further extends controllability by pre-
dicting multi-granular emotion distributions and allowing direct
manipulation at the utterance, word, or phoneme level, using
ranking-based scoring to derive normalized intensity targets for
supervision. EME-TTS [9] additionally introduces explicit em-
phasis control and mitigates emotion–emphasis interference by
reweighting attention within a masked emphasis region. While
these designs enable precise control, they can also degrade per-
ceptual quality, making the synthesized speech sound less natu-
ral. In particular, word-level control may lead to local prosodic

distortions, including abrupt pitch shifts, unnatural durations,
and overly salient target words. Consequently, maintaining per-
ceptual naturalness remains challenging for existing word-level
emotion control methods.
To address this limitation, we model the neutral-to-
emotional deviation itself as a word-level control signal. We
define emotion residual vectors (ERVs) as word-aligned resid-
uals in a pretrained self-supervised speech (S3L) representa-
tion space. We adopt S3L representations because they provide
rich paralinguistic and prosodic cues without requiring manual
prosody annotations [10–14]. Recent work [15] shows that S3L
residuals between matched word instances encode structured
prosodic shifts and are concentrated in a compact subspace.
Based on this observation, we interpret the difference between
aligned emotional and neutral words as a local signal of emo-
tional prosody. We inject ERVs into a neutral-conditioned TTS
backbone and control intensity by scaling the injected deviation
with word-level control weights. Based on this formulation, our
work makes the following three contributions:
1. We show that ERV based injection enables word-level emo-
tional intensity control while preserving naturalness.
2. We propose a bottleneck residual subspace that provides a
more stable modulation signal for intensity scaling.
3. We propose a three-stage training pipeline that integrates
ERV extraction, bottleneck injection learning, and text-and-
emotion based prediction for controllable emotional TTS.

2. Proposed Method
In this section, we describe our overall procedure for deriving
ERVs and integrating them into a TTS system. The overall ar-
chitecture is illustrated in Fig. 1(a). The proposed framework
follows a three-stage training scheme:
1. Pretrain the FastSpeech2 [16] backbone with speaker and
utterance-level emotion conditioning.
2. Freeze the backbone and train a residual injection module
(RIM) with the global emotion fixed to neutral.
3. Construct projected-ERV targets using the fixed bottleneck
projection and fine-tune a RoBERTa-based [17] predictor to
estimate word-level projected ERVs.
During inference, the predictor outputs projected ERVs condi-
tioned on text and the target emotion. These vectors are in-
jected into the neutral-conditioned backbone and scaled by con-
trol weights, enabling word-level emotional intensity control.

3. Experiments and Results
3.1. Data
We conducted experiments on the English subset of emotional
speech database (ESD) [18], which contains 10 speakers and
five emotions (neutral, angry, happy, sad and surprise). ESD
provides parallel utterances with identical transcripts across
emotions, enabling same-speaker neutral–emotional pairing.
We followed the train/validation/test split used in prior work [9]
on ESD (14,900 / 950 / 1,550 utterances). Using these parallel
pairs, we extracted 88,400 word-level ERV instances, 22,100
per non-neutral emotion.
3.2. Implementation Details
The acoustic backbone followed FastSpeech2 [16] with four
FFT blocks in both encoder and decoder, hidden size 256, two
attention heads, filter size 1024, and convolution kernel sizes
(9,1). We trained the model with Adam (β 1 = 0.9,β 2 = 0.98,
ϵ= 10 −9, and weight decay= 0) for 200,000 steps with
a batch size of 64. We employed an iSTFTNet vocoder [22]
trained on ESD with 16 kHz mel-spectrograms. It adopts the
C8C8I architecture with two upsampling layers of factors6and
8. Mel-spectrograms were extracted by applying the short-time
Fourier transform (STFT) to 16 kHz audio with hop length 192,
window length 768, FFT size 768, and an 80-bin Mel filter-
bank. To extract ERVs, we used WavLM-Base [20] features
and aggregated layers 7–12 to form 768-dimensional word-level
residual targets. We set the bottleneck dimension toa= 32by
default, and varied it in our ablation studies. For the projected-
ERV predictor, we fine-tuned a pretrained RoBERTa-Base [17]
encoder using AdamW for 20 epochs with a batch size of 8. We
set the learning rate toη RoBER T a= 5×10−5 for the RoBERTa
parameters andη head = 3×10 −4 for the prediction head.

3.3. Model Performance
We conducted both subjective and objective evaluations for
overall speech quality and emotional expression. For subjective
evaluation, 15 listeners rated naturalness with the Naturalness
Mean Opinion Score (NMOS) and emotional expressiveness
with the Emotional Mean Opinion Score (EMOS) on a 5-point
scale. For objective evaluation, we computed the UTokyo Mean
Opinion Score predictor (UTMOS) [23] and emotion classifica-
tion accuracy (Emo.Acc.) with a pretrained Emotion2vec-plus-
large model [24] on synthesized samples from the ESD test set.
We compared the proposed method with FastSpeech2-
based emotion-controllable baselines: (i) FS2+emo, our Fast-
Speech2 backbone trained with standard utterance-level emo-
tion embeddings; (ii) EME-TTS, which supports emotion con-
trol with word-level emphasis conditioning; and (iii) HED-TTS,
which predicts hierarchical emotion distributions for word and
phoneme-level control. For a fair comparison of utterance-level
emotional rendering, we set the word-level control weights to
αw = 1for all words in our model. Table 1 shows that
the proposed method achieved comparable performance to the
FS2+emo backbone, while substantially outperforming base-
lines. These results indicate that ERV-based modulation pre-
served overall speech quality and emotional expressiveness.
We further analyzed the effect of the intensity scaling fac-
torαon prosody. For utterance-level intensity control, Figure 2
shows that asαincreases, the average pitch changes in emotion-
specific directions (e.g., increasing for happy/surprise and de-
creasing for sad). Although the model was trained withα= 1
on all emotion categories, we observed that the pitch trends re-
main directionally consistent even whenα>1at inference.

3.4. Word-level Intensity Controllability
We conducted an A/B preference test with 15 listeners to evalu-
ate word-level controllability and its naturalness. Given paired
samples from our model and word-level controllable baselines,
listeners selected which sample renders the highlighted words
more naturally within the sentence. We reported the results in
a Win–Tie–Lose format, whereWinis the percentage of trials
preferring our method,Loseprefers the baseline, andTieindi-
cates no preference. As shown in Table 2, the proposed method
was preferred over the baselines, indicating improved percep-
tual naturalness for localized intensity control.
To further analyze the preference results, we compared
prosodic realizations of a representative example. For this anal-
ysis, we assigned higher control weights to target words (e.g.,
αw = 1.0) and set the other words to a lower value (e.g.,
αw = 0.5). Fig. 3 visualizes the pitch contour of an exam-
ple utterance in thehappycondition, where the target word re-
gion is highlighted in yellow. The proposed method increased
pitch around the target words while maintaining smooth transi-
tions with surrounding regions, resulting in a more natural em-
phasis intonation within the utterance. In contrast, HED-TTS
produced a local pitch excursion at the target words, which re-
duced prosodic coherence with the surrounding context in this
example.

4. Conclusion
In this paper, we proposed ERVs and a bottlenecked residual
injection framework for word-level emotional intensity control
in TTS. Experiments indicated that ERVs captured emotion-
related prosodic deviations, and that projecting ERVs into a
low-dimensional bottleneck preserves most of the modulation
information useful for synthesis. The bottleneck representa-
tion also simplified prediction, enabling a RoBERTa-based pre-
dictor to estimate projected ERVs and provide word-level in-
tensity control while maintaining perceptual naturalness (e.g.,
improved A/B preference for highlighted-word naturalness).
These findings suggest the feasibility of a practical pipeline
in which high-dimensional S3L representations are compressed
into a compact space that remains predictable and controllable,
rather than being handled directly in the original space.
A limitation of our approach is its reliance on paral-
lel, word-aligned neutral–emotional pairs for ERV extraction.
Nonetheless, our results show that such residuals provide an in-
formative and structured signal for prosody control. Extending
residual-based control to less constrained data settings is an im-
portant direction for future work.
