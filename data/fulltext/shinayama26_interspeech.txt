Upcycling Pretrained Transformers into Mixture-of-experts
for Multilingual Speech Recognition
Kentaro Shinayama, Kohei Matsuura, Jaeyoung Lee, Masato Mimura
NTT, Japan
kentaro.shinayama@ntt.com
Abstract
Fine-tuning large pretrained models is a powerful way to obtain
high-accuracy systems for downstream ASR tasks. However,
when fine-tuning on highly diverse data, such as multilingual
corpora, performance can degrade compared with training sepa-
rate models per language, largely due to limited model capacity.
In this paper, we propose a simple capacity expansion method
for multilingual fine-tuning by upcycling the feed-forward net-
work (FFN) components of a pretrained model into a post-hoc
Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) architecture. This conversion sub-
stantially increases the model’s representational capacity while
preserving inference efficiency: at test time, we activate only a
single upcycled FFN expert, keeping the number of active pa-
rameters identical to the original dense model. We investigate
two expert selection strategies: (1) hard routing based on lan-
guage identity, and (2) soft routing using a learned router. Ex-
periments on 10-language speech recognition with Common-
V oice and additional ASR evaluations on four Asian languages
demonstrate that the proposed upcycling-to-MoE approach con-
sistently improves multilingual fine-tuning effectiveness.
Index Terms: multilingual speech recognition, mixture-of-
experts, pretrained Transformers, Whisper
1. Introduction
Multilingual automatic speech recognition (ASR) plays a cen-
tral role in enabling seamless cross-linguistic communication.
By training a single model to recognize multiple languages,
multilingual ASR removes the need to maintain separate mono-
lingual systems and can improve recognition for low-resource
languages through cross-lingual knowledge sharing. This
shared modeling allows the system to exploit common acous-
tic characteristics and phonetic regularities across languages.
However, when linguistically diverse languages are trained
jointly without carefully accounting for inter-language simi-
larity, multilingual ASR can even underperform monolingual
models [1][2], a phenomenon commonly viewed as nega-
tive transfer in machine learning [3]. This degradation may
arise from two primary factors. First, end-to-end (e2e) ASR
systems[4] are typically trained to predict highly language-
dependent grapheme units, which limits cross-lingual knowl-
edge sharing. Second, model capacity is inherently constrained,
making it difficult to represent diverse language-specific pat-
terns within a single parameter space.
Importantly, this issue can persist even when multilingual
systems are obtained by fine-tuning large-scale pretrained mod-
els such as XLS-R [5] and Whisper [6]. Although these mod-
els learn rich acoustic representations, their final predictions re-
main grapheme-based, and model capacity is still shared across
languages, resulting in similar negative-transfer effects [7].
One possible approach to mitigating this problem is to
adopt mixture-based, parameter-efficient adaptation methods,
such as LoRA-based mixture-of-experts (MoE), which have
been explored in recent multilingual ASR studies [8, 9, 10, 11,
12]. By routing inputs to different lightweight experts, these
methods aim to better accommodate heterogeneous data distri-
butions. However, because each expert is implemented via low-
rank or bottleneck modules, the effective representational ca-
pacity remains limited, which can hinder scalability to increas-
ingly diverse multilingual settings.
In this paper, we propose a more direct capacity-expansion
strategy by upcycling the feed-forward network (FFN) layers of
a pretrained Transformer [13] into a MoE [14, 15, 16, 17] archi-
tecture. Rather than inserting small auxiliary modules, we repli-
cate and reorganize the original high-capacity FFN layers into
multiple experts, thereby increasing model capacity while pre-
serving pretrained knowledge. This design enables more flexi-
ble modeling of language-specific patterns and aims to enhance
the effectiveness of multilingual fine-tuning.
2. Preliminaries
2.1. E2e multilingual ASR
E2e multilingual ASR aims to perform speech recognition
across multiple languages with a single neural network. The
output layer’s vocabulary can be either a union of all grapheme
units across involved languages [18] or more universal byte-
level tokens [6]. This approach eliminates the need for separate
models per language and, more importantly, showed improved
performance for low-resource languages [18][19].
Large-scale pretrained multilingual models now dominate
this paradigm. XLS-R [5], based on wav2vec 2.0 [20], was pre-
trained on over 100 languages, and Whisper [6] demonstrated
strong zero-shot multilingual ASR performance. To enable effi-
cient adaptation of these models for target tasks, adapter-based
methods introduce lightweight modules with few trainable pa-
rameters [21, 22]. However, joint multilingual training often
suffers from negative knowledge transfer among distant lan-
guages. Prior work has addressed this issue using language-
specific adapters [23, 24], selective parameter updates [25], and
language-universal output units such as IPA [2, 7].
2.2. Mixture-of-experts in speech processing
Mixture-of-experts (MoE) models [14, 16, 15, 17] increase ef-
fective model capacity by dynamically routing each input (or
token/frame) to a subset of expert modules. Originally popular-
ized in large-scale language modeling, MoE has also been suc-
cessfully adopted in speech processing. Prior work has incor-
porated MoE into acoustic models to improve recognition accu-

2.3. Upcycling Transformer
Recent work in large language models has introducedupcy-
cling, which converts a pretrained dense model into an MoE
model without retraining from scratch [29, 30, 31, 32]. Instead
of independently initializing experts, upcycling replicates pre-
trained parameters to form multiple experts, preserving prior
knowledge while expanding capacity.
In Transformer architectures [13], FFN layers account for
a large portion of the parameters and perform key non-linear
transformations. Prior studies show that upcycling only FFN
layers achieves a favorable balance between capacity and com-
putation [29, 30, 31, 32], keeping self-attention shared.
Although widely studied in LLMs, this upcycle-to-MoE ap-
proach has not been systematically explored for speech founda-
tion models. For multilingual ASR, converting dense FFNs into
experts offers a promising way to expand language-specific ca-
pacity and mitigate negative transfer.
3. Proposed method
3.1. Upcycling Transformer FFN into sparse MoE
We propose a multilingual ASR framework that upcycles the
FFN layers of a large pretrained Transformer into a sparse MoE
architecture. In contrast to parameter-efficient approaches that
insert lightweight modules (e.g., LoRA)[8], our method ex-
pands capacity directly by converting the original high-capacity
FFN layers into multiple experts. Our design follows the sparse
MoE paradigm of Switch Transformer [15]. Rather than com-
bining all experts through a weighted sum, we activateonly one
expert per input token. Consequently, the number of active pa-
rameters per token matches that of the original dense model.

negative transfer across languages.
3.2. MoE on Whisper decoder
We adopt Whisper [6] as our backbone model due to its
strong multilingual ASR performance. Whisper follows a
Transformer-based encoder-decoder architecture. In our ap-
proach, we upcycle the decoder FFN layers into MoE modules,
while keeping the encoder unchanged. The encoder is expected
to provide largely language-agnostic acoustic representations
learned from large-scale multilingual pretraining. In contrast,
the decoder operates at the token level and is more language-
dependent because it predicts grapheme sequences. Introduc-
ing MoE into the decoder therefore promotes better separation
of language-specific patterns, while retaining shared acoustic
representations in the encoder.

4. Experimental Evaluations
4.1. Datasets and models
We conduct experiments on the CommonV oice [33] corpus
v16.1. Our primary setting consists of ten languages, includ-
ing five Western languages (WE-5langs) and five non-Western
languages (Other-5langs). The selected languages are summa-
rized in Table 1. ForOther-5langssubset, we purposely select
linguistically and geographically diverse languages. In addi-
tion, we constructed a new dataset by combining four OpenSLR
speech datasets in Asian languages, namely, Javanese, Tamil,
Vietnamese, and Chinese, which we refer to asSLR Asian-
4langs, and conducted experiments on this dataset as well to
evaluate the generalization capability of our method. We eval-
uate performance using word error rate (WER) forWE-5langs,
while we use character error rate (CER) for other languages,
because these languages lack explicit word boundaries in their
writing systems.
All experiments are conducted using the ESPnet
toolkit [34]. We use Whisper-small variant as the backbone
model by default, which consists of a 12-layer Transformer
encoder and a 12-layer decoder, except for the ablation study
in Table 5 where we compared larger Whisper models. All
models are trained using the Transformer learning rate schedule
with the peak of3.0×10 −5 and 2000 warmup steps, for 20
epochs. We average the parameters of five checkpoints with the
lowest validation CERs to build the final model for evaluation.
We compare the proposed method with several baselines.
MonoFT serves as an upper bound, where the Whisper de-
coder is fine-tuned independently for each language without
cross-lingual interference. MultiFT represents standard multi-
lingual fine-tuning, where the decoder is jointly trained on all
languages. We also implement a LoRA-based MoE baseline by
inserting 10 LoRA experts with rank 16 into each FFN layer
with a trainable router as defined in 3.3.1.
4.2. Results
Table 2 presents the results on the CommonV oice 10 languages.
The fact that the multilingual model (MultiFT, E2) consistently
performs worse than the monolingual models (E1) suggests that
single-model multilingual training may be suboptimal due to
negative knowledge transfer. All FFN-upcycling variants out-
perform MultiFT baseline, and all these improvements are sta-
tistically significant at the 1% level. Among these configura-
tions, the language-wise hard routing (E8) achieves the best
overall performance, surpassing both MultiFT and the LoRA-
based MoE approach (E3). Notably, this hard-routing strategy
even outperformed the MonoFT upper bound with a single net-
work. No significant improvement from language embeddings
was observed with a smaller number of experts (N=5; E4 and
E6). However, with more experts (N=10; E5 and E7), language
embeddings yielded statistically significant gains. This suggests
that language-aware routing becomes effective when sufficient
expert capacity is available.
Although the soft routing improves over the multilingual
baseline, it underperforms hard routing, while it has a clear ad-
vantage that it works well with fewer experts than the number of
involved languages and thus is more parameter-efficient (E4 and
E6). Since all experts are initialized from identical pretrained
FFN parameters in upcycling, soft routing may fail to induce
sufficient diversification. In contrast, hard routing enforces ex-
plicit language-level partitioning, promoting clearer specializa-
tion. Improving expert diversification for soft routing [29, 30]
remains future work.

In the experiments in Table 2 and 3, we upcycled all de-

Table 4 presents the results on the effect of MoE placement,
where, for example, layer 1 denotes the lowest layer. From this
table, we can see that models with MoE applied to higher layers
consistently achieve better performance than lower layers. In
particular, upcycling the top 3 or top 6 layers achieves perfor-

with much fewer parameters. This suggests that language-

decoder layers, and that selectively introducing MoE in these
layers is sufficient to obtain most of the performance gains.

4.3. Discussion
From the results in Tables 2–5, we derive three key findings.
First, directly expanding model capacity via FFN upcycling is
more effective than relying on lightweight LoRA-based experts,
in particular for high-resource languages. By preserving the full
capacity of the original FFN within each expert, the model can
better capture diverse, language-specific patterns.
Second, introducing MoE in the upper decoder layers is
particularly effective, suggesting that language-dependent rep-
resentations are mainly formed near the token prediction stage.
Notably, applying MoE only to the top 3-6 layers achieves per-
formance comparable to full-layer MoE while substantially re-
ducing the number of trainable parameters. This indicates that
selective upper-layer upcycling offers an efficient trade-off be-
tween capacity expansion and parameter efficiency.
Third, the strong performance of hard routing (E8), relative
to language-dependent soft routing based on language embed-
dings (E6 and E7), suggests that explicit language-level separa-
tion effectively mitigates negative transfer. Its superiority over
the MonoFT upper bound (E1) further indicates that the pro-
posed architecture strikes a good balance between specializa-
tion and shared knowledge, benefiting from both cross-lingual
transfer and enhanced language-specific modeling capacity.
5. Conclusion
We proposed a capacity-expansion method for multilingual
ASR by upcycling the feed-forward network (FFN) layers of a
pretrained Transformer into a sparse Mixture-of-Experts (MoE)
architecture. By activating only a single expert per token,
our approach preserves inference cost while increasing total
model capacity. Experiments on multilingual ASR benchmarks
demonstrated that the proposed method consistently outper-
forms multilingual fine-tuning and LoRA-based MoE baselines.
Language-wise hard routing achieved the strongest improve-
ments and even surpassed monolingual fine-tuning. Further-
more, applying MoE only to upper decoder layers retained most
of the performance gains while reducing trainable parameters.
These results indicate that selective FFN upcycling provides an
effective and scalable solution for mitigating negative transfer
in multilingual ASR. Thus, our method removes the burden of
carefully designing the language set for multilingual finetuning.
