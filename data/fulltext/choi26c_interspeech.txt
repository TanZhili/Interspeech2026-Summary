Abstract
Mistimed backchannels can interrupt ongoing speech and de-
grade user experience. AConsiderate Listenerconcentrates
backchannel interventions near pause-proximate regions in real
time, since backchannels cluster around speaker pauses. How-
ever, not all pauses invite a backchannel. Pauses that follow
utterances calling for a reply may be pragmatically inappro-
priate for backchannel intervention. Acoustic cues alone may
not capture the linguistic context needed to distinguish these
cases, so acoustic-only predictors tend to over-predict. We there-
fore propose a zero look-ahead streaming backchannel predictor
combining pause-aware soft scaling with Q-Former fusion of
partial ASR hypotheses. We introduce Semantic False Discov-
ery Rate (Semantic FDR) to quantify backchannel predictions in
pragmatically inappropriate pause regions. On a Korean coun-
seling corpus, our approach reduces Semantic FDR by 53.8%
and improves Macro-F1 by 2.16 pp compared to an acoustic-only
baseline.
Index Terms: streaming backchannel prediction, considerate
listener, pause-aware soft scaling, text-guided fusion, semantic
false positives
1. Introduction
Backchannels (BCs) are brief listener responses that convey
attention and empathy without claiming the conversational
floor [1]. Prior work has shown that BC-capable agents tend
to increase engagement and self-disclosure [2, 3, 4]. However,
BC effectiveness depends critically on timing and context. A
supportivemm-hmmfeels natural during a pause after an on-
going narrative. Yet, the same token mid-utterance can disrupt
the flow, and even a pause-timed BC may feel intrusive if the
pause follows a direct question that calls for a reply [5]. This
contrast motivates theConsiderate Listenerprinciple: a sys-
tem should concentrate BC interventions near pause-proximate
regions and avoid overlapping ongoing speech, prioritizing non-
disruptiveness over response frequency [6, 7]. Yet the discourse
context of a pause matters as much as its timing.
Streaming BC prediction involves three interrelated chal-
lenges. The first concerns latency. A block-wise joint ASR–BC
models often rely on look-ahead frames, which introduce a delay
at the BC decision point. The second is class imbalance, as BC
events are highly sparse and can bias learning while weakening
metrics such as Macro-F1 [8]. The third concerns pragmatic
appropriateness. Pauses following initiating actions that make a
response relevant [9], such as direct questions or directives, tend
to make a response relevant rather than invite a BC. We refer to
such pause blocks asNOBC-PauseFPregions, that is, acousti-

practice, such intrusions can disrupt turn-taking and degrade the
perceived naturalness of the agent.
To address these challenges, we propose a zero look-ahead
streaming BC predictor that combines pause-aware soft scaling
with text-guided fusion. A pause detector (PD) estimates the
pause probability for each block and uses this probability to
softly scale acoustic features, biasing predictions toward pause-
proximate regions in line with theConsiderate Listenerprinci-
ple. Streaming partial ASR hypotheses are additionally fused
via Q-Former cross-attention, which may help suppress BC pre-
dictions inNOBC-PauseFPblocks. Our main contributions
are as follows:
•Considerate Listener framework:We formalize non-
disruptiveness as a primary design criterion and introduce
Semantic False Discovery Rate (Semantic FDR), the fraction
of predicted BCs occurring in pragmatically inappropriate
pause blocks (Section 4.5).
•Pause-aware soft scaling:PD-based probability-weighted
scaling down-weights non-pause blocks, concentrating deci-
sions near pause-proximate regions and mitigating effective
class imbalance.
•Text-guided fusion:Q-Former cross-attention over partial
ASR hypotheses provides discourse context that acoustic cues
alone may not capture, which may help suppress Semantic FPs
inNOBC-PauseFPblocks.
2. Related Work
Prior work has identified prosodic features that tend to invite
BCs, including sustained low-pitch regions [10] and utterance-
boundary cues [11], and corpus analyses confirm that BCs fre-
quently co-occur with speaker pauses [12]. Fors [13] further
reported that BCs tend to cluster near transition relevance place
(TRP) pauses, while most intra-utterance pauses do not coin-
cide with BC production. In Korean, however, reactive tokens
are also produced at intra-turn unit boundaries formed by con-
junctive verbal suffixes, not only at turn-final positions [14, 15].
Taken together, these observations suggest that acoustic silence
alone may be insufficient to determine BC opportunity, and that
identifying the appropriate pause requires understanding its dis-
course role, not only its acoustic properties.

3. Proposed Method
As introduced in Section 1,NOBC-PauseFPregions are acous-
tically detectable pauses where BC intervention is pragmatically
inappropriate, specifically pauses following initiating actions
that make a response relevant [9]. Our system addresses these
challenges using two conditioning signals: a pause detector
(PD) that softly scales acoustic features toward pause-proximate
regions, and text-guided fusion of partial ASR hypotheses to
help suppress BC predictions inNOBC-PauseFPblocks (Fig-
ure 1). Region-level annotations are mapped to streaming blocks
for training and evaluation, aligning them with the block-wise
decision points of the streaming model.
3.1. System Architecture
Building on the block-wise streaming ASR+BC multitask sys-
tem of [8], three modules share a common acoustic encoder: a
pause detector (PD), an ASR decoder, and a BC predictor. The
acoustic encoder processes input in fixed-size blocks without
look-ahead frames, ensuring zero look-ahead at every BC de-
cision point. The BC predictor also includes a Q-Former that
extracts textual context from the ASR decoder hidden states. At

4.1. Dataset
We use a small private Korean counseling corpus (116 sessions,
∼99 hours) that is manually annotated for BCs. We split the
data by session into 83/22/11 for train/validation/evaluation. We
define pause intervals as inter-word gaps of at least 200 ms based
on WhisperX word timestamps [24, 25]. These pause intervals
are mapped to pause blocks (streaming blocks that overlap the
intervals) and used for PD supervision. Each BC event is mapped

or directives, where BC intervention was judged pragmatically
inappropriate. Disputed cases were excluded.
4.2. Model Configuration
We adopt a streaming acoustic encoder, a CTC/attention ASR
decoder[8], and incorporate a pause detector (PD) and a Q-
Former module. STFT frame and hop sizes are 20 ms and 10 ms,
respectively. We setL block=40,L hop=4, andL look-ahead=0. Par-
tial ASR hypotheses are compressed with a BLIP-2-style Q-
Former (N q=16, 4-layer decoder, 4 heads, FFN dim 2048).
The BC Prediction module uses a 1-layer Transformer encoder
for acoustic–text fusion. The PD and BC classifiers are each
implemented as a linear layer.

5. Results
5.1. Classification Performance
Table 3 reports precision, recall, BC-class F1, and Macro-F1.
Model (A) attains the highest recall (80.40%) but the lowest
precision (24.72%) and Macro-F1 (66.77%). The acoustic-only
model does not explicitly constrain decision timing and therefore
tends to produce BC predictions broadly across pause blocks,
consistent with the known tendency of acoustic-only systems to
over-predict under class imbalance.
Adding pause-aware soft scaling in (B) yields a significant
improvement in Macro-F1 (+1.00 pp,p <0.01). This is con-
sistent with the PD providing explicit pause supervision that
guides BC predictions toward pause-proximate regions, which
may reduce predictions outside pause boundaries.
Text fusion in (C) achieves the best Macro-F1 (69.14%,
p <0.01) while largely preserving recall (79.54%), suggest-
ing that partial ASR hypotheses provide additional signal be-
yond acoustics. The precision gain over (B) (+2.00 pp) further
suggests that discourse context helps filter BC predictions in
pragmatically inappropriate regions.
The full system (D) achieves the highest precision (28.85%)
and a Macro-F1 of 68.93% (p <0.01), which is marginally
below Model (C). We tentatively attribute this gap to slight
over-suppression: both conditioning signals may jointly down-
weight blocks that could otherwise yield correct BC predictions,
which could explain the lower recall relative to Model (C). As
shown in Section 5.2, however, this suppression appears to be
concentrated in pragmatically inappropriate blocks rather than
BC-inviting ones.

5.2. Pragmatic Quality: Semantic FDR
Table 4 reports Semantic FDR forNOBC-PauseFPanalysis.
Model (A) produces 197 Semantic FPs among 3,418 BC pre-
dictions (5.76%). This is consistent with acoustic features alone
providing limited ability to distinguish turn-holding pauses from
BC-inviting ones [13].
Pause-aware soft scaling in (B) reduces Semantic FDR to
4.83% (p <0.01). The remaining errors suggest that pause
proximity alone may be insufficient for pragmatic appropriate-
ness, as the model cannot distinguish the discourse function of
a pause using acoustic features alone.
Text fusion in (C) yields a larger reduction to 3.74%
(p <0.01), suggesting that partial transcript cues help reduce
BC predictions in pragmatically inappropriate contexts. The
improvement over (B) indicates that discourse context provides
information complementary to pause proximity.
The full system (D) achieves the lowest Semantic FDR of
3.07% (p<0.01), reducing Semantic FPs from 197 to 91 out of
2,960 total BC predictions, a 53.8% reduction. Pause-aware soft
scaling constrainswhenthe model responds, while text fusion
constrainswhetherthe discourse context warrants a response.
Their combination appears to targetNOBC-PauseFPcases
more precisely than either signal alone.
Overall, Models (C) and (D) achieve similar Macro-F1
scores (69.14% vs. 68.93%), yet their Semantic FDR differs
(3.74% vs. 3.07%), reflecting the two-motive structure of the
Considerate Listenerframework: pause-aware soft scaling con-
strains decision timing, while text-guided fusion constrains de-
cision appropriateness. This gap suggests that Macro-F1 alone
may not fully capture pragmatic quality.
6. Conclusion
We introduced theConsiderate Listenerframework, which pri-
oritizes non-disruptive, pause-proximate backchannel interven-
tions over response frequency in streaming settings. Pause-
aware soft scaling implements the first design criterion by con-
centrating decisions near pause boundaries, while Q-Former-
based text fusion addresses pragmatically inappropriate pause
regions. On a Korean counseling corpus, the full system reduces
Semantic FDR by 53.8%. It improves Macro-F1 by 2.16 pp over
the acoustic-only baseline, suggesting that pragmatic appropri-
ateness and classification performance may not trade off under
streaming constraints.
Our evaluation is limited to a single Korean corpus, and
text fusion may be sensitive to ASR errors under strict zero
look-ahead inference. Future work may examine robustness to
ASR errors, cross-lingual generalizability, and application to
backchannel category prediction.
