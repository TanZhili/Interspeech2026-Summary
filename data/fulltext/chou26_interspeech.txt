Abstract
Speech LLMs enable emotion-aware voice interaction, yet their
emotion judgments can be shaped by contextual identity pri-
ors beyond the audio signal. We isolate this effect by holding
each utterance fixed while varying only prompt-injected speaker
descriptors (accent, country, language), across MSP-Podcast
and BIIC-Podcast. Across multiple speech LLMs, identity text
alone yields statistically reliable performance disparities, mea-
sured as the spread between best- and worst-performing iden-
tity conditions, indicating systematic shifts in emotion predic-
tion. Saliency analysis shows that identity-driven prediction
transitions often co-occur with larger changes in acoustic focus
over the audio-token span, suggesting altered reliance on speech
evidence. To reduce this sensitivity, we apply a lightweight
LoRA adapter with two prompt strategies. Overall, we provide
a prompt-controlled framework to quantify, interpret, and re-
duce identity-conditioned sensitivity in speech LLM emotion
perception.
Index Terms: speech emotion recognition, speech large lan-
guage models, hidden priors
1. Introduction
The progression of speech LLMs has made emotion-aware
voice interaction increasingly practical by integrating paralin-
guistic cues beyond text, such as prosody, intensity, and timing,
into unified instruction-following interfaces [1, 2]. This is at-
tractive for service-oriented agents, where perceived empathy
and interaction quality drive user acceptance across domains
from healthcare to broader assistive services [3, 4]. Unlike con-
ventional SER pipelines that map audio to labels, speech LLM
behavior is inherently context-conditioned by the audio sig-
nal, input prompt, and pretrained data distribution. Moreover,
speech representations encode personal attributes, and regulat-
ing identity information can implicitly alter downstream affec-
tive behavior [5]. These factors bind speaker-related informa-
tion to emotion prediction and complicate the evaluation of per-
formance stability under controlled conditions.
A growing body of SER research has formalized evalua-
tion perspectives and representation learning strategies that bet-
ter reflect how emotion differs across individuals. Recent eval-
uation work probes SER behavior across accent, language, and
pitch [6, 7], while other studies show that incorporating speaker
attributes in multi-task learning can improve performance [8]
and that discouraging speaker-identifying information can pro-
mote more speaker-invariant affect representations [9]. Further-
more, Chou et al. argue that speaker variation reflects overlap-
ping sociocultural and physiological factors and should be con-

sidered at the individual level rather than only at the group level
[10]. However, identity is not always perceived from acoustic
cues alone: in many settings, it is also conveyed through con-
textual information provided before or alongside the same utter-
ance (e.g., a brief description of “who is speaking”, “the speaker
is from X”, or “speaks with Y accent”). This raises the possi-
bility that identity effects on emotion perception may arise even
when the speech evidence itself is unchanged, and that such ef-
fects may be amplified in prompt-based speech LLM interfaces
where identity descriptors are easy to inject as text.
Human perception research provides a clear precedent that
contextual identity cues can influence interpretation even when
the stimulus itself is held constant. For example, listeners can
shift their category judgments for identical speech sounds based
only on beliefs about a speaker’s regional background [11], and
professionally trained raters can change perceptual assessments
when presented with speaker identity information [12]. More
broadly, accent and “foreignness” cues can influence pragmatic
evaluation and credibility judgments under controlled condi-
tions [13]. These studies motivate a controlled probe of speech
LLM emotion perception, in which speaker identity descriptors
serve as contextual signals that may induce hidden priors. Here,
“speaker identity descriptors” denotes prompt-provided accent,
country, and language statements, not verified speaker identity.
Holding the audio fixed, we examine whether these descriptors
shape emotion predictions and whether LoRA can reduce this
sensitivity.
We investigate hidden priors in speech LLMs through two
hypotheses:
H1: Speaker identity can systematically shift emotion percep-
tion.
H2: Identity-induced prediction changes are accompanied by
shifts in acoustic focus.
Across multiple speech LLMs and identity-conditioned
prompts, we find statistically significant disparities under
F1gap, a spread-based metric between the best- and worst-
performing identity conditions, supporting H1 that identity cues
alone can shift emotion predictions under fixed audio. We
further observe evidence consistent with H2: identity-driven
prediction transitions more often co-occur with larger saliency
shifts over the audio-token span than cases where predictions re-
main unchanged, suggesting accompanying changes in acoustic
focus. Finally, we propose reduction methods using lightweight
LoRA (fine-tuned with base or mixed identity-conditioned
prompts) that significantly reduce identity-conditioned prompt
sensitivity. To our knowledge, this is one of the first studies to
treat speaker identity as a prompt-controlled variable for isolat-
ing hidden priors in speech LLM emotion perception.

2. Methodologies
We adopt a controlled prompt perturbation design to isolate
whether provided textual speaker identity statements would
trigger identity priors within speech LLM that alter the emotion
recognition result. Because speech LLMs accept both acous-
tic input and natural language instructions, we can supply ex-
plicit identity-related context (e.g., accent, country, language)
alongside an unchanged audio signal. We leverage this setup to
create prompt conditions that keep the audio fixed while vary-
ing only the identity statement. Under this design, any change
in predicted emotion is attributable to the identity information
provided in text rather than differences in acoustic evidence.
2.1. Speaker Identity Conditioned Emotion Recognition
We use the following instruction format for speech emotion
recognition, where the prompt includes an insertion field for
speaker identity conditioning:
What emotion is expressed in the audio?
{identity prompt}
Output exactly one of the following emotion
labels: Neutral|Angry|Happy|Sad
Answer:
The line{identity prompt}is a dedicated insertion field
that we replace with a speaker identity statement; leaving it
empty gives the base prompt. This keeps the task instruction
and label space fixed while allowing identity-related context to
be varied in a controlled manner.
We consider the following three identity attributes:
• Accent:The speaker has a{id accent}accent.
• Country:The speaker is from{id country}.
• Language:The speaker is speaking in{id lang}.
Here,{id ∗}is instantiated from the 12 descriptor values in Ta-
ble 1. Each attribute-value pairing defines one condition, yield-
ing3×12prompt variants per utterance. The language condi-
tion is treated as a textual-context probe even when it does not
match the audio language.

2.2.2. H2: Saliency-Based Acoustic Focus Analysis
To test whether identity-induced prediction changes are accom-
panied by shifts in acoustic focus, we track how gradient-based
saliency over the audio tokens changes when only the identity
statement in the prompt is varied. For each utterance under an
identity-conditioned prompt, we first obtain the model’s first
predicted token, which determines the constrained label output.
Conditioning on this token, we compute the token saliency of
the corresponding label logit with respect to the input embed-
ding sequence, producing a saliency vector per token. We then
reduce each vector to a scalar saliency magnitude by taking the
absolute sum over hidden dimensions.

2.3. Identity Prior Reduction Method
We use LoRA [15] as a lightweight adaptation strategy to re-
duce sensitivity to identity priors. We freeze the audio encoder
and adapt only the language model with LoRA. This targets at-
tention projections and feed-forward transformations that inte-
grate acoustic representations with textual context.
We train two LoRA variants that differ only in the prompt
distribution used during fine-tuning:
•Base prompt: prompts without any identity information.
•Mix prompt: a mixture of random identity-inserted prompts,
excluding the base prompt.
For each training instance in the mix setting, we sample one of
the three identity attributes in section 2.1 and one identity value
from Table 1. Sampling is controlled so that within each emo-
tion class, each identity attribute and each identity value appear
equally often, making the joint frequency of emotion, attribute
type, and identity value approximately uniform. This construc-
tion makes identity statements non-predictive of emotion labels
during training, encouraging the model to rely on acoustic evi-
dence rather than textual identity priors.
3. Experiment Setup
3.1. Datasets
We evaluate on two speech emotion datasets drawn from real
world conversational audio. MSP-Podcast version 1.12 [16]
provides curated emotion labels for English speech, and BIIC-
Podcast [17] provides a complementary Mandarin dataset. To
control for label imbalance and keep results comparable across
datasets, we use a fixed, class balanced sampling protocol over
four emotions,Neutral,Angry,Happy, andSad. For evaluation,
we use the MSP-Podcast test split and the full BIIC-Podcast set,
sampling 2,000 utterances per emotion (8,000 total) from each
dataset. For fine-tuning, we use the MSP-Podcast training split
and construct an approximately balanced training set by target-
ing∼10,800 utterances per emotion. For emotions with fewer
available instances, we oversample training examples to match
the target count.
3.2. Evaluation Setup
H1: Identity cues shift emotion perception.We evaluate
pre-trained Qwen2-Audio [18], Qwen3-Omni [19], DeSTA2.5-
Audio [20], and Phi-4 [21] under controlled identity-
conditioned prompts. All models use deterministic decoding
(temperature= 0, top-k= 1). We report macro-F1 andF1 gap,
whereF1 gap measures the spread between the best- and worst-
performing identity conditions. To test whether the observed
F1gap exceeds what would be expected if identity statements
were irrelevant, we conduct a per-utterance permutation test
over the 12 identity variants, under the null that permuting iden-
tity assignments does not change the prediction distribution for
that utterance.
H2: Identity-induced prediction changes coincide with
shifts in acoustic focus.To probe whether identity-driven pre-
diction transitions are accompanied by stronger redistributions
of acoustic reliance, we run gradient analysis using Captum [22]
on Qwen2-Audio and compute an audio-span saliency-shift
score for each identity-prompt pair on the same utterance via
equation 4. We group scores by the resulting predicted emotion
transition between the two prompts, and evaluate each transition
group with a within-utterance permutation test (50,000 permu-
tations). This procedure shuffles saliency-shift scores among
identity pairs within the same utterance, breaking the alignment
between transition labels and shift magnitudes while preserving
utterance-level variability. We control for multiple comparisons
across the 16 transition groups using Benjamini–Hochberg FDR
correction.
3.3. Textual Identity Sensitivity Reduction Method Setup
We fine-tune Qwen2-Audio with LoRA by freezing the audio
encoder and adapting the language model for 10 epochs (rank
r= 8,α= 16, dropout= 0.1). The adapter is applied to

4. Experimental Result and Analysis
4.1. H1: Identity Cues Shift Emotion Predictions
From Table 2, permutation tests show that all pre-trained mod-
els achieveF1 gap values significantly above the null expecta-
tion. This indicates that varying only the inserted speaker iden-
tity statement induces systematic performance discrepancy even
though the acoustic evidence and label set are unchanged, sup-
porting H1.
Sensitivity also varies with different identity attributes.
Across models, the language scenario typically induces the
largestF1 gap among accent, country, and language. For exam-
ple, on MSP-Podcast with Qwen2-Audio, the angry F1 differs
substantially across language prompts: Yoruba reaches 0.5808,
while English, which is the correct language of the samples,
yields 0.5414, and Turkish drops to 0.4322. Compared with
accent and country cues, language statements plausibly evoke
stronger expectations about communicative context and speak-
ing style, which can steer the model toward prior-driven inter-
pretations when acoustic evidence is weak or ambiguous.
BeyondF1 gap discrepancy, Figure 2 visualizes how iden-
tity prompting changes themarginalprediction distribution. For
Qwen2-Audio, swapping only the injected identity statement
produces a clear reallocation of predicted label across emotions.
For example, under the accent condition, the Ethiopian accent
yields a markedly higher fraction of Sad predictions, whereas
the Japanese and French accents yield the fewest. Notably, the
relative ordering of Sad frequency is consistent across datasets
(more obvious than for the other emotions), suggesting a repeat-
able shift in which certain identity descriptors steer predictions
toward Sad under the controlled prompt setting, independent of
correctness.

Acoustic Focus Shifts

language attribute, Neutral↔Angry reaches 0.044 on MSP-
Podcast and 0.050 on BIIC-Podcast, with only a few deviations

4.3. Effectiveness of proposed mitigation method
From Table 2, both LoRA substantially reduceF1gap relative to
the unadapted models (e.g., in the language attribute on MSP-
Podcast, mix-prompt LoRA drops from 0.1239 to 0.0025).
Under permutation testing, mix-prompt LoRA is not signifi-
cantly different from the permutation null distribution, indicat-
ing that identity sensitivity is strongly reduced under this pro-
tocol. Base-prompt LoRA yields a comparable reduction and is
also non-significant in most settings, except for BIIC-Podcast
accent, which remains significant but small (0.0039).
Among pre-trained models, DeSTA2.5-Audio shows the
smallest gaps (e.g., 0.0388 in the language attribute on MSP-
Podcast) while also achieving the highest macro-F1 (0.5638),
suggesting that stronger emotion-recognition competence can
damp identity-driven dispersion even whenF1 gap remains sig-
nificant under the permutation null. Overall, both the pre-
trained comparisons and the LoRA results point to the same
pattern: stronger emotion-recognition capability tends to coin-
cide with weaker sensitivity to injected identity descriptors.
5. Conclusion
We investigate hidden priors in speech-LLM emotion recogni-
tion under a controlled fixed-audio prompt setting. Across mod-
els, identity cues alone shift emotion predictions, with language
cues typically producing the largest dispersion. Saliency anal-
ysis shows that prediction transitions across identity prompts
are generally distinguishable from within-utterance random-
ness, whereas no-transition cases usually are not, suggesting
that saliency shifts more often co-occur with identity-induced
label changes. Finally, lightweight LoRA substantially reduces
F1gap, and mix-prompt LoRA becomes indistinguishable from
the null distribution. A limitation is that speakers embody richer
and overlapping identity attributes than the cues studied here;
prompts combining multiple or more detailed identities remain
underexplored.
