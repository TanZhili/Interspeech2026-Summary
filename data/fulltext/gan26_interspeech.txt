Abstract
Phonetic accommodation refers to speakers’ adjustment of
acoustic-phonetic patterns toward a speech model. Prior work
comparing accommodation to human and AI voices has rarely
included L2 populations and typically examined a narrow set of
phonetic features. This study investigated the accommodation
of 28 L1 Mandarin learners of L2 English to a Microsoft Azure
TTS-generated voice and a native human voice in a sentence-
shadowing task. Acoustic analyses targeted stop-consonant
voice onset time (VOT), the tense–lax contrast /i/–/I/ in dura-
tion and spectral distance, and speech rhythm. Results showed
stronger VOT convergence toward the human voice, but greater
adjustment in vowel duration contrast and rhythm toward the
AI voice, with no difference in vowel spectral contrast. These
findings suggest that model-type effects on L2 phonetic accom-
modation are feature-dependent and AI and human voices may
play complementary roles in supporting L2 phonetic acquisi-
tion.
Index Terms: phonetic accommodation, human-computer in-

1. Introduction
Phonetic accommodation refers to speakers’ adjustment of their
acoustic-phonetic patterns towards those of another speaker
[1]. This phenomenon has been widely documented in human-
human interaction [2] and, with the emergence of artificial voice
technologies, also reported in human-device speech contexts
(e.g., [3, 4, 5, 6, 7]). As V oice-AI becomes increasingly inte-
grated into everyday communication, now pervasive in smart
devices, virtual assistants and digital content, a growing body
of work has begun to investigate how phonetic accommoda-
tion elicited by AI voices and human voices compare (e.g.,
[8, 9, 10, 11, 12, 13]). However, these studies have exclu-
sively focused on native speakers, with limited attention paid to
second language (L2) populations. It remains unclear whether
L2 speakers, whose phonetic knowledge is still developing and
whose phonological categories remain more plastic, respond to
AI voice input in the same way as they do to human voice in-
put. This gap is particularly significant considering phonetic
accommodation also serves as an important mechanism under-
lying L2 phonetic acquisition [14, 15]. The Interactive Align-
ment Model (IAM) [16, 17, 18] describes phonetic accommo-
dation as automatic perception-production coupling, which cor-
responds to the central process involved in L2 speech learning,
where learners try to align their speech patterns with those of
the target language input. As suggested by the revised Speech
Learning Model (SLM-r) [19, 20], repeated iterations of this

perception-production loop, where perception tunes phonetic
representations to guide production and production in turn feeds
back into the learner’s experience, would gradually drive acous-
tic cue reweighting and phonetic category restructuring, finally
contributing to L2 phonetic development. In light of this, com-
paring phonetic accommodation to human and AI voices can
help clarify the respective roles of AI and human voice input in
L2 phonetic acquisition. This question is of great practical sig-
nificance as technologies such as text-to-speech (TTS) systems
and interactive voice agents, now capable of generating near-
native speech and providing more customizable and accessible
service than ever, increasingly complement and, in some cases,
replace human voice input in language learning scenarios [21].
It is therefore crucial to understand whether AI and human voice
models differ in their efficacy in L2 pronunciation training. To
address the aforementioned research gap and provide empirical
insights into L2 pedagogical practice, the present study com-
pares how L2 Mandarin speakers of English accommodate to
AI versus human voices in a sentence-shadowing task.
Previous research on native speakers’ accommodation to
AI versus human voices has shown that the patterns are highly
selective and modulated by multiple factors, including speaker
characteristics such as gender [8], age [10] and cognitive pro-
cessing style [11]; model voice gender [8]; expressiveness of
the target words [12]; social and functional pressure [13]; and
conversational roles of the speakers [9]. Perceptual studies re-
ported consistent stronger convergence to human than device
voices [8, 10, 22]. In contrast, acoustic analyses have revealed
a feature-specific picture. In [11, 12], greater convergence to
human than AI voices was found for vowel/word duration, but
no differences observed for fundamental frequency (F0). [6]
examined a broader feature set in German and found a human
advantage over HMM- and diphone-synthesized voices only for
vowel quality ([e:] vs. [E:]); for allophonic variants ([Iç] vs.
[Ik]) and temporal/spectral similarity measures such as DTW
cost and match value, the diphone-synthesized voice elicited
convergence as strong as the natural voice. To date, only one
study has extended this line of inquiry to L2 learners: [23] found
non-native speakers accommodated to a human voice under a
machine guise to a similar extent as L1 German speakers but it
didn’t involve a real synthetic voice or a direct human-versus-
synthetic comparison. Moreover, given the technological shift
to end-to-end neural TTS and the markedly improved natural-
ness of current synthetic voices, findings based on diphone-
and HMM-based synthesis may not generalize. The present
study addresses this using a modern neural TTS voice in a
sentence-shadowing task, a less interactive paradigm that min-
imizes social variables while mirroring the listen-and-repeat
practice commonly used in L2 pronunciation training. The
study also controls for previously identified modulating factors

2. Methods
2.1. Participants
Twenty-eight female native Mandarin speakers (M ± SD = 20.6
± 0.6 years; range 20–22) were recruited. All were Economics
majors with normal vision and hearing, and had passed the Col-
lege English Test Band 6 (CET-6), a national English profi-
ciency test corresponding to an upper-intermediate level (ap-
proximately B2 on the Common European Framework of Ref-
erence for Languages, CEFR). None used English regularly out-
side coursework. All participants received compensation upon
completion.
2.2. Stimuli
2.2.1. Sentences and target tokens
Stimulus sentences were all drawn from the ALLSSTAR corpus
[28]. Target vowels (/i/ and /I/) were all under primary stress to
ensure sufficient duration for acoustic analysis, and no sentence
contained additional unstressed instances of the same vowel.
Target stop consonants (/p, t, k, b, d, g/) were all syllable-initial
stops not preceded by sibilants or nasals within the same word
to minimize coarticulatory effects on VOT [29]. These criteria
led to selection of 47 declarative sentences, with a mean length
of 5.96 words (SD = 1.56), ranging from 4 to 12 words. To-
gether with three filler sentences (each 5 words) containing no
target phones, the full set comprised 50 sentences in total. Table
1 presents the counts of target tokens in the final stimulus set.

2.2.2. Model voices
The stimulus set for the shadowing task was produced by two
model voices. A 19-year-old female native American English
speaker from the ALLSSTAR corpus [28] served as the human
model, selected for her clear, neutral speech. The AI voice was
generated on the Microsoft Azure TTS platform using the fe-
male voice ”en-US-LunaNeural”, a neural TTS voice described
as ”warm, sincere, and pleasant.”
2.2.3. Preprocessing of model voices
Audio from both model voices was converted to 48 kHz
mono 16-bit W A V with 20 ms leading and 220 ms trailing si-
lence to prevent truncation and ensure smooth trial transitions.
Loudness was pair-matched between AI and human sentences

2.3. Experiment Design and Procedure
The experiment used a two-session, within-participant
crossover design. It began with a baseline reading-aloud task,
followed by two shadowing sessions separated by a 10-day
interval. In the first session, half of the participants shadowed
the AI voice and the other half shadowed the human voice; in
the second session, the order was reversed (See Figure 1). The
first three trials of each task were filler sentences serving as
warm-up. Baseline reading orders were counterbalanced and
shadowing trials were randomized.
Recording sessions were conducted in a small sound-
attenuated booth on the campus of a university in Tianjin,
China. The ambient noise floor inside the booth was checked
prior to recording and was approximately -32 dBFS. The exper-
iment ran on a fanless MacBook using the Gorilla platform [30]
for stimulus presentation. Audio was recorded with a Zoom
H1n (48 kHz/16-bit W A V , 80 Hz high-pass filter) positioned
20–25 cm from the participant. Participants wore wired Apple
EarPods during the shadowing task. Both the reading-aloud and
shadowing tasks were self-paced, during which the participants
were instructed to maintain a consistent mouth-microphone dis-
tance and avoid unnecessary movements. The shadowing task
was framed as listen-and-repeat, with no explicit instruction to
imitate and without revealing whether the voice was AI or hu-
man. All instructions were given in Mandarin Chinese. Each
recording session lasted approximately 25–30 minutes.
3. Analyses and Results
3.1. Data preprocessing and feature extraction
After manual segmentation and exclusion of filler sentences,
the analysis dataset comprised 3,945 sentence-level clips (1,315
baseline and 2,630 shadowing, evenly split across model voice
types). One participant omitted a sentence in Session 1 so
all recordings of that sentence from this participant were ex-
cluded. All clips were force-aligned using Montreal Forced
Aligner (MFA), producing utterance-, word-, and phone-level
TextGrids, with a syllable tier added manually. VOT bound-
aries were obtained using AutoVOT [31]. V owel duration, for-

3.4. Statistical modelling
Linear mixed-effects models were fitted in R (lmerTest) [36]
with DID as the dependent variable and model voice (AI vs.
Human) as the fixed effect of interest. Models initially included
session and the model voice×session interaction, which was re-
moved when non-significant. A single model was fitted for DID
in VOTnorm including participant, sentence, and word as ran-
dom effects. V owel-contrast models for DID in DR and DID
in SD included participant as the intercept, with speech rate as
a covariate. For rhythm, two separate models were fitted with
random intercepts for participant and sentence: one for DID in
duration-based measure, the other for DID in intensity-based
measure, with all metrics used in each measure entered simul-
taneously in each model. Baseline-distance checks were con-
ducted for each feature; when baseline mismatch was observed,
baseline-to-model distance was added as a covariate.

3.5. Results
Across features, session effects were generally absent; only
duration-based rhythm showed a voice×session pattern, so it
is reported separately by session. For DR, SD, and both rhythm
measures, baseline mismatch was observed and baseline-to-
model distance was added as a covariate, which did not change
the qualitative results.
Participants showed greater accommodation to the human
than to the AI model in normalized VOT (β= 0.00402, SE
= 0.00152,t= 2.64,p=.008), suggesting a human ad-
vantage for VOT convergence. In contrast, accommodation in
vowel duration ratio (DR) was stronger in the AI condition
(β=−0.2136, SE= 0.0209,t=−10.22,p < .001). No
reliable AI–Human difference was observed for vowel spectral-
distance (SD) accommodation (β=−0.0606, SE= 0.0833,
t=−0.73,p=.470). For duration-based rhythm, AI–Human
contrasts were non-significant in both sessions (Session 1: es-
timate= 0.278, SE= 0.153,z= 1.82,p=.068; Session 2:
estimate= 0.027, SE= 0.153,z= 0.18,p=.857), provid-
ing no robust evidence for a model-voice difference. By con-
trast, intensity-based rhythm showed stronger accommodation
for AI than for the human model (β=−0.427, SE= 0.0358,
t=−11.91,p < .001), indicating a reliable AI advantage for
intensity-based rhythm adjustment. See Table 2 for a compact
summary and Figure 2 for visualization of the found effects.
4. Discussion and Conclusion
Results of the present study have demonstrated different ac-
commodation patterns across the three phonetic features, which
replicates the feature-dependent picture observed in L1 litera-
ture [11, 12, 6]. It suggests that the feature-specific nature of
human versus AI voice accommodation is not limited to native
speakers but extends to L2 learners.
Stronger VOT convergence toward the human than the AI
voice was found. VOT is a continuous acoustic dimension re-
quiring precise target interval matching on the VOT continuum.
A possible parallel case is vowel quality, another continuous,
fine-grained feature that entails accurate spectral target posi-
tioning in F1-F2 space, for which [6] also found human voice
advantage over synthetic ones. The converging patterns sug-
gest such subtle features may be especially sensitive to the fi-
delity and naturalness of the model voice. Human voices pre-
serve context-conditioned timing patterns (e.g., modulation by
stress, phrase position, and coarticulation), which provide mul-
tiple exemplars across contexts, enabling learners to extract the

regularities governing VOT realization. The AI voice, despite
being state-of-the-art neural TTS (Microsoft Azure), may still
implement these patterns less faithfully or present VOT with

other possibility is that this specific human voice model used in
the present study may have offered more salient aspiration cues
(e.g., higher-energy aspiration), making the VOT interval eas-
ier to perceive and reproduce. Future research should system-
atically examine how specific acoustic properties of TTS and
human voices, such as aspiration intensity, burst characteristics,
and contextual timing pattern affect VOT accommodation.
A different pattern emerged for vowel contrasts. For the
vowel duration contrast, greater alignment was found toward
the AI rather than the human voice model, which contrasts with
previous studies that showed greater convergence towards the
human speech in vowel duration [11, 12]. However, human
voices in those studies were paired with human faces, explic-
itly signaling the voice identity and adding a visual cue. In the
present study, by contrast, participants were not made aware
of the voice identity and could not reliably distinguish them in
the informal post-experiment interviews. This methodological
difference matters because alignment has been found to be en-
hanced under a human guise [4]. The revealed human iden-
tity in prior work may have amplified alignment toward the hu-
man voice, contributing to the observed human advantage. It’s
also noteworthy that the vowel duration contrast measures rel-
ative timing between tense and lax vowels, rather than abso-
lute duration. With potentially less variation in speech rate and
prominence patterns than human speech, the AI voice may have
realized this contrast more consistently and provided a clearer
template, making the tense-lax timing relationship easier to ex-
tract. For spectral contrast, no difference emerged between
voice models. Unlike [6], which examined absolute spectral
targets and found human advantage, the present study measured
relational spectral contrast. As with duration contrast, this re-
lational target may benefit from AI voice model’s stable cate-
gory boundaries, even if its absolute formant precision hasn’t
reached human parity. Moreover, L1 Mandarin speakers tend to
rely more on duration than spectral cues for tense-lax distinc-
tions [27]. If learners attend less to spectral information, any
potential advantage of either voice model may be attenuated,
resulting in comparable adjustment toward both.
For speech rhythm, intensity-based metrics showed greater
adjustment toward AI than human voice, while duration-based
metrics showed no difference. [6] found that an HMM-
synthesized voice can elicit prosodic convergence comparable
to natural voices in pitch-accent realization. Extending this, the
present results suggest that for overall rhythm, a modern neural
TTS voice may elicit even stronger convergence than the hu-
man voice. Moreover, unlike human speech, which shows nat-
ural variability in stress and reduction across contexts, AI voice
might exhibit more regular intensity contours and repeatable
prominence placement across sentences, making the rhythmic
patterns easier to track during rapid shadowing. Many partici-
pants also indicated in the post-experiment interviews that they
captured the consistent rhythm pattern across sentences in the
AI voice, confirming its perceptual saliency. Duration-based
metrics showed no difference between human and AI model
voices, probably because the durational differences in conso-
nantal and vocalic intervals are too subtle to be perceived [37].
Additionally, perceptual research has shown that expressiveness
strengthens human vocal alignment toward V oice-AI [3]. It is
possible the AI voice used here was perceived as more emotion-
ally expressive than the human voice, further facilitating conver-
gence. Future research should disentangle the roles of acous-
tic regularity and perceived expressiveness in driving rhythmic
convergence, and investigate whether these factors modulate the
difference in accommodation to AI versus human voices.
This study provides empirical evidence on L2 learners’
phonetic accommodation to AI versus human voices, address-
ing a critical gap in the literature. It reveals potentially comple-
mentary strengths of AI and human voices across phonetic di-
mensions, providing guidance for TTS voice integration in L2
pronunciation training. Future work should test a broader range
of voice models, language varieties and phonetic features, ex-
amine how individual differences and voice properties modulate
these effects, and investigate whether the patterns generalize to
more interactive tasks. A native speaker control group should
also be included to examine whether L1 and L2 speakers show
different accommodation patterns to human versus AI voices.
