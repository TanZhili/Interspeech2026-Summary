Abstract
This study investigates the impact of the common cold on speech
using a new ecologically valid corpus. TheCommon Cold Cor-
puscomprises paired recordings of 85 German speakers, col-
lected remotely via personal devices during acute upper respira-
tory tract infections and healthy states. Unlike prior lab-based
datasets focussing on severe cases, this corpus captures mod-
erate symptoms in everyday acoustic environments. We eval-
uate eGeMAPS features against modern speaker embeddings
(ECAPA2, TitaNet, ReDimNet). While acoustic analysis reveals
consistent downward trends in pitch and harmonics-to-noise ra-
tio when ill, only few features survive alpha-error correction. In
contrast, speaker embeddings exhibit significant, directed dis-
placement in the identity space under moderate symptomatology.
Results suggest that neural models constitute a valuable, more
sensitive addition to prosodic analyses, providing a robust foun-
dation for non-invasive digital biomarkers in realistic settings.
Index Terms: common cold, upper respiratory tract infections,
speaker embeddings, acoustic phonetics, speech corpus
1. Introduction
Speech is the primary modality of human communication,
conveying not only linguistic information but also paralin-
guistic and biological cues related to speaker identity and
health [1, 2, 3, 4]. Acute upper respiratory tract infections (UR-
TIs) frequently disrupt this channel by inducing physiological
changes such as vocal fold inflammation, increased mucus pro-
duction, and altered resonance characteristics [ 5, 6]. These
changes can affect voice quality, articulation, and spectral prop-
erties [7, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12, 13, 14].
Previous work has demonstrated that pronounced URTI
symptoms are detectable at the acoustic level, particularly un-
der controlled recording conditions and high symptom sever-
ity [7, 15]. Similarily, robustness studies in speaker verification
have shown that embedding-based systems degrade under acous-
tic domain shifts such as noise, channel mismatch, or compres-
sion artifacts [16]. However, the influence of short-term, natu-
rally occurring physiological variation, such as moderate acute
illness, on modern speaker embeddings remains insufficiently
understood. In contrast to technical mismatch, illness-related
variation originates from changes in speech production rather
than the transmission channel, potentially affecting speaker rep-
resentations in different ways. Existing corpora typically capture
either cross-speaker variability or highly controlled illness con-
ditions. They do not enable systematic within-speaker analyses
of short-term physiological variation under real-world recording
conditions.

Despite the increasing reliance on deep speaker embeddings
for speaker verification and anonymisation [17], little is known
about their stability under acute biological perturbations in eco-
logically valid recording scenarios. In particular, there is a
lack of publicly documented corpora that systematically cap-
ture paired healthy and ill recordings from the same speakers
collected under real-world conditions.
To address this gap, we introduce theCommon Cold Corpus,
a longitudinal dataset comprising paired recordings of speakers
in healthy and acutely ill (URTI) states. The corpus is collected
remotely using personal devices in everyday environments, en-
abling realistic within-speaker comparisons across health condi-
tions. In this paper, we (i) describe the corpus design and data
collection procedure, (ii) present acoustic analyses contrasting
healthy and ill speech under moderate symptom severity, and
(iii) quantify illness-related displacement in modern speaker em-
bedding spaces across multiple architectures. By combining
verification metrics with embedding shift and identity-crossover
analyses, we examine the stability of speaker identity under
acute physiological variation. To our knowledge, this is the first
study to systematically quantify illness-related embedding dis-
placement using paired healthy/ill recordings collected under
ecologically valid conditions.
2. Related Work
The systematic analysis of “cold speech” gained promi-
nence through the Interspeech 2017 Computational Paralin-
guistics Challenge (ComParE), which featured theCold Sub-
Challenge[ 7]. Using the Upper Respiratory Tract Infection Cor-
pus (URTIC), several studies demonstrated that machine learning
systems can distinguish between cold and non-cold speech above
chance level [8, 11, 18, 19, 20, 21, 22]. In this setting, health sta-
tus was derived from a self-reported global illness severity score
(0–7 scale) binarised at a threshold of 6, such that the cold class
predominantly reflected strongly symptomatic cases. Record-
ings were collected under controlled acoustic conditions using
standardised hardware. Under these circumstances and in similar
studies, acoustic correlates of URTI – including increased jitter
and shimmer, reduced harmonics-to-noise ratio (HNR), altered
spectral tilt, and reduced articulatory precision – were identi-
fied [8, 9, 11, 12, 14]. These findings show that URTI symptoms
can cause measurable signal-level changes when both symptom
severity and recording conditions are tightly controlled.
Modern speaker verification systems rely on deep neural
speaker embeddings such as x-vectors [23], ECAPA-TDNN [24],
TitaNet [25], ECAPA2 [26], and ReDimNet [27]. These mod-
els are optimised to maximise inter-speaker separation while
minimizing intra-speaker variability. Robustness studies show
that non-neutral speech, including emotional speech [28], Lom-

More recently, Ferro Filho et al. [ 16] evaluated ECAPA-

compression, and sampling-rate degradation. They reported con-
sistent performance degradation across models, demonstrating
that embedding stability cannot be assumed under technical and
environmental mismatch. These findings raise the question of
whether similar vulnerabilities arise under physiological pertur-
bations that originate from changes in speech production rather
than the acoustic transmission channel.
Physiological variation represents a distinct type of domain
shift. Acute illness affects the speech production mechanism
itself, potentially introducing subtle but systematic changes that
may not be captured by classical acoustic markers, particularly
under moderate symptom severity and heterogeneous recording
conditions. While illness detection under high severity has been
studied, and embedding robustness under technical mismatch has
been investigated, the impact of moderate, naturally occurring
illness on speaker identity stability in modern embedding spaces
remains insufficiently understood. To our knowledge, longitu-
dinal analyses using paired healthy and ill recordings collected
under ecologically valid conditions are still unexplored.
The present work addresses this gap by introducing and
analysing the newly developed Common Cold Corpus. Beyond
corpus presentation and its acoustic analysis, including statis-
tical comparisons of ill vs. healthy speech, we systematically
quantify illness-related displacement in embedding space, in-
cluding verification performance, intra-speaker dispersion, and
directional shift characteristics across multiple contemporary
speaker embedding architectures.
3. The Common Cold Corpus
Speech data were collected remotely via SoSci Survey [31], en-
abling participants to record speech with their personal devices
in everyday acoustic environments [ 32, 33, 34]. Data collec-
tion began in 2024 and is ongoing; the present analysis includes
recordings from 2024–2025. Each participant completed two
sessions: one during a self-reported acute URTI episode (“ill
condition”) and one in a subjectively healthy state (“healthy
condition”), conducted at least seven days apart. This paired de-
sign allows controlled within-speaker comparisons across health
states. Each session comprised a fixed set of prompted speech
tasks, including read speech and short spontaneous speech. Par-
ticipants readNorth Wind and Sun (NWS)[ 35] andThe Butter
Story (TBS)in German [ 36]. Both tasks provide a standardised,
phonetically balanced reading task, with TBS eliciting more
naturalistic speech than NWS.

cases recorded under laboratory conditions, the present corpus
enables the investigation of moderate illness effects under ecolog-

analysis of acoustic variation and embedding-level displacement
within the same speakers.
4. Acoustic Analysis: Ill vs. Healthy Speech
To replicate previously reported cold-related acoustic changes,
we analysed the more severity-diverse Common Cold Corpus.
Using eGeMAPS feature set [38] and openSMILE, 88 functional
features were extracted; F0 mean and median were additionally
converted from semitones to Hz, resulting in 90 parameters.
Acoustic analysis was conducted separately for the two text
promptsNWSandTBSto assess whether acoustic changes are
consistent across different phonetic material. Statistically, we
compared the differences of each speaker between healthy and
ill condition for every acoustic feature. Given potential non-
normality of feature differences, we assessed normality using
the Anderson–Darling test. Statistical significance was then eval-
uated using paired t-tests for normally distributed differences
and Wilcoxon signed-rank tests otherwise. To account for multi-
ple comparisons across the 90 features, p-values were adjusted
(p.adj) using the Benjamini-Hochberg (BH) procedure.
For theNWSprompt, several pitch and periodicity features
showed significant changes. The 80th percentile of F0 exhibited
the most robust decrease under illness (p=<.0001; p.adj=.0008),
suggesting a reduction in the upper vocal reach when ill. Ad-
ditionally, the Harmonics-to-Noise Ratio (HNR) significantly
decreased in illness (p=.0002; p.adj=.0095), indicating increased
breathiness in that condition. Acoustic features only significant
before BH correction are listed in Table 1. These parameters fur-
ther encompass F0 differences in mean, median as well as range
which all decrease in the ill condition. Moreover, the standard
deviation of HNR increased when ill while the standard devia-
tion of the spectral slope (0–500 Hz) decreased. Lastly, voiced
segments were shorter under the ill condition and the standard
deviation of the second Formant bandwidth was narrower.
The results forTBSrevealed similar trends, although the
effects were not significant after BH correction. Aligning with
the findings for the previous reading task, we observed nominally
significant differences (p<.05 unadjusted) in mean HNR, the
80th percentile of F0, mean and median F0 (Hz), all following
the same direction as in the first prompt: a decrease in pitch
stability and periodicity. Additionally, mean local jitter increased
when the speakers were ill and the standard deviation of F0 was

5. Speaker Embedding Analysis
To complement the acoustic feature analysis, we investigated
how illness affects speaker representations in modern embedding-
based verification systems. We conducted a text-controlled
speaker embedding analysis using paired healthy and ill record-
ings from all 85 speakers. For each speaker, all available healthy
recordings with the same text prompt were compared to the
corresponding ill recording.
All recordings were resampled to 16 kHz mono and trun-
cated to a maximum duration of 15 s (corresponding to the me-
dian reading duration). Speaker embeddings were extracted with
four architectures (ECAPA-TDNN via SpeechBrain, Titanet,
ECAPA2, and ReDimNet) and L2-normalised. Verification used
cosine-similarity scoring following standard embedding-based
speaker verification practice [23]. All architectures were evalu-
ated using their publicly available pretrained implementations
and recommended configurations.
Verification was conducted in a text-controlled setup with a
closed gallery. For each ill condition query embedding, the
gallery contained all healthy recordings with the same text
prompt (NWSorTBS). To preserve within-speaker session vari-
ability, centroid pooling was not applied in the primary analysis;
instead, each healthy recording contributed an individual compar-
ison score. Trials were partitioned into genuine (ill vs. healthy,
same speaker) and imposter (ill vs. healthy, different speaker)
comparisons, resulting in 188 genuine comparisons across all
speakers and prompts. Per model, 188 genuine and 15,792
imposter trials were evaluated. Performance metrics included
ROC-AUC, Equal Error Rate (EER), and Cohen’s d between
genuine and imposter score distributions.
Despite condition-induced embedding displacement, verifi-
cation performance remained high across all architectures (Ta-
ble 3). Titanet achieved the lowest EER (1.51%, AUC 0.9893),
followed by ReDimNet (1.84%, 0.9897), while ECAPA2 (2.83%,
0.9853) and ECAPA-TDNN (3.10%, 0.9859) showed slightly
higher error rates.
As a within-condition baseline, we computed healthy–
healthy verification using cross-text pairing (NWSvs.TBS, and
vice versa) while preserving the same trial construction and scor-
ing procedure as in the cross-condition experiments. This avoids
trivial self-comparisons and provides a reference performance
level without physiological mismatch. Healthy–healthy cross-
text verification yielded low error rates across models (ECAPA-
TDNN 1.64%, Titanet <0.01%, ECAPA2 1.14%, ReDimNet
2.32%). Compared to this baseline, the ill condition increased
EER by approximately 1–2 percentage points for most architec-
tures, while overall separability remained high.
To further assess the stability of the verification results,
we evaluated centroid-pooled healthy embeddings (averaging
healthy recordings per speaker and prompt). Results were un-
changed relative to the unpooled analysis (differences only at
machine precision), indicating that healthy-session variability
does not materially affect verification outcomes.
To assess duration sensitivity, we repeated the full evaluation
using a maximum duration of 10 s. Titanet remained unchanged,
whereas ECAPA-TDNN and ReDimNet showed only minor
degradation (ECAPA-TDNN: EER +0.49 pp; ReDimNet: +0.53
pp). ECAPA2 exhibited stronger degradation (+2.37 pp), indicat-
ing greater sensitivity to reduced signal duration. Overall, 15 s
yielded more stable performance across models.

6. Discussion
A central finding is that deep speaker embeddings show mea-
surable displacement even when traditional acoustic markers
lose statistical significance after correction. While prior studies
reported clear acoustic separability under high symptom severity
and controlled conditions [7, 8, 11, 18, 20, 21, 22], we observe
embedding shifts even at moderate symptom levels under eco-
logically valid conditions. This suggests that modern speaker
representations capture condition-related variation differently
from the investigated acoustic features, with illness effects dis-
tributed across multiple dimensions rather than concentrated in
canonical markers such as pitch or intensity.
Although most acoustic features did not remain significant
after BH-correction, unadjusted trends help explain the observed
embedding displacement. Reductions in F0 mean, median,
and range, lower HNR and higher jitter, indicate physiologi-
cal changes that may shift speakers from their healthy baseline.
These patterns replicate clinical findings on vocal fold oedema,
increased vibrating mass, and irregular vibration during UR-
TIs [5, 39]. The sign-flip permutation analysis further suggests a
directed illness-related perturbation rather than random variation.
It cannot be guaranteed that the observed displacements are
illness-specific, as speaker embeddings may also be affected
by channel conditions, environmental noise, and speaking style.
Nevertheless, the paired-speaker design of the corpus eliminates
additional inter-speaker variability that is typically present in
studies comparing different speakers in healthy and ill states.
Sensitivity was model-dependent: TitaNet [25] showed the
largest shift, ReDimNet [27] remained comparatively stable, and
ECAPA2 [26] was more affected by signal truncation. Thus,
robustness to acoustic-domain shifts does not necessarily imply
robustness to physiological variability, with implications for
verification reliability across temporary health states.
The increase in intra-condition variance and partial identity
crossover (6.38%) raises concerns for biometric and forensic
applications. While illness-induced shifts may be exploitable
as digital biomarkers [ 1], they may also increase the risk of
condition-dependent false non-matches.
Limitations include the relatively small corpus size com-
pared to ComParE benchmarks [ 7], reduced statistical power
due to ecological variance, and uncontrolled confounders such
as device characteristics, microphone placement, environmental
conditions, hydration status, and time of day. Nevertheless, the
consistent within-speaker displacement observed across partici-
pants suggests a meaningful physiological contribution.
The present study represents the first release of the Com-
mon Cold Corpus. Ongoing data collection has already ex-
panded the corpus to 157 German-language recordings (approxi-
mately 18 hours) and added sustained vowels (/a:/ and /i:/) for
more detailed analyses of phonation and voice quality. Future
work should leverage the expanded dataset to improve statistical
power, incorporate symptom severity information, investigate
symptom-specific effects, and further disentangle physiological
and recording-related variability.
