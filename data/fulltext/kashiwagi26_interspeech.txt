Abstract
Multi-talker automatic speech recognition (ASR) has attracted
increasing attention for overlapping speech scenarios. Hypoth-
esis Clustering and Merging (HCM) achieves strong perfor-
mance by clustering hypotheses in transcript space, but it does
not explicitly consider speaker identity during clustering. As
a result, HCM may fail when multiple speakers utter identi-
cal content and may not fully utilize enrollment information
in target-speaker settings. In this paper, we incorporate con-
tinuous speaker embeddings into the HCM framework by re-
defining the clustering distance in joint transcript-speaker space.
The proposed method improves robustness in target-speaker-
free scenarios under identical-content conditions and enables
more effective use of enrollment information in target-speaker
multi-talker ASR. Experimental results show consistent im-
provements over conventional HCM, achieving up to 46% rel-
ative WER reduction under identical-content conditions while
maintaining competitive performance on LibriMix benchmarks.
In addition, the proposed speaker-aware formulation improves
target-speaker multi-talker ASR by enabling embedding-based
speaker selection, achieving 23% WER reductions compared to
discrete speaker-ID prompting.

1. Introduction
Multi-talker automatic speech recognition (ASR) has attracted
increasing attention due to its importance in real-world conver-
sational scenarios involving overlapping speech [1–10]. While
modern ASR systems achieve near-human performance in
single-speaker conditions, practical environments such as meet-
ings and daily conversations frequently involve multiple speak-
ers speaking simultaneously. Robust multi-talker ASR is there-
fore essential for the deployment of speech technology in re-
alistic settings. The core challenge of multi-talker ASR lies
in jointly estimating linguistic content and speaker assignment
from a mixed signal. Given an input mixture containing speech
from multiple speakers, the system must recover a set of tran-
scripts without prior knowledge of speaker ordering. Beyond
transcription accuracy, the model must be scalable to cope with
various number of speakers and overlap patterns as the number
of speakers and overlap patterns vary.
Several paradigms have been proposed to address this prob-
lem. Permutation Invariant Training (PIT) resolves permuta-
tion ambiguity by minimizing loss over all possible output as-
signments [11–15]. Serialized Output Training (SOT) repre-
sents multi-speaker output as a single serialized sequence with
speaker-change tokens [16–19]. More recently, Hypothesis
Clustering and Merging (HCM) [20] introduced a hypothesis-
space formulation. Instead of enforcing fixed speaker streams
during decoding, HCM generates multiple hypotheses and per-
forms clustering and merging in transcript space. This decou-
pling of hypothesis generation and speaker assignment realizes
flexible handling of varying speaker numbers and strong empir-
ical performance.
Despite its effectiveness, HCM performs clustering solely
based on transcript-level similarity and does not explicitly in-
corporate speaker identity information. This design leads to two
practical limitations. First, when multiple speakers utter iden-
tical or highly similar phrases, transcript similarity becomes
non-discriminative, and hypotheses originating from different
speakers may be merged incorrectly. Second, in target-speaker
scenarios where enrollment utterances are provided, the dis-
crete speaker-token formulation may not fully preserve the cor-
respondence between enrollment information and the resulting
hypothesis clusters. These limitations indicate that transcript
similarity alone is insufficient for robust speaker separation in
hypothesis space.
To address these issues, we incorporate continuous speaker
embeddings into the HCM framework. Speaker embeddings,
widely used in speaker recognition and diarization, encode
speaker identity in a continuous acoustic space [21–23]. We
redefine the HCM clustering distance to operate in joint tran-
script–speaker space, integrating transcript similarity and em-
bedding distance while preserving the original decoding ar-
chitecture. The proposed formulation provides a unified so-
lution for both target-speaker-free and target-speaker multi-
talker ASR. In target-speaker-free settings, speaker-aware clus-
tering restores robustness under identical-content conditions.
In target-speaker settings, enrollment embeddings are directly
compared with clustered speaker representations, enabling
more consistent utilization of enrollment information without
relying solely on discrete speaker tokens. Experimental re-
sults demonstrate consistent improvements over conventional
HCM. Under identical-content conditions, the proposed method
achieves up to 46% relative WER reduction while maintain-
ing competitive performance on standard LibriMix benchmarks.
In addition, embedding-based speaker selection further im-
proves recognition accuracy in target-speaker multi-talker ASR,
achieving approximately 23% relative WER reduction in the
clean two-speaker condition.
In summary, this paper makes the following contributions:
(1) we identify practical limitations of transcript-only cluster-
ing in HCM; (2) we propose a speaker-aware extension that
integrates continuous embeddings into hypothesis clustering;
(3) we demonstrate improvements in both target-speaker-free
and target-speaker multi-talker ASR without degrading stan-
dard performance.

2. Related Work
2.1. Multi-Talker ASR
Multi-talker ASR can be broadly categorized into target-
speaker-free and target-speaker settings. In the target-speaker-
free scenario, the system aims to recognize all speakers in
a mixture without prior enrollment information. In contrast,
target-speaker ASR assumes that an enrollment utterance of a
specific speaker is provided and focuses on recognizing that
speaker’s speech within the mixture.
Most recent studies have focused on the target-speaker-
free setting. PIT [11–15] resolves permutation ambiguity
by minimizing the loss over all possible output assignments.
SOT [16–19] models multiple speakers as a single serialized se-
quence with speaker-change tokens. More recently, HCM [20]
introduced a hypothesis-space formulation, where multiple de-
coding hypotheses are generated and subsequently clustered
and merged to produce speaker-specific outputs.
While these approaches achieve strong performance in the
target-speaker-free setting, practical applications often require
compatibility with both target-speaker-free and target-speaker
scenarios [24]. Designing a unified framework that supports
both settings remains an important challenge.
2.2. Hypothesis Clustering and Merging
HCM [20] introduces a hypothesis-space formulation of multi-
talker ASR. Instead of enforcing explicit speaker streams during
decoding, HCM first generates multiple decoding hypotheses
and subsequently clusters and merges them to produce speaker-
specific outputs.
HCM constructs discrete speaker tokens using speaker em-
beddings extracted from a pretrained and fine-tuned TitaNet en-
coder [21]. Given a speech segmentX, a speaker embedding is
computed as:

responding token is prepended to the transcript, allowing the
decoder to condition on speaker identity.
At inference time, the decoder estimates posterior proba-
bilities over speaker tokens based on CTC branch. The top-N
tokens are selected, and decoding is performed conditioned on

Although HCM was originally proposed for target-speaker-
free MT-ASR, it can easily be extended to target-speaker sce-
narios by conditioning decoding on a discrete speaker token. In
practice, an enrollment utterance is first converted into a speaker
embedding using the same TitaNet encoder, and the nearest k-
means centroid index is selected as the speaker token. This to-
ken is then used as a decoding prompt.
HCM achieves strong performance by decoupling hypoth-
esis generation from speaker assignment. However, it also has
inherent limitations. First, clustering is defined purely in tran-
script space. When multiple speakers utter identical or highly
similar phrases, the normalized edit distance between hypothe-
ses becomes zero. As a result, text-based clustering may incor-
rectly merge utterances from different acoustic sources under
identical-content conditions. Second, although HCM can be ex-
tended to target-speaker scenarios by conditioning decoding on
discrete speaker tokens, these tokens are derived from k-means
quantization of continuous speaker embeddings. These tokens
are primarily designed to generate diverse hypotheses rather
than to faithfully represent true acoustic speaker identity. Con-
sequently, when enrollment utterances are provided, the discrete
token representation may not fully preserve the detailed speaker
characteristics. The correspondence between enrollment infor-
mation and clustered hypotheses can be imperfect. These ob-
servations suggest that transcript-only clustering and discrete
speaker tokens are insufficient to fully exploit speaker informa-
tion in hypothesis space.
2.3. Speaker Embeddings
Speaker embeddings encode speaker identity into continuous
acoustic representations. Representative approaches include x-
vectors [27], ECAPA-TDNN embeddings [23], and large-scale
encoders such as TitaNet [21]. These embeddings are widely
used in speaker verification and diarization, where clustering is
performed directly in embedding space.
In the original HCM framework, speaker embeddings ex-
tracted by a fine-tuned TitaNet model are clustered using k-
means to construct discrete speaker tokens for training and de-
coding. However, the continuous embedding space itself is not
directly utilized during hypothesis clustering.
In contrast, our work incorporates continuous speaker em-
beddings directly into the hypothesis clustering objective. By
operating in joint transcript–speaker space, we enable consis-
tent use of speaker representations for both target-speaker-free
clustering and target-speaker selection, providing a unified ex-
tension of HCM across multi-talker ASR settings.
3. Proposed Method
The proposed method extends HCM [20] by integrating speaker
embeddings into the clustering mechanism. All decoding and
token construction procedures remain identical to HCM. We
modify only the hypothesis clustering stage and extend it to both
target-speaker-free and target-speaker MT-ASR scenarios.

4. Experiments
4.1. Experimental Setup
All experiments followed the configuration of the original HCM
system [20]. We used an encoder-decoder architecture con-
sisting of a Conformer encoder with 12 blocks (model dimen-
sion 256, 4 attention heads, convolution kernel size 31) and a
6-layer Transformer decoder (model dimension 256). The to-
tal number of parameters was approximately 30M, identical to
the HCM baseline. Speaker embeddings were extracted using
a pretrained TitaNet-large model [21]. The k-means clustering
withM= 1024clusters was applied to training-set speaker
embeddings to construct discrete speaker tokens. These tokens
were prepended to reference transcripts during training. Models
were trained on LibriMix [29] using the same training splits as
HCM. Training was performed for 100 epochs, and the top-10
checkpoints were selected and averaged based on development-
set WER.
During inference, decoding was performed with beam size
1 to save the computational complexity. Posterior probabili-
ties over speaker tokens were computed, and the top-N= 20
speaker tokens were selected. Decoding was performed inde-
pendently for each selected token, yielding a hypothesis set.
After that, AHC was applied to cluster hypotheses. Within each
cluster, ROVER was used for hypothesis merging. We evalu-
ated three distance measures: squared L2 distance, cosine dis-
tance, and inverse PLDA score to augment the normalized edit
distance with a speaker embedding distance term as described
in Section 3.
Evaluation was also conducted on LibriMix and on a
pseudo identical-content dataset constructed from VCTK [30].
Word Error Rate (WER) was used as the evaluation metric.

4.3. Target-Speaker MT-ASR
Table 2 reported results for the target-speaker MT-ASR sce-
nario. In the discrete token prompting baseline, an enrollment
utterance was first converted into a speaker embedding, which
was then quantized to the nearest k-means centroid. The corre-
sponding discrete speaker token was used as a prompt for the
HCM decoder.
In the two-speaker clean setting, embedding-based selec-
tion reduced WER from 18.7% to 14.4%, corresponding to a
23.0% relative improvement. In the two-speaker noisy condi-
tion, WER decreased from 28.5% to 25.2%, yielding an 11.6%
relative reduction.
In the three-speaker clean condition, a modest improve-
ment was observed (38.0% to 35.8%). In the highly challeng-
ing three-speaker noisy setting, WER exceeded 120% for both
strategies, indicating severe recognition difficulty due to ex-
treme overlap and noise. In this regime, the task itself became
intrinsically challenging, and the difference between strategies
remained marginal.
Overall, the results demonstrated that speaker-aware clus-
tering naturally extended to target-speaker ASR scenarios, par-
ticularly under moderate overlap conditions.
5. Conclusion
This paper revisited HCM and analyzed its reliance on
transcript-level similarity for hypothesis clustering. While
HCM achieves strong performance, its transcript-only cluster-
ing leads to limitations under identical-content conditions and
does not fully exploit enrollment information in target-speaker
settings. To address these issues, we proposed a speaker-aware
extension of HCM that integrates continuous speaker embed-
dings into the clustering distance. By operating in joint tran-
script–speaker space, the proposed framework provides a uni-
fied formulation for both target-speaker-free and target-speaker
multi-talker ASR. Experimental results demonstrated substan-
tial improvements under identical-content conditions, achieving
up to 46% relative WER reduction, while maintaining compet-
itive performance on standard LibriMix benchmarks. Further-
more, embedding-based speaker selection yielded a 23% rel-
ative WER reduction in the clean two-speaker target-speaker
condition. These results indicate that explicitly incorporating
speaker representations into hypothesis clustering is effective
for improving robustness across multi-talker ASR scenarios.
Future work includes investigating alternative clustering meth-
ods beyond k-means, such as graph-based or spectral clustering
approaches, which may better exploit the structure of speaker-
aware hypothesis representations. In addition, the computa-
tional complexity of HCM remains a practical concern due to
the need for generating and clustering multiple hypotheses. De-
veloping more efficient formulations that preserve performance
while reducing computational cost will be an important direc-
tion for future research.
