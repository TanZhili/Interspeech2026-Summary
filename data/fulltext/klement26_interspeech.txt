Abstract
Deep learning-based speech enhancement typically relies on
paired data, creating a domain gap between training and de-
ployment. Unsupervised methods based on generative adver-
sarial networks (GANs) use unpaired data as source priors, but
existing single-branch models often suffer from source leak-
age due to a dominant consistency loss or a weak clean speech
prior. In this work, we present a comprehensive analysis of prior
data in unsupervised GAN-based speech enhancement and uti-
lize a dual-branch framework that explicitly models both clean
speech and noise. Our experiments show that mismatched pri-
ors increase source leakage. By utilizing aligned noise prior
data—which are easy to collect in practice—we significantly
mitigate speech-noise leakage, prevent the over-suppression of
speech, and improve perceptual quality. This demonstrates
that leveraging environment-specific noise data is an effective
strategy for improving unsupervised enhancement in target do-
mains.
Index Terms: Speech Enhancement, Unsupervised Learning,
Deep Learning, Generative Adversarial Networks
1. Introduction
Speech enhancement (SE) aims to recover clean speech from an
observed noisy signal. While supervised deep neural networks
(DNNs) have driven significant progress in SE [1, 2, 3, 4], they
rely on strictly paired noisy and clean speech corpora. Because
obtaining perfectly paired real-world data is impossible, models
are typically trained on artificially simulated data, which intro-
duces a domain gap between training and deployment. To over-
come this limitation, recent research has increasingly shifted
toward unsupervised approaches.
One prominent line of work, including MetricGAN-U [5]
and MOS-GAN [6], guides model training using external non-
intrusive quality metrics like DNSMOS [7]. While these meth-
ods do not require clean speech data, their performance is in-
herently tied to the accuracy of the external black-box metrics,
and achieving better performance requires the costly re-training
of these metric models.
To avoid relying on external metrics, an alternative family
of unsupervised methods utilizes unpaired clean speech data to
define structural priors. Recent approaches based on variational
auto-encoders [8] and diffusion models [9] successfully enforce
these data-defined priors. However, their reliance on itera-
tive inference makes them computationally expensive and slow.
This poses a significant challenge for real-time, low-latency ap-
plications such as hearing aids or voice calls; hence, we focus on

Generative Adversarial Network (GAN)-based methods, which
perform enhancement efficiently in a single forward pass.
In recent GAN-based unsupervised SE studies, methods
such as unSE [10] and unSE+ [11] successfully enforce a clean
speech prior using a discriminator, while ensuring consistency
through reconstruction losses computed between the enhanced
speech and the noisy input. However, these single-branch
approaches estimate clean speech directly from the noisy in-
put without explicitly modeling the noise component. Con-
sequently, they cannot incorporate explicit knowledge about
the environmental noise to guide the enhancement process,
which can lead to noise leaking into the clean speech estimate
when consistency constraints (reconstruction losses) dominate
the clean speech prior.
In this work, we explore the role of data priors via dual-
branch unsupervised GAN-based speech enhancement models
that modelbothspeech and noise, leading to improvements in
enhancement quality and less source leakage. Our results show
that mismatched priors directly cause source leakage. Con-
versely, imposing an aligned noise prior results in a more ac-
curate noise estimate, which prevents the clean speech branch
from estimating leftover noise to satisfy the consistency loss.
This significantly reduces the amount of noise leaked into the
clean speech estimate and enables the use of environment-
specific noise recordings, which are often easy to collect in
practice, to improve the enhancement performance. The code-
base is released on GitHub 1.
2. Proposed Method
The proposed method is illustrated in Figure 1. It is an
adversarially-trained model consisting of a generator and a set
of discriminators. The generator is an encoder-decoder archi-
tecture that reconstructs the input audio, similar to Neural Au-
dio Codec (NAC). Internally, the proposed method consists of
two branches that output clean speech and noise audio. The
combination of the two audio signals is forced to reconstruct the
input. To force the model to separate the input into twodistinct
source signals, we use adversarial training with source-specific
discriminators to impose prior distributions on each branch. To
train each discriminator, we use two independent corpora —
clean speech and noise, defining how branch-specific outputs
should sound.

2.4. Why does it work?
Adversarial training with branch-specific discriminators con-
strains each branch to follow the distribution defined by the
corresponding data, i.e.,p( ˆxCS )≈P CS andp( ˆxN )≈P N .

Simultaneously, the combination of the two outputs must re-
construct the original noisy input. This dual constraint prevents
the clean speech branch from generating arbitrary clean speech
solely to satisfy the adversarial objective, as such signal would
increase the consistency loss. Consequently, the clean branch is
compelled to generate an enhanced version of the noisy input—
one that both aligns with the clean speech prior and contributes
to accurate input reconstruction. Ultimately, the model learns
to partition the signal by balancing the competing consistency
and adversarial objectives.
3. Experimental Setup
3.1. Model
We built the model architecture on top of the Descript Audio
Codec (DAC) [14]. The encoder contains four residual blocks
with downsampling factors[2,4,5,8], resulting in 320 down-
sampling ratio (50Hz for 16kHz input) and 1024-dimensional
embeddings. The decoder mirrors the encoder. Furthermore,
each branch contains a transformer with 8 layers, 8 attention
heads, and feed-forward dimensionality of 1536. We initialize
the encoder and decoder weights from a pre-trained DAC as
it improves convergence and enhancement performance com-
pared to training entirely from scratch.
We use three discriminator ensembles. The noisy input re-
construction one,D NS , exactly follows the DAC setup [14]—
multi-period (MPD) with periods[2,3,5,7,11]and multi-
band multi-scale STFT (MS-STFT) discriminator with 5 bands,
STFT window sizes[2048,1024,512], hop length 1
4 of the win-
dow size, and 32 convolution filters. Although this discrimina-
tor is not essential, we observed slightly worse quality of the
produced audio when not employing it. For the branch-specific
discriminators that define speech and noise priors, we use a
single-band MS-STFT discriminator, as we observed that us-
ing MPD did not improve the performance, with STFT window
sizes[2048,1024,512,256,128]and 128 convolution filters.
3.2. Data
We resample all datasets to 16kHz. For the comparison
with prior work and prior selection experiments, we use
VCTK+Demand (VD) [18] subset containing 28 speakers for
training (out of which two speakers are reserved for validation),
and the standard 2 speakers test set of 824 utterances. We use
two kinds of prior data: in-domain (ID) consisting of VCTK
clean speech and Demand noise, and out-of-domain (OOD)
consisting of clean speech and noise data from URGENT chal-
lenge Task1 [19] without VCTK or Demand noise.
3.3. Training
We train our models on 3 seconds long randomly sampled au-
dio chunks using AdamW [20] with weight decay of0.02. We
train all models for up to 50k iterations, each consisting of first
updating the discriminators and then the generator. The learn-
ing rate (LR) is linearly warmed up for 5k iterations, achiev-
ing peak LR 2e-4, and then decayed according to the cosine
scheduler throughout the rest of the training. We use gradient
clipping of 1.0 and mixed precision training in bfloat16. The
best-performing model is selected based on the validation set.
3.4. Metrics
To evaluate the model performance and compare to prior works,
we follow the setups defined in [5, 10] and use DNSMOS [7]
to judge the quality of the enhanced speech (cleanliness),
PESQ [21] to evaluate perceptual speech quality, and CSIG,
CBAK, COVL composite metrics [22] to predict MOS for:
the amount of speech distortion, the intrusiveness of the back-
ground noise, and the overall clean speech quality, respectively.
Furthermore, to examine the clean speech and noise leakage
between the branch-wise estimates, we use SI-SDR [17]-like
scaling to measure the amount of noise contained in the clean
speech estimate:

4.2. Clean Speech Prior Selection
To investigate the impact of prior data matching, we evalu-
ate our models using ID and OOD clean speech datasets (Sec-
tion 3.2). Table 2 shows that ID clean speech significantly im-
proves PESQ and composite metrics across all noise conditions.
Fundamentally, the model partitions the signal by balancing
adversarial priors and the mixture consistency loss (Lcon). With
an OOD clean speech prior, the adversarial gradients are often
not strict enough to penalize non-speech components. Conse-
quently, if the noise branch imperfectly estimates the true noise,
the consistency loss forces the clean speech branch to recon-
struct this leftover noise.
This dynamic causes source leakage and degrades cleanli-
ness. As shown in Figure 2, an OOD clean speech prior (Fig-
ure 2d) results in substantial noise leakage compared to an ID
prior (Figure 2b), which strictly penalizes non-speech artifacts.

4.3. How Does Noise Prior Affect Clean Speech?
Building on Section 4.2, we analyze the impact of explicit noise
modeling. Switching from an OOD to an ID noise prior (Fig-
ure 2d vs. 2c) yields a more accurate clean speech estimate. A
strong ID noise prior allows the noise branch to act as a dedi-
cated sink, relieving the clean speech branch from reconstruct-
ing leftover noise to satisfy the mixture consistency loss (Lcon),
as reflected in the last two rows of Table 2.
To isolate this effect, we evaluate models on VCTK mixed
with simulated wind noises from the URGENT challenge [19]
with SNR from 0dB to 15dB, ensuring the noise corpora con-
tain no speech. Figure 3 demonstrates that an OOD noise prior
(Figure 3c) causes substantial speech leakage into the noise es-
timate. Conversely, an ID noise prior (Figure 3b) explicitly pre-
vents this leakage as the noise prior in this case is easily learn-
able; hence strong.

5. Conclusion
In this work, we have investigated the role of prior data in un-
supervised GAN-based speech enhancement and introduced a
dual-branch framework that explicitly models clean speech and
noise. Our experiments showed that prior alignment strongly
influences source separation behavior and that mismatched pri-
ors lead to increased leakage between speech and noise esti-
mates. By using aligned noise priors, the proposed framework
improves signal partitioning, reduces cross-source leakage, and
yields better perceptual speech quality. These findings support
explicit noise modeling compared to the previous single-branch
models and demonstrate that environment-specific noise record-
ings—often easy to collect in practice—can effectively guide
the enhancement process. Future work will focus on improving
robustness to mismatched priors and extending the analysis to
more diverse noise conditions.
