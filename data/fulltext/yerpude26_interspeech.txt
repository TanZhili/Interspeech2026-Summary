Attention-Based Multiple Instance Learning with Tabular Stacking for
Ambulatory Detection of PVH and NPVH

Abstract
Phonotraumatic vocal hyperfunction (PVH) and non-
phonotraumatic vocal hyperfunction (NPVH) are voice
disorders that result from excessive laryngeal muscle activity.
The PVH is associated with tissue lesions, while the NPVH
refers to functional dysregulation without tissue damage.
Ambulatory accelerometers enable the ecological assessment
of daily voice behavior, which is noisy and variable. Our
method consists of a tabular model at the subject level and a
deep multiple instance learning (MIL) model with fixed-length
windows of frame-level features. The predictions of these two
models are combined via a stacking ensemble method. The
tabular model employs CatBoost with day-level features to
account for long-term voice behavior, while the MIL model
uses a 1D residual network with gated attention pooling
to focus on relevant voice segments. The proposed method
achieves an official test AUC of 0.891 for PVH (rank 3) and
0.861 for NPVH (rank 1) on the NeckVibe Challenge 2026.
Index Terms: ambulatory voice monitoring, vocal hyperfunc-
tion, multiple instance learning, CatBoost, attention-based pool-
ing, quantile transformers
1. Introduction
V ocal hyperfunction has been defined as the “overuse or ineffi-
cient use of laryngeal muscles for phonation,” and is commonly
classified into two types: phonotraumatic vocal hyperfunction
(PVH), which is accompanied by vocal-fold lesions such as
nodules or polyps; and non-phonotraumatic vocal hyperfunc-
tion (NPVH), which exhibits hyperfunction without such le-
sions [1, 2, 3]. Both forms have the potential to cause persis-
tent voice problems, so it is important to detect and treat vocal
hyperfunction at an early stage [4]. Figure 1 contrasts the two
conditions with normal vocal folds.
The clinical assessment of vocal hyperfunction has tradi-
tionally relied on laboratory recordings. Although such methods
are useful, they capture only a limited range of vocal behav-
iors and do not accurately reflect voice production in real-world
scenarios [4]. This highlights the need for continuous, everyday
voice monitoring systems [1].
Ambulatory monitoring with a tiny neck-surface ac-
celerometer (ACC) is a feasible way to collect real-world vocal
behavior [1]. As the ACC signal does not capture intelligible
speech, it can monitor voice for multiple days while preserving
privacy and enables estimation of CPP, spectral, and impedance-
based inverse filtering (IBIF) parameters [1, 5]. ACC param-
eters have been used to differentiate PVH patients from con-
trols with statistical and conventional machine-learning classi-
fiers [6, 1, 2], and recent work has examined NPVH and com-
pared ambulatory with laboratory measurements [7, 4, 8].
Figure 1:Schematic comparison of normal vocal folds, phono-
traumatic vocal hyperfunction (PVH), and non-phonotraumatic
vocal hyperfunction (NPVH).
Most prior approaches, however, rely on hand-crafted sum-
mary features and shallow models, with labels available only
at the subject level even though the recordings split into many
short segments whose informative parts are hard to identify un-
der supervision [6, 1]. Advances in weakly supervised [9] and
attention-based time-series learning [10] motivate a multi-scale
approach combining global distributional modeling with local
event detection.
The NeckVibe Challenge [11] provides weeklong neck-
ACC recordings with subject-level PVH and NPVH labels. As
outlined in Figure 2, we propose a stacked two-branch archi-
tecture: a subject-level tabular model over day-aggregated dis-
tributional statistics trained with CatBoost [12], and a deep
multiple-instance learning (MIL) model [13] over short win-
dows of 50 ms frames whose convolutional embeddings are
pooled by gated attention into a subject-level prediction.
The main contributions of this study are:
•Robust ambulatory preprocessing and statistics:A frame-
filtering and feature-engineering pipeline that selects only
voiced, non-singing, device-on frames; removes extreme out-
liers; encodes missingness explicitly, and constructs subject-
level distributional descriptors over multiple mask condi-
tions.
•Dual-branch CatBoost–MIL architecture:A tabular Cat-
Boost branch on day-level statistics and a deep MIL branch
on 12 s windows with SE (squeeze-and-excitation) ResNet
encoders and gated attention pooling, both tailored to subject-
level PVH and NPVH detection.
•Stacked ensemble and ablation:A stacking ensemble that
combines rank-normalized probabilities from both branches,
with ablations and comparisons against earlier subject-level
ensembles and CNN–LSTM baselines on 5-fold stratified
GroupKFold cross-validation.

2. Method
2.1. Frame-level preprocessing
The NeckVibe dataset [11] provides 50 ms frame-level fea-
tures from multi-day neck-surface accelerometer recordings, in-
cluding acoustic channels, IBIF-derived signals where avail-
able, and frame-wise masks. We retain only voiced, non-
singing, device-on frames (recordingOn=1,voiced=1,
voiced singing=0), remove per-channel amplitude outliers
via robustz-score thresholding, and encode missing values as
zeros with appended binary missingness indicators to distin-
guish true zeros from sensor dropout [14].
2.2. Tabular subject-level representation
PVH produces transient phonatory bursts detectable in short
windows, while NPVH reflects sustained inefficiency dis-
tributed across days [7]. For each acoustic and IBIF channel
under multiple mask conditions, we compute day-level robust
statistics (mean, variance, skewness, kurtosis, IQR, MAD, tail
percentiles, Gini coefficients, and frame-to-frame differences),
then aggregate across days via mean, minimum, and maximum
to yield a fixed-dimensional subject-level feature vector. For
NPVH, interaction features — phonation efficiency (CPP/f 0),
closure-periodicity ratios, and harmonic stability ratios — are
additionally engineered to capture subtler hyperfunctional pat-
terns. We train CatBoost [12] as the tabular classifier, tuning
depth, learning rate (≈0.01), iterations (≈1500),L 2 regular-
ization, and class weighting via 5-fold stratified GroupKFold;
NPVH requires stronger class weighting and regularization to
handle its greater imbalance and heterogeneity.
2.3. Multiple-instance learning branch
Each subject is modeled by the deep branch as a bag of fixed-
length windows, each of which consists of a series of succes-
sive 50 ms frames across all channels. For PVH, each frame
includes 12 raw acoustic channels, an energy channel, a global
missingness indicator, and 117 auxiliary channels obtained by
broadcasting day-level statistics over time, for a total of 131
channels. We limit the number of sampled windows per sub-
ject at 24 windows of 12 s each, padding shorter recordings
where necessary. The 12 s window length was selected to cap-
ture complete phonatory phrases while remaining computation-
ally manageable; the 24-window cap balances coverage with
training stability across subjects of varying recording length.
Each window is passed through a 1D convolutional stem
followed by four residual blocks [15] with squeeze-and-
excitation modules [16], producing a 256-dimensional embed-
ding per window. SE (squeeze-and-excitation) modules were
chosen over plain CNNs for their ability to recalibrate channel-
wise feature responses, which is critical given the heteroge-
neous nature of ACC channels. A gated attention MIL mecha-
nism [13] then aggregates the window embeddings into a single
subject-level representation. Attention weights are computed as:

2.4. Stacking ensemble
For each subject, OOF probabilistic predictions from both
branches are obtained under 5-fold stratified GroupKFold, en-
suring all days per subject remain in the same fold. For NPVH,
rank-based normalization (Quantile Transformer to uniform)
is applied to correct fold-wise calibration drift [18]; PVH re-
tains raw probabilities. The meta-feature vector comprises the
(possibly transformed) tabular and MIL probabilities, their ab-
solute difference, and their product, capturing both agreement
and disagreement between branches. A logistic regression meta-
classifier with balanced class weights is trained on these OOF
meta-features [19].

3. Experimental Setup
3.1. Dataset and tasks
The NeckVibe Challenge dataset [11]. comprises long-term
neck-surface accelerometer (ACC) recordings from 582 indi-
viduals: 213 PVH patients with 169 matched controls, and 116
NPVH patients with 84 matched controls. The primary input
consists of frame-level features derived from ACC at 50 ms res-
olution —f 0, SPL, CPP, H1–H2, spectral tilt, L/H ratio, and
glottal measures based on IBIF (absent for a subset of subjects
and handled in preprocessing). We define two binary tasks: (i)
PVH vs. non-PVH and controls, (ii) NPVH vs. non-NPVH and
controls All experiments use only the official training split [11].
3.2. Evaluation metrics and protocol
To maintain consistency with the NeckVibe challenge evalu-
ation, we report the subject-level area under the ROC curve
(AUC) as our primary metric [11]. For baselines and ablations,
we additionally report accuracy, F1-score, precision, sensitiv-
ity, and specificity at thresholds selected by default (0.5) or via
per-fold grid search over[0.1,0.9].
To prevent data leakage, we employ 5-fold Stratified
GroupKFold with subject ID as the grouping key. For each fold,
both branches are trained on four folds and evaluated on the
held-out fold, generating OOF predictions that train the stack-
ing meta-classifier. This protocol is applied separately for PVH
and NPVH; final AUCs are based on OOF predictions.

3.3. Implementation details
CatBoost hyperparameters (depth, learning rate, iterations, reg-
ularization) are selected via inner CV; class imbalance is han-
dled viascale pos weight. The MIL models are imple-
mented in PyTorch [20] with Adam/AdamW, cosine learning-
rate scheduling, early stopping on validation AUC, and dropout
0.3 in the MLP head. GPU is used for training; inference runs
on CPU. The stacking meta-classifier and quantile transformers
are implemented in scikit-learn [21] and trained separately for
PVH and NPVH on OOF predictions.
4. Results
4.1. Official challenge evaluation
Our system achieved AUC 0.891 on PVH (Task 1, rank 3) and
AUC 0.861 on NPVH (Task 2, rank 1) [11]. Table 1 lists repre-
sentative prior results for contextual reference; direct compari-
son is not valid due to differing datasets and task definitions.
4.2. Internal cross-validation
Table 2 reports OOF AUC with 95% bootstrap CIs (2000
stratified resamples). The stacked ensemble outperforms both
individual branches on every task. For PVH, the stacked
CI[0.850,0.917]lies entirely above the MIL-only CI
[0.797,0.872], confirming a statistically meaningful gain
from stacking. For NPVH, CIs partially overlap (stacked:
[0.697,0.810]; CatBoost:[0.696,0.805]), consistent with
greater class imbalance and subtler phonatory signatures [7].

4.3. Calibration
Figure 3 shows a diagram for both tasks. The stacked model
benefits from quantile normalization to stabilize fold-wise cali-
bration drift, and PVH predictions closely follow the diagonal.
NPVH is consistently underconfident; observed positive rates
only reach 0.3–0.5 at projected probability of 0.8–0.9, which
is consistent with intra-class diversity [7] and class imbalance
(≈20%positives).
4.4. Ablation and hyperparameter tuning
Table 3 reports component ablations under identical splits. Re-
moving the MIL branch reduces PVH AUC by 0.010 and NPVH
by 0.002; removing CatBoost interaction features costs 0.027 on
PVH. Excluding day-level statistics, missingness indicators, or
quantile normalization, each degrades NPVH AUC, underscor-
ing the importance of global temporal context, sensor-dropout
modelling [14], and calibration correction [18].
5. Conclusion
We presented a dual-branch CatBoost–MIL stacking architec-
ture for subject-level PVH and NPVH detection from multi-day
neck-surface accelerometer recordings, achieving official test
AUCs of 0.891 (PVH, rank 3/6) and 0.861 (NPVH, rank 1/3)
in the NeckVibe Challenge [11]. Combining long-term dis-
tributional statistics via CatBoost with attention-based MIL
and rank-normalised logistic stacking proved complementary
across both tasks, with the tabular branch driving NPVH per-
formance and the MIL branch capturing transient phonatory
events critical for PVH [1, 7]. Future work will explore multi-
task PVH/NPVH formulations, subject-adaptive modelling for
NPVH, and validation on independent groups.
6. Discussion
Although attention-based MIL, SE-ResNet encoders, and stack-
ing are established elsewhere, their adaptation to ambulatory
neck-ACC biomarkers is new for vocal hyperfunction, and the
two branches mirror the disorders’ pathophysiology: the MIL
branch localizes the brief, high-intensity events characteristic of
PVH within its 12 s windows, whereas CatBoost’s day-level dis-
tributional statistics capture the diffuse, sustained inefficiency
of NPVH [3, 22]. Consistent with this, the ablations show the
MIL branch helping PVH most and the tabular branch dominat-
ing NPVH. As the full system improves over the CatBoost-only
model by only 0.001 (PVH) and 0.002 (NPVH) AUC, we do
not claim a large gain from the deep branch; its value is com-
plementary, adding calibration stability and an interpretable at-
tention map over the segments driving each decision [13].
