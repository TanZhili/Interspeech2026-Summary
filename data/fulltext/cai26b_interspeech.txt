Scene Classification via Semantic Adversarial Training

Abstract
Short-duration Acoustic Scene Classification (ASC) is essential
for building responsive environmental sensing systems. How-
ever, standard ASC models show severe performance collapse
when restricting to short observation windows. Our study
reveals that the degradation stems from temporal prediction
inconsistency—a misalignment between the long-term scene
context and the model’s predictions among short observation
windows. To address this, we propose the Semantic Adver-
sarial Training (SAT) framework. SAT introduces an aux-
iliary adversarial objective that competes with the standard
scene classification task, forcing the feature extractor to discard
duration-dependent semantic variances while learning scene-
discriminative features. Experimental results demonstrate a di-
rect correlation between temporal prediction consistency and
model accuracy. Crucially, SAT significantly mitigates the per-
formance drop on high-discrepancy samples, yielding a more
robust and temporally consistent ASC system.
Index Terms: Short-duration acoustic scene classification, tem-
poral prediction consistency, adversarial training.
1. Introduction
Acoustic scene classification (ASC) [1] aims to recognize the
surrounding environment of an audio recording, such as a park,
shopping mall, or metro station. With the proliferation of fast-
response applications on environmental sensing systems, there
is an increasing demand for ASC systems that can perform
accurate inference within short-duration observation windows
(e.g., 1 second) [2, 3, 4]. However, reducing the input duration
typically results in a severe performance collapse [5]. Recent
works attempt to mitigate this degradation through general deep
learning techniques, such as implementing more powerful ar-
chitectures [6], scaling up via pre-training on external datasets
[7, 8], or leveraging transfer learning strategies [9, 10]. De-
spite these advances, the underlying reasons why ASC models
struggle with short-duration inputs have rarely been discussed
or addressed.
A primary explanation lies in the temporally unstable
acoustic content within real-world recordings. Hu et al. [11] re-
ported that ASC models tend to focus on foreground event fea-
tures when trained on long-duration recordings. Wu et al. [12]
further observed that the consistent background textures are
more critical for short-duration classification. Building on these
findings, we hypothesize that the temporally unstable acoustic
signal causes a behavioral inconsistency in standard ASC mod-
els among short observation windows. As illustrated in Figure

To address this challenge, we propose the Semantic Adver-
sarial Training (SAT) framework. Unlike traditional approaches
that treat events as features to be learned [13, 14, 15], SAT re-
gards local events as “adversaries” to be suppressed. The fea-
ture extractor is optimized through a min-max game between
the scene classifier and the event discriminator to extract scene-
discriminative yet duration-invariant features from short scene
recordings. The proposed method is evaluated on the TAU Ur-
ban Acoustic Scenes 2020 Mobile Development Dataset, where
we regard the 10 seconds as the full-duration context and 1
second as the target short-duration setting. Experimental re-
sults demonstrate that SAT effectively improves the classifica-
tion accuracy on high-discrepancy segments, and helps to pro-
duce more consistent predictions compared to standard training.
The main contributions are summarized as follows, we:
1. identify and formulate the problem of temporal prediction in-
consistency in short-duration acoustic scene classification.
2. propose the SAT framework, which leverages adversarial
training to suppress local event biases.
3. demonstrate that SAT effectively helps to produce consistent
predictions within short observation windows.

3. Experiment
3.1. Dataset and Pre-processing
We evaluate the proposed SAT framework on the DCASE Chal-
lenge Task 1 dataset, TAU Urban Acoustic Scene 2020 Mobile
Development Dataset (TAU20) [20]. TAU20 contains approx-
imately 64 hours of audio recordings collected from 10 Euro-
pean cities across 10 scene classes. The audio clips are pro-
vided in single-channel, 44.1 kHz, 24-bit format. The official
7 : 3train-test split is used. As TAU20 contains 10-second
clips, we slice the original recordings into non-overlapping 1-
second segments for the short-duration setting. The acoustic
feature extraction follows [21]. Audio is resampled to 32 kHz
and converted to log-Mel spectrograms using a 4096-point FFT,
96 ms window, 16 ms hop size, and 512 Mel bins.
3.2. Baseline
We adopt TF-SepNet [22] as the baseline model, which is a
widely used convolutional neural network (CNN) with low-
complexity design in recent DCASE Challenges. During ex-
periments, the default settings of scene classification head in
the original work is followed, and all previous layers are treated
as the feature extractor.
3.3. Implementation Details
We adopt the training protocol from [21], with 150 epochs,
batch size 512, and default Adam optimizer. The learning rate
warms up exponentially for 14 epochs, then decays linearly
from epoch 50 to 0.5% of the peak. Data augmentations include

For the SAT module, the event discriminator shares an iden-

3.4. Evaluation Protocols
The classification accuracy (%) is used as the evaluation metric
for the ASC task. During evaluation, we use LGPD described
in Section 2.1 as a stratification tool. The test set is partitioned
into two equal-sized subsets based on their LGPD scores:
• Stable Set (Bottom-50% LGPD): Represents “easy” samples
where the standard model maintains high consistency with
the global context.
• Unstable Set (Top-50% LGPD): Represents “hard” samples
where models exhibit high prediction discrepancy.

3.5. Experimental Results
Robustness Against Prediction Inconsistency.Figure 4 plots
the classification accuracy of 1-second segments against LGPD.
The baseline model suffers a significant degradation as the pre-
diction discrepancy increases, dropping from 95.8% to 34.7%.
This result confirms our core hypothesis that standard models
are highly sensitive to temporal context loss. In contrast, our
method effectively mitigates the degradation, substantially out-
performing the baseline in high-discrepancy quantiles. Specif-
ically, SAT achieves an accuracy of 46.7%, representing a
+12.0% improvement over the baseline in the highest discrep-
ancy quantile. Nevertheless, these gains come with a marginal
performance cost on stable samples. For example, SAT yields
94.6% accuracy on the first quantile, compared to the 95.8%
baseline. We argue this trade-off is strategically favorable as
our method secures reliability where standard models collapse
by sacrificing negligible accuracy on the simplest queries. The
widening gap between two lines demonstrates that while stan-
dard models suffer from temporal prediction inconsistency, our
proposed framework maintains significantly higher robustness.
Comparative Evaluation.We compare SAT with existing
common frameworks that leverage sound event information. As
shown in Table 1, SAT achieves the best overall accuracy of
63.5%, outperforming baseline by 3.9%. More importantly, our
method yields the most significant gains on the unstable subset
while simultaneously reducing the stability gap to 25.2. These
results suggest that while modeling acoustic scene with events
improves performance, SAT provides a more effective mech-
anism for filtering out duration-dependent inconsistencies, re-
sulting in a more robust system with short-duration inputs.

4. Conclusion
In this work, we addressed the critical challenge of performance
degradation in short-duration ASC. We identified temporal pre-
diction inconsistency as a critical failure mode and introduced
LGPD to quantify the model’s sensitivity to context loss. To
mitigate this, we proposed the SAT framework, which leverages
an adversarial objective to filter out duration-dependent local
variances while preserving global scene semantics. Experimen-
tal results demonstrate that SAT effectively improves tempo-
ral prediction consistency on short scene segments. However,
the current framework relies on off-the-shelf pre-trained sound
event taggers, which may introduce label noise. Future work
will explore other techniques to eliminate this dependency and
further enhance generalization.
