Achieving voicelessness in coda stop contexts: Insights from combined
electroglottography and laryngoscopy

Abstract
V oicelessness in English coda stops can be produced with ei-
ther glottal spreading or glottal constriction, with consequences
for voice quality of preceding vowels. Recent evidence from
electroglottography (EGG) suggests that in Australian English
voicelessness is achieved via glottal constriction for /t/, whereas
glottal spreading is the strategy for /k/ and no consistent pat-
tern is identified for /p/. However, EGG measures glottal state
indirectly via electrical impedance and provides no direct in-
formation on supraglottal configuration, which may play a role
in constriction. In this paper, we combine EGG with laryngo-
scopic imaging to investigate glottal/laryngeal settings during
voiceless coda stop production and assess the extent to which
inferences from EGG align with observed glottal/supraglottal
behaviour. Results provide support for recent findings and re-
veal that constriction is the preferred strategy for voiceless stops
at all places of articulation in phrase final position.
Index Terms: electroglottography, laryngoscopy, laryngeal ar-

1. Introduction
In the production of voiceless obstruents, voicelessness can be
achieved through either glottal spreading (as in [h]) or glottal
constriction (as in [P]), as both strategies result in an inhibition
of phonation (i.e. voicing): the former through the abduction
of the vocal folds, which prevents the necessary transglottal
pressure build up for regular vocal fold vibration, and the lat-
ter through complete glottal adduction and occlusion of airflow
[1, 2]. For voiceless fricatives, glottal spreading is the necessary
strategy, as continuous strong airflow is required, whereas for
voiceless stops, the strategy employed appears to be more flex-
ible [1]. This is particularly the case for voiceless coda stops,
and the strategy that is employed for achieving voicelessness
can affect the voice quality of a preceding vowel, particularly
as the transition to the coda stop approaches: glottal spreading
may result in increased breathiness and/or preaspiration on the
preceding vowel, whereas glottal constriction may lead to in-
creased laryngealisation (i.e. a brief period of creaky voice) on
a preceding vowel, and in some cases the realisation of a glottal
stop occuring in conjunction with, or in place of, the coda stop
[3, 4, 5].
Glottalisation, defined here as a full or incomplete glottal
stop and/or laryngealisation on the preceding vowel, has long
been reported in many varieties of English [4, 5, 6, 7, 8]. This
is generally reported as occurring in conjunction with coda /t/,
though it may also occur in conjunction with the other voice-
less coda stops /p, k/, albeit to a lesser extent in some varieties.
This is for example the case in American English (AmE), where
it is known to occur regularly with /t/, less often with /p/, and
seldom with /k/ [4, 9]. Glottalisation, especially in conjunction
with coda /t/, often occurs in contexts with a following sonorant,
such as /n/ [4, 9, 10], and rates of glottalisation may increase
in phrase final position for voiceless stops at all places of artic-
ulation (POAs) [9, 11, 12], although this may be impacted by
phrase-final instances of prosodic creaky voice.
An explanation for the imbalance according to POA was
provided by [11], who suggested that glottal constriction was
necessary to inhibit voicing in the case of /t/ compared to /p, k/,
as in the latter voicing inhibition could be achieved by stiffen-
ing of the posterior part of the tongue, thereby reducing intrao-
ral pressure and leading to a drop in airflow, effectively ceas-
ing voicing. Alveolar articulation with the anterior part of the
tongue precludes this action in /t/. More recently, [4] pro-
posed that glottal constriction is the default strategy for achiev-
ing voicelessness in stops at all three POAs. According to this
account, the discrepancy in terms of glottalisation results from
a greater tendency for lenition in /t/ compared to /p, k/, and as a
consequence of this lenition perceptible evidence of the glottal
constriction is more frequently observed.
A recent study examined whether there was evidence of
glottal constriction in voiceless stops at all three POAs in Aus-
tralian English (AusE) using electroglottography (EGG) [13]
(see also [14]). They measured open quotient (OQ) values
through the vowel: OQ is a measure of the proportion of the
cycle that the glottis is determined to be open and is corre-
lated with voice quality, with higher associated with increased
breathiness, and lower values associated with increased glottal
constriction. Their results showed that OQ values decreased
in vowels preceding /t/, but increased in vowels preceding /k/,
with intermediate values in vowels preceding /p/. These results,
which were also evident in acoustic measures, suggest that in
AusE voicelessness is achieved by glottal constriction in /t/, and
by glottal spreading in /k/, with an inconsistent pattern for /p/.
As the pre-sonorant environment has been shown to increase
the likelihood of glottalisation [4, 9, 10, 15], items in this study
were produced in carrier phrases with a following sonorant seg-
ment. However, as noted above, rates of glottalisation for /t/
are reported to increase in phrase-final position [9, 11, 12], and
glottalisation has been reported to occur for stops at all POAs in
final position, including in AusE [16, 17], perhaps as a mecha-
nism for enhancing the contrast with voiced stops, due to a lack
of other available cues in this prosodic position [11].
EGG provides information about electrical conductance;
when conductance is high, glottal contact is assumed, and from
this information about the glottal state is inferred. Due to its
non-invasive nature, it therefore provides a simple method for
examining glottal activity. However, EGG provides no direct
information on glottal configuration. Moreover, EGG data can
provide no insight into the degree of supraglottal activity, e.g.

In this paper, we present preliminary results from an ex-

lessness in coda stops in AusE using a combination of EGG
and laryngoscopy. This will enable us to relate patterns of vo-
cal fold contact to directly observed laryngeal activity. In ad-
dition to pre-sonorant environments as examined in [13], we
will also examine items produced in phrase-final position, to
explore whether previous suggestions in the literature regarding
increased rates of glottalisation are borne out [9, 11, 12], and
whether glottal/laryngeal constriction is visible at all POAs.
2. Methods
2.1. Participants
We report on data collected from three male participants aged
between 25 and 45 years old. All participants were L1 speak-
ers of AusE, who had completed all of their schooling in Aus-
tralia. Three other participants within the same age range orig-
inally took part in the experiment. One speaker did not toler-
ate the procedure well and as such did not complete the task.
The remaining two participants completed the task; however,
subsequent inspection of their acoustic and electroglottographic
recordings revealed substantial signal interference, rendering
the data unsuitable for analysis without extensive post-hoc ma-
nipulation. This interference was not apparent during data col-
lection and was later determined to be to be consistent with
impulse-like electrical artefacts arising from crossed EGG and
laryngoscope cabling. This research was approved by the Mac-
quarie University Human Research Ethics Committee, and all
participants provided written informed consent.
2.2. Task
Data collection took place in the Macquarie University Speech
and Hearing Clinic. Synchronised audio and EGG data were
captured with a Laryngograph EGG-D200 with a 48 kHz sam-
ple rate. Participants were comfortably seated, with the elec-
trodes positioned on each side of the thyroid cartilage, held
in place with an elastic collar. High definition laryngoscopic
video was captured using a Pentax VNL11-J10 flexible naso-
laryngoscope, recorded via a PENTAX Medical Defina ENT
video processor using a 150W xenon light source. The pro-
cedure was performed by an accredited clinician. The laryn-
goscope was inserted through one of the nostrils, progressed
through the nasal cavity to the pharynx and positioned to cap-
ture a superior view of the larynx during speech production.
Participants produced a set of /VC/ stimulus items, where V
was the high front vowel /i:/ and C was one of the three voice-
less stops /p, t, k/. The high front vowel /i:/ was selected as
it provides an optimal view of the larynx, which is often ob-
scured by the epiglottis/tongue root retraction that occurs in the
production of other vowels [20, 22]. Three repetitions of each
item were captured in two contexts: 1) as a single word in iso-
lation: this will henceforth be referred to as final position; 2)
with a following word consisting of an alveolar nasal and a high
front vowel /ni:/, with an accent on the following word: this will
henceforth be referred to as pre-nasal position.
A range of additional items with non-high front vowel qual-
ities and flanking consonants were also captured to explore their
impact on larynx visibility; unsurprisingly, these items did not
image as clearly as the stimulus items and are not analysed here.

2.3. Analysis
The audio data were segmented and force aligned using Web-
Maus [23], resulting in corresponding labelled Praat [24]
TextGrids. Each target vowel segment was identified and pro-
cessed using PraatDet [25], which provides estimates of open
quotient (OQ) for each glottal cycle. These were calculated us-
ing the hybrid method [2, 26, 27] based on the identification of
glottal closing instants at the peak of the first derivative of the
EGG signal, and glottal opening instants at the point the ampli-
tude of the EGG signal fell below 25%. OQ estimates across
the vowel segments were time normalised and z-scored within
speaker. As we are interested in the effects of a following coda
stop on the OQ during the vowel, we examined OQ trajectories
within the second half of each vowel. Video data captured by
the laryngoscope at a rate of 25 f.p.s were post-processed using
Adobe PremierePro software [version 26.0] and synchronised
with the audio/EGG data. Video frames occurring within the
target vowel segments were examined visually using a custom
MATLAB script described in [28].
In light of the small number of participants and elicited
items, a fully quantitative analysis of the EGG data is not war-
ranted due to data sparsity. We therefore adopt a descriptive,
qualitative approach, characterising the observed patterns us-
ing LOESS smooths with bootstrapped confidence intervals in
[29]. These patterns are further illustrated and interpreted with
reference to the corresponding laryngoscopic data.

3.2. Laryngoscopy data
Inspection of the laryngoscopic images generally accords with
the patterns identified in the EGG data, in particular with regard
to the apparent differences in strategy for achieving voiceless-
ness in alveolar and velar POAs in pre-nasal position. Figures
2 and 3 provide exemplar illustrations of the differences in la-
ryngeal settings during phonation associated with vowels pre-
ceding /t/ vs /k/ in pre-nasal position, as produced by two of
the participants. The top row of each figure illustrates the lar-
ynx during the vowel preceding /t/; the bottom row illustrates
the larynx during the vowel preceding /k/. In all cases, the fi-
nal four frames (corresponding to frames A, B, C, D, with D
the final frame) during the vowel segment are displayed. De-
spite clear differences in the laryngeal anatomy of the partic-
ipants—M06 (shown in Figure 3) exhibits a narrower pharynx
and shorter overall anterior-posterior laryngeal length compared
to M05 (shown in Figure 2)—both display a similar pattern
in which glottal/epilaryngeal constriction is consistently main-
tained throughout the vowel in the alveolar context, whereas in
the velar context increased glottal opening and a reduction in
epilaryngeal constriction is visible as the vowel progresses. On
visual inspection, there appear to be differences in the degree to
which laryngeal constriction is applied by different speakers in
the /t/ context. As suggested in the EGG results and in [13], no
clear pattern was observed for /p/ in pre-nasal position.

4. Discussion and Conclusions
The results presented above are consistent with and confirm pre-
vious suggestions regarding how voicelessness is achieved in
AusE coda stops in pre-sonorant environments [13, 14]: voice-
lessness is primarily achieved through glottal constriction for
/t/, and through glottal spreading for /k/. To this we can add
that a degree of laryngeal constriction is also implemented to
achieve voicelessness in /t/, although appropriate quantification
of this and the degree to which it is consistent across speakers
remains a task for future work to address.
In contrast, in final position we observed glottal/laryngeal
constriction for voiceless stops at all POAs. This may explain
why in some previous acoustic studies on AusE similar lev-
els of glottalisation for all voiceless stops have been observed
[16, 17]. Whether this implies that glottal/laryngeal constriction
is the default strategy for achieving voicelessness in this partic-
ular phrase position [4] or whether this is due to an interaction
between glottal constriction and phrase-final creak will need to
be teased apart in future work [8, 30, 31].
We also note that this effect of constriction in final posi-
tion was not categorical, and late glottal/laryngeal spreading af-
ter constriction throughout the majority of the vowel was ob-
served in some items. Such realisations differ from the glot-
tal/laryngeal spreading observed in the velar context in pre-
nasal position, which unfolded gradually throughout the vowel.
These late spreading realisations raise questions about the tim-
ing and coordination of glottal/laryngeal gestures with suprala-
ryngeal gestures and may suggest that the articulators are reset
rapidly in final position, while the oral stop closure is being
held. An alternative interpretation may be that constriction in
these cases is indeed due to prosodic phrasing (i.e. phrase-final
creak) and not associated with coda stop voicelessness, which
is why the release is visible at the very end of the vowel.
Overall, the patterns observed in this study are broadly con-
sistent with suggestions by [11], who proposed that glottalisa-
tion is necessary for /t/ but not /p, k/, and that voiceless stops at
all POAs are glottalised in final position. However, under this
account, we should expect to observe similar behaviour for both
/p/ and /k/. Here, we see relatively consistent glottal spreading
for /k/, but as in previous research [13], a consistent pattern was
not observed for /p/. This raises the question of why this may be
the case. Is glottalisation simply optional for /p/? Or is there a
social or coarticulatory reason to account for why /p/ more often
shows constriction than /k/ but much less consistently than for
/t/? Examination of a greater range of speakers and following
phonetic environments will shed light on this question.
There are a number of clear limitations to this study. First,
it represents a highly constrained laboratory task that is far re-
moved from naturally occurring speech. Additionally, the anal-
ysis is based on a limited number of items produced by a small
participant sample, precluding robust quantitative analysis. In
the pre-nasal position, the following nasal was homorganic with
/t/, but not /p/ or /k/, which represents a potential confound.
We emphasise that these results are preliminary; in our ongoing
work we plan to extend this research to a greater number of par-
ticipants and a range of following environments, to enable more
generalisable conclusions to be drawn.
Despite the limitations, the preliminary findings reported
here provide converging evidence that previous inferences
drawn from EGG data are well supported: in pre-sonorant en-
vironments, voicelessness is achieved through glottal (but also
supraglottal) constriction for /t/, whereas glottal spreading is the
preferred strategy for /k/. In final position, however, voiceless-
ness appears to be achieved by glottal/laryngeal constriction for
all voiceless stops. Moreover, this study demonstrates that the
combination of EGG and laryngoscopy is a valuable method-
ological approach for direct observation of glottal/supraglottal
configurations and how these correspond with differences in
voice quality, and for evaluating the extent to which inferences
drawn from EGG data align with observed laryngeal behaviour.
