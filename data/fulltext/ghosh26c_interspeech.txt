Abstract
Visual Forced Alignment (VFA) aims to temporally localize lin-
guistic units within silent talking-face videos, enabling align-
ment when audio is noisy or unavailable. We propose V-Align, a
structured framework that formulates VFA as an optimal mono-
tonic path traversal problem over a frame–phoneme compati-
bility lattice. Given the lip-motion embeddings and transcript-
derived phoneme representations, V-Align learns a soft traver-
sal posterior and recovers discrete boundaries via dynamic pro-
gramming. Training follows a two-stage strategy: Stage 1 en-
courages optimal path learning without explicit boundary super-
vision and already achieves competitive performance relative to
fully supervised VFA baselines, while Stage 2 refines bound-
ary precision using additional word-level supervision. Exper-
iments on LRS2 and LRS3 demonstrate state-of-the-art MAE
and frame accuracy. Code and demo are available at:https:
//valign-interspeech.github.io/.

1. Introduction
With the rapid growth of media consumption through social
platforms and streaming services, viewers increasingly rely on
subtitles [1] that are tightly synchronized with both spoken di-
alogue and the visual content. However, manually annotat-
ing precise time-alignment information between transcripts and
speech is expensive and labor-intensive. To reduce this bur-
den, forced alignment techniques automatically align a given
transcription with its corresponding speech signal, produc-
ing word or phoneme-level timestamps. Modern audio-based
forced aligners, such as WhisperX [2] and Montreal Forced
Aligner (MFA) [3] have achieved strong performance under
clean recording conditions.
Nevertheless, these systems often fail when audio is noisy,
corrupted, or entirely missing [4], as commonly encountered in
real-world media, archival footage, or privacy-preserving silent
videos. In such scenarios,Visual Forced Alignment(VFA) [5, 6]
offers an alternative by temporally grounding transcripts di-
rectly from lip movements in talking-face videos, enabling
alignment in the absence of reliable audio. Research on vi-
sual forced alignment remains far less explored than its acous-
tic counterpart. Early progress in visually grounded temporal
localization emerged through visual keyword spotting [7, 8],
which aims to identify the temporal occurrence of a single query
word within a silent talking-face video. Although such ap-
proaches could in principle be extended to full-transcript align-
ment, repeating keyword localization across all words is com-
putationally expensive and may lead to overlapping or inconsis-
tent boundaries. The first dedicated VFA framework, DVFA [5],

phoneme timestamp supervision using structured objectives that

2. Method
Visual Forced Alignment (VFA) seeks to temporally ground a
transcript within a silent talking-face video by localizing the on-
set and offset of linguistic units along the visual timeline. Given
a video sequence
x∈R T×H×W×3,(1)
and its corresponding phoneme sequence
p= (p 1,...,p Tp),(2)
our goal is to recover a monotonic segmentation of the
video into phoneme-consistent temporal regions.
Since speech progresses sequentially, VFA naturally ad-
mits a structured formulation: we view alignment as anopti-
mal monotonic path traversalthrough a frame–phoneme com-
patibility lattice. Rather than predicting boundaries directly, V-
Align learns a distribution over valid monotonic traversals, from
which discrete phoneme boundaries are recovered via optimal
path decoding.

4. Experimental Setup
Dataset:V-Align is compared against the baselines on
LRS2 [13] and LRS3 [14] . The LRS2 dataset is a compre-
hensive collection of naturally-occurring videos, featuring a va-
riety of news and talk show clips sourced from the BBC. The
LRS3 dataset includes TED and TEDx talks, offering a larger
and more diverse set of spoken English sequences.
Metrics:To compare with other methods, we used the same
experimental setup as our baselines. MAE (Mean Absolute Er-
ror) and ACC (accuracy) are used as metrics. MAE is the time
differences in milliseconds between predicted and true word
boundaries. ACC refers to the frame-level prediction accuracy,
which is obtained by dividing the number of correctly predicted
frames by the total number of frames in the video.
Implementation details:V-Align is trained for 400k steps
in Stage 1, followed by an additional 100k steps of supervised
refinement in Stage 2. We train with a batch size of 32 using the
Adam optimizer, with an initial learning rate of1 −4 annealed
to1−6. The temperatureτis set to 0.0005. All experiments are
conducted on a single NVIDIA 2080 Ti GPU. We will release
the code and trained weights to facilitate future research in VFA.
Montreal Forced Aligner [3], an audio forced alignment tool is
used to generate ground truth word alignment labels following
the baselines [6, 5]. Both backbone encoders are kept frozen
during training, allowing the alignment module to focus solely
on structured traversal learning. The losses are weighed equally.

6. Ablation Study
6.1. Effect of Training Objectives
We analyze the contribution of each training objective on the
LRS2 dataset, as summarized in Table 3. Using only the bina-
rization loss leads to unstable alignment learning, as sharpening
the decoded path without learning global structure is insuffi-
cient. The forward-sum objective alone performs substantially
better, highlighting the importance of modeling globally con-
sistent monotonic traversals. The joint optimization of forward-
sum and binarization further improves alignment quality. Word-
level supervision alone yields strong performance due to its di-
rect boundary guidance. Finally, incorporating word-level su-
pervision on top of these objectives yields the best overall per-
formance, indicating that supervised refinement is most effec-
tive when built upon a strong monotonic alignment prior.
6.2. Effect of Monotonic Decoding Strategy

traversal decoding, we replace the proposed optimal traversal
inference with two alternative decoding strategies applied to
the learned traversal posterior: (i) frame-wise argmax decoding
and (ii) greedy monotonic decoding. Frame-wise argmax as-
signs each frame independently to the phoneme with maximum
posterior probability, without enforcing temporal consistency.

7. Robustness Analysis
Audio forced alignment performs reliably under clean condi-
tions but deteriorates significantly as audio quality degrades.
Table 4 shows the performance of MFA and WhisperX under
increasing noise levels, evaluated relative to clean MFA align-
ment. Here, the signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) controls the severity
of corruption, with lower values indicating stronger noise. As
SNR decreases, both systems exhibit increasing boundary error
and reduced frame accuracy.
We further analyze the impact of such degraded supervision
on V-Align. While Stage 1 learns alignment structure with-
out boundary supervision, Stage 2 relies on MFA-generated
word boundaries. Table 5 compares the impact of training
with degraded supervision, with and without the optimal path
objectives. While performance deteriorates in both cases as
supervision quality decreases, the degradation is substantially
more severe when training relies solely on MFA alignment su-
pervision. These results highlight the practical importance of
boundary-free alignment learning, particularly for audio-visual
datasets [16] where speech is noisy or unreliable.
8. Conclusion
We introduced V-Align, a structured framework for Visual
Forced Alignment that formulates alignment as an optimal
monotonic path traversal over a frame–phoneme compatibil-
ity lattice. By learning a soft traversal posterior and recov-
ering discrete boundaries via dynamic programming, V-Align
achieves precise and temporally consistent localization. A two-
phase training strategy enables the model to first learn align-
ment structure without explicit boundary supervision and sub-
sequently refine boundaries using additional supervision. Our
experiments demonstrate state-of-the-art performance on LRS2
and LRS3. These results highlight the effectiveness of struc-
tured optimal path learning for visual-only alignment.
