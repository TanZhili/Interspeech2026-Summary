Duration-aware self-attention for speech deepfake detection

Abstract
Current speech deepfake detection systems mostly use fixed-
length training and evaluation through audio padding or trun-
cation. However, the utterances’ durations are inherently vari-
able, which poses a challenge to deepfake detection. To ad-
dress this limitation, we propose incorporating the timing em-
beddings computed from the audio duration and the segment
offset into self-attention. The objective is to correct the atten-
tion bias map when computing the attention coefficients, so that
the timing embeddings guide a better learning of positional in-
teractions. The resulting method is called duration-aware self-
attention (DASA), which is implemented in three ways. Exper-
iments on ASVspoof21, In-the-Wild, and CodecFake show the
effectiveness of the relative positional encoding based DASA.
This result indicates that the proposed method is beneficial to
alleviate duration variations.
Index Terms: speech deepfake detection, self-attention, timing
embedding, duration robustness
1. Introduction
Speech deepfake detection aims to differentiate whether a given
speech signal is uttered by a human or generated by machine
learning algorithms. Along with the development of speech
synthesis and voice conversion, speech deepfake detection has
witnessed rapid advances in recent years. For example, var-
ious deepfake algorithms have been proposed and a diversity
of speech deepfake datasets have been released. Among these
datasets, the ASVspoof series [1, 2, 3] are popular today.
The common training and evaluation pipelines use fixed-
length segments [4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10]. Padding is necessary when
the audios are shorter than the preset length, whereas truncation
is used when the audios are long. Although the fixed-length
pipelines have achieved outstanding performance on specific
datasets, it may struggle in real-world situations where the ut-
terances have variable lengths, leading to performance degrada-
tion.
Intuitively, padding a short utterance by repeating the sig-
nal itself introduces abrupt boundary changes that can confuse
the model, whereas the truncation operation can remove critical
portions of the audio where the artifacts are prominent. Since
the model treats each audio segment as an isolated event, which
is detached from its global context, fixed-length training and
evaluation inevitably face challenges in variable-length deep-
fake detection. Inspired by the Stable Audio [11] which in-
corporates timing embeddings into conditional audio generation
for advanced length generalization, we propose explicitly using
the timing embeddings to address the variable-length challenge
in speech deepfake detection. By conditioning on the segment’s
offset and the duration of the whole audio, the model is able to
process local acoustic features under the global temporal con-
text.
There are various ways to incorporate the timing embed-
dings into a detection model. First, the timing embeddings can
be concatenated to the frame-level representations before en-
tering the backend classifer. Another way is to implement an
adaptive layer normalization (adaLN) layer [12] by learning a
scale and a shift parameters of the LN output, conditioning on
the timing embeddings. Yet, we can introduce such embed-
dings into the self-attention mechanism to develop duration-
aware self-attention (DASA). This method is inspired by the
fact that Transformers [13] and Conformers [14] are widely
used in speech processing, and self-attention is one of the core
components of these architectures. Especially, because Con-
formers can capture both the global and local interdependen-
cies of a sequence, they are beneficial for deepfake detection in
highlighting both long-term inconsistencies and local artifacts
in fake audios [5, 7]. As an improved variant of the Conformer,
the ConFusionformer [15] uses a more efficient basic block and
implements attention fusion for enhanced locality modeling.
In this paper, we adopt three strategies to incorporate
the timing embedding into self-attention: frame-independent
DASA, frame-dependent DASA, and relative positional encod-
ing dependent DASA. The basic idea is to correct the attention
bias map when computing the attention coefficients, so that the
timing embedding can encourage a better learning of positional
interactions. The contributions of this paper are summarized as
follows:
1. We propose using the timing embeddings, computed from
the offset of the segment and the duration of the audio, to
increase model’s robustness to duration variations.
2. Three DASA mechanisms are introduced to highlight differ-
ent levels of conditioning on the timing embedding.

2.2. Transformer, Conformer, and ConFusionformer
The basic Transformer block consists of a self-attention net-
work (SAN) and a feed-forward network (FFN). Although self-
attention is good at capture global interactions across the whole
sequence, it does not explicitly model the local information,
which is crucial for speech processing tasks.
The Conformer [14] incorporates a CNN into each Trans-
former block to improve its locality modeling capability. As
shown in Figure 1, a Conformer block is comprised of an SAN
and a CNN, sandwiched by two FFNs. Unlike the residual paths
of the SAN and CNN, the output of each FFN’s residual path is

divided by 2 prior to the addition. Due to the advantage to cap-
ture both the global and local interdependencies of a sequence,
Conformers have been widely used in automatic speech recog-
nition, speaker recognition, and anti-spoofing.
The ConFusionformer [15] is an improved Conformer for
superior local information modeling ability due to the atten-
tion fusion mechanism. Unlike the Conformer block, a Con-
Fusionformer block comprises an SAN, a CNN, and an FFN
in sequence, as shown in Figure 1. Under similar model sizes
or computation, a ConFusionformer can obtain better perfor-
mance.
3. Methodology
This section first introduces the timing embedding which con-
tains the duration and offset information of the audio segment.
The principle of duration-aware self-attention is then detailed.
3.1. Timing embedding
In Stable Audio [11], the authors proposed employing timing
embeddings to generate variable-length audio signals. The tim-
ing embeddings are the learned embeddings containing the du-
ration information of the audios and the offset information of
the segments, as shown in Figure 1. These timing embed-
dings are appended to the text tokens prior to the cross-attention
mechanism to improve the model’s length generalization capa-
bility. In this paper, we adopt timing embeddings for discrim-
inative tasks so that the deepfake detectors can be invariant to
duration variations.
Given the audio durationt d and the segment offsett o, the
duration embeddingt d and the offset embeddingt o are com-
puted as their Fourier feature mappings [17], respectively:

3.2. Duration-aware self-attention (DASA)
The standard self-attention models the relationship between
temporal frames based on their acoustic similarity. One poten-
tial limitation of such mechanism is that self-attention ignores
the fact that the deepfake artifacts are often non-uniformly dis-
tributed across the frames. For instance, some speech synthe-
sizers can present specific artifacts at the beginning of a speech
signal, whereas others can lose coherence across long ranges.
By injecting timing embeddings into the self-attention mecha-
nism, each frame in the sequence is able to be guided by the
global timing context, which enables refined analysis of local
acoustic patterns and thus leads to enhanced robustness to dura-
tion variations.

4. Experimental setup
This section presents the detailed configurations of the training
and evaluation pipelines.
4.1. Datasets
The training set of the ASVspoof19 [1] logical access
(ASVspoof19-LA) data was used as training data, whereas
the ASVspoof 2021 [2] LA (ASVspoof21-LA) and deepfake
(ASVspoof21-DF) data were used for evaluation. To further
demonstrate the effectiveness of the proposed method, the In-
the-Wild [18] and CodecFake [19] data were also adopted for
evaluation.
4.2. Model structure
The pre-trained wav2vec 2.0 XLS-R model 2 [20] was
adopted as the front-end feature extractor. Conform-
ers with 6 blocks (Conformer-6) and 9-layer Confusion-
formers (Confusionformer-9) were used in the experiments.
The number of base channels of both Conformer-6 and
Confusionformer-9 was set to 160, and the number of atten-
tion heads is 4. For Confusionformer-9, attention fusion was
not applied. Channel-wise attentive pooling [21] operation was
applied after the final Conformer/Confusionformer block. The
maximum relative distanceRin Equation 7 is 63. The dimen-
siond F of the learnable frequency vectorwin Equations 8 and
9 is 32 andtmax was set to 15s. We also reproduced the exper-
iments using the Conformers with temporal-channel modeling
(TCM) [7] and the AASIST model [4, 22].
4.3. Training and evaluation
During training, audios were padded or clipped to create seg-
ments of approximately 4 seconds. The RawBoost tool [23] was
used for data augmentation. For ASVspoof21-LA, a combina-
tion of the linear and non-linear convolutive noise, impulsive
signal-dependent additive noise, and signal-independent addi-
tive noise (Algorithm 4) was adopted. For ASVspoof21-DF,
In-the-Wild, and CodecFake, only the signal-independent addi-
tive noise (Algorithm 3) was used. The audio duration and the
segment offset were exploited during training. The extraction
of timing embeddings for short and long utterances is shown
in Figure 1, which uses padding and clipping, respectively. In
evaluation, we set the offset to 0 and used the whole utterance.
The Adam optimizer was used with a weight decay of 5e-5
for Conformer-6 and Confusionformer-9. The learning rate was

5. Results
The performance was evaluated in terms of EER. The best result
and the average result from 3 independent runs were reported.
5.1. Main results
The results of various models are shown in Table 1.
By comparing Row 1 with Rows 9–11, we see that al-
though ConFusionformer-9 slightly underperformed Mamba
on ASVspoof21-LA, it achieved better performance on
the other three datasets. This observation indicates that
ConFusionformer-9 is a strong baseline for speech deepfake de-
tection. Conformer-6, on the other hand, shows poorer perfor-
mance than ConFusionformer-9 on all datasets.
Comparing Row 1 with Rows 2–4, we observe that the
RPE-dependent DASA outperformed the standard self-attention
for ConFusionformer-9, whereas the frame-independent and
frame-dependent DASAs even degraded the performance. Sim-
ilar results are observed for Conformer-6 when comparing Row
5 with Rows 6–8. Because frame-independent DASA only
learns a bias per head, the model may have limited ability to
correct the attention logits. On the other hand, frame-dependent
DASA performs frame-wise interaction between the timing em-
bedding and the whole sequence. Since there is no constraint on
the interactions, the resulting bias can introduce noise into the
attention map, causing performance degradation. Because RPE-
dependent DASA integrates the timing embedding into RPE,
the resulting bias map is limited to the maximum relative dis-
tanceR, avoiding an aggressive correction to the attention map.
Generally, RPE-dependent DASA makes a consistently positive
contribution to deepfake detection than the other two strategies.
5.2. Effect of duration variations
To investigate the duration robustness of the RPE-dependent
DASA, we reported the performance of ConFusionformer-9 on
ASVspoof21-DF by creating evaluation segments in 2, 4, and 6
seconds. Table 2 shows that the RPE-dependent DASA outper-
formed the standard self-attention in most cases except when
the duration was set to 4s. For the standard SA, the best result
was achieved when the training duration was equal to the evalu-
ation duration. When there was duration mismatch, the perfor-
mance degraded rapidly. On the other hand, the RPE-dependent
DASA shows lower variations in the results. Generally, DASA
presents better robustness to duration variations than SA.
6. Conclusions
In this paper, we have proposed a new duration-aware self-
attention (DASA) mechanism by incorporating the timing em-
beddings computed from the audio duration and the segment
offset. With the guidance of timing embeddings, the self-
attention maps are appropriately corrected by the timing-related
bias. Experiments on ASVspoof21, In-the-Wild, and Codec-
Fake show the priority of DASA to the vanilla self-attention,
indicating its robustness to duration variations.
