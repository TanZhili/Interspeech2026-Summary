Abstract
Most existing neural speech codecs are optimized for fixed sam-
pling rates and lack multi-rate adaptability, leading to signifi-
cant performance degradation at mismatched rates. To address
this, we propose a multi-sampling-rate neural speech codec sup-
porting16/24/48 kHzaudio in a unified model. The archi-
tecture consists of a shared encoder-decoder backbone and a
universal Residual Vector Quantization (RVQ) module, inte-
grated with two lightweight sampling-rate-aware components:
an adapter for learnable waveform conversion to a unified in-
ternal temporal grid, and a transformation modulator for inter-
mediate feature calibration to ensure cross-sampling-rate repre-
sentation consistency for shared quantization. We further adopt
a three-stage progressive training strategy to stabilize optimiza-
tion. Experiments show that our unified codec matches the per-
formance of sampling-rate-specific models while simplifying
deployment by eliminating the need for multiple sampling-rate-
specific instances.
Index Terms: neural speech codec, multiple sampling rates,
unified architecture, affine transformation
1. Introduction
Speech coding is a cornerstone of modern communication,
striving to minimize transmission and storage costs while
maintaining high perceptual fidelity [1]. In practical de-
ployments, speech signals are processed at diverse sampling
rates—typically16 kHz(wideband),24 kHz(super-wideband),
and48 kHz(full-band)—to accommodate varying device ca-
pabilities, network bandwidths, and application requirements.
While recent neural speech codecs have revolutionized the
rate-distortion trade-off through deep generative modeling and
learned quantization [2, 3], their efficacy is often bound to a
fixed sampling rate.
The deployment of neural codecs in multi-sampling-rate
environments remains inherently challenging. A primary ob-
stacle is the temporal and spectral inconsistency across sam-
pling rates: convolutional receptive fields, defined by sam-
ple points, correspond to varying physical durations at differ-
ent rates, while distinct frequency ranges shift the underlying
spectral statistics. These discrepancies lead to a sampling-rate-
dependent distribution shift, which complicates shared quanti-
zation and degrades reconstruction quality. Consequently, cur-
rent systems must either rely on computationally expensive ex-
ternal resampling or maintain multiple rate-specific models, sig-
nificantly increasing deployment and maintenance complexity.
Recent literature has begun to explore multi-sampling-rate
neural coding, yet existing solutions often fall short of a truly

2. Proposed Method
2.1. Overall Network Architecture
We aim to build a single neural speech codec for multiple sam-
pling rates using one set of model weights. Two challenges
arise: (i) The same physical duration corresponds to different
numbers of samples across sampling-rates, therefore, the shared
encoder/decoder extracts features that fail to align on a unified
temporal scale. (ii) Sampling rate determines signal bandwidth
(e.g., higher sampling-rate preserves more high-frequency con-
tent), which shifts spectral statistics and intermediate feature
distributions, making it difficult to learn unified representations
and to share a single quantization module across sampling rates.
As shown in Fig. 2, we use a fully shared time-domain
backbone with two lightweight sampling-rate-aware compo-
nents. Sampling-Rate Adapter (SRAT) performs learnable
sampling-rate conversion at the waveform input/output inter-
face of the encoder/decoder to map signals to a consistent inter-
nal temporal grid. Sampling-Rate Modulator (SRMT) applies
sampling-rate-conditioned affine transformation within network
blocks to reduce cross-sampling-rate feature mismatch and en-
hance the consistency of latent representations for the shared
RVQ module [7].
Encoder.Given an input waveformxwith sampling rate
s∈{16,24,48}kHz, the encoder first applies an input SRAT
to align it to an internal temporal grid (we use16 kHz). This
alignment facilitates parameter sharing while still allowing re-
construction at the target rate via the output SRAT. The aligned
signal is then processed by stacked encoder blocks, each con-
sisting of a SEANet-style module [8], an SRMT layer, and tem-
poral downsampling. An LSTM [9] models longer-range de-
pendencies, and the resulting latent sequence is discretized by
the shared RVQ into code indices.
Decoder.The decoder mirrors the encoder. It maps RVQ
indices back to a latent sequence, applies an LSTM, and recon-
structs the internal waveform through symmetric upsampling
blocks with SRMT. Finally, an output SRAT converts the recon-
structed internal waveform to the target sampling rates, produc-
ingˆx.
All backbone parameters, including RVQ codebooks, are
shared across sampling rates; sampling-rate awareness is intro-
duced only via SRAT and SRMT, enabling straightforward de-
ployment with a single model. The entire architecture is fully
causal, allowing low-latency streaming inference.
2.2. Sampling-Rate Adapter (SRAT)
A unified codec should ensure that internal representations cor-
respond to consistent physical durations across sampling rates.
If raw waveforms at different sampling rates are directly pro-
cessed by a shared encoder, the same receptive field in samples
corresponds to different durations in seconds. This mismatch
leads to temporal misalignment and reduces the transferability
of learned analysis and synthesis filters. To address this issue,
we introduce SRAT modules at the input and output interfaces.
Input SRAT.The input SRAT maps an input waveform
sampled at ratesto a unified internal temporal grid using learn-
able one-dimensional convolutional rate-conversion layers. For
integer ratios, SRAT can be implemented with strided convolu-
tions that jointly learn filtering and decimation under the codec
objective. For non-integer ratios, we implement rational-factor
conversion by cascading simple upsampling and downsampling
stages. This design avoids fractional strides and improves nu-
merical stability. In our implementation,24 kHzto16 kHzcon-
version is implemented via a learnable×2upsampling followed
by×3downsampling.
Output SRAT.The output SRAT performs the inverse con-
version using transposed convolutions to restore the target sam-
pling rate and temporal resolution. SRAT is applied only at the
waveform interfaces. Therefore, the encoder-decoder backbone
operates on a consistent internal temporal grid and can be fully
shared across sampling rates, while waveform reconstruction is
handled only by the lightweight interface layers.
2.3. Sampling-Rate Modulator (SRMT)
SRAT aligns the temporal grid at the input and output inter-
faces. However, intermediate feature distributions can still vary
with sampling rate because the effective bandwidth and spec-
tral statistics change across rates. Such distribution shifts make
it harder to learn unified representations and to share a single
quantization module without sacrificing quality. To mitigate
this issue, we propose SRMT, a lightweight conditioning layer
inserted into each encoder/decoder block, as shown in Fig. 3.

2.4. Progressive Training Strategy
Training a single codec across multiple sampling rates is chal-
lenging because the model must reconstruct high-sampling-rate
signals while learning a stable shared RVQ quantizer under het-
erogeneous feature distributions. We adopt a three-stage pro-
gressive training strategy to improve optimization stability. Fol-
lowing previous work [11, 12], we use the weighted objective
L=λ recLrec +λ advLadv +λ f eatLf eat +λ cmLcm,(2)
whereL rec,L adv,L f eat, andL cm denote reconstruction,
adversarial, feature-matching, and RVQ commitment loss, re-
spectively.
Stage 1.We pre-train the model on48 kHzdata usingL rec
andL cm. This stage initializes the shared backbone and RVQ
codebooks with high-resolution signals.
Stage 2.We perform multi-sampling-rate joint training by
mixing16 kHz,24 kHz, and48 kHzdata. During this stage,
SRAT and SRMT are enabled to align temporal structures and
to calibrate intermediate feature distributions across sampling
rates. This improves the stability of backbone and quantization
sharing across sampling rates.
Stage 3.After reconstruction and quantization stabilize,
we enableL adv andL f eat for perceptual refinement. We use a
shared multi-scale STFT discriminator [11] across all sampling
rates so that the perceptual criterion remains consistent across
conditions and bias across sampling rates is reduced.

3.2. Comparative Studies
Table 1 compares the proposed unified codec with previous neu-
ral speech codecs across multiple sampling rates. MSR (Mul-
tiple Sampling Rate) indicates whether a single model with
one set of weights can support multiple rates. Unlike previ-
ous sampling-rate-specific codecs that rely on separate models,
ours supports 16, 24, and48 kHzspeech using a single shared
architecture and weight set.
Across LibriTTS at16 kHzand24 kHzand VCTK at
48 kHz, the proposed method achieves competitive quality rela-
tive to sampling-rate-specific baselines. For example, at24 kHz
and1.5kbps, our model reaches a ViSQOL of4.22, and at
48 kHzand1.5kbps it achieves a PESQ of2.750. This uni-
fied design avoids maintaining separate models for different
sampling rates, and it does not require an external resampling
pipeline during inference.
In terms of model size and computational efficiency, the
proposed model leverages a single shared architecture with a
fixed parameter count of 19.65M to support all target sampling
rates, incurring only 2.14/2.58/2.34 G MACs at16/24/48 kHz,
respectively. Notably, the24 kHzsetting incurs slightly higher
computation due to SRAT’s extra convolutional operations for
stable rate conversion. Overall, the proposed model achieves
both a low parameter count and consistently low computational
complexity.

3.3.2. Effect of SRAT and SRMT
We conduct component-wise ablations to evaluate SRAT and
SRMT. All variants share the same backbone and RVQ config-
uration. Table 3 reports results at16,24, and48 kHz.
Impact of SRAT.Disabling SRAT causes consistent degra-
dation across sampling rates. Without a unified waveform in-
terface, discrete-time steps correspond to different physical du-
rations, changing the effective receptive field in seconds and
reducing the transferability of analysis and synthesis opera-
tors. This shows that interface alignment is important for multi-
sampling-rate modeling.
Impact of SRMT.Omitting SRMT causes larger drops on
perceptually sensitive metrics, highlighting the role of feature
calibration. At24 kHz, PESQ decreases from2.353to1.979.
This suggests that sampling-rate-dependent feature shifts make
shared quantization less effective, while affine modulation helps
maintain more consistent latent representations.
Removing both modules.Dropping both SRAT and
SRMT yields the lowest scores, indicating that waveform-level
alignment and feature calibration are complementary for reli-
able sharing across sampling rates.
3.3.3. Effect of Progressive Training Strategy
We evaluate the three-stage progressive training strategy by
training the unified model without it (Table 3). Quality de-
creases consistently across16/24/48 kHz, suggesting that op-
timizing all sampling rates and all objectives from the beginning
makes convergence harder for a fully shared codec. In contrast,
progressive training acts as an optimization curriculum: it first
anchors the model on reconstruction, then introduces sampling-
rate diversity, and finally adds perceptual objectives. This or-
dering reduces early cross-sampling-rate condition interference
and improves overall perceptual fidelity.
3.3.4. Latent Consistency Analysis
To quantify cross-sampling-rate consistency in the learned la-
tent space, we compute the Maximum Mean Discrepancy
(MMD) [25] between latent representations extracted from in-
puts at different sampling rates.
Fig. 4 compares three models: the proposed full model, a
single-sampling-rate model trained only at16 kHz, and an ab-
lated model without SRAT and SRMT. The full model consis-
tently achieves the lowest MMD for all sampling-rate pairs (16
vs.24,16vs.48, and24vs.48 kHz), indicating substan-
tially improved latent consistency across sampling rates. In
contrast, the16 kHz-only model shows the largest MMD val-
ues, especially for the16vs.48 kHzpair. Removing both
SRAT and SRMT also increases MMD markedly, confirming
that temporal-grid alignment and feature-statistics calibration
are both important for learning a shared latent space.

4. Conclusion
This paper proposes a unified neural speech codec that sup-
ports16,24, and48 kHzspeech within a single set of model
weights. The framework shares the encoder-decoder backbone
and the RVQ quantizer across sampling rates, and introduces
two lightweight sampling-rate-aware components. SRAT per-
forms learnable sampling-rate conversion at the input/output
interfaces to align inputs and outputs to a consistent internal
temporal grid without an external resampling pipeline. SRMT
applies sampling-rate-aware affine transformation inside net-
work blocks to reduce feature distribution mismatch and facili-
tate effective sharing of a single RVQ module across sampling
rates. A three-stage progressive training strategy further sta-
bilizes multi-sampling-rate optimization. Experiments demon-
strate that the unified model approaches sampling-rate-specific
specialists across all target sampling rates while simplifying de-
