Beyond Standard Greek: Adapting Whisper for Greek Dialects through
Curriculum Multitask Learning

Abstract
In this work, we investigate training strategies for improved
Whisper adaptation for Greek dialects under low-resource con-
straints. We propose a unified framework that integrates three
pillars: data augmentation with an adjacent donor language,
multitask learning (joint speech recognition and translation),
and a staged curriculum. Our curriculum is implemented as
a progressive training pipeline, beginning with cross-lingual
translation and donor-language supervision, gradually transi-
tioning to dialect-specific ASR. Empirical results across three
distinct low-resource settings (Cypriot, Cretan, and Messenian)
demonstrate that our method consistently outperforms conven-
tional fine-tuning across various model scales. Furthermore, de-
tailed ablations validate our design choices, confirming that the
synergy between curriculum learning and multitask supervision
is essential for effective adaptation.
Index Terms: automatic speech recognition, low-resource lan-

1. Introduction
Adapting Automatic Speech Recognition (ASR) systems to re-
gional dialects remains a persistent challenge, even in the era
of large-scale self-supervised models. For Greek, Vakirtzian
et al. [1] and Tsoukala et al. [2] highlight this gap through
a benchmark encompassing a broad range of Greek varieties.
Their findings reveal extremely high Word Error Rates (WERs)
(>100%) in zero-shot settings, particularly for highly di-
vergent varieties shaped by sustained language contact—such
as Aivaliot and Cappadocian under the influence of Turkish,
and Griko under the influence of Italo-Romance, as well as
Northern Modern Greek varieties (e.g., Lesbian). Even after
supervised fine-tuning, a substantial “dialectal tax” remains,
with error rates often doubling relative to Standard Modern
Greek (SMG), largely due to orthographic variation, dialec-
tal distance, and language-contact effects. This pattern ex-
tends beyond Greek. Across more than thirty Indic varieties,
Dhasmana et al. [3] show that phylogenetic proximity to a
high-resource language does not guarantee robustness; modest
dialect-specific fine-tuning can outperform transfer from related
mainstream varieties. Similarly, studies on Scottish English ac-
cents [4] reveal significant degradation despite large-scale mul-
tilingual pretraining, with accent-specific adaptation introduc-
ing trade-offs such as contextual bias and overfitting. Together,
these findings suggest that dialects constitute distinct acous-
tic–linguistic domains rather than minor variants of a canonical
form.
Under low-resource conditions, various adaptation strate-
gies have been explored. Mansour et al. [5] combine su-
pervised fine-tuning, pseudo-labeling, and synthetic augmen-
tation for Sudanese Arabic, achieving relative improvements
but still trailing standardized benchmarks. The MADASR ini-
tiative [6] likewise reports persistent gaps across closely re-
lated South Asian varieties. Despite advances in self-supervised
learning, limited dialectal transcriptions and phonological di-
vergence continue to hinder robustness. Xiao et al. [7] further
identify a U-shaped adaptability pattern in Whisper, showing
that distributing adaptation capacity across layers can reduce
errors by up to 29% in extreme low-resource settings.
Recent work on adapting Whisper to low-resource and
domain-mismatched scenarios compares full fine-tuning, selec-
tive freezing, and parameter-efficient methods such as Adapters
and LoRA [8]. Results indicate that even fewer than 20 hours of
supervision can yield substantial gains, while lightweight mod-
ules achieve competitive performance at lower computational
cost. Complementary approaches include synthetic data genera-
tion for Hindi–English code-mixed speech [9] and architectural
extensions for streaming ASR via hybrid CTC–attention frame-
works [10], both demonstrating notable robustness improve-
ments. Multi-task learning [11] has also proven effective for
dialectal ASR by jointly optimizing transcription with auxiliary
objectives. For example, joint dialect identification and ASR
improves performance in Telugu [12] and Japanese [13], partic-
ularly for low-resource dialects. In Greek ASR, Paraskevopou-
los et al. [14] propose Mixed Multi-Domain Self-Supervision
(M2DS2), integrating CTC and contrastive objectives to sta-
bilize adaptation, while Damianos et al. [15] extend this ap-
proach with Multi-Stage Domain Adaptation (MSDA) and Meta
Pseudo-Labeling, achieving further gains. Beyond multitask
optimization, curriculum learning [16] structures training by ex-
ample difficulty rather than random sampling. Adaptive cur-
ricula that combine signal-based difficulty metrics with student
progress signals have achieved up to 33% relative WER reduc-
tion across several low-resource languages [17], underscoring
the importance of structured training under data scarcity.
In this work, building on recent work on Greek ASR
adaptation [14, 15], we explore whether curriculum learning
principles can be extended to structured cross-task adaptation
within Whisper. Although multitask and curriculum learn-
ing have each improved ASR, their combination for dialect
adaptation remains underexplored: multitask approaches opti-
mize objectives jointly without controlling the adaptation tra-
jectory, while curriculum methods structure data by difficulty
but not by supervision type. We propose a staged multitask
curriculum for low-resource dialect ASR, introducing a gradual

2. Methodology
Figure 1 illustrates our proposedstaged multitask curriculum
for dialectal ASR. Our approach extends prior domain adap-
tation methods by introducing a structured progression across
both task and domain dimensions, operating directly at the su-
pervision level to gradually shift training from donor-language
translation to dialect-specific ASR. The framework consists of
four sequential stages forming a curriculum across (a) task
space (translation→ASR) and (b) domain space (Standard
Greek→Dialect).
2.1. Stage 0: Donor Language Translation
The initial stage operates exclusively in the source domain and
consists entirely of Greek-to-English speech translation (ST)
data. This foundation phase establishes stable multilingual
acoustic–semantic representations under high-resource condi-
tions. By training solely on translation supervision, the model
learns aligned acoustic features before any dialectal variability
is introduced.
2.2. Stage 1: Mixed Domain
The first transition stage introduces dialectal speech through a
mixed-domain sampling strategy. Training data are drawn with
probabilityαfrom the donor language ASR corpus (Standard
Greek) and with probability1−αfrom the Dialect ST corpus.
Dialectal acoustics are therefore introduced under transla-
tion supervision, while source-language transcription remains
active. This configuration initiates domain shift while main-
taining semantically structured supervision, reducing instability
that may arise from early dialect-only ASR training.

2.3. Stage 2: Adaptation
At this stage, supervision operates entirely within the dialect do-
main. The sampling probability for Dialect ASR is setβ, while
Dialect ST samples are drawn with probability1−β. The tran-
sition now primarily occurs along the task axis, progressively
increasing transcription exposure while retaining partial trans-
lation supervision. Translation data provide regularizing signals
during specialization toward dialectal ASR.
2.4. Stage 3: Target Domain
The final stage consists exclusively of Dialect ASR data. With
both domain and task transitions completed, training converges
fully to the target objective, enabling specialization to low-
resource dialect transcription.

3. Experimental setup
3.1. Acoustic Model
We use Whisper [18], a large-scale multilingual Transformer
encoder–decoder model trained on approximately 1 million
hours of weakly labeled speech data and an additional 4 mil-
lion hours of pseudo-labeled audio obtained via self-training.
Its large-scale multilingual pretraining enables strong zero-shot
generalization; however, recent evaluations reveal substantial
degradation under dialectal mismatch, motivating structured
adaptation strategies. In our experiments, we evaluate three
model scales:Whisper-small,Whisper-medium, andWhisper-
large-v3, to analyze the impact of model capacity on dialect
adaptation performance.

3.2. Dialects
We experiment on three Greek dialects—Cypriot, Cretan, and
Messenian—all belonging to the southern dialect zone, which
also includes SMG, but classified into distinct subgroups [19].
Their distribution in the Eastern Mediterranean is shown in Fig-
ure 2.
Figure 2:Geographical distribution of the dialectal data exam-
ined in this work.
Cypriot Dialect: Cypriot Greek (CG) belongs to the South-
Eastern Greek dialect group and is spoken by more than
700,000 speakers in Cyprus and diaspora communities (Pop-
ulation Census of Cyprus, 2011). It is considered the most vital
non-standard variety of Modern Greek [20]. For ASR train-
ing, we use version 23.0 of the Mozilla Common V oice Spon-
taneous Speech dataset for Cypriot Greek (“el-CY”), compris-
ing 1,284 validated clips (approximately 10.9 hours) from 10
speakers, publicly available through the Mozilla Data Collec-
tive.3 Audio was further segmented into sentence-level utter-
ances using forced alignment, and segments longer than 30 s
were removed to match Whisper’s input constraints. As CG
lacks a fully standardized orthography, transcripts were pro-
duced by native speakers and linguistically homogenized to
reduce spelling variation. A deterministic Unicode-level nor-
malization pipeline was applied to all transcripts: lowercasing,
canonical normalization, punctuation standardization, removal
of non-linguistic artefacts, monotonic conversion, and correc-
tion of mixed Greek–Latin spellings. Importantly, dialect-
specific lexical forms are preserved rather than mapped to SMG,
maintaining phonological distinctiveness during training.
Cretan Dialect: Cretan Greek is spoken primarily on the is-
land of Crete and remains highly vital in everyday communi-
cation [21]. It exhibits distinctive phonological and lexical fea-
tures shaped by geographical isolation and historical contact.
We use the corpus introduced by Vakirtzian et al. [1], consist-
ing of archival radio broadcasts (1998–2001) from Radio Mires
(Messara region, Heraklion), digitized from the Vikelaia Mu-
nicipal Library archive. The recordings include folklore nar-
ratives representative of the local variety. Initial transcriptions
were generated using Whisper Large-v2 and manually corrected
in collaboration with members of the local community. The cor-
pus contains 12,921 tokens across 2,589 utterances (2h 1m 35s
original duration; 1h 21m 12s after preprocessing).
Messenian Dialect: Messenian is spoken in the southwestern
Peloponnese and belongs to the broader Peloponnesian dialect
group. Although historically related to varieties contributing
to SMG [22, 21], documented material reveals notable phono-
logical deviations and internal variation [23, 24]. We use the
Messenian corpus introduced in Vakirtzian et al. [1] to ensure
comparability with prior dialectal ASR evaluations. The corpus
comprises 4,721 tokens across 590 utterances (39m 45s original
duration; 37m 42s after preprocessing).

3.3. Speech-to-Text Translation and Standard Greek Data
To support speech-to-text translation (ST) supervision within
the proposed curriculum framework, all Greek and dialectal
transcripts were paired with English translations. English trans-
lations were generated using Llama-Krikri-8B-Instruct [25].
All automatically generated translations were subsequently
manually reviewed and corrected to ensure semantic adequacy
and proper alignment with the corresponding audio segments.
All datasets were structured in a unified HuggingFace format
consisting of three fields:audio(16 kHz waveform),text,
andtask. The task field specifies whether the objective is
speech transcription or speech-to-text translation, while the text
field contains the corresponding target sequence. This uni-
fied representation enables flexible sampling across tasks and
dialects during staged curriculum training. For high-resource
standard-language supervision in the early curriculum stages,
we additionally employ the Greek Podcast Corpus (GPC) [26].
GPC consists of 3,124 hours of weakly supervised Greek pod-
cast audio, with transcriptions generated using the WhisperX
pipeline [27]. In our experiments, we use the GPC-5h subset,
comprising 5 hours per category from four thematic domains
(True Crime, Business, Comedy, and Education).
3.4. Experimental Settings
3.4.1. Fine-Tuning Baseline
As a baseline, we perform conventional supervised fine-tuning
(FT) of a pretrained Whisper model [18] separately for each
dialect. Audio is resampled to 16 kHz and truncated to 30 sec-
onds. Training is performed for a maximum of 2,048 update
steps. During inference, greedy decoding is applied with a max-
imum generation length of 225 tokens. To promote stable adap-
tation, the encoder is frozen while keeping the convolutional
feature extractor trainable.
3.4.2. Staged Multitask Curriculum Training
For curriculum-based adaptation, we adopt a four-stage bi-
modal training procedure. For Cypriot Greek and Cretan,
each stage uses a fixed budget of 8,000 samples drawn from
task–domain pools (Greek ASR, Greek→EN translation, Di-
alect ASR, Dialect→EN translation). For Messenian, due to
limited data availability, 2,000 samples are used per stage.
ASR–translation ratios vary across stages according to pre-
defined schedules. Specifically, we consider a progressive
shift from 0.4/0.6 to 0.6/0.4, a symmetric 0.5/0.5 configura-
tion maintained across both stages, a translation-leaning sched-
ule (0.2/0.8 to 0.4/0.6), and an ASR-leaning schedule (0.6/0.4
to 0.8/0.2). Finally, to isolate the role of translation supervi-
sion, we construct a three-stage curriculum variant that excludes
translation entirely. In this ASR-only setting, training relies ex-
clusively on transcription data while preserving the same staged
domain transition from Standard Greek to the target dialect.
Training proceeds sequentially, with each stage initial-
ized from the final checkpoint of the previous one. Task-
specific decoder prompts are dynamically injected during train-
ing based on the task field of each sample, which indicates
whether the sample corresponds to transcription or translation.

4. Results
Table 1 reports WER and CER across dialects under zero-shot,
standard fine-tuning (FT), and curriculum training. Curriculum
consistently outperforms both baselines across all dialects and
model sizes, with particularly large gains for smaller models.
For Cypriot (8K), thesmallmodel improves from 81.27% WER
(zero-shot) to 35.61% with curriculum, whilelarge-v3reaches
24.89%. Similar reductions are observed for Cretan, and in the
low-resource Messenian setting (2K), curriculum remains effec-
tive (e.g., 67.49%→30.25% forsmall), indicating robustness
under limited supervision.
The effect of different curriculum schedules:Table 2 exam-
ines different supervision schedules on Cypriot Greek. All vari-
ants share a translation-only initialization stage, followed by
two stages and final dialect ASR specialization. For thesmall
model, a balanced 50/50 strategy performs best (33.56 WER),
outperforming translation-heavy and ASR-heavy variants. The
mediummodel shows limited sensitivity to ratio selection, while
large-v3performs best under the original gradual-shift sched-
ule. These results show that, while the tuning of the curriculum
schedule is model-dependent, a balanced curriculum offers a
good starting point for all Whisper variants.
The effect of including donor language data:In Table 3
we evaluate the effect of including Greek pre-alignment data
(GPC). For Cypriot and Cretan, differences are minor, indicat-
ing that curriculum adaptation remains stable in higher-resource
settings. However, in low-resource Messenian, performance de-
grades more noticeably without GPC, especially forsmalland
large-v3.

5. Conclusions & Future Work
We introduced a staged multitask curriculum for low-resource
dialectal ASR, progressively transitioning from standard-
language speech translation to dialect-specific transcription.
By structuring adaptation across both task and domain dimen-
sions, the proposed framework reduces distributional mismatch
and stabilizes training under limited supervision. Across three
Greek dialects and multiple Whisper model scales, curriculum-
based training consistently outperformed conventional fine-
tuning, with particularly strong gains under severe domain
shift. Future work will explore the role of linguistic proxim-
ity in structured dialect adaptation, examining whether curricu-
lum gains correlate with phonological or orthographic distance
across varieties. We also plan to investigate parameter-efficient
adaptation strategies and phonology-aware regularization to fur-
ther enhance robustness under low-resource conditions. Fi-
nally, we plan to extend our work to more challenging dialec-
tal settings, including Northern Greek varieties and contact-
influenced varieties shaped by Turkish and Italo-Romance.
