Bridging Languages and Modalities: Lightweight Cross-Lingual Text and
Speech Summarization for Low-Resource Scenarios

Abstract
Cross-lingual summarization aims to condense written or spo-
ken content in one language into a coherent and concise sum-
mary in another language. This task requires understanding the
source language’s nuances, filtering for importance, and recon-
structing meaning in a different grammar and vocabulary; these
operations are particularly difficult to perform when the avail-
able data are scarce to train models. In this paper, we propose a
novel framework that uses multilingual sentence and utterance
embeddings to process both speech and text inputs under strict
data constraints. Based on the CrossSum dataset and a new
cross-lingual speech evaluation dataset we collected for three
low-resource languages, our experimental results highlight the
strong potential of our approach for cross-lingual summariza-
tion, particularly in low-resource spoken language scenarios.
Index Terms: crosslingual summarization, sentence embed-
dings, utterance embeddings, low-resource languages
1. Introduction
The surge in multilingual content has intensified the need for
Cross-Lingual Summarization (CLS), which generates sum-
maries in a target language from source material in another.
This task goes beyond standard summarization, requiring the
model to maintain salient information while navigating the
structural and lexical disparities between distinct languages.
The classic approach to cross-lingual text summarization
decomposes the task into two main categories: the translate-
first paradigm and the summarize-first approach [1, 2, 3]. These
approaches either convert source documents into the target lan-
guage before summarization or first distill the content in the
source language and then translate the summary. This frame-
work allows for independent model training using non-parallel
data, yet it is often hindered by translation errors and informa-
tion loss during condensation.
Similarly, in cross-lingual speech summarization, cascaded
pipelines are commonly used [4, 5]. In this setting, audio is
first transcribed using an automatic speech recognition (ASR)
system. The transcript is then either translated into the target
language using a machine translation model before being sum-
marized by a monolingual summarization system, or directly
processed by a multilingual model such as mBART [6], mT5
[7], flanT5 [8], or more recent large language models (LLMs)
like GPT [9, 10] and LLaMA [11]. However, these systems still
suffer from propagation of ASR errors.
More recent work has explored end-to-end (E2E) sum-
marization approaches, where a single model directly gener-
ates summaries from either audio or text. For example, [12]
proposes a universal model handling multiple tasks and lan-
guages, evaluated on speech and text translation as well as sum-
marization tasks in two high-resource languages (English and
Portuguese). Two additional studies investigate cross-lingual
text summarization, particularly in low-resource settings. First,
[13] proposes an mBART-based training strategy that gradu-
ally builds summarization and cross-lingual capabilities. Dur-
ing fine-tuning, they further incorporate discrete prompt-based
joint training optimized with reinforcement learning to improve
performance in low-resource cross-lingual summarization. Sec-
ond, [14] uses a retrieval-augmented approach with a multi-
lingual data store, retrieving semantically similar examples to
guide a target LLM in generating cross-lingual summaries, im-
proving quality in low-resource scenarios. However, both ap-
proaches operate on textual inputs, and low-resource cross-
lingual speech summarization remains largely underexplored.
In parallel, recent advances in speech representation learn-
ing have introduced utterance-level speech embeddings as a
powerful alternative to traditional frame-level acoustic features
(10–20ms). These embeddings are generated over longer seg-
ments (5–10s) and can be aligned semantically across lan-
guages. For instance, SAMU-XLSR [15] adapts the XLS-
R speech encoder [16] to produce utterance-level embeddings
comparable to language-agnostic sentence embeddings like
LaBSE [17], using a teacher-student framework where the
frozen LaBSE model guides the speech encoder via cosine sim-
ilarity. Similarly, SONAR [18] aligns speech embeddings with
a shared sentence embedding space derived from an NLLB
model [19], starting from a pretrained w2v-BERT 2.0 speech
encoder [20]. A more recent model, SENSE [21], also leverages
w2v-BERT 2.0 as the speech encoder and BGE-M3 [22] as the
teacher model. These models provide rich, multilingual, and
semantically aligned representations suitable for downstream
tasks like E2E cross-lingual summarization.
Motivated by these advances, we propose a unified E2E
framework for cross-lingual text and speech summarization
based on the use of speech and sentence embeddings. Instead of
relying on intermediate ASR transcripts or machine translation,
our approach directly generates abstractive French summaries
from either speech or text inputs by first projecting them into
corresponding cross-lingual semantic embedding spaces. By
operating at the embedding level, our framework naturally sup-
ports both cross-lingual and cross-modal transfer. Moreover,
it remains effective in both high and low-resource settings, re-
quiring only limited speech data to produce high-quality sum-
maries. The main contributions of this study are: (1) A unified
lightweight E2E framework for cross-lingual text and speech
summarization with only 140M trainable parameters, that lever-
ages semantic sentence embeddings for text and speech utter-
ance embeddings for audio, enabling effective learning in low-
resource conditions. (2) A new open-source evaluation dataset,
coined as ABT-SpeechSUM, for cross-lingual speech summa-

2. Proposed Approach
2.1. Summary generation using semantic embeddings
We propose an abstractive summarization model that gener-
ates French text summaries from either speech or text inputs
(Figure 1). When the input is speech, the audio signal is first
split into segmentss i, each encoded into a vectorv i using a
speech utterance embedding model. When the input is text,
the document is divided into sentencess i, each sentence be-
ing encoded into a vectorvi using a sentence embedding model.
The resulting sequence of vectors[v1, v2, v3, . . . , vn]is fed into
a modified seq2seq model trained to produce the final sum-
mary. To guarantee compatibility with the input requirements
of the seq2seq model, we incorporate an additional linear pro-
jection layer that adjusts the dimensionality of speech (or text)
embeddings. In this study, we employ a pretrained token-
based encoder-decoder seq2seq model and modify its archi-
tecture to accept these semantic embeddings. To achieve this,
we remove the encoder’s embed-tokens layer, which generates
token-level embeddings. As a result, the modified encoder be-
comes sentence-based as it operates directly on sentence-level
or speech utterance-level representations, while retaining the
unchanged decoder architecture and token-based processing.

2.2. A multistage training
We adopt a two-stage training strategy:
Stage 1: Adapting the seq2seq model parameters to sen-
tence embeddings.We first train the model on a large-scale
text summarization dataset using sentence embeddings as in-
put representations. To transition from the original token-level
design, this stage recalibrates the model parameters to operate
within a sentence-level embedding space.
Stage 2: Task-specific fine-tuning.In the second stage, we

continue training using the summarization dataset specific to the
target task. Fine-tuning is driven by the input medium: sentence
embeddings are used for text summarization, while speech em-
beddings are applied to audio tasks. Since speech embedding
models are typically trained via a teacher–student approach,
sentence and speech embeddings of the same teacher-student
approach are closely aligned. Therefore, the speech embedding
model used in Stage 2 must correspond to the sentence embed-
ding model used in Stage 1, ensuring embedding space consis-
tency across both training stages.
Throughout both stages, the sentence/speech embedding
model remains frozen. Only the projection layer and the modi-
fied seq2seq model are trained.
2.3. Text sentence embedding model
The Massive Text Embedding Benchmark (MTEB) [23] pro-
vides an evaluation of text sentence embedding models across
58 datasets in 112 languages and 8 embedding tasks. Using the
MTEB leaderboard, we selected BGE-M3 [22] for its strong
performance in reranking, bitext mining, and semantic similar-
ity, as well as its manageable size (560M parameters), open-
source availability, and language-agnostic design.
2.4. Speech embedding model
As the sentence and speech embedding models should be
of the same teacher-student approach, we selected SENSE
[21], an open source teacher-student framework that aligns
speech and text embeddings via knowledge distillation from
the BGE-M3 model. Built upon the pretrained multilingual
w2vBERT 2.0 [20], SENSE uses attentive pooling to condense
frame-level audio into a single utterance-level embedding. By
learning to predict BGE-M3 text embeddings, SENSE creates a
semantically aligned space across 83 languages where speech,
transcripts, and translations cluster together.
2.5. Seq2seq Model
To develop our summarization model for generating French
summaries, we selected BARThez [24], a 165M-parameter
seq2seq model based on BART [25]. To reconcile the dimen-
sionality mismatch between our 1024-dimensional embeddings
and the 768-dimensional input required by BARThez, we em-
ployed a linear projection layer with GeLU activation (Fig-
ure 1). We designate this modified seq2seq architecture as
SBARThez(Semantic-BARThez).
3. Cross-lingual Text Summarization
3.1. Dataset
Our objective is to generate a French textual summary from an
input document written in a low-resource language. To evaluate
the cross-lingual capabilities of our approach, we use CrossSum
[26], a large-scale multilingual dataset designed for evaluating
cross-lingual summarization systems (Text X→Text Y).
3.2. Model Training and Baselines
SBARThez training:As explained in section 2.2, the train-
ing of SBARThez is done in two stages. In the first stage, we
use the French subset of the MLSUM corpus [27], a large-scale
textual news summarization dataset, containing approximately
392K French articles paired with their reference summaries. We
use AdamW (batch size 16), optimizing pretrained seq2seq pa-
rameters at a learning rate of1×10 −5 and the projection layer
at1×10 −3, with uniform weight decay of1×10 −5 and cross-
entropy loss. In the second stage, we fine-tune the model on
the French portion of CrossSum (French text documents paired
3487
with French summaries) to improve its relevance to the dataset’s
specific requirements. Although SBARThez could potentially
benefit from further multilingual data, we deliberately excluded
source-language specific resources. This highlights the model’s
ability to leverage language-agnostic sentence embeddings to
process diverse languages with minimal data.
SBARThez evaluation:We generate text sentence embed-
dings for each source language using BGE-M3, and perform
inference with SBARThez fine-tuned on CrossSum-FR.
Baselines:We consider the following baselines:
• M2M100+BARThez: This cascaded approach first translates
the source document from language X into French using
the Machine Translation (MT) system M2M-100 1.2B [28].
The translated document is then summarized using BARThez
fine-tuned on CrossSum-FR.
• M2M100+mT5-large: This second cascaded approach trans-
lates the source document from language X into French using
M2M-100 1.2B, and the translated document is then summa-
rized using mT5-large fine-tuned on CrossSum-FR.
• mT5-large: We fine-tune mT5-large on the French portion
of CrossSum for fair comparison with other systems. Dur-
ing evaluation, the model directly processes documents in
language X, leveraging its multilingual E2E capabilities to
generate summaries without explicit translation. We also ex-
perimented with smaller variants (mT5-small and mT5-base),
but their performance was consistently inferior to mT5-large;
therefore, we report only the strongest variant in our results.
3.3. Results
Results are reported in Table 1 using BertScore without rescal-
ing [29] and Rouge-L [30]. The evaluation set combines the
test and dev sets, unlike for French where only the test set
is used. We evaluate our approach on 11 low-resource lan-
guages (Pashto, Marathi, Yoruba, Somali, Azerbaijani, Tamil,
Burmese, Gujarati, Igbo, Kirundi, Pidgin) and 3 high-resource
languages (French, English, Japanese) to compare its behav-
ior against the baselines. Overall, cascaded approaches per-
form better in high-resource settings. However, for Japanese
(JA), our method achieves superior results and remains com-
petitive across the other high-resource languages. Notably,
SBARThez outperforms all baselines on most low-resource lan-
guages, while achieving results comparable to both E2E mT5
and cascaded mT5 on Pashto (PS), Burmese (MY) and Kirundi
(RN). Importantly, languages such as Igbo (IG), Kirundi (RN),
and Pidgin (PCM) were not seen in the training of the BGE
embedding model. Despite this limitation, SBARThez still
achieves strong results and outperforms all other methods on
IG and PCM. In contrast, for RN and PCM, we were unable
to find suitable MT systems to translate these languages into
French, rendering the cascaded approach ineffective in the ab-
sence of available MT models. Our model’s results are ob-
tained with only 140M trainable parameters, significantly fewer
than the baselines. The total parameter count of our model is
700M (560M for the BGE-M3 encoder and 140M for the modi-
fied seq2seq model with the projection layer), whereas all other
baselines exceed 1B total parameters. This demonstrates the
effectiveness of our lightweight approach, achieving strong per-
formance with substantially fewer parameters.

a wide range of recordings of traditional Breton speakers gath-

the collected data consists of traditional tales and narratives. For
Tunisian, we use the TEDxTN corpus [31], a Tunisian Arabic
to English speech translation dataset derived from TEDx talks.
The corpus provides segmented audio files aligned with En-
glish translations, including start and end timestamps for each
segment. To obtain document-level pairs, we reconstruct the
full English translation for each audio file by concatenating the
translated segments based on their corresponding timestamps.
This process results in complete Tunisian Arabic speech record-
ings paired with their full English translations.
Synthetic summary generation:We generate one textual
summary in French for each audio file using its correspond-
ing textual translation. For Armenian and Breton, summaries
are generated from the collected French translations, while for
Tunisian, we use the English translations reconstructed from
the speech translation corpus. Since no gold-standard human-
written summaries are available for these low-resource lan-
guages, the collection of human summaries would be expensive
and time-consuming. Instead, we generate synthetic summaries
using a LLM. To this end, GPT-4o mini was prompted to act as
a professional annotator, producing concise, information-dense
French summaries of one to four sentences. To assess summary
quality, we manually verify the summaries in the test split of
each language subset. This verification aims to ensure that (i)

4.2. Model Training and Baselines
We split the ABT-SpeechSUM corpus as shown in Table 3 to
train our model and the baseline systems.
SBARThez training:Following the same procedure as in the
previous section, we first train SBARThez on the French subset
of MLSUM to adapt the model to sentence embeddings. For
the second stage of training, we fine-tune the model on ABT-
SpeechSUM using two configurations :
•Tunisian.We train SBARThez with the Tunisian split train-
set of ABT-SpeechSUM.
•Breton and Armenian.Due to the limited number of audio
samples available for Breton and Armenian, training separate
models leads to instability. Therefore, we merge the Breton
and Armenian train splits into a single training set. Since both
datasets belong to the same domain (novels and tales), this
joint training yields a single model, which is then evaluated
separately on the Breton and Armenian test sets.
For both configurations, SBARThez is trained in two different
approaches: (1)SBARThez-textis trained on sentence embed-
dings derived from text translations of the audio data. Specif-
ically, for Tunisian, we use the English translations of audio
recordings, while for Breton and Armenian we use their French
translations. These translations are then encoded using BGE-
M3 to obtain sentence embeddings, which serve as training in-
puts. (2)SBARThez-speechis trained directly on low-resource
speech in an end-to-end framework. Audio files are first seg-
mented using Silero-V AD [32] for voice activity detection. The
resulting speech segments are then processed by SENSE to gen-
erate speech embeddings, which are used to train SBARThez.
In summary, SBARThez-text relies on sentence embeddings
extracted from translated text, whereas SBARThez-speech is
trained on speech embeddings computed directly from the orig-
inal low-resource audio.
SBARThez evaluation:For evaluation, we generate speech
embeddings for each low-resource language using SENSE, and
perform inference with the trained SBARThez models.
Baselines:We consider the following baselines:
• W-L ST (X→en)+M2M100(en→fr)+mT5-large: This cas-
caded approach first translates source speech in language
X (Tunisian, Armenian, or Breton) into English using the
speech translation module of Whisper-Large [33]. The result-
ing English text is then translated to French using M2M-100
(1.2B) and then summarized using mT5-large. For Tunisian,
mT5 is fine-tuned on the English translations of the Tunisian
training split, so the M2M100 module is not used. For Bre-
ton and Armenian, it is fine-tuned on the French translations
of the combined Breton and Armenian training splits, follow-
ing the same mixed-training strategy as SBARThez to ensure
stable training and fair comparison.
• W-L ASR(X)+M2M100(X→en/fr)+mT5-large: In this
pipeline, the audio is first transcribed using Whisper-Large.
The transcription is then translated with M2M-100. For
Tunisian, translation is performed into English, as mT5-large
is fine-tuned on English translations of the Tunisian subset.
For Breton and Armenian, translation is performed into
French for the same reason.

4.3. Results
Results for cross-lingual speech summarization are presented
in Table 4. SBARThez-speech achieves the best performance
across all three low-resource languages, outperforming all base-
lines on both Rouge-L and BertScore. Remarkably, SBARThez-
text, although trained solely on textual data and evaluated on
speech inputs (i.e., across modalities), remains competitive with
the baselines and even surpasses the cascaded approaches for
Breton and Armenian. This highlights the robustness and cross-
modal generalization ability of our method, demonstrating its
capacity to generate summaries from low-resource speech sig-
nals, even when trained exclusively on textual data in a high-
resource language. Importantly, our model remains lightweight:
only 140M parameters are trainable, compared to 1.2B for mT5-
large. At inference time, SBARThez totals 700M parameters,
whereas cascaded systems exceed 2.7B, further highlighting the
efficiency of our approach.
