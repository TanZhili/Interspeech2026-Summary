Abstract
Sleep-disordered breathing, including snoring, hypopnea, and
obstructive apnea, is the hallmark of obstructive sleep apnea,
whose severity is quantified by the Apnea-Hypopnea Index
(AHI). As conventional polysomnography is costly and clin-
ically demanding, microphone-only detection has emerged as
a noninvasive alternative for screening. We propose ALE-
Confidence Fusion Sound Event Detection (ACF-SED), the first
framework that directly injects the per-frame confidence map
of an Adaptive Line Enhancer (ALE) into Transformer atten-
tion biasing and feature modulation, rather than as mere pre-
processing. A Multi-Resolution ALE Bank aggregates three
parallel NLMS filters at distinct decorrelation delays, while a
Confidence-Guided Cross-Path block couples event and noise
streams via gated cross-attention. On the Audio-Polygraphy
Dataset for Sleep Apnea Analysis, ACF-SED achieves state-
of-the-art detection across Event-F1, Segment-F1, and PSDS,
enabling end-to-end AHI estimation.
Index Terms: sound event detection, adaptive line enhancer,
snoring, hypopnea, apnea
1. Introduction
Sleep is an essential physiological process for maintaining both
physical and mental health. Insufficient sleep or poor sleep
quality is closely associated with various health problems, in-
cluding cardiovascular disease, cerebrovascular disease, stroke,
and depression [1, 2]. Among these, obstructive sleep apnea
(OSA) is the most common sleep disorder across all age groups.
According to the World Health Organization (WHO) [3], OSA
is defined as a chronic condition characterized by repeated par-
tial (hypopnea) or complete (apnea) interruptions of breathing
during sleep caused by upper airway obstruction.
The severity of obstructive sleep apnea (OSA) [4] is typi-
cally assessed using the Apnea–Hypopnea Index (AHI), which
represents the number of apnea and hypopnea events per hour
of total sleep time. According to the criteria of the American
Academy of Sleep Medicine (AASM) [5], an AHI of less than
5 is considered normal, 5 to less than 15 indicates mild OSA,
15 to less than 30 indicates moderate OSA, and an AHI of 30
or higher indicates severe OSA. The current gold standard for
diagnosing OSA is polysomnography (PSG), a comprehensive
test conducted in sleep centers in which multiple sensors are
attached to the patient’s body to monitor various physiological
signals overnight. However, PSG has several limitations, in-
cluding high cost, long waiting times, patient discomfort, and

as an initial step in OSA analysis, leading to growing interest
in audio-based approaches that rely on signal processing and
artificial intelligence techniques [10, 11].
The significance of these events extends beyond OSA it-
self. Sleep-disordered breathing is increasingly recognized as a
risk factor and early biomarker for cognitive decline and demen-
tia [12, 13]. This has motivated multimodal sleep analysis that
models audio together with electroencephalography (EEG) and
other signals [14, 15]. More recently, large language models
(LLMs) have fused such heterogeneous streams for neurocog-
nitive screening, including dementia detection [16, 17]. Since
EEG-based pipelines require electrodes and specialized facili-
ties, a robust audio-only detector remains valuable as a low-cost
screening tool and acoustic front-end for such systems.

Fusion-Sound Event Detection), a framework that directly in-
jects the per-frame ALE confidence map derived solely from

2. Related Works
Sound event detection (SED) is a fundamental research area in
audio signal processing that aims to identify the presence and
temporal boundaries of sound events within audio recordings.
With the recent advancements in deep learning, the performance
of SED systems has significantly improved, and models based
on convolutional neural networks (CNN), recurrent neural net-
works (RNN) [19], and Transformers [20, 21, 22] have become
dominant approaches in this field.
2.1. Deep Learning Architectures for Sound Event
Convolutional Recurrent Neural Networks (CRNN) [19] have
emerged as particularly effective architectures for polyphonic
sound event detection. The combination of CNNs for feature
extraction and RNNs for temporal modeling has been shown
to significantly outperform traditional approaches across vari-
ous sound event detection datasets. While the CNN layers ex-
tract high-level features invariant to local spectral and temporal
variations, the RNN layers capture long-term temporal context
within audio signals. This hybrid approach has been widely
adopted in subsequent studies and forms the foundation of many
SED systems.
In addition to CRNNs, transformer-based architectures
have recently gained significant attention in the audio domain.
The Audio Spectrogram Transformer (AST) [20] applies the
Vision Transformer architecture to audio signals by convert-
ing them into spectrogram representations, achieving state-of-
the-art results on multiple audio classification benchmarks such
as AudioSet [23], ESC-50 [24], and Speech Commands V2
[25]. The AST architecture completely removes the reliance
on CNNs, demonstrating that a purely attention-based model
can be sufficient for audio classification tasks. More recently,
efficient transformer variants such as Fast Audio Spectrogram
Transformer (FAST) [21] and Causal Audio Transformer (CAT)
[22] have been proposed, reducing computational complexity
while maintaining competitive performance.
2.2. Audio Feature Representation
The choice of audio feature representation has a significant im-
pact on the performance of SED systems. Mel-spectrograms
and mel-frequency cepstral coefficients (MFCC) are among the
most commonly used features for audio signal analysis and clas-
sification [26]. A mel-spectrogram applies a mel-scale filter
bank to the power spectrogram, providing a time–frequency
representation aligned with human auditory perception.
Recent studies [27, 26] have compared the effectiveness
of various audio features in deep learning-based classification
tasks. Comparative analyses indicate that Mel-spectrogram
representations generally outperform MFCCs when used with
CNN architectures, as they preserve more spatial information in
the time–frequency domain. Some studies have also explored
combining multiple features to leverage their complementary
strengths.
2.3. Supervised and Self-Supervised Learning
One of the main challenges in SED research is the scarcity
of strongly labeled data with precise temporal annotations.
Weakly supervised sound event detection (WSSED), which re-
lies only on clip-level labels without timestamp information,
has emerged as a practical alternative [28, 29]. In WSSED, a
multiple instance learning (MIL) framework [30, 31] is com-
monly employed, where an audio clip is treated as a bag and its
frames as instances. Attention mechanisms and pooling strate-
gies play a crucial role in aggregating frame-level predictions
into clip-level outputs while preserving localization capability.
Self-supervised learning (SSL) approaches have also shown
the potential to learn audio representations from unlabeled data.
These methods leverage pretext tasks to learn general-purpose
features that can be fine-tuned for downstream applications. Re-
cent studies [32, 33] have demonstrated that self-supervised pre-
training on large-scale datasets such as AudioSet can signifi-
cantly improve performance across various audio tasks.
3. Proposed Method
3.1. Learnable Adaptive Line Enhancer (ALE)
An adaptive filter [34] is a system that has a linear filter with a
transfer function controlled by variable parameters and a mech-
anism to adjust those parameters according to an optimization
algorithm [35]. Due to the complexity of the optimization pro-
cess, almost all adaptive filters are implemented as digital fil-
ters. In some applications, adaptive filters are required because
certain parameters of the target processing task (for example,
the positions of reflective surfaces in a reverberant space) are
either unknown in advance or subject to change. A closed-loop
adaptive filter improves its transfer function using feedback in
the form of an error signal [36].
The term “adaptive” refers to the ability to adjust or respond
to changing data or environmental conditions over time. An
adaptive filter, therefore, is one whose filter coefficients or tap
weights change over time according to a specific criterion, de-
pending on the characteristics of the input signal. Unlike con-
ventional filters with fixed coefficients, an adaptive filter opti-
mizes its performance by continuously tuning its parameters to
match environmental or signal variations [37].
An Adaptive Line Enhancer (ALE) is a specific type of
adaptive filter designed to extract narrowband signals buried
in broadband noise. Instead of requiring a separate reference
signal, the ALE uses a delayed version of the input itself as a
reference. By doing so, it enhances periodic or slowly varying
components (such as sinusoids) while suppressing uncorrelated
noise components. This makes ALEs particularly useful in ap-
plications such as speech enhancement, biomedical signal de-
noising, and detection of periodic signals in noisy environments
[38, 39, 40].
The ALE is a specialized form of Adaptive Noise Canceling
(ANC) that operates using only a single microphone. The core
principle of the ALE is to separate the periodic and stochas-
tic components of a signal by employing a delay element. The
ALE can also be implemented in the short-time Fourier trans-
form (STFT) domain, where the adaptive filter offers low com-
putational cost and can be computed separately for each fre-
quency bin [41, 42]. This is illustrated in Figure 1. Specifically,
a delayed version of the input signal is used as a reference. The
periodic components are extracted by the adaptive filter, usually
using a normalized least-mean-square (NLMS) algorithm [43],
operating as follows [42]:

In traditional ALE systems, the learning rate µ is typically
set as a fixed hyperparameter that controls the adaptation speed
of the filter coefficients, requiring careful manual tuning to bal-
ance convergence speed and steady-state error. However, by
makingµ a trainable parameter within a deep learning frame-

optimal adaptation rate for each specific signal condition and
noise environment. This approach transforms the classical ALE
into a differentiable module that can be integrated into end-
to-end neural architectures, where the learning rate parameter
is updated through backpropagation alongside other network
weights. By treating µ as a learnable parameter, the model
can dynamically adjust its adaptation behavior based on the in-
put signal characteristics, potentially learning different learning
rates for different frequency bands or time segments, which is
particularly beneficial for non-stationary signals like speech or
ECG (Electrocardiogram).
3.2. ALE-Confidence Fusion Framework
Having established the trainable ALE module in the preceding
section, we now describe the core novelty of this work: the
ALE-Confidence Fusion Sound Event Detection (ACF-SED)
framework. Existing ALE-based SED pipelines treat the ALE
as a static pre-processing stage only the enhanced mel spectro-
gram E is forwarded to the neural network, while the filter’s
internal state—the per-frame confidence map C[k,m ]—is dis-
carded. ACF-SED recovers this information and injects it di-
rectly into the attention and feature-modulation mechanisms of
a convolutional and Transformer-based backbone, providing a
principled inductive bias without additional labelled data.

3.2.1. Multi-Resolution ALE Bank (MRAB)
The decorrelation delayτ in the NLMS filter determines which
periodic components are suppressed: a filter with delay τ re-
moves signal components whose autocorrelation remains high
at lag τ·thop, where thop = 10 ms is the STFT frame shift.
Different noise sources in sleep audio exhibit distinct periodic-
ity time scales:
• Short-term correlations (τ =1): Rapid oscillatory compo-
nents such as high-frequency harmonic noise from medical
equipment (e.g. ventilators, cardiac monitors) and the upper
harmonics of snoring waveforms maintain strong correlation
at very short delays. A filter with τ =1 is sensitive to these
fast-varying periodic structures and effectively isolates tran-
sient snoring onsets from the background.
• Medium-term correlations (τ =3): The fundamental fre-
quency of snoring typically falls in the range 60-300 Hz.
At τ =3 frames, the filter aligns with the periodicity of
mid-frequency snoring components and captures background
breathing harmonics that persist at this time scale, which a
short-delay filter would miss.
• Long-term correlations (τ =6): Low-frequency noise com-
ponents arising from slow respiratory rhythm and airway
turbulence maintain coherence over longer intervals. A
study [42] demonstrated that non-stationary harmonic noise
in clinical environments contains slowly varying periodici-
ties that require longer decorrelation delays to suppress ef-
fectively. Theτ =6 filter targets these components, providing
a clean residual that more faithfully represents aperiodic ap-
nea events.
No singleτ value can simultaneously suppress noise across
all three time scales. Using only τ =1 leaves medium- and
low-frequency periodic noise in the residual, contaminating the
confidence map with false high-confidence values. Using only
τ =6 fails to capture fast-oscillating snoring components, caus-
ing them to leak into the aperiodic residual and inflating false
apnea detections. MRAB addresses this by running all three fil-
ters in parallel on the shared STFT and aggregating their confi-
dence maps through the Learnable Confidence Pooler, enabling
the model to exploit complementary noise-removal coverage
across temporal scales. A single NLMS filter with fixed decor-
relation delay τ is sensitive only to periodicities at the corre-
sponding time scale. Sleep-disordered breathing events span a
wide range of durations, so a single filter is unlikely to be an
ideal match for all of them. MRAB addresses this by running
three filters in parallel on the same STFT coefficientsY [k,m ]:
