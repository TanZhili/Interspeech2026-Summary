Modeling for Emotion Recognition in Conversation

Abstract
Emotion Recognition in Conversation (ERC) task aims to pre-
dict emotions for each utterance within a dialogue. Despite re-
cent advances, two key challenges remain: (1) effectivemulti-
modal fusion, and (2)modeling emotional dynamics. To tackle
these challenges, we propose the two-stageMultimodalFusion
andEmotionalDynamicsModeling (MF-EDM) framework. In
Stage 1, we introduce an Intra-Utterance Graph in which an
early-fused multimodal anchor node participates in graph-based
message passing, enabling structured cross-modal fusion. In
Stage 2, to address Challenge 2, we introduce two psychologi-
cally motivated GNNs: Emotional Inertia GNN (EIGNN) mod-
elsinertiaas intra-speaker emotional persistence, while Emo-
tional Contagion GNN (ECGNN) capturescontagionas cross-
speaker emotional interaction patterns. MF-EDM achieves
state-of-the-art performance with 74.24% / 74.49% (weighted-
F1 / Accuracy) on IEMOCAP, 67.4% / 68.77% on MELD,
74.48% / 77.24% on K-MIND, and 55.05% / 56.15% on M3ED.
Index Terms: multimodal emotion recognition in conversation,
multimodal fusion, emotional dynamics
1. Introduction
Emotion Recognition in Conversation (ERC) task aims to pre-
dict the emotion of each utterance in dialogue. Recent ERC
studies widely adopt multimodal approaches that jointly model
textual, acoustic, and visual cues within each utterance. How-
ever,multimodal fusionremains challenging due to signifi-
cant semantic discrepancies and inherent information redundan-
cies [1]. Existing multimodal fusion strategies, as shown in
Figure 1—(a)Early, (b)Late, and (c)Graph-based fusion—
present distinct trade-offs.Early fusion[2, 3, 4] concatenates
modalities at the feature level, enabling stable integration but
often blurring modality-specific cues.Late fusion[5] processes
each modality separately and combines modality-specific repre-
sentations at the final fusion step, preserving modality-specific
cues but limiting inter-modal interaction during representation
learning.Graph-based fusion[6, 7, 8, 9] models unimodal fea-
tures as nodes to capture inter-modal dependencies; however,
most approaches rely on uniform message passing without a
structural anchor for multimodal aggregation, which may lead
to suboptimal modality alignment [10]. Motivated by this lim-
itation, we introduce the Intra-Utterance Graph (Figure 1(d)),
which places an early-fused multimodal anchor node within the
graph to guide structured multimodal aggregation.
Modeling emotional dynamicspresents another challenge
in ERC, as emotions evolve across speakers over time. Psy-
chological studies characterize this evolution through two core

andEmotionalDynamicsModeling (MF-EDM), a two-stage
graph-based framework. Stage 1 tacklesmultimodal fusionby
constructing an Intra-Utterance Graph with an early-fused mul-

the Emotional Inertia GNN (EIGNN), which models intra-
speaker emotional persistence, and the Emotional Contagion
GNN (ECGNN), which captures cross-speaker emotional in-
teraction patterns. We evaluate MF-EDM on four benchmark
datasets—IEMOCAP [15], MELD [16], K-MIND [17], and
M3ED [18]—using weighted F1 (w-F1) and Accuracy (Acc).

2.2. Stage 2: Emotional Dynamics Modeling
To capture temporal emotional dynamics, we introduce two
Graph Neural Network (GNN) modules inspired by psycho-
logical studies: (i) the Emotional Inertia GNN (EIGNN),
which models intra-speaker emotional persistence, and (ii) the
Emotional Contagion GNN (ECGNN), which captures cross-
speaker emotional interaction patterns. Given a dialogue with
Nutterances, the constructed graph consists of4Nnodes,
where each utterance is represented by four modality-specific
nodeshx

speaker emotional persistence, we extend the Intra-Utterance

intra-speaker temporal edges to form the EIGNN. These tem-

andF I (future) window centered at the current indexk. The
edges are directed, enabling asymmetric message passing that
reflects intra-speaker emotional persistence. Given the node

2.3. Differences from Graph-based Baselines
Compared with representative graph-based multimodal ERC
baselines, including MMGCN [19], MM-DFN [1], M 3Net [7],
and GraphSmile [29], MF-EDM differs at both stages. In
Stage 1, prior methods mainly reason over unimodal rep-
resentations or modality-pair relations, and fuse them after
graph propagation. Our Intra-Utterance Graph instead places
an early-fused multimodal anchor node inside message pass-
ing for in-graph multimodal aggregation. InStage 2, prior
baselines model dialogue context without explicitly decompos-
ing speaker-related emotional dynamics. MF-EDM separates
them into EIGNN for intra-speaker emotional persistence and
ECGNN for cross-speaker emotional interaction patterns.
3. Experiments
Datasets.We evaluate our model on four multimodal con-
versational emotion recognition benchmark datasets: IEMO-
CAP [15], MELD [16], K-MIND [17], and M3ED [18]. IEMO-
CAP contains 151 dyadic conversations (7.4K utterances) la-
beled with six emotions:happy,sad,neutral,angry,excited,
andfrustrated. MELD consists of 1,433 multi-party conver-
sations (13.7K utterances) annotated withanger,disgust,sad-
ness,joy,surprise,fear, andneutral. K-MIND includes 816
Korean two-speaker conversations (70K utterances) and M3ED
comprises 990 Mandarin conversations (24.4K utterances) fol-
lowing MELD’s label set. We exclude CMU-MOSEI [30] be-
cause it contains monologue-style utterances rather than inter-
active dialogues. Test-set metadata and Inertia/Contagion sub-
set counts are summarized in Table 1.
Experimental Settings.We evaluate all models using weighted
F1 (w-F1) and Accuracy (Acc). All experiments are imple-
mented in PyTorch and conducted on a single NVIDIA RTX
4090 GPU. As shown in Table 2, the hyperparameter settings
are tuned separately for each dataset.

To validate MF-EDM, we conduct three types of exper-
iments. First, to evaluate overall performance and multilin-
gual generalizability, we compare MF-EDM with representa-
tive graph-based multimodal ERC models. Unlike prior work
mainly evaluated on IEMOCAP and MELD, we additionally
include K-MIND and M 3ED to assess multilingual robust-
ness. Second, to validate the necessity of the Intra-Utterance
Graph in Stage 1, we replace it with three fusion paradigms
—Early[2, 3, 4],Late[5], andGraph-based fusion[19, 6, 7, 8,
9]—while keeping other components unchanged. These vari-
ants are denoted as MF-EDM Early Fusion, MF-EDMLate Fusion, and
MF-EDMGraph-based Fusion. Finally, to evaluate the contribution
of emotional dynamics modeling in Stage 2, we perform ab-
lation studies by removing EIGNN and ECGNN individually.
Furthermore, we analyze model performance by grouping ut-
terances intoInertiaandContagionsubsets 1 [31, 32], enabling
fine-grained evaluation of intra-speaker emotional persistence
and cross-speaker emotional interaction patterns.

Exploring Emotional Dynamics Modeling in Stage 2.
Table 4 reports the Stage 2 ablation results, where we com-
pare the complete MF-EDM with variants that remove either
EIGNN or ECGNN. On the full dataset (Full), removing either
module lowers ERC performance, indicating that intra-speaker
persistence and cross-speaker interaction cues provide comple-
mentary information for emotion prediction.
The subset analysis reveals a clear division of roles be-
tween EIGNN and ECGNN. On theInertiasubset, EIGNN only
(w/o ECGNN) consistently outperforms ECGNN only, indicat-
ing that same-speaker temporal modeling is particularly impor-
tant when the target emotion preserves the speaker’s preced-
ing state. In contrast, on theContagionsubset, ECGNN only
consistently outperforms EIGNN only, indicating that cross-
speaker temporal modeling is more effective when the target
emotion shifts from that preceding state. In severalInertiasub-
sets, EIGNN only also slightly exceeds the full model, sug-
gesting that cross-speaker message passing can sometimes di-
lute speaker-specific continuity signals. The strength of this ef-
fect may depend on dataset composition, as the relative pro-
portions ofInertiaandContagioncases differ across bench-
marks (Table 1). Nevertheless, real dialogues involve both
continuity-preserving and transition-related emotional dynam-
ics. MF-EDM therefore adopts a joint EIGNN–ECGNN design,
enabling the model to capture both same-speaker emotional per-
sistence and cross-speaker interaction cues.
5. Conclusion
We proposedMultimodalFusion andEmotionalDynamics
Modeling (MF-EDM), a two-stage graph-based framework for
multimodal Emotion Recognition in Conversation (ERC). MF-
EDM addresses two central challenges in ERC: effectivemul-
timodal fusionandemotional dynamics modeling. In Stage 1,
we tacklemultimodal fusionby constructing an Intra-Utterance
Graph with an early-fused multimodal anchor node, enabling
structured in-graph multimodal aggregation. In Stage 2, we
modelemotional dynamicsthrough two psychologically mo-
tivated GNNs: EIGNN for intra-speaker emotional persis-
tence and ECGNN for cross-speaker emotional interaction
patterns. Experiments show that MF-EDM achieves state-
of-the-art performance across four multilingual ERC bench-
marks—IEMOCAP, MELD, K-MIND, and M 3ED. Stage 1
comparisons across multimodal fusion strategies support the
benefit of the multimodal anchor node overEarly,Late, and
conventionalGraph-basedfusion variants. Stage 2 ablation
analyses further reveal the complementary roles of EIGNN and
ECGNN in modeling inertia- and contagion-related emotional
dynamics. These results demonstrate the effectiveness of the
proposed two-stage framework.
While MF-EDM uses speaker labels for graph construc-
tion, for future work, utilizing automatic speaker attribution
can further improve robustness when speaker information is
noisy or unavailable. In addition, the Stage 2 ablations sug-
gest possibilities for adaptive pathway gating as a promising
extension for balancing EIGNN and ECGNN under different
inertia–contagion distributions.
