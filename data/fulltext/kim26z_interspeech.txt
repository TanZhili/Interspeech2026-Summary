Abstract
Current Large Audio Language Models (LALMs) can describe
the sounds present in audio but cannot localize their occur-
rence—a critical gap for applications. Prior efforts to add tem-
poral reasoning either rely solely on coarse multi-choice dis-
tinctions or depend on LLM-inferred timestamps whose accu-
racy is unverifiable. We address this with a time-aware audio
instruction-tuning dataset, AudioGround-IT, that provides de-
terministic boundary supervision, yielding 49.9K instructions
over 835 hours of audio across four temporal tasks. To lever-
age this supervision for temporal grounding in LALMs, we pro-
pose AudioGround, a lightweight extension of SALMONN that
uses a sliding-window Q-Former to compress encoder features
and receives timestamp conditioning and absolute time em-
beddings. Evaluations on multiple temporal grounding bench-
marks show that AudioGround substantially outperforms prior
LALMs, demonstrating that deterministic boundary supervision
transfers effectively to real-world audio.
Index Terms: temporal audio grounding, audio moment re-
trieval, large audio language models, boundary supervision
1. Introduction
Large Audio Language Models (LALMs) have demonstrated
remarkable capabilities across a range of audio understanding
tasks, from captioning and classification to open-ended ques-
tion answering [1, 2, 3, 4, 5]. Yet in many real-world scenarios,
understanding which sounds are present is only half the prob-
lem; knowing when they occur is equally critical [6]. A secu-
rity system must pinpoint the exact moment a window shatters
within hours of surveillance audio; a meeting assistant must
locate when a decision was made in a lengthy recording. De-
spite its practical importance, current LALMs struggle with this
fine-grained temporal grounding. They can describe an acoustic
scene holistically, but when asked, “When does the dog bark?”,
they either respond vaguely or provide inaccurate time bound-
aries.
Prior work has addressed temporal localization in audio
through various angles. A Sound Event Detection (SED) sys-
tem can localize events, but it assumes a fixed closed vocab-
ulary [7, 8]. Audio Moment Retrieval (AMR) extends this to
natural language queries over minute-long audio [9], yet it is
typically implemented as a task-specific pipeline rather than
as a capability embedded in general-purpose LALMs. A re-
cent line of work has begun injecting temporal reasoning into
LALM training, but the supervision remains insufficient for
fine-grained grounding. Existing approaches either cast tem-

to verify [10]. Even when architectures explicitly target fine-
grained grounding [11], the training signal remains limited, in-

In this paper, we aim to equip LALMs with fine-
grained temporal grounding capabilities by leveraging dedi-
cated instruction-tuning data with deterministic labels. We con-
struct AudioGround-IT, a time-aware audio instruction tuning
(IT) dataset, whose core principle is boundary supervision:
given a natural-language query and an audio clip, the model
must output exact start and end timestamps. The dataset is con-
structed using a controlled synthesis pipeline that concatenates
AudioCaps [14] clips to generate longer audio with determin-
istic event boundaries, ensuring ground truth accuracy without
relying on LLM-generated captions or timestamps. Beyond the
primary grounding task, we include three tasks — duration es-
timation, event ordering, and frequency counting — that re-
quire the model to use the same boundary information under
varying reasoning demands, thereby reducing the risk of learn-
ing shortcuts tied to any single question format. To handle the
resulting audio inputs effectively, we introduce a lightweight
architectural modification to SALMONN [15] that extends Q-
Former [16] with sliding-window compression and absolute
time embedding, thereby preserving temporal resolution.

2. Audio Temporal Grounding
Temporal grounding aims to localize the time interval rele-
vant to a natural-language query within an untrimmed record-
ing [19]. In the video domain, this task has been extensively
studied as moment retrieval [20, 21], and recent Video LLMs
incorporate timestamp tokens and time-aware instruction tuning
to improve boundary prediction [22, 23, 24]. In audio, temporal
localization has primarily been addressed through sound event
detection [7, 8, 25], which predicts boundaries for a fixed vocab-
ulary of event classes. Audio moment retrieval (AMR) [9, 18]
extends to open-vocabulary queries, but these methods remain
task-specific pipelines separate from general-purpose LALMs.
Recent works have begun incorporating temporal understand-
ing into LALM training [4, 10]. However, neither provides the
direct boundary supervision needed for fine-grained temporal
grounding.
3. AudioGround-IT
We construct our dataset with a controlled synthesis pipeline
to equip LALMs with fine-grained temporal grounding capa-
bilities. Table 1 summarizes the key differences from existing
audio temporal datasets.
3.1. Data Overview
Our key principle is that, regardless of the question type, the
model must answer with explicit start-end timestamps[t s,t e]
or a set of such intervals. By repeatedly learning to localize ev-
idence segments in the audio, the model is encouraged to in-
ternalize “what happens when” reasoning rather than producing
ungrounded free-form text. The dataset comprises four temporal
tasks:Grounding(localizing a queried sound event),Duration
(estimating its temporal span),Frequency(counting occur-
rences), andOrdering(inferring precedence by grounding both
events). The resulting dataset comprises 49.9K instruction-
tuning samples totaling over 835 hours of audio. Detailed per-
task statistics are summarized in Table 2. Despite differing rea-
soning requirements, all four share a unified output: the tem-
poral interval(s)[t s,t e]that support the answer. This consistent
supervision target provides steady training pressure toward tem-
poral localization across diverse temporal phenomena.

auxiliary tasks require the model to reason about the same

3.2. Data Construction
Source and query design.We build the dataset on the Audio-
Caps [14] training split, using its human-written captions di-
rectly as natural language queries.
Clip composition.Each AudioCaps sample (∼10 s) serves as
an atomic building clip. We concatenate multiple clips with
short silence gaps (0.3-0.5 s) to construct audio samples rang-
ing from 20 to 120 seconds. Since we control the concatenation
order, every event boundary is deterministically known from the
construction process, requiring no post-hoc annotation.
A key concern in composition is semantic ambiguity: if
two co-occurring clips describe similar events, the model can-
not learn to distinguish them as separate targets. Therefore, we
compute sentence embeddings for all captions using Sentence-
BERT [26], and apply a cosine similarity thresholdτto govern
clip pairing. Filler clips within the sample must satisfy pairwise
similarity belowτ, ensuring that non-target events remain easily
distinguishable. Conversely, when constructing a longer target
span from multiple clips, we select clips with similarity above
τso that the composed segment is semantically coherent. We
setτ= 0.33, corresponding to approximately one standard de-
viation above the mean of the pairwise similarity distribution
(µ= 0.21,σ= 0.12).
Bias Control.Temporal grounding models are sensitive to dis-
tributional biases in training data [27, 28]. We control two
sources of bias at the dataset level. For temporal position, we
divide the normalized answer onset into five equal bins and bal-
ance sample counts across them, preventing the model from de-
faulting to early or late segments. For length, we similarly strat-
ify total audio lengths and answer span durations into prede-
fined bins and sample them approximately uniformly to reduce
overfitting to a narrow duration range. As shown in Figure 2
(left), this yields an almost uniform distribution of total audio
lengths across four bins, except the last one.

4.1. Frame-level Interpolation
AudioGround employs a dual-encoder setup consisting of a
Whisper speech encoder and a BEATs audio encoder, both
operating at 50Hz. For long audio, inputs are split into non-
overlapping 30-second chunks, encoded independently, and
concatenated along the temporal axis, yielding

4.2. Time-aware Q-Former
As the concatenated encoder representationZgrows linearly
with audio length and is computationally expensive for direct
LLM consumption, we adopt a sliding-window Q-Former for
local temporal compression and temporally aware window-level
representations.

This string is tokenized and provided to the Q-Former as ad-
ditional text input alongside the learnable query tokens, follow-
ing the approach of TimeChat [23]. As a result, the Q-Former
produces time-aware query representations at the window level.

5.2. Datasets
We evaluate on three benchmarks from prior work [9], Clotho-
Moment, UnA V-100 subset, and TUT-Sound Events 2017.
Since most LALMs can process only up to 30 seconds, we con-
struct the clipped setting by extracting a 30-second segment that
contains the ground-truth interval, enabling a fairer zero-shot
evaluation. To avoid additional temporal biases, we clip each
recording to 30 seconds by preserving the ratio of the answer
position to the total audio length, so that the clipping process
does not shift the answer toward a particular temporal region.
5.3. Evaluation
We adopt Recall1 (R1) as the primary metric, measuring
whether the Intersection over Union (IoU) between the top-
predicted segment and the ground truth exceeds a threshold
θ∈{0.5,0.7}[9, 21]. Since LALMs generate free-form text
rather than structured tuples, we apply heuristic parsing rules to
extract predicted timestamps from the model’s responses [23].
Outputs may contain varied phrasings, units, or extraneous text;
our parser handles these variations to ensure consistent metric
computation across all models.
5.4. Overall Result
Table 3 shows that AudioGround consistently achieves the best
zero-shot moment retrieval among LALMs across all three
benchmarks in the original setting. Existing LALMs largely fail
at this task; for example, SALMONN fails to follow the ground-
ing instruction, resulting in near-zero retrieval performance and
indicating a lack of temporal grounding ability. The clipping
setting can boost some baselines by shortening the temporal
search space, but the effect is consistent across models, sug-
gesting limited robustness. While AM-DETR remains a strong
upper bound, AudioGround substantially narrows the gap in a
zero-shot setting.

tamps with an MLP treats time more discretely, making it less
effective at capturing continuous temporal relationships.
6. Conclusion
We present AudioGround-IT, a time-aware instruction-tuning
dataset with deterministic boundary supervision across four
temporal tasks, and AudioGround, a lightweight temporal ex-
tension of SALMONN that combines timestamp conditioning
with absolute time embedding. With only 49.9K samples, Au-
dioGround substantially outperforms all prior LALMs on zero-
shot moment retrieval across three benchmarks, demonstrating
that deterministic segment supervision is the key bottleneck for
temporal grounding in LALMs.
