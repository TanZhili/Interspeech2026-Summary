Can Speech LLMs Approximate Human Ratings of Accentedness and
Comprehensibility? Evidence from Correlational and Feature-Based Analyses
Wenwei Dong ID 1, Catia Cucchiarini ID 1, Roeland van Hout1, Helmer Strik ID 1,2
1 Centre for Language and Speech Technology, Radboud University, The Netherlands
2 Donders Institute for Brain, Cognition and Behaviour, Radboud University, The Netherlands

Abstract
Accentedness and comprehensibility scales are widely used to
evaluate pronunciation development in second language learn-
ers. However, such assessments rely heavily on human rater
evaluations. This study investigates whether a speech large lan-
guage model (LLM) can approximate human judgments of ac-
centedness and comprehensibility. We first compare correla-
tions between LLM scores and human ratings. We then apply
linear mixed-effects models to examine whether LLM scores
capture learner progress across pre- and post-test conditions.
Finally, by combining segmental and suprasegmental measures
with Lasso regression, we analyze whether the LLM employs
acoustic cues similar to those human raters rely on when as-
signing scores. The results show that the LLM scores are mod-
erately correlated with human ratings, capture pre–post test
progress, and exhibit overlapping features with human evalu-
ations. However, future research could explore fine-tuning the
LLM and incorporating linguistic knowledge.
Index Terms: L2 English, speech LLM, accentedness, compre-
hensibility
1. Introduction
With the popularity of international trade and travel, language
learning is becoming increasingly important. In response to this
demand, computer-assisted language learning (CALL) tools
have overcome the spatial and temporal constraints of tradi-
tional classroom instruction, making language learning more
convenient and flexible. At the same time, the goals of lan-
guage learning have shifted. Since most conversations take
place among non-native speakers, achieving native-level lan-
guage proficiency is arguably unnecessary, and more realistic
goals have been suggested, like improving comprehensibility
and reducing accentedness. Comprehensibility is defined as the
listener’s perception of how easily they understand second lan-
guage (L2) speech utterances [1], and accentedness is defined
as the listener’s perception of the strength of the L2 speaker’s
foreign accent [1]. These scales have been applied to many lan-
guages, like French, German, and Spanish [2, 3, 4]. However,
evaluating accentedness and comprehensibility in L2 speech
typically relies on expert human raters, which is costly and
time-consuming [5].
To address these challenges, automatic pronunciation as-
sessment technology has been suggested as a potential solution.
Such systems typically employ acoustic features (e.g., prosodic
and fluency measures) [6, 7, 8] and automatic speech recogni-
tion (ASR)-based metrics [9, 10]. While these approaches have
demonstrated moderate success, they usually assess pronuncia-
tion through predefined dimensions (accuracy, fluency, prosody,
etc.), which cannot fully reflect human perceptual judgments
on comprehensibility and accentedness. Recent advances in
speech large language models (LLMs) offer a potential alter-
native. A growing body of research has shown that speech
LLMs can serve as effective tools for assessing L2 pronunci-
ation [11, 12]: they can take both prompts and speech files
as input and provide pronunciation evaluation and feedback
to teachers and learners. Trained on large-scale speech data,
speech LLMs encode rich representations of segmental accu-
racy, prosody, fluency, and timing directly from raw speech. As
such, they may be capable of approximating human judgments
of accentedness and comprehensibility without extensive fea-
ture engineering. However, empirical evidence regarding their
alignment with human ratings remains limited. Furthermore, it
is unclear whether speech LLM scores are sensitive to learner
language development over time.
Several studies have focused on gaining a better under-
standing of the features on which human judgments of these two
concepts are based. Pronunciation errors at the segmental level
have been found to have a negative influence on comprehensi-
bility [13]. In research on the suprasegmental level, several fea-
tures have been found to have correlations with comprehensi-
bility and accentedness, such as speech rate [14] and pitch [15].
A study by [16] combined segmental and suprasegmental level
measures and features, and applied a data-driven method. The
results showed that suprasegmental level features have a larger
influence on human judgments of accentedness, while segmen-
tal level features have a larger influence on human judgments
of comprehensibility. An important follow-up question at this
point is whether speech LLMs employ the same segmental and
suprasegmental cues as those human raters rely on when they
assign accentedness and comprehensibility scores. This ques-
tion is addressed in the present study. In particular, we inves-
tigate the possibility of using a speech LLM to automatically
assess the accentedness and comprehensibility of L2 English
learners who practiced with a CALL system. We address the
following research questions:
1. To what extent can speech LLMs be used to evaluate accent-
edness and comprehensibility in L2 English learners?
2. Do speech LLMs employ similar segmental and supraseg-
mental cues as those human raters rely on when they assign
accentedness and comprehensibility scores?
In order to answer these two research questions, we ap-
plied the speech LLM ”Qwen3-Omni-30B-Instruct” [17] and
experimented with different conditions. This paper makes three
important novel contributions: a) we systematically evaluate a
speech LLM for automatic accentedness and comprehensibility
assessment under zero-shot and few-shot prompting, b) we ex-
amine whether LLM-generated scores capture learner progress
using linear mixed-effects models in comparison with human

2. Methodology
2.1. Data collection and annotation
In total, 65 English as a foreign language (EFL) students from
a high school in Indonesia participated in this study. All the
students were 10th graders, aged 14 to 17, and had been learn-
ing English for 5 to 10 years. The data collection was carried
out in three steps: recording speech files (pre-test); collecting
practice data (ASR-based CALL system); and recording speech
files (post-test). For step 2, we developed an ASR-based CALL
system. Students were asked to read the texts displayed on their
computers or smartphones, which were then passed to the ASR
engine for evaluation. The practice lasted 36 days in a free for-
mat. The project contained 7 units, with 3 lessons each, which
always consisted of a conversation, a word list, and a sentence
list. In total, the whole project contained 308 unique texts. In
this study, we selected 33 participants who had demonstrated
having completed all practice procedures. As a result, there
were 28 sentences in the pre- and post-test and the total number
of utterances used in the current study is 1848: 33 learners x 28
sentences x 2 (pre- and post-test).
Nine native Indonesian experts rated the speech files,
providing judgments on comprehensibility and accentedness.
Raters with proficiency levels ranging from B2 to C1 accord-
ing to the Common European Framework of Reference for
Languages (CEFR) were selected for this task. They were
asked to complete the rating procedure within three weeks and
were unaware of the study’s pre–post design. As suggested by
[1, 18], we used a nine-point rating scale for both accentedness
and comprehensibility. Inspired by [19]’s study, we also re-
versed the rating scales so that higher ratings are associated with
speech samples that sound “less foreign”: this entails sound-
ing more native-like (accentedness) and being easier to under-
stand (comprehensibility). The intraclass correlation coefficient
(ICC) was used to investigate the reliability of the scores, which
we found to be highly reliable (accent, pre = .989, post = .987;
comprehensibility, pre = .980, post = .976). In the following
experiments, we used the mean of the nine expert scores as the
final score for each utterance. Among these 33 learners, we se-
lected the 2 learners with the lowest and highest average accent-
edness and comprehensibility scores as the sample set and used
the remaining 31 learners as the test set for the speech LLM.
2.2. Speech large language model
In our experiments with the speech LLM, we used ”Qwen3-
Omni-30B-Instruct” [17], which can accept speech files, im-
ages, videos, and prompts as inputs. The model can generate
both text and speech outputs. It is an open-source LLM that
demonstrates high performance in speech-related tasks, such as
ASR and audio reasoning. In our experiments, we used only
speech files and prompts as input, and text as output. In the
prompts, we provided definitions of accentedness and compre-
hensibility and asked the speech LLM to assign a score based on
its judgment of the speech file. We conducted two experimental
conditions: zero-shot and few-shot prompting.
1.Zero-shot prompting experiments:
(a) Providing definitions, along with speech files in the
prompt, and requesting both scores together.
(b) Providing definitions and speech files separately in the
prompt, and requesting scores separately.
(c) Providing definitions, speech files, and reading texts sepa-
rately in the prompt, and requesting scores separately.
2.Few-shot prompting experiments:
(a) Providing definitions, along with multiple example speech
files and corresponding human scores in the prompt, and
requesting scores separately.
In the few-shot prompting experiments, we provided exam-
ple speech files with high, intermediate, and low human ratings
and examined how the number of examples and their types in-
fluenced the speech LLM’s performance. Model performance
was evaluated on the utterance level using Spearman’s rank cor-
relation coefficient (SPCC) between human and LLM scores, as
well as Mean Squared Error (MSE).
2.3. Segmental and suprasegmental features and scores
In our study, we extracted features and scores at the utterance
level from both the segmental and suprasegmental aspects us-
ing Praat [20], the openSMILE toolkit, and an ASR model.
First, we used a Praat script to compute 16 features: duration
of utterance, gravity center, the first three formants, fundamen-
tal frequency (F0), the mean, minimum, maximum, and stan-
dard deviation values for pitch and intensity, and speech rate
(words per second). Next, we used the extended Geneva Mini-
malistic Acoustic Parameter Set (eGeMAPS) [21], which is in-
cluded in the openSMILE package. This set was initially de-
signed for emotion recognition tasks and has been widely used
in suprasegmental tasks [22, 23]. It contains 88 acoustic fea-
tures, including frequency-related features, like formants, band-
width, and loudness. In addition, eGeMAPS includes spectrum-
related (MFCC, etc.) and temporal descriptors (the number of
loudness peaks per second, etc.) of human speech sounds.
The ASR model in this study was the Whisper model
[24], which is an open-source model and is designed based
on the Transformer model structure, an encoder-decoder model
for sequence-to-sequence tasks. The Whisper model has been
trained on multitask training data such as multilingual speech
recognition and speech translation, and has achieved competi-
tive performance on several speech tasks [25, 26, 27]. After ob-
taining recognition text from the Whisper model, we compared
it with the reading text to determine word distance, including in-
sertions, deletions, and substitutions. The word distance (Whis-
per Word Distance) was calculated as follows:
W ord Distance=insertions+deletions+substitutions
A total of 105 features and scores were extracted for each
utterance, including 16 (Praat), 88 (eGeMAPS), and 1 ASR
score.
2.4. Statistical analysis
In our statistical analysis, we applied linear mixed-effects mod-
els and segmental–suprasegmental analyses to examine whether
speech LLM scores capture learners’ progress in accentedness
and comprehensibility and whether they employ similar cues
when assigning these scores.

2.4.2. Segmental and suprasegmental analysis
In the study by [16], segmental and suprasegmental scores and
features were combined, and the results showed that the seg-
mental level (pronunciation errors) has a greater impact on com-
prehensibility scores, while the suprasegmental level (loudness-
, intensity-related features) has a greater impact on the accent-
edness scores. In the present study, we investigate whether
the speech LLM can accurately capture the constructs of ac-
centedness and comprehensibility and assign scores based on
appropriate segmental and suprasegmental cues, as human ex-
perts do. We first combined features and scores from segmen-
tal and suprasegmental levels, and applied Lasso regression to
reduce the number of predictors and identify the most impor-
tant features. We then ranked these features based on the abso-
lute SPCC between the features and the accentedness and com-
prehensibility scores. The SPCC indicates the strength of the
correlation between features and scores. Finally, we compared
the similarities and differences in feature rankings between the
evaluations by the human raters and the speech LLM.

4. Discussion and Conclusions
In this paper, we studied how well speech LLM scores on ac-
centedness and comprehensibility align with human ratings. We
also conducted segmental-suprasegmental analyses to investi-
gate whether human raters and the speech LLM employed sim-
ilar cues when assigning these scores.
We first experimented with zero-shot and few-shot prompt-
ing conditions with the speech LLM (Table 1). From our zero-
shot experiments, when provided with only the definitions of
accentedness and comprehensibility in the prompt, the speech
LLM demonstrated a moderate positive correlation with human
ratings. We further provided reading text, and both SPCCs from
these two measures decreased; we assume that, after provid-
ing reading text, the speech LLM places too much emphasis on
pronunciation errors, whereas these two measures can be influ-
enced by both segmental and suprasegmental cues [16]. In our
few-shot prompting experiments, we tested with sample sizes
ranging from 1 to 5 and with different sample types (low, in-
termediate, and high scores). The best SPCCs were obtained
when we provided 2 to 3 of low- and high-score samples. The
best MSE was obtained by providing 2 of low-, intermediate-
, and high-score samples (Exp. 6), indicating that the speech
LLM can adapt its scoring from a few human-score examples;
too many samples are apparently confusing.
We then conducted linear mixed-effects models and
segmental-suprasegmental analyses (Table 2). Both accent-
edness and comprehensibility scores from the speech LLM
showed significant differences between the pre- and post-tests,
consistent with results from human raters. Moreover, the post-
test scores were significantly higher than the pre-test scores,
indicating that the speech LLM can capture the pronunciation
progress made by CALL system users. We further calculated
sentence level (N=28) scores by averaging ratings across speak-
ers, and speaker level (N=31) scores by averaging ratings across
sentences. The SPCCs were much higher at the sentence level
