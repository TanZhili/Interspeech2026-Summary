Daily Affect Inference from Longitudinal Speech-based Journals:
A Comparison of Acoustic and Linguistic Models

Abstract
Speech-based journaling provides both acoustic cues and se-
mantically rich content, offering strong potential for mental
health monitoring. But how well do current models infer in-
dicators such as mood and stress from naturalistic data? We
introduce a longitudinal corpus of 61 speakers and 769 daily
recordings, labeled with the Multidimensional Mood Question-
naire and Perceived Stress Scale, to compare eGeMAPS and
wav2vec2 models with GBERT-large and zero-shot Mistral-
7B-Instruct. We reveal that linguistic models clearly domi-
nate at the population-level: GBERT reaches .122 concordance
for arousal, and Mistral .466 for valence and .360 for stress,
whereas acoustic results are near zero. Mistral, however, is
geared towards inferring trait-like differences rather than daily
speaker variations. The acoustics indicate a relation between
pause variations and valence at the speaker-level. We conclude
that our models are limited in their ability to monitor individuals
and require personalization.
Index Terms: affective computing, speech emotion recogni-
tion, natural language processing, mental health monitoring,
mood prediction, stress prediction, large language models
1. Introduction
Mental disorders are increasingly conceptualized as dynamic
states rather than static traits [1], partially because there are
many biases in classic psychometric approaches, failing to cap-
ture human behavior [2, 3]. This means that the disorders are
understood to manifest in variable, context-dependent constel-
lations of symptoms that vary between individuals and within
the same individual over time [3, 4]. According to this view,
these symptoms can be considered to constitute opposed to re-
flecting the disorder [5]. Tracking daily changes in mental
health indicators such as mood and stress is therefore essen-
tial for understanding individual mental health trajectories and
enabling timely intervention [6, 7].
In clinical science, this ‘state-oriented’ view of mental dis-
orders has led to the proliferation of digital phenotyping, which
applies ecological momentary assessment (EMA) tools to cap-
ture individual’s daily life experience [8]. Usually, these mea-
surements are comprised of self-reported questionnaires [9, 10].
However, these questionnaires neglect a fundamental mode of
human self-expression – natural (spoken) language. What peo-
ple say in response to a particular question, andhow they say it,
carries important information about their mental health status
in the moment they speak and reveals their attitude or mood to-
wards the topic they are discussing [11, 12, 13]. Consequently,
speech-based journaling – a natural expression of experiences
and feelings – is an ecologically-valid and highly-informative
tool for tracking mental health symptoms in daily life.
Recent work has begun to explore this connection, mark-
ing a transition from the use of speech analysis to character-
ize individuals as (not) having a particular mental disorder [13,
14] and towards its use for identifying states given longitudinal
samples [15, 16, 17, 18, 19]. These works differentiate them-
selves across two axes: the protocol used to elicit speech and
the measurement of mental health status; both have important
ramifications for the clinical utility of the resulting tools.
With respect to the elicitation protocol, some works opted
for voice-based analysis relying on designated, semantically-
neutral content, such as sustained vowels, repeated syllables, or
read speech [16, 18]. This choice is underpinned by the assump-
tion that mental health states are primarily reflected in the par-
alinguistic information stream. In contrast, other works chose
(prompted) free speech, asking participants to respond to one or
more open-ended questions, such as “ How are you feeling to-
day?” [15, 17]. These works allow joint semantic and acoustic
modeling and assume that the juxtaposition of both is predictive
of underlying mental health states.
In addition, previous work used different psychometric
measures of mental health. While all relied on psychometrically
validated scales, these scales vary substantially with respect to
their temporal range. Some works chose measures that require
participants to reflect on their mood and behavior over the last
1-2 weeks [15, 18] while other works adopted a more localized
view of mental health states by asking participants to rate their
mood in the present moment [16, 17].
Ultimately, speech-based journaling applications aim to
complement or even substitute questionnaire-based EMA by
achieving similar validity. Unfortunately, previous works fall
short of this goal, as they report moderate ( r < .5) or even
zero correlations between speech and EMA measures, regard-
less of whether they relied on traditional statistical analysis [15,
18] or modern-day deep learning (DL) models [16, 17]. Cru-
cially, all works obtained their highest performance through
personalization, be it through an investigation of intra-speaker
variability [15, 18] or DL-based adaptation to speakers [16,
17], demonstrating that the prediction of self-reported scores
through global modeling remains challenging [20, 21].
Our work extends prior research as follows: First, given the
lack of comparable datasets, we collected a longitudinal dataset
of free daily speech recordings reflecting on the day, paired with
EMA ratings of arousal, valence, calmness, and stress. This

2. Methodology
Study Protocol: We collected data in a 2-week observational
study of university students in Germany. Participants were re-
quired to be (i) older than 18, (ii) university students, (iii) fluent
in German, and (iv) have a smartphone. Participants were ran-
domly assigned to either group typing or speaking their jour-
nal entries, with the goal of comparing the affective expressivity
in these two modalities in a future analysis. Based on the as-
signment, the daily measurements included a speech-based or a
text journal entry answering the question “What were your high-
lights and lowlights of this day? ”, as well as responses to the
Multidimensional Mood State Questionnaire (MDBF) [24]
– capturing valence (MDBF V), arousal (MDBF A), and calm-
ness (MDBFC) – and the EMA-version of the Perceived Stress
Scale 4 (PSS-4) [25] to assess stress levels. Both daily question-
naires were formulated to reflect the day, equivalent to the jour-
nal entry, and not report the current mood. Assessments were
completed daily after 6pm. After the 2-week data collection
phase, the participants received individual summaries of their
data. The study received an ethics approval, was registered at
the German register for clinical studies (DRKS00034660), and
a study protocol is currently under review. In this analysis, we
only consider the data from the speaking group.
Dataset and Preprocessing: The dataset contains 61
speakers after excluding 7 participants given (1) the adherence
was less than 50% or (2) no transcripts could be created. The
demographics are as follows: 90.2% female, 8.2% male, and
1.6% other, and the mean (SD) age being 23.0 (3.2) years. The
dataset comprises 769 audio samples, and associated PSS-4 and
MDBF scores. As the targets stem from different scales, all
were rescaled to [0,1] for the entire analysis. Figure 1 shows (a)
the distribution of recording durations, and (b) the distributions
of the targets across all participants. Given the low standard
deviation of MDBFC within and between (both< .010), this di-
mension is excluded from the analysis. For MDBF V, MDBFA,
and PSS-4, respectively, the mean (SD) within-person standard
deviation is .180 (.063), .203 (.057), .164 (.037).
Raw audio waveforms were resampled to 16 kHz. To
achieve better transcription performance using Whisper Large-
v3 [26] and for phrase-level features, we split the samples into
phrases using energy-based audio segmentation; segmenting at
pauses of at least 1 second, where silence was operationalized
as segments with an intensity 14 dB below the file’s mean loud-
ness (dBFS). As the context can matter substantially in journal-
ing, we segmented the transcriptions into overlapping windows
of 5 consecutive phrases with a stride of 3 phrases.
GBERT-l: Given German data, we used the German BERT
model GBERT-large [27] as encoder, adding a dropout layer (p
= 0.3) and a linear regression head for multi-target prediction.

Mistral-7B-I: We used zero-shot Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.2
(no fine-tuning) as a large language model for resource-
constrained usage. Prompted in German, the model rated each
text window as a psychologist on the three target dimensions
and returned only the scores. It was loaded in 8-bit quantization
and decoded greedily (no sampling, max new tokens = 50) for
deterministic outputs. Failed generations were excluded.
eGeMAPS: We extracted acoustic features at the phrase
level and averaged them, yielding a single feature value per
participant and day. We used openSMILE [28] to extract
the 88 eGeMAPS (v02) features [29]. Additionally, we in-
cluded valence, arousal, and dominance predictions retrieved
from the pretrained wav2vec2-large-robust-12-ft-emotion-msp-
dim (w2v2-l-emo) model, which is fine-tuned for emotion pre-
dictions [22]; our main hypothesis for those features is that
a model trained to predict affect should correlate with self-
reported measures of affect. We also included the following
rhythm features: total phoneme count, variability, and normal-
ized variability of vowel and consonant durations, proportion of
speaking time occupied by vowels, standard deviation of vowel
and consonant durations, and coefficients of variation for vowel
and consonant durations, speaking rate, articulation rate, and
relative speech duration. Lastly, we added the total duration
and the number of pauses, yielding 92 features. The features
were normalized (mean = 0, std = 1) using the global training
statistic. We trained a multi-output random forest regressor to
predict the three targets simultaneously.
w2v2-L-emo: We further employed w2v2-l-emo [22] via
autrainer [30]. The model was adapted for regression by adding
a pooling layer and linear output head to predict each target
dimension. Fine-tuning used Adam (1 × 10−3, batch size = 16)
for up to 12 epochs with early stopping.

Evaluation: We separate our analysis into two parts: a
population-level analysis that jointly considers all data from all
speakers, and a speaker-level analysis that focuses on each in-
dividual speaker.
Population-level: We evaluate performance using the root
mean squared error (RMSE) and the concordance correlation
coefficient (CCC), the standard metrics in the A VEC chal-
lenges on mood recognition [14]. As Mistral-7B-I was not fine-
tuned, performance metrics were calculated on the entire dataset
directly. For all other models, we employed 5-fold cross-
validation using fixed speaker-independent 80/20 splits for all
models. Hyperparameters were optimized on the 20% valida-
tion split within each training fold using mean squared error
(MSE) loss. Phrase and window-level predictions were mean-
aggregated to the sample-level. Performance metrics were com-
puted on all samples pooled across the folds’ test sets, thus be-
ing comparable to Mistral-7B-I. Bootstrapped 95% confidence
intervals were calculated across the pooled test predictions. We
compare model performance to a mean predictor, using the
training-set average as prediction in the folds’ test sets.
Speaker-level: Given the emphasis of previous work on
personalization [16, 17, 18], we also investigated how daily
mood affects speech within each speaker. For the machine
learning models, individual-level performance was computed
for each speaker and correlated with their average mood (for
each dimension separately). Moreover, our statistical analysis
focused on within-subject variability by introducing the speaker
identity as a random effect, mirroring previous work [18].
3. Results and Discussion
Population-level analysis: Table 1 reports mean and 95%
confidence intervals for RMSE and CCC for all three targets:
MDBFA, MDBF V, and PSS-4. Performance varied substan-
tially across affective dimensions. Valence emerged as the
most predictable target, stress showed moderate predictabil-
ity, and arousal yielded the weakest results overall. Zero-shot
Mistral-7B-I achieved the strongest performance in terms of
CCC (MDBF A : .094;MDBF V : .466;PSS − 4 : .360),
followed by the fine-tuned GBERT-l. Nevertheless, the non-
overlapping confidence intervals of Mistral-7B-I and GBERT-
l, for both valence and stress, indicate a substantial perfor-
mance difference in favor of Mistral-7B-I. When considering
the RMSE, however, Mistral-7B-I only achieved similar perfor-
mance to the mean predictor for stress. For arousal GBERT-l
outperformed all models with a CCC of.122.
Interestingly, while both text-based models achieved con-
sistently positive CCC values across all dimensions, the audio-

Acoustic analysis: Finally, we end by examining whether
eGeMAPS features track speaker-level day-to-day changes, by
reporting linear mixed-effects (LME) model coefficients with
95% confidence intervals for the three targets in Fig. 3, similar
to Lewis et al. [18]. From the large set of extracted features, we
selectively present those highlighted in Lewis et al. [18]. Over-
all, coefficients were centered near zero, indicating limited cor-
respondence between acoustic markers and intra-individual af-
fective fluctuations, yielding a similar magnitude as for Lewiset
al. [18]. After B–H FDR correction, only two features showed
significant speaker-level associations, both for MDBF V: the
number of pauses (negative) and valence predicted by the pre-
trained w2v2-l-emo model (positive). Considering confidence
intervals that exclude zero, three groups emerge for valence:
pause-related features, duration, and w2v2-l-emo predictions,
the former two also being reported by Gerczuk et al. [17]. No
significant speaker-level associations were observed for stress
or arousal. However, for stress, there is a similar indication for
longer durations and more pauses with higher stress.
The lack of significant arousal features starkly contradicts
prior literature, showing strong correlations between arousal
and acoustic parameters [23]. However, the significant coef-
ficients for valence indicate that there is an affective signal in
the data. We hypothesize that, in daily speech, the acoustic cues
primarily reflect the current activation state – such as fatigue –
rather than the reflective arousal of the narrative and the self-
reports. While the narrative’s valence may be subtly conveyed
at the individual level, global models are not able to capture it.
Summary: We explored the ability of global models to in-
fer mood and stress from speech-based journals on both a pop-
ulation and a speaker-level. As our text-based models were able
to outperform their audio-based counterparts, our work indi-
cates that free-speech should be preferred over structured tasks
for tracking daily mood and stress. Additionally, acoustic mod-
els may be more sensitive to transient states, potentially limiting
their ability to infer reflective arousal and stress. Nonetheless,
audio features, such as those related to pauses, show potential
for monitoring individual variations in valence.
Limitations: Our dataset has a small sample size and a
strong gender imbalance. Self-reported EMAs may be affected
by recall bias, social desirability, or variable interpretation.
Some participants showed low within-person variability, and
contextual factors (time of day, fatigue, recording quality) may
have systematically influenced acoustic features.
4. Conclusion
We conclude that the difficulty of using speech-based journaling
to predict daily mood and stress partially stems from the reflec-
tive nature of journaling, which captures a summarized evalua-
tion of the day, rather than a purely momentary affective state,
as well as the inherent challenges in predicting self-reported af-
fect. Furthermore, while population-level differences are de-
tectable, performance on repeated measures within individuals
lags behind, suggesting that the approaches we investigated are
better suited for capturing stable, trait-like tendencies than dy-
namic state fluctuations. Nevertheless, speech-based journal-
ing remains a promising modality for mental health monitoring,
as free-form language provides rich semantic information and
complementary acoustic cues. Future work could focus on im-
proving temporal sensitivity and adding personalization to in-
crease performance and the clinical utility of speech analysis.
