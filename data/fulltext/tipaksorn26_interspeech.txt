A V-FlowSep: Audio-Visual Target Speaker Separation via Flow Matching
Pattara Tipaksorn1, Wayupuk Sommuang2, Kwanchiva Thangthai1
1 Core Artificial Intelligence Research Team, NECTEC
2 Sirindhorn International Institute of Technology (SIIT), Thammasat University
pattara.tip@nectec.or.th, wayupuk.som@dome.tu.ac.th, kwanchiva.tha@nectec.or.th
Abstract
This work addresses the task of separating a target speaker’s
voice from mixtures using visual cues. While existing discrim-
inative approaches achieve strong speech intelligibility, gener-
ative diffusion-based methods improve perceptual quality but
require many iterative refinement steps. To address these limi-
tations, we propose A V-FlowSep, an audio-visual target speaker
separation based on conditional flow matching to learn direct
transport paths from mixture to clean mel-spectrogram. We
adopt a latent Diffusion Transformer where temporal visual
cues from the target speaker are integrated via cross-attention.
We demonstrate that A V-FlowSep can generate high perceptual
quality with as few as a single inference step. Experiments on
V oxCeleb2 and LRS2 across both speech-speech and speech-
noise scenarios, show that our approach achieves competitive
or superior performance compared to existing baselines, with
strong cross-dataset generalization and practical computational
efficiency.

1. Introduction
The human auditory system exhibits a notable ability to at-
tend selectively to a target speaker in the presence of interfer-
ing acoustic sources, a phenomenon commonly known as the
cocktail party effect [1]. Developing machine systems capa-
ble of performing similar separation remains challenging. Ex-
isting research has addressed this problem through audio-only
speech separation [2, 3, 4]. However, these methods rely solely
on acoustic mixtures and their performance can degrade signif-
icantly in noisy conditions [5]. To address this limitation, many
studies have explored audio-visual speech separation (A VSS),
which leverages visual cues such as lip movements and fa-
cial appearance to provide complementary information about
speaker identity and speech content, helping resolve ambigui-
ties that are difficult to infer from audio alone.
Recent A VSS methods can be broadly categorized into de-
terministic and generative approaches [6]. Deterministic ap-
proaches utilize both lip motion and facial attributes [7, 8] or lip
movements alone [9, 10, 11] to directly estimate clean speech
or time-frequency masks from the mixture. While these ap-
proaches demonstrate strong intelligibility, they often produce
over-smoothed spectrograms that average out the fine-grained
details of natural speech. On the other hand, generative ap-
proaches, particularly diffusion models [12, 13] can learn to
produce clean speech from a latent space and have shown the
ability to generate diverse and high-quality samples. However,
they tend to produce speech with undesirable artifacts and are
limited by the multi-step iterative inference process, limiting

To address these challenges, we present A V-FlowSep, a
novel audio-visual target speaker separation model based on
conditional flow matching [14] with a Diffusion Transformer
(DiT) [15] backbone. We adopt flow matching for its ability to
learn straight transport paths enabling efficient few-step infer-
ence, and leverage DiT with cross-attention to integrate visual
information from the target speaker. Our contributions are: (1)
A V-FlowSep achieves high-quality separation with as few as a
single inference step, compared to approximately 30 steps re-
quired by diffusion-based methods; (2) despite training on a
substantially smaller dataset, our model achieves comparable
in-domain performance in speech-speech scenarios and supe-
rior performance in speech-noise conditions, with strong zero-
shot cross-dataset generalization, demonstrating greater data ef-
ficiency and robustness to domain shift; and (3) we provide
analysis showing that A V-FlowSep produces more balanced
separation between dominant and weaker speakers across gen-
der conditions. Our work is publicly available online1.

2. Method
In this section, we introduce A V-FlowSep, which formulates
audio-visual target speaker separation as a conditional transport
problem. As illustrated in Fig. 1, A V-FlowSep aims to separate
clean speechx∈R T from a mixture signaly∈R T . The
framework consists of three primary components: (1) a visual
temporal encoder [16] that extracts target speaker featuresF v,
(2) a flow matching module built on a DiT [15] that learns to
separate the target speaker’s mel-spectrogramM x1 from the
mixture mel-spectrogramM y, and (3) a vocoder [17] that re-
constructs the waveform ˆxfrom the estimated mel-spectrogram
ˆMx1. We describe each component in detail below.
2.1. Mel-Spectrogram Representation
Given a raw waveformy∈R T , we extract a mel spectrogram
My∈R F×T m using a standard short-time Fourier transform
(STFT) and mel-filterbank transformation, whereFdenotes the
number of mel frequency bins andTm is the number of mel time
frames. This representation serves as the input to our model.
2.2. Visual Temporal Encoder
To condition separation on the target speaker, we extract tem-
poral visual features capturing visual dynamics and appearance.
We adopt the visual frontend from TalkNet-ASD [16], consist-
ing of a 3D convolutional layer, ResNet-18, and a visual tempo-
ral convolutional network (V-TCN), to extract temporal visual
featuresF v∈R Tv×D, whereT v denotes the number of video
frames andDis the feature dimension.
2.3. DiT-Based Separation Network
To effectively separate the target speaker’s speech using vi-
sual cues, we adopt a DiT [15] as the vector field estimator
uθ shown in Figure 2. The input is formed by concatenating
Mxt∈R F×T andMy∈R F×T along the channel dimension,
yieldingR 2×F×T , which is linearly projected to hidden dimen-
siond. The network is conditioned on both the flow timestep
and visual features. The timesteptis injected via Adaptive
Layer Normalization (AdaLN) [15, 18], where sinusoidal em-
beddings oftpredict scaleγand shiftβparameters through
an MLP. Following [11, 12], visual information is integrated
via cross-attention, with audio tokens as queriesQ a and visual
featuresF v as keysK v and valuesV v, enabling the model
to leverage lip dynamics, facial appearance, and their tempo-
ral correspondence with speech. Each DiT block comprises
AdaLN-modulated self-attention, cross-attention, and a feed-
forward network with residual connections. The architecture
stacksNblocks to produce the predicted vector fieldu θ.

2.5. V ocoder-Based Waveform Reconstruction
The estimated clean mel-spectrogram ˆMx1 ∈R F×T is con-
verted back to a waveform using a V ocos [17] vocoder: ˆx1 =
V ocos(ˆMx1). Operating in the compact mel-spectrogram space
rather than on complex spectrograms as in DiffSep [13] and
A VDiffuSS [12], reduces the learning complexity and enables
faster sampling, as demonstrated in prior work [22, 25].
3. Experiments
We design experiments to reflect realistic deployment for audio-
visual speaker separation, where models must handle both com-
peting speakers and environmental noise while generalizing to
unseen speakers and recording environments. We evaluate in
two settings: in-domain testing to measure separation under
matched conditions, and zero-shot dataset to assess generaliza-
tion without adaptation.
3.1. Datasets and Evaluation Protocol
We conduct experiments on V oxCeleb2 [26] and LRS2 [27],
both sourced from YouTube with diverse speakers and acous-
tic conditions. V oxCeleb2 is used for training and in-domain
evaluation, while LRS2 is reserved exclusively for zero-shot
cross-dataset testing. We evaluate under two mixture scenar-
ios: (1)speech-speech, where two utterances from different
speakers are mixed following the split protocol in [10], and
(2)speech-noise, where clean speech is mixed with non-speech
noise from AudioSet [28]. In both cases, SNRs are uniformly
sampled between−5and5dB. The V oxCeleb2 training set
comprises 400,000 mixtures with 30,000 development samples.
For evaluation, we construct 6,000 mixtures for each test sce-
nario on both V oxCeleb2 and LRS2. We compare A V-FlowSep
against SepFormer [3] (audio-only), VisualV oice [8] and A V-
MossFormer2 [24] (discriminative audio-visual), and A VDif-
fuSS [12] (diffusion-based).
3.2. Evaluation Metrics
To assess the performance, we evaluate using both reference-
free and reference-based metrics. For reference-free evaluation,
we adopt DNSMOS [29], reporting speech quality (SIG), back-
ground noise (BAK), and overall quality (OVRL) scores. For
reference-based evaluation, we employ wide-band PESQ [30]
and mel cepstral distortion (MCD). Since our model operates at
24 kHz via the V ocos [17], outputs are resampled to 16 kHz for
evaluation. We note that vocoder-based may introduce minor
distortions due to compression and phase estimation [17, 31];
thus, both reference-free and reference-based metrics are re-
ported for comprehensive evaluation.

frames face-cropped to112×112and processed through the
visual frontend and V-TCN (Section 2.2). For audio, we use
100-dimensional log mel-filterbank features from 24 kHz audio
with a hop length of 256. The DiT velocity field estimator (Fig-
ure 2) follows the DiT-S configuration [15] with 12 layers, 6
attention heads, and a hidden dimension of 384. We train using

4. Results
4.1. Comparison with State-of-the-Art Methods
To validate the effectiveness of the proposed method, we re-
port results on V oxCeleb2 and LRS2 under both speech–speech
and speech–noise scenarios in Table 1. All results are obtained
using publicly available pretrained weights for each method.
On V oxCeleb2-2Mix, A V-MossFormer2, which is trained on
a substantially larger dataset, achieves the highest perceptual
scores in SIG, OVRL, and PESQ. A V-FlowSep achieves compa-
rable in-domain performance, suggesting greater data efficiency
of the flow matching framework. The diffusion-based A VDif-
fuSS demonstrates competitive perceptual smoothness but re-
quires 30 inference steps and 60.2M parameters. A V-FlowSep
achieves higher PESQ and consistently lower MCD than A VD-
iffuSS with a single step and 53.6M parameters, indicating that
conditional flow matching enables efficient high-quality gener-
ation. Under the speech–noise setting, A V-FlowSep attains the
highest overall DNSMOS and lowest MCD, suggesting effec-
tive background suppression and improved speech naturalness.
4.2. Zero-shot Evaluations
To assess generalization, models trained on V oxCeleb2 are eval-
uated on LRS2 without fine-tuning, simulating realistic deploy-
ment with unseen speakers, recording setups, and acoustic en-
vironments. SepFormer exhibits relatively stable performance
under domain shift, remaining comparatively robust to acous-
tic variation. In contrast, VisualV oice maintains strong percep-
tual performance and even improves on certain metrics, point-
ing to effective cross-modal generalization. A V-MossFormer2,
however, experiences a larger drop in PESQ and MCD, sug-
gesting greater sensitivity to distribution changes. In contrast,
A V-FlowSep maintains competitive PESQ and consistently low
MCD across both speech–speech and speech–noise conditions,
with only minor degradation compared to in-domain results.
These results indicate that the proposed method remains stable
under simultaneous acoustic and visual domain shifts.

4.3. Speaker Dominance and Gender Robustness Analysis
We analyze robustness with respect to dominant-weak speaker
imbalance and acoustic similarity across gender conditions, as
shown in Table 2. In V oxCeleb2-2Mix, one speaker typically
appears more dominant in the mixture, where S1 has a higher
signal-to-noise ratio than S2. This imbalance makes recovery
of the weaker speaker more challenging. Across methods, per-
formance is consistently higher for S1, and cross-gender mix-
tures generally yield higher overall scores than same-gender
mixtures, most noticeably for SepFormer and VisualV oice. A V-
MossFormer2 achieves the highest absolute PESQ for both
speakers but retains a noticeable S1-S2 gap. In contrast, A V-
FlowSep produces slightly lower absolute PESQ but shows
smaller dominance gaps. Similar trends are observed in MCD,
where A V-FlowSep consistently shows smaller inter-speaker
differences across gender conditions. These results suggest that
the proposed method reduces sensitivity to SNR imbalance and
achieves more uniform separation quality for both dominant and
weaker speakers.
4.4. Discussions
During analysis, we observed that under zero-shot evaluation
audio-visual models may occasionally separate the interfering
speaker instead of the target. We further analyze this speaker
confusion on LRS2. To quantify this, we compute MCD be-
tween the separated output and both the target and interfering
references. A sample is considered confused when the MCD of
the target speaker exceeds the MCD of the interfering speaker
(i.e., MCDtarget>MCD interfering), indicating that the output more
closely resembles the wrong speaker. As shown in Table 3,
the confusion rate for A V-FlowSep and A V-MossFormer2 is
12.60% and 33.90%, respectively, whereas the lip-region-only
model VisualV oice exhibits a substantially lower rate of just
0.11%. This suggests that face-condition may rely more heav-
ily on speaker identity and facial appearance cues observed dur-
ing training, rather than learning robust audio-visual correspon-
dence. While face-based conditioning generally yields superior
separation quality, as demonstrated in Table 1 and corroborated
by prior work [8], this trade-off between performance and ro-
bustness remains an important direction for future work.
5. Conclusion
We presented A V-FlowSep, an audio-visual target speaker sep-
aration framework based on conditional flow matching with a
Diffusion Transformer backbone. Our model achieves high-
quality separation with as few as a single inference step, sub-
stantially improving efficiency over diffusion-based methods.
Experiments on V oxCeleb2 and LRS2 show competitive or su-
perior performance across speech-speech and speech-noise sce-
narios, with strong zero-shot generalization and more balanced
separation between dominant and weaker speakers. Future
work includes mitigating visual identity shortcuts, extending to
multi-speaker scenarios, and integrating with downstream tasks
such as speech recognition.
