Mispronunciation Modeling via PPG-Based Phone Editing: A Data
Augmentation Framework for Dysarthric Speech Recognition
Tsai-Hsiu Ko, You-Cyuan Jhuo, Chung-Hsien Wu
Department of Computer Science and Information Engineering, National Cheng Kung University,
Tainan, Taiwan

Abstract
Automatic Speech Recognition (ASR) systems face signifi-
cant challenges in recognizing dysarthric speech due to severe
acoustic variability, frequent mispronunciations, and extreme
data scarcity. To address these issues, this paper proposes a
novel speaker-dependent data augmentation framework utiliz-
ing phonetic posteriorgram (PPG)-based phone editing. By
quantifying individual pronunciation variations, our method se-
lectively edits the phonetic representations of typical speech
to emulate the mispronunciation patterns of target dysarthric
speakers. This technique is further integrated with speaking
style conversion and phonetic-level speed perturbation to com-
prehensively capture both the articulatory and prosodic char-
acteristics of dysarthria. We evaluated our approach on the
UASpeech corpus by fine-tuning a HuBERT ASR model. Ex-
perimental results demonstrate that the proposed method ef-
fectively enhances recognition robustness, achieving an overall
word error rate of 19.53%.

1. Introduction
Dysarthria is a motor speech disorder resulting from neurolog-
ical conditions such as cerebral palsy and Parkinson’s disease,
characterized by muscle weakness and reduced articulatory co-
ordination. While automatic speech recognition (ASR) sys-
tems offer a promising alternative to assist dysarthric individ-
uals in daily communication, state-of-the-art deep neural net-
work based architectures still struggle to adapt to disordered
speech [1, 2]. This performance degradation is primarily driven
by the severe acoustic mismatch between typical and dysarthric
speech, manifested as articulatory imprecision, irregular speech
rhythms, and reduced overall intelligibility.
The development of robust dysarthric ASR systems is fun-
damentally hindered by two prevalent challenges: extreme data
scarcity and high speech variability. On the one hand, collecting
extensive dysarthric speech corpora is practically difficult due
to the physiological burden on patients, recruitment challenges,
and privacy concerns. On the other hand, the immense vari-
ability of dysarthric speech exacerbates the existing challenge
of data scarcity. Pronunciation tendencies and acoustic traits
vary significantly not only across different speakers depending
on the severity of their condition but also remain highly incon-
sistent within the repeated utterances of the same individual.
To mitigate the impact of data scarcity, researchers have de-
veloped specialized data augmentation strategies. For instance,
phonetic-level speed perturbation [3] has been employed to ad-
just the tempo of typical speech to match the slower and irreg-
ular prosody of dysarthric speakers. Other approaches utilize
generative adversarial networks (GANs) to model the spectro-
temporal differences between typical and dysarthric speech
[4, 5, 6], or leverage voice conversion [7, 8, 9] and text-to-
speech [10, 11, 12] techniques to incorporate the specific timbre
and speaker embeddings of dysarthric individuals.
Despite these promising advancements, a fundamental
challenge remains: existing augmentation techniques primar-
ily manipulate global acoustic features such as duration, pitch,
and energy, but fall short in modeling fine-grained, speaker-
dependent articulatory defects. Individuals with dysarthria fre-
quently exhibit unique and highly variable mispronunciation
patterns—such as specific phoneme substitutions—which can-
not be accurately simulated by global acoustic modifications
alone.
Consequently, generating augmented speech that accurately
represents both the acoustic characteristics and the specific pro-
nunciation tendencies of dysarthric speakers is crucial. While
phonetic posteriorgrams (PPGs) and phone editing techniques
[13, 14, 15] have proven effective for highly interpretable and
disentangled pronunciation control in tasks like accent conver-
sion, their application to simulating pathological mispronunci-
ations for dysarthric ASR data augmentation remains largely
unexplored.
Motivated by these limitations, this study aims to develop
a highly personalized data augmentation framework grounded
in the careful analysis of speaker-dependent pronunciation pat-
terns. By modeling how each individual mispronounces spe-
cific phonemes, we can generate augmented data that accurately
align with their unique pronunciation tendencies. Furthermore,
by leveraging the robust representations of self-supervised
learning models pre-trained on large-scale typical speech, and
combining them with specialized augmentation techniques in-
cluding speaking style conversion and phonetic-level speed per-
turbation, we seek to comprehensively reconstruct both the ar-
ticulatory and prosodic characteristics of dysarthric speech to
improve ASR adaptation under low-resource conditions.
2. Proposed Method
The overall system framework is depicted in Figure 1, consist-
ing of three main components: (1) pronunciation variation anal-
ysis for PPG-based phone editing, (2) speaking style conversion
via a PPG-to-speech synthesizer, and (3) phonetic-level speed
perturbation.
2.1. Pronunciation variation analysis
To model the mispronunciation tendencies of individual
dysarthric speakers, we first extract the phoneme sequences
from both dysarthric and paired control (typical) utterances.
This is achieved using a high-fidelity PPG extractor [15],

Once the phoneme sequences of dysarthric utterances have
been determined, we establish the mapping relationship be-
tween the expected phoneme, denoted as/i/, and the actually
pronounced phoneme, denoted as/j/. This relationship is de-
fined as a phonetic mapping (/i/→/j/). To ensure the re-
liability of these mappings, we apply a rule-based algorithm
following a specific priority order. First, under a one-to-one
mapping assumption—a specific phoneme in an utterance is as-
sumed to be pronounced at most once—we prioritize accurate
pronunciation assignments by immediately matching identical
phonemes (/i/=/j/) between the two sequences. For the
remaining unmapped phonemes, substitutions (/i/̸=/j/) are
assigned, but these are strictly subject to two constraints: (1)
phonetic class consistency: a vowel must map to a vowel, and
a consonant to a consonant; and (2)same neighbor existence:
the paired phonemes must share at least one identical adjacent
phoneme to preserve the phonetic context. Finally, any expected
phoneme that still cannot be mapped under these constraints is
classified as a deletion (/i/→/sil/). In contrast, insertions
are excluded from the mapping process to prevent introducing
unnecessary noise during acoustic model training.
Subsequently, the frequencies of all phonetic mappings
for a specific dysarthric speaker are aggregated and column-
normalized to construct a speaker-dependent phonetic mapping
matrixM. Since 40 distinct phonemes are defined in the CMU
Pronunciation Dictionary 1, there exist40×40possible pho-
netic mapping types. An example matrix is shown in Figure 2.
In this matrix, the diagonal entryM /i/,/i/ represents the pro-
nunciation accuracy for a given expected phoneme/i/, while
the off-diagonal entryM /i/,/j/ indicates the probability of/i/
being substituted as/j/. This probabilistic matrix efficiently
quantifies the highly variable and unique pronunciation tenden-
cies of the target speaker, providing the foundational rules for
the subsequent PPG-based phone editing process.

2.3. PPG-to-speech synthesizer
To reconstruct speech waveforms from the edited PPGs while
accurately reflecting the vocal characteristics of the target
dysarthric speaker, we employ a PPG-to-speech synthesizer
modified from the any-to-any voice conversion framework,
UUVC [16]. The synthesizer utilizes a speech decomposition

strategy to decouple speech attributes, enabling the integration
of the modified phonetic content with the specific speaking style
of the target dysarthric speaker.
We introduce two critical modifications to the original
UUVC architecture for this task. First, the HuBERT [17] dis-
crete units originally used for content representation are re-
placed with our continuous PPGs, facilitating direct synthesis
from the phone-editing outputs. Second, because PPGs in-
herently preserve frame-level temporal alignments, the dura-
tion network in UUVC is removed. During synthesis, the at-
tribute encoder extracts the speaker embedding and pitch em-
bedding from a reference dysarthric utterance, then PPGs and
speaker embedding are passed through the pitch-energy net-
work to obtain the predicted pitch and predicted energy. These
decoupled features are then integrated using a source-filter-
energy network: a source network takes PPG concatenated with
speaker embedding as input, while the input of the filter net-
work is PPG concatenated with pitch embedding. The energy
network receives the predicted energy as its input. The outputs
of these three networks are combined to generate the target mel-
spectrogram, which is subsequently converted into a waveform
using a HiFi-GAN vocoder [18].
The synthesizer is trained as an autoencoder in a self-
supervised manner, where the input and target are from the same
utterance. To ensure robust generalization, the model is first
pre-trained on a typical speech corpus then fine-tuned on the
dysarthric dataset. To further enhance the naturalness of syn-
thetic speech, adversarial training is incorporated using a dis-
criminator implemented as a sequence of convolutional residual
blocks. During training, the mean of the predicted and ground
truth bin weights for pitch and energy is used as the input for
the source-filter-energy network, rather than relying solely on
one or the other. We employ a joint training strategy with the
goal of minimizing the total lossL total, which is defined as:
Ltotal =L mel +L pitch +L energy +L adv (1)
Here,L mel applies an L1 loss to minimize the difference be-
tween the predicted and ground truth mel-spectrograms.L pitch
andL energy apply binary cross-entropy losses between the pre-
dicted and ground truth bin weights for pitch and energy. Fi-
nally,L adv is a least-squares adversarial loss [19] derived from
a discriminator.

2.4. Speed perturbation
Dysarthric speech is often characterized by abnormally slow
and highly irregular rhythms. To accurately capture these
unique prosodic features, we apply phonetic-level speed pertur-
bation following [3] rather than uniform global time-stretching.
Given a paired control utteranceCand a dysarthric ut-
teranceDwith identical content, we first employ a forced
alignment model based on the Gaussian Mixture Model-Hidden
Markov Model via the Kaldi toolkit [20] to determine the pre-
cise phoneme boundaries and durations in both utterances. Let
lC/i/ andl D/i/ denote the duration of a specific phoneme
/i/in the control and dysarthric utterances, respectively. The
phonetic-level scaling factor for this phoneme is computed
as the ratiol D/i//lC/i/. Using these factors, we apply the
Waveform Similarity Overlap-Add (WSOLA) algorithm [21] to
stretch or compress each phoneme segment in the control utter-
ance while preserving its original pitch. Figure 4 shows the
spectrograms ofNATHANIELproduced by the dysarthric and
control speaker. Phonetic-level stretching of the typical utter-
ance yields more fine-grained alignment than uniform scaling

and dysarthric utterances, respectively.
3. Experimental setup and result
3.1. Datasets and experimental setting
We evaluated the proposed framework primarily on the
UASpeech [22], which comprises recordings of 15 dysarthric
speakers and 13 control speakers with the same age and gender.
Dysarthric speakers are categorized into four intelligibility sub-
groups: high, medium, low, and very-low. The corpus is struc-
tured into three blocks. Following standard evaluation proto-
cols, all utterances from the control speakers along with blocks
1 and 3 from the dysarthric speakers were utilized for training.
Block 2 of dysarthric speech was strictly reserved for testing.
Furthermore, the VCTK corpus [23], consisting of speech from
110 English speakers with diverse accents, was adopted exclu-
sively to pre-train the PPG-to-speech synthesizer.
For the phone editing process, preliminary evaluations were
conducted to determine the optimal thresholds on ASR perfor-
mance. We set the pronunciation accuracy thresholdαto 70%

3.2. Synthesizer evaluation
To assess the fidelity of the PPG-to-speech synthesizer, we
evaluated the generated speech in terms of naturalness and
speaker similarity. Naturalness was objectively measured using
MOSNet [24], which estimates a mean opinion score (MOS)
ranging from 1 to 5. Speaker similarity was quantified us-
ing the speaker embedding similarity (SES) metric. To ex-
tract the required bottleneck features for SES, a speaker clas-
sifier was trained on the UASpeech using a cross-entropy ob-
jective. The architecture of this classifier comprises a speech
encoder—based on a CNN feature extractor and the first Trans-
former layer of wav2vec 2.0 [25]—followed by a linear projec-
tion layer. The SES is then calculated as the cosine similarity
of the bottleneck features between the synthesized and ground-
truth dysarthric utterances, with values ranging from -1 to 1.
As shown in Table 1, the synthetic speech achieves a
MOSNet score of 2.55, closely approximating the 2.68 score
of the ground-truth speech. This demonstrates the synthe-
sizer’s capability to produce speech without severe distortion
while effectively preserving the inherent qualities of dysarthria.
Furthermore, the high SES score of 0.71 confirms that the
source-filter-energy architecture successfully captures and re-
tains the timbre and speaker-specific characteristics of the target
dysarthric individuals.
3.3. ASR performance
To benchmark the effectiveness of our proposed phone editing
approach, we implemented the GAN-based method [6] as the
baseline. To ensure a fair comparison, the augmented datasets
generated by both were augmented to the same volume using
speed perturbation. For the ASR task, we employ the HuBERT
Large via the Fairseq toolkit [26]. During adaptation to the aug-
mented UASpeech dataset, the model weights are frozen for the
first 10,000 updates, followed by fine-tuning for a maximum of
25,000 updates. The optimization is performed using the Adam
optimizer with a peak learning rate of2×10 −5, guided by a tri-
stage learning rate scheduler. The overall system performance
is evaluated using the word error rate (WER) metric.
Table 2 demonstrates that our approach consistently outper-
forms the baseline across all intelligibility subgroups, achieving
an overall absolute WER reduction of 2.35%. Notably, the most
significant improvements are observed in the low and very-low
intelligibility categories, where our method surpasses the base-
line by 3.69% and 4.54%, respectively. These findings highlight
the benefits of capturing the speaker-dependent pronunciation
patterns of dysarthric speech, demonstrating the effectiveness
of our approach in enhancing speech recognition accuracy.

3.4. Ablation study
To investigate the individual contributions of the proposed data
augmentation components, an ablation study was conducted.
We evaluated the effects of three core factors: (1)phone edit-
ing, as detailed in Section 2.2, compared to random phoneme
replacement; (2)speaker-dependent speaking style conver-
sion, which incorporates the target dysarthric speaker’s at-
tributes (Section 2.3) into the synthesis process rather than pre-
serving the control speaker’s characteristics; and (3)phonetic-
level speed perturbation, as described in Section 2.4, evalu-
ated against no duration modification.
Table 3 summarizes the ASR performance under vari-
ous configurations. Regarding phone editing, substituting
phonemes based on the proposed mapping algorithm yields su-
perior recognition accuracy compared to random phoneme re-
placement or no editing. This validates the necessity of model-
ing speaker-specific mispronunciations rather than introducing
random phonetic noise. Furthermore, incorporating the specific
speaking style of the target dysarthric speaker—rather than re-
taining the control speaker’s acoustic identity—consistently im-
proves performance, demonstrating the critical role of matching
global timbre and energy patterns.
Finally, the application of phonetic-level speed perturbation
further reduces the WER across all intelligibility subgroups.
The integration of three techniques achieves the most substan-
tial performance gains, confirming that comprehensive adap-
tation to localized articulation, global timbre, and irregular
prosody is essential for recognizing severe dysarthric speech.
4. Conclusion
In this study, we proposed a novel data augmentation frame-
work for dysarthric speech recognition. By analyzing indi-
vidual mispronunciation tendencies and employing PPG-based
phone editing, our method effectively incorporates specific ar-
ticulatory errors into typical speech. Experimental results in
UASpeech demonstrate that our approach significantly outper-
forms the competitive GAN-based baseline, achieving an over-
all WER of 19.53% and providing substantial recognition im-
provements for individuals with severe dysarthria.
