Abstract
Prosodic cues such as rhythm and lexical stress affect intelligi-
bility; they stand out as signals that guide perception. While
these cues are shared across many languages, lexical stress is
realized differently in Arabic and English, making stress detec-
tion challenging in multilingual and transfer settings. We pro-
pose a framework that uses self supervised speech representa-
tions as fixed features and a two stage classifier: a syllable level
Pre-net and a word context Post-net that models inter syllable
dependencies. We compare monolingual training, joint Arabic
English multilingual training, cross lingual transfer using dif-
ferent self-supervised learning (SSL) model’s feature vectors
input into Pre-net DNN and Post-net TDNN models. Multi-
lingual training retains near monolingual accuracy of 97% and
90% on English and Arabic respectively, whereas cross-lingual
transfer is direction-dependent; the Post-net improves robust-
ness and multilingual pre-training yields the strongest transfer.
Index Terms: Lexical stress detection, Self-supervised speech,
multilingual lexical stress.
1. Introduction
Computer-Aided Pronunciation Learning (CAPL) systems sup-
port L2 learning through self-paced practice and real-time cor-
rective feedback. A central component of modern CAPL en-
vironments is automatic pronunciation evaluation, which pro-
vides learners with feedback on both segmental accuracy and
prosodic realization. Within this context, automatic lexical
stress assessment is particularly important, since stress place-
ment directly impacts intelligibility as well as perceived pro-
nunciation quality. Although stress models are often deployed
in CAPL applications, their core technical objective is the auto-
matic classification of stress patterns.
Most lexical stress detection techniques combine temporal
indicators (e.g., fundamental frequency, intensity, and duration)
with spectral representations like Mel-frequency cepstral coef-
ficients (MFCCs), which indicate variations in energy and for-
mants of a stressed versus an unstressed syllable [1, 2]. Many
classifier models have been studied. Recent studies focused pri-
marily on the use of neural networks [3]. For example, Li et al.
[4] evaluated automatic lexical stress recognition of L2 English
speech with prosodic features and compared Gaussian Mixture
Model(GMM), Deep Belief Network (DBN), and Deep Neural
Network (DNN). They achieved the best result of 87.9% ac-
curacy with DNN. In similar manner, Shahin et al. [5] used
combinations of time and frequency domain information with
both DNN and CNN models to automatically identify lexical
stress in three different languages (L2 English, Arabic, and
child speech) with errors of 9% and 7% for the two English

2.0 [8] are better at detecting syllable stress in non-native En-

bilingual and cross-language environment while adding many
aspects missing from earlier monolingual works.
In this work, we evaluate two classifier architectures:
a feed-forward DNN and a Time-Delay Neural Network
(TDNN), following the configuration described by Aluru et al.
[9]. Our primary aim is to investigate Arabic–English multi-
lingual and cross-lingual lexical stress detection under a con-
trolled experimental protocol, examining how SSL representa-
tions transfer between two typologically distinct stress systems.
We further examine the impact of representation choice by us-
ing three SSL encoders as fixed feature extractors, enabling a
systematic comparison of pre-trained embeddings under identi-
cal training and evaluation conditions. All models are trained
and tested on the Arabic and English subsets of the Common
V oice corpus with consistent data splits, ensuring comparability
across architectures and feature representations.
The remainder of this paper presents the background,
methodology, and evaluation results for Arabic and English lex-
ical stress detection using deep neural architectures.
2. Background: Arabic and English lexical
stress
Lexical stress or primary stress is a prosodic feature which tells
us which syllables in a word are stressed. The features of stress
are duration, intensity and pitch. The rules for stress assign-
ment vary greatly from one language to another. In Modern
Standard Arabic (MSA) Arabic, the language is weight sensi-
tive with a small stress window, whereas in English, stress is
contrastive and the rules governing stress assignment interact
with each other and with the lexical class of the word, with the
stressed and unstressed morphemes that are added to it, and with
the rhythmic requirements of the word. The stress in English is
highly lexicalized, and the rules are full of exceptions and alter-
natives.
2.1. Arabic lexical stress in MSA
Arabic is linguistically a highly diverse language with poten-
tially different stress patterns for different dialects. However,
a recognized dialect among all Arabic speakers is MSA. Most
MSA Arabic stress is quantity sensitive [10, 11], that is, stress

in most Arabic dialects is conditioned by syllable structure and
weight. The constituents of a syllable are typically onset, nu-
cleus and coda, and although MSA allows a more restricted set
of syllables for stress than does, for example, Standard Malay,
these are all binned into light, heavy or super heavy based on
weight of syllable: a light syllable in MSA is always a simple
Consonant-V owel (CV) syllable, a heavy syllable always one
of CVC or CVV , and a super heavy one of CVCC, CVVC, or
CVVCC (where V indicates a long vowel as well as a short one).
Stress in MSA is typically assigned to one of the last three sylla-
bles within any given word, and follows strict stress rules when
being applied:
1. ultimate syllable stressed if it is superheavy.
2. else if the penultimate is stressed if it carries a heavy syllable.
3. else the antepenultimate is stressed.
Arabic stress rules are quite systematic, and so have a very
high accuracy requirement on syllable structures and weight
assignments. A representative example is the wordMadrasa
(’school’), which comprises three syllables (MAD-RA-SA) with
stress realized on the first syllable. This system is a part of the
reason why MSA is commonly used in applications in which
stress needs to be automatically applied, and in which it is also
important to have a controlled experimental environment [11].
2.2. English lexical stress
Lexical stress in English is contrastive and meaningful. A
classic example of a disyllabic noun/verb alternation isPER-
fect/perFECT. The stress of the pairs can vary: with stress on
the first syllable of the nouns and on the second in the verbs,
but with no general rule to apply to all words in the lexicon
[12]. In general stress in English is less rule bound and is there-
fore lexically specified with a relation to morphology. Stress in
derived forms is always regular. Some common (re- and in-)
prefixes and (-ic and -ity) suffixes are often associated with spe-
cific stress positions. Stress in English is usually described as
being composed of three stress levels: primary, secondary and
un-stressed [13].
2.3. Arabic-English contrast and implications for multilin-
gual settings
The two sets of descriptions differ from each other in terms of
what is being stressed and what is being used as a reference. In
the case of MSA stress, it is described as a stress on the syl-
lable with the highest weight, using a constrained right-edge
window. In contrast, the stress on English words is described
in terms of metrical structure and morphology/lexicon, and ac-
companied by reduction of the vowel in the unstressed posi-
tion (e.g. [11, 10, 14, 15]). Studies on Arab English learners
report systematic bidirectional transfer between Arabic and En-
glish stress patterns, counter to unidirectional L1-to-L2 systems
[16].
3. Methodology
3.1. System description
Figure 1 shows the overall system pipeline. Speech input is
passed through forced alignment and a pre-trained SSL encoder
for feature extraction. A phonetiser [17] provides Buckwalter
transcription for Arabic and CMUdict ARPA transcription for
English. Syllabification and stress labeling are applied to the
normalized transcription, and syllable embeddings are obtained
by averaging frame vectors within syllable boundaries. A Pre-
net DNN is trained on syllable embeddings for syllable-wise
stress classification. Word embeddings are formed by concate-
nating syllable vectors and passed through a Post-net TDNN,
initialized with Pre-net weights to assist convergence.
Figure 1:Flowchart of the proposed system
3.2. Syllabification and stress labeling
In this paper, syllabification for the English dataset was done
by using the Carnegie Mellon University Pronouncing Dictio-
nary (CMUdict). The CMUdict is a lexical resource that in-
cludes pronunciation for all the words with phonemes and stress
marked. The words in the dataset were matched with their CMU
dictionary entries, and the syllables, along with the stress mark-
ers (”1”, ”2”, or ”0”, for primary stress, secondary stress, or no
stress), were used. This provides a standard procedure for all
words in the English dataset and serves as a strong baseline for
cross-language comparison with the proposed Arabic dataset.
Primary and secondary stress labels were collapsed into a sin-
gle stressed class to enable a direct binary comparison with the
Arabic stress labels, which do not distinguish secondary stress.
For the MSA we know the rules for the syllable structures,
and the stress rules which depend on the vowel qualities (quan-
tity sensitive). Our approach for the syllabification is as fol-
lows. A text in Buckwalter transcription of the phonemes of the
words of the text is given as input to the program. Then the
consonant–vowel sequences CVCVCVVCVC are derived. The
matching of these sequences to the known MSA syllable pat-
terns together with the canonical constraints given by the stress
assignment yields the syllable level symbols. These are then
replaced by the corresponding phonemes which give the time
aligned syllables with the required stress labels for each word in
the text following the well known conventions for MSA. Since
Common V oice is a read speech corpus, speakers follow canon-
ical stress patterns as intended, supporting the reliability of the
automatically derived labels.
3.3. Feature extraction
Lexical stress is most commonly described as involving pre-
dictable differences in duration, pitch and intensity for a tar-
get syllable relative to its surrounding syllables. Typically,
stressed syllables are realised with a higher fundamental fre-
quency, greater energy and longer segmental duration [16]. To
model these context dependent features we first obtain context
dependent frame level features using an SSL model. This model
is a Transformer based context network that is capable of rep-
resenting both the local phonetic detail of the signal in a frame
and the more global prosodic relationships that may exist be-
tween frames. Each frame is modeled by a fixed dimensional
vector of sized.
Three SSL encoders (multilingual XLS-R, multilingual
WavLM and English HuBERT) are used to generate the 1024-
dimensional frame-level embeddings. We believe that the ex-
tra dimensions in the 1024-dimensional vector will include the

prosodic information that differentiates between stressed and
unstressed syllables. The average frame vector for the frames
corresponding to each syllable is calculated by using the forced-
alignment time stamps, to generate the embedding of the current
syllable that will be classified in the future stages.
1. WavLM is a self-supervised encoder trained on 16 kHz audio
samples from 94k hours of speech in English and 23 Euro-
pean languages and is commonly used as a feature extractor
for various applications [18].
2. The HuBERT model used here is the large model pre-trained
on 16 kHz audio samples from 60k hours of Libri-Light En-
glish speech using a masked prediction loss and is used as a
strong English baseline for feature extraction [19].
3. XLS-R is a multilingual Wav2Vec model pre-trained on
16 kHz audio samples from 436k hours of speech in 128 lan-
guages including English and Arabic [20].
3.4. Classifiers
Deep neural networks (DNN) have become the new standard
for speech classification tasks [21, 22, 23]. Our work exploits a
two stage design, as proposed in [9], using the Wav2Vec contex-
tual embeddings at the different stages instead of the commonly
used hand-crafted prosodic features, for the lexical stress detec-
tion. So, we have a syllable-level Pre-net DNN and a word-level
Post-net TDNN, as shown in Figure 2. We start by describ-

lexical stress detection
ing the data pre-processing done before feeding the data to the
neural network. We use word level syllable embeddings of di-
mension 1024 and train a classifier to predict stress patterns for
each individual syllable in a word. The classifier is a neural
network with an input layer, fully connected hidden layers with
Rectified Linear Unit (ReLU) activation, and an output layer
with a single output neuron that outputs a posterior distribution
over stressed classes. We chose the number of hidden layers
and their dimensions based on experimental optimization of the
model. Optimization was done using the binary cross-entropy
loss.
The Post-net uses a short-range temporal context to model
the dependencies within a word at the level of individual syl-
lables. We stack time-delay (TD) layers on top of the syllable
sequence to predict the per-syllable posteriors given the preced-
ing and following syllables in the word. The syllable sequence
for each word along with the corresponding syllable labels are
padded to a fixed lengthx(wherexis the maximum number of
syllables observed in the training data which isx= 9). For a
word withnsyllables,x−npositions are padded. The padded
positions are masked so that they do not contribute to the TDNN
computation or the loss.
For every non-masked syllable in the input, the model sam-
ples the posterior at inference time and the class label for each
frame is obtained by thresholding the stressed-class output. The
weights of the TDNN are initialized with the weights of the Pre-
net. The Post-net TDNN shares the same layer structure and
dimensions as the Pre-net DNN to enable this weight transfer.
Early stopping with patience of 10 epochs based on validation
loss is applied to the training procedure, and the correspond-
ing checkpoint is selected with the best validation loss. The
accuracy and F1-score are computed using scikit-learn [24].
Our models are implemented and trained with TensorFlow and
Keras [25].
3.5. Speech corpus
The Common V oice 12 dataset for Arabic contains 82601 utter-
ances from 554 different speakers of various nationalities. All
recorded Arabic is MSA, hence the corpus dialect-neutral. It
contains 459,166 word tokens with 59,727 unique words, pro-
viding 1.2 million syllables. The stress labels in the dataset are
heavily imbalanced with a 3:7 stressed:unstressed distribution.
The English dataset is derived from the English subset of
the Common V oice speech corpus. We extracted 80,000 utter-
ances that contain 301,670 words with 32,401 unique words and
1.31M syllables excluding single-syllable words. We converted
the stress labels from their original form to ’1’ for primary and
secondary stress and ’0’ for no stress. The stress/un-stress ra-
tio was 5:6. English variety metadata was not tracked in this
study; the subset used is believed to consist predominantly of
American English speakers, though this was not verified.
Phoneme alignments were used for deterministic syllabi-
fication and stress assignment, with a subset validated using
PRAAT [26]. Data were partitioned into training, validation,
and test sets using a 70/15/15 split by filename; speaker meta-
data was unavailable, so speaker-disjoint splits could not be ex-
plicitly verified.

the 82% accuracy reported by Shahin et al. [5], while the mono-
lingual English benchmark of 97.49% similarly exceeds their
reported 91% accuracy, confirming that SSL representations
provide a strong foundation for both languages.
In contrast, cross-lingual transfer (Train: English-Test:
Arabic, and Train: Arabic-Test: English) produces a clear per-
formance drop relative to multilingual and monolingual train-
ing (Figure 3). When transferring from English to Arabic,
accuracy averages 68.12% in the English-to-Arabic direction,
with XLS-R Post-net achieving the highest accuracy of 79.17%,
whereas Arabic-to-English transfer is consistently higher, aver-
aging 76.09% with XLS-R Post-net again achieving the best
result of 86.69%. This directional asymmetry suggests that the
learned stress cues are not equally portable in both directions.
A plausible explanation is that MSA stress is largely quantity-
sensitive and strongly constrained by syllable weight and vowel
length, yielding comparatively consistent acoustic correlates,
while English stress is shaped by lexical/morphological struc-
ture and frequently accompanied by vowel reduction in un-
stressed syllables, which can increase variability and reduce
transferability to Arabic. In a cross-lingual setting, the multilin-
gual XLS-R model performed the best by outperforming both
the WavLM and HuBERT encoders. It was also seen that XLS-
R feature vectors had close, or better, performance in Pre-net
Accuracy results when compared with the Post-net results of
other models.
Importantly, adding temporal modeling via the TDNN Post-
net substantially reduces the cross-lingual gap across all en-
coders (Figure 3), with the largest improvements observed in
the most challenging transfer setting (English→Arabic). In
particular, XLS-R yields the most robust cross-lingual perfor-
mance once paired with the Post-net, achieving the highest
transfer accuracies in both directions and remaining closest to
the monolingual benchmarks, albeit still below them by approx-
imately a single-digit to low double-digit margin. This pattern
is consistent with the expectation that broader multilingual pre-
training improves the portability of prosodic representations.
The layer-sensitivity plots (shown in Figure 4) further refine
this picture by showing accuracy as a function of the number of
hidden layers in the classifier. Two trends are consistent across
encoders. First, multilingual and monolingual benchmark con-
ditions are relatively stable across layer settings, indicating that
strong in-language performance does not depend critically on
a narrow choice of layer configuration. Second, cross-lingual
conditions show greater sensitivity, with modest but noticeable
fluctuations across layer counts and more consistent gains for
the Post-net than the Pre-net. Despite all of this, if we consider
even the smallest of gains, the model across all three encoders
seems to display the best accuracy results when configured with
three or four layers. In practice, these figures suggest that while
layer selection can fine-tune transfer performance, the domi-
nant drivers of robustness are (i) using a multilingual-pretrained
encoder (XLS-R being more suitable for cross-lingual perfor-
mance and the others for multilingual) and (ii) incorporating ex-
plicit within-word temporal dependency modeling through the
Post-net TDNN.
5. Conclusion
Building on prior SSL-based stress detection work in mono-
lingual non-native English settings [6, 7], this paper extends
the framework to Arabic–English multilingual and cross-lingual
lexical stress detection using a 2-stage Pre-net/Post-net archi-
tecture. Multilingual fine-tuning achieves near-monolingual
performance on both languages. Cross-lingual transfer shows
a direction-dependent performance drop, more prominent in
the Arabic-to-English direction. The Post-net consistently im-
proves cross-lingual performance, and XLS-R yields the most
consistent results across different classifier depths.
