Abstract
Continuous emotion recognition (CER) relies on time-
continuous annotations that reflect annotators’ sustained emo-
tion perception. In practice, ground-truth annotations are often
observed to have a lag, which leads to temporal misalignment
between model predictions and annotator labels. The most com-
mon solution is delay compensation based on a relative tempo-
ral shift between input and labels, assuming that annotations
are delayed reports of judgements formed based on the sig-
nal at a given moment. While effective, this shift-based de-
lay compensation (SDC) requires explicit temporal shifting and
truncation at sequence boundaries, and its performance can be
sensitive to the choice of delay parameter. In this paper, we
revisit delay compensation and introduce accumulated delay
compensation (ADC), implemented via feature-level temporal
accumulation. Instead of explicitly shifting labels, ADC ac-
cumulates past input features, incorporating historical context
while inducing an intrinsic delay without modifying the origi-
nal input–label alignment. Experiments on the RECOLA cor-
pus demonstrate ADC is substantially more robust to the choice
of delay parameter when compared with SDC. Further analy-
sis reveals dimension-specific temporal effects: arousal favours
shorter integration windows, whereas valence benefits from a
longer window. These findings suggest that ADC offers a supe-
rior approach for delay compensation compared to the standard
approach for CER.

1. Introduction
Automatic emotion recognition (AER) aims to infer affective
states from observable signals such as speech, text, and facial
expressions [1, 2, 3]. Continuous emotion recognition (CER)
extends this task to time-varying prediction of dimensional la-
bels such as arousal and valence, treating emotion estimation
as a temporal regression problem [4, 5, 6]. Since CER labels
are derived from sustained human annotation, the dynamics this
annotation process has a potentially significant effect on model
training and evaluation. For instance, ground-truth (GT) anno-
tations are often observed to have a lag, arising from various
factors including time required for human annotators to form
a judgement, delays in the physical annotation process (such
moving a joystick), etc. This in turn has follow on impacts
on model training and inference [7, 8, 5]. Delay compensation

has therefore become a common pre-processing step in training
CER systems. Among its implementations, reaction-lag-based
shifted delay compensation (SDC) is the most widely adopted
approach, where the annotations are shifted in time to account
for an assumed reporting delay [8, 9]. While effective, SDC re-
quires explicit temporal shifting and sequence truncation, and
its performance can be sensitive to the choice of delay parame-
ter [8].
It is worth noting that SDC is typically implemented as a
constant offset of 2–4 s, with this value empirically estimated
from the training and validation datasets used to train the CER
model [8, 10, 5]. While decisional and motor processes during
annotation may contribute to such offsets, neural evidence in-
dicates that affective responses can emerge within hundreds of
milliseconds [11]; simultaneously, non-perceptual factors such
as motor execution have not been directly measured in prior
CER studies. Taken together, these considerations suggest that
the assumptions underpinning SDC may lack nuance and de-
lay compensation methods that are more robust to estimates of
delay might be of value.
Furthermore, previous work has shown that incorporating
past temporal context systematically influences CER perfor-
mance [12]. Given the observed prediction–label misalignment
and the potential role of temporal context, we propose an alter-
native to shift-based delay compensation based on the assump-
tion that annotators’ emotion perception may reflect a gradually
evolving evaluative process based on the annotators accumu-
lating/integrating information over some temporal context win-
dow. The notion of cognitive inertia has been used to describe
evaluative states as progressively formed through accumulation
of evidence over time [13]. Motivated by this view, we in-
troduce accumulated delay compensation (ADC), implemented
via feature-level temporal accumulation. Rather than shifting
labels, ADC averages input features over some past window
which are then provided to the back-end as the ‘delay com-
pensated features’. As the integration window increases, the
group delay of this averaging filter grows, which in turn leads
to current predictions being shaped by preceding (delayed) in-
puts rather than solely by instantaneous changes.
We conducted systematic experiments on the RECOLA
corpus to compare ADC with conventional SDC across tempo-
ral configurations, based on speech-based CER tasks. Our anal-
ysis examines (1) the effective accumulation window length, (2)
comparative performance between ADC and SDC, and (3) ro-
bustness to temporal parameter selection. To model continuous-
time prediction dynamics, we adopted the Neural Ordinary Dif-
ferential Equation (Neural ODE) framework [10, 14] as the pre-

2. Speech-Based CER with Proposed
Accumulated Delay Compensation
An overview of the proposed framework is shown inFigure 1.
The pipeline comprises three stages: (1) feature extraction from
the raw audio signal, (2) input-level temporal accumulation to
obtain context-integrated features, and (3) time-series predic-

reduce potential feature bias, while keeping the encoder frozen
prevents interaction between the front-end and ADC, allowing
clearer evaluation of the proposed ADC method.

2.3. Implementation of CER
2.3.1. Data Preprocessing
The RECOLA corpus [20] was used following the official train-
ing and development partitions, where the development set
served for both early stopping and final evaluation. Each utter-
ance is 300 s long with annotations at 0.04 s resolution, yield-
ingT utterance = 7501frames including the initial frame. Gold
standard annotations were adopted as GT labels. Audio record-
ings were resampled to 16 kHz and processed using a frozen
pre-trained wav2vec2.0 base encoder [16]. Each 300–s record-
ing was segmented into 60 non-overlapping 5–s chunks before
feature extraction. The resulting 768-dimensional latent fea-
tures were concatenated to form full-length sequences. Since
the encoder outputs frames at approximately 49 Hz while GT
annotations are at 25 Hz, two-frame mean pooling was ap-
plied for temporal alignment, followed by boundary adjustment
(prepending the first frame and repeating the last frame if nec-
essary) to ensure exact length matching.

3. Experimental Results
3.1. Comparing ADC and SDC
To compare ADC and SDC, experiments were organised around
the effective delayτ. Prior work has reported that the optimal
SDC delay typically lies within 2–4 s [8]. Based on this ob-
servation, we evaluatedτ∈{1,1.5,2,2.5,3,3.5,4,5,6,7}s,
where the interval 1–4 s densely covers the commonly reported
optimal region and larger delays up to 7 s allow examination of
performance degradation beyond this range. For ADC, the ac-
cumulation window lengthT N s was determined according to
TN ≈2τ, and integer valuesN∈[1,15]were evaluated to
provide a dense coverage for analysing ADC behaviour.
Two complementary comparisons were conducted:
practice-level and fair comparisons. First, a practice-level
comparison follows the explicit realignment-based SDC
(RA-SDC) implementation commonly adopted in prior work
[10, 15]: during training, the firstτ–s annotation frames and
the lastτ–s feature frames are truncated to compensate for
the delay; during evaluation, the lastτ–s predicted frames are
discarded and the first prediction is replicated to match the GT
length. Under this comparison (Table 1), for arousal, ADC
achieves its peak atτ= 2 s (T N = 4 s) with CCC = 0.816,
while RA-SDC achieves its peak atτ= 1.5 s with CCC =
0.808. For valence, the best ADC performance occurs atτ=
6 s (TN = 12 s) with CCC = 0.485, though one seed is invalid
(CCC<0.1); excluding invalid runs, the best performance is
observed atτ= 4.5 s (T N = 9 s) with CCC = 0.484, while
RA-SDC achieves its peak atτ= 3 s with CCC = 0.473. For
both arousal and valence, ADC performs comparably to, and
sometimes slightly better than, RA-SDC. Moreover, the best
ADC performance exceeds the baseline of this study (0.746
for arousal and 0.385 for valence). These results demonstrate
the effectiveness of ADC for improving CER performance
and show that, without explicit realignment, ADC can achieve
performance comparable to the commonly applied RA-SDC.
However, the boundary manipulations involved in the
practice-level comparison confound the effect of delay compen-
sation with boundary processing. To isolate the delay mecha-
nism, we adopt a fair comparison setting in which both meth-
ods operate on the same extended sequence ˜x[n], defined as
˜x[n] =x[n]forn≥0and ˜x[n] =x[0]forn <0. The com-
parison then reduces to applying two filters to the same ˜x[n]:
the moving-average filterh[n]and the pure delay filterh SDC[n].

3.2. Robustness of ADC
The two approaches exhibit different sensitivity to temporal pa-
rameter selection. As illustrated inFigure 2A, sensitivity is
quantified using a seed-level interval around the seed-specific
optimum. For each seed, the temporal parameterT opt corre-
sponding to the highest CCC is first identified. The sensitiv-
ity interval is then defined as the span over which CCC re-
mains above a fixed proportion (95% or 98%) of this peak value.
When measured in the original parameter space (i.e., accumula-
tion window lengthT N for ADC orτ SDC), the interval is eval-
uated on a∆ = 1s grid. When analysed in terms of effec-
tive delayτ, the interval is evaluated on a∆ = 0.5s grid. If
a pointT opt±k∆is not available among the evaluated runs,
linear interpolation between neighbouring runs is used to es-
timate the corresponding CCC value.Figure 2Breports the
resulting sensitivity spans in the original parameter space. Un-
der this representation, ADC consistently maintains a broader
near-optimal region than SDC for both arousal and valence, in-
dicating lower sensitivity to the choice of the temporal param-
eter. A complementary comparison under the unified effective
delayτis shown inFigure 2C. In this representation, ADC still
demonstrates greater robustness for arousal, whereas no clear
robustness advantage is observed for valence. Overall, these
results suggest that ADC provides improved robustness rela-
tive to SDC in the original parameter space, and specifically
for arousal under the unified delay representation.

noise suppression. Based on this ranking, we selected the top

applied only to the selected individual dimensions, while the
remaining dimensions were processed using SDC aligned with
the corresponding effective delay (rounded to the nearest inte-

were the dominant factor, dimensions with higherρ i would be
expected to exhibit larger performance improvements.

parameter. Based on this, the top three and bottom three dimen-

smoothing evaluations were subsequently conducted based on
these dimensions.

4. Conclusion
This study revisited delay compensation in continuous emotion
recognition by introducing input-level temporal accumulation
as a structural alternative to shift-based alignment. Unlike SDC,
which explicitly realigns supervision through a predefined tem-
poral offset, ADC preserves the original input–label alignment
while inducing an intrinsic group delay determined by the inte-
gration window. In contrast to prior filtering-based approaches
for modelling temporal delay and contextual reliance, our for-
mulation is decomposable and cognitively interpretable, provid-
ing a smoother and more robust framework for modelling the
temporal dynamics of human emotion perception. Empirically,
ADC achieves performance comparable to SDC, while show-
ing lower sensitivity to temporal parameter selection, indicating
improved robustness. These results suggest that feature-level
temporal accumulation provides an interpretable and effective
formulation of delay, based on structural temporal integration
rather than explicit label shifting. More broadly, the findings
highlight the importance of considering annotators’ temporal
perceptual processes in CER investigations.
