Abstract
V oice Activity Detection (V AD) is the foundational gate-
keeper of the power–performance envelope in always-on edge
devices and conversational AI systems. An ideal V AD must pro-
vide ultra-low algorithmic latency for barge-in and end-of-turn
detection, maintain noise robustness, and minimize computa-
tion and footprint for edge deployment.
We propose QuadV AD, a highly optimized, small-footprint
neural V AD operating at a fine-grained 4 ms resolution. An
efficient data synthesis and alignment pipeline leveraging Mon-
treal Forced Aligner and fine-tuning on the TIMIT Acoustic-
Phonetic Corpus ensures precise boundary detection. Experi-
ments show improved onset detection with QuadV AD. The ar-
chitecture combines convolutional blocks with an LSTM Cell,
totaling 25 KB and achieves an RTF of 0.0014 on a 13th Gen
Intel Core i7 laptop. QuadV AD effectively balances latency,
robustness, size and compute, making it well suited for deploy-
ment in conversational AI systems and always-on speech inter-
faces.

1. Introduction
The rapid evolution of conversational AI has shifted user ex-
pectations from simple command-and-control interfaces to con-
tinuous, full-duplex interactions. In these modern dialogue
systems, V oice Activity Detection (V AD) acts as the funda-
mental gatekeeper. Its role extends beyond simple silence re-
moval; it dictates the system’s ‘reflexes’, determining when a
user has finished speaking (end-of-turn detection) or when they
are interrupting the agent (barge-in). V AD is also essential for
energy efficiency in always-on devices such as smart speak-
ers, where the microphones operate continuously. Even a few
milliseconds of delay in detection can truncate the beginning
of speech, potentially degrading downstream word error rate
(WER) performance. Conversely, false positives lead to unnec-
essary computational load and increased power consumption
as high-performance application processors are awakened from
deep-sleep states. Thus, an ideal V AD system should exhibit
low detection latency, a compact model footprint with minimal
computational complexity, and strong robustness under noisy
acoustic conditions.
Current state-of-the-art approaches typically do not sat-
isfy all the above-mentioned constraints simultaneously. Signal
processing-based methods, such as WebRTC V AD [1], are com-
putationally negligible but struggle significantly in low Signal-
to-Noise Ratio (SNR) environments.

Deep learning-based V AD systems have largely replaced
traditional signal processing-based [2, 3] and statistical model-
based approaches [4, 5] due to their superior robustness in
noisy and real-world conditions. Recent research has focused
on lightweight convolutional and hybrid architectures that of-
fer competitive performance while maintaining low complexity
for real-time and embedded deployment. One such model is
MarbleNet [6], which employs deep 1D time–channel separa-
ble convolutions to reduce the complexity of the model. This
design results in a lightweight architecture with 88k parameters.
Although this architecture is computationally efficient, it lacked
explicit mechanisms for modeling long-term temporal depen-
dencies, which limited performance in challenging conditions.
Recurrent neural networks (RNNs), long short-term memories
(LSTMs), along with temporal convolutional networks (TCNs)
were introduced to better exploit temporal context, enabling im-
proved modeling of speech dynamics. ResectNet [7] introduced
an efficient and lightweight online V AD that uses sinc convolu-
tions for feature extraction, followed by depthwise and point-
wise group convolutions, frequency shift modules, and a RNN
for temporal modeling. With approximately 4.2k parameters,
the model targets embedded deployment, reducing compute and
memory cost without compromising detection accuracy. Li et
al. [8] proposed a lightweight V AD model based on a TCN
that processes longer audio contexts. The authors introduced a
smoothed loss to suppress transient spikes and a weighted loss
to reduce onset and offset boundary errors, improving tempo-
ral consistency and segmentation accuracy. Transformer-based
V AD [9] models have recently emerged, driven by advances in
self-attention for speech and audio processing, but they typi-
cally incur substantial computational cost and increased latency.
Silero V AD [10] and TenV AD [11] are open-source neural V AD
models trained on large-scale multilingual corpora, providing
robust performance across diverse languages and acoustic envi-
ronments. However, all the aforementioned V AD systems op-
erate at higher frame resolutions ranging from 16 ms to 40 ms
and primarily emphasize either robustness or computational ef-
ficiency. Such larger frame sizes introduce ‘quantization jitter,’
where speech onset and offset are tied to a coarse time grid,
often leading to the loss of critical initial phonetic transients.
This motivates the development of a high-resolution V AD sys-
tem that balances latency, accuracy, size and computational ef-
ficiency.
In this paper, we propose a resource-efficient QuadV AD
system that produces decisions at a 4 ms resolution, balancing
power consumption for always-on devices with performance for
downstream speech processing and conversational AI. Section
2 describes the data synthesis strategy that was used to train the
proposed V AD system. Section 3 details the proposed method-
ology, including the model architecture, loss function, and train-

2. Data Curation Strategy
To build a robust high-resolution QuadV AD, an in-house data
strategy was developed to cover a wide variety of signals en-
countered in real-world scenarios. The initial training dataset
comprises 200 hours of data, augmented from 100 hours of se-
lected clean speech from the LibriSpeech corpus [12] as it pro-
vides corresponding text transcripts and sufficient speaker vari-
ability. Text-to-audio alignment is used to extract precise word-
level boundaries required for estimating V AD labels. The audio
dataset is filtered to remove DC offsets, clipping artifacts, spec-
tral distortions, and low-RMS signals to ensure high-fidelity in-
puts and reduce factors that may adversely affect text–audio
alignment. We evaluated several alignment tools, including
NeMo Forced Aligner [13], Montreal Forced Aligner (MFA)
[14] (with pre-trained English US arpa acoustic and language
models), Parakeet-tdt-0.6b-v3 [15], and Whisper (Large-v3)
[16], on a subset of TIMIT test set [17] to compare their word-
level alignment performance against hand-verified ‘golden’ la-
bels. Our analysis showed that MFA produced reliable align-
ments, with a maximum offset of approximately 50 ms, pri-
marily near sentence boundaries. In contrast, Whisper exhib-
ited offsets of 80–100 ms, NeMo showed deviations between
150–200 ms, and Parakeet exhibited even larger errors. This
makes MFA more suitable for generating reliable frame-level
supervised labels for high-resolution V AD training. Leading
and trailing silences were trimmed for 50% of the selected cor-
pus. Subsequently, stochastic silence modeling was applied to
approximately 50% of the corpus, where 1–3 eligible inter-word
gaps (>50ms), subject to availability, were uniformly sampled
per utterance. Silence segments of 0.2–3 seconds were then in-
serted at the midpoints of the selected gaps to simulate natural
conversational pauses.
To mitigate boundary jitter, QuadV AD is fine-tuned on an
50-hour augmented dataset simulated from the 5.4-hour train-
ing partition of the TIMIT corpus. Leveraging TIMIT’s hand-
verified alignments enforces a precise 4 ms temporal resolution
during transitions, enabling QuadV AD to achieve significantly
higher boundary precision. For this dataset, we concatenate two
distinct utterances separated by a silence segment with a dura-
tion uniformly sampled between 0.5 and 3 seconds.
Both datasets are resampled to 16 kHz, normalized to−25
dBFS, and labeled at a 4 ms non-overlapping frame resolution,
with ‘1’ for speech and ‘0’ for non-speech. The real-world
acoustic environment is simulated by first applying reverbera-
tion, followed by additive noise and gain scaling for 85% of
data from two datasets.
1. Reverberation: The RIR files, provided in DNS Challenge
[18] are used to simulate reverberant acoustic conditions.
Specifically, we convolve clean speech signals with the
released room impulse responses to generate reverberant
speech, thereby creating controlled and reproducible rever-
beration effects for our experiments.
2. Noise Mixing: We use noise samples from ESC-50 [19],
MUSAN [20], LibriV AD [21], and QUT Kitchen [22]. For
each dataset, 80% of the samples from each noise class are
randomly selected for training, while the remaining 20% are
held out for testing. During training, 1–4 noise segments are
randomly drawn from the 80% partition, potentially from the
same or different noise classes, and a 3–5 s random crop of
the selected noise is superimposed onto reverb-augmented
speech. The segmental SNR is sampled from[−5,20]dB
and restricted to 5-20 dB for babble-heavy and low-gain seg-
ments to preserve speech intelligibility.
3. Dynamic Range: The resulting signals are subsequently
scaled within the range of[−60,−15]dBFS to simulate near-
and far- conditions typical for edge-device microphones.
3. Methodology
3.1. Model Architecture
As shown in Figure 1, we propose an ultra-light weight archi-
tecture containing approximately 6k trainable parameters with
an estimated total size of 25 kB. The model employs a three-
stage pipeline consisting of learned spectral decomposition, hi-
erarchical local encoding and temporal context modeling.
3.1.1. Spectral Decomposition and Magnitude Estimation
Unlike conventional V AD systems that rely on a fixed Short-
Time Fourier Transform (STFT), QuadV AD employs a bank of
learnable convolutional filters to perform spectral decomposi-
tion in Stage 1. The time-domain input signal is segmented into
4 ms non-overlapping frames. For each frame, a 3 ms preceding
context is concatenated, resulting in a 7 ms input segment that
is fed into Stage 1.
A convolutional block with a kernel size of 64, stride of 16,
and 34 output channels processes the 7 ms input sampled at 16
kHz. The output of this stage has dimensions (B, T = 4, C =
34). The objective of this stage is to help the network learn a
complex-valued representation of the input signal. It does this
by separating the signal into learned real and imaginary com-
ponents using specialized convolutional filters. To compute a
magnitude-equivalent feature, the first 17 output channels are
interpreted as the real components, and the remaining 17 chan-
nels as the imaginary components. The magnitude is then cal-
culated accordingly, resulting in features of size (B, T = 4, C =
17). This design enables the model to adapt its spectral sensitiv-
ity, improving its ability to distinguish between noise-corrupted
speech segments and noise-only segments.
3.1.2. Stage 2: Hierarchical Local Encoding
The magnitude-derived features are then passed through a four-
layer hierarchical encoder arranged sequentially. Each layer
comprises a convolutional block with a kernel size of 3, fol-
lowed by a ReLU activation function. The layers use channel
dimensions of 16, 8, 8, and 16, with corresponding strides of
1, 2, 2, and 1, respectively. This stage serves as a local resolu-
tion refiner and compress the spectral information into a com-
pact feature representation. This hierarchical compression pri-
oritizes the most salient acoustic features required for accurate
4 ms boundary detection while maintaining low computational
complexity. The output feature dimension is (B, T = 1, C = 16).
3.1.3. Stage 3: Temporal Modeling and Classification
The final stage integrates local spectral cues into a broader tem-
poral context using an LSTM cell with hidden size of 16. This
temporal modeling smooths the high-resolution encoder outputs
to reduce the frame-level spurious transitions between speech
and non-speech. The LSTM outputs are subsequently passed
through a convolutional layer with a single output channel, fol-
lowed by a sigmoid activation function, producing frame-wise
speech probabilities at 4ms temporal resolution.

3.2. Multi-Objective Optimization Loss Function
We use a multi-objective loss function (1) consisting of binary
cross-entropy (BCE) (2) and smoothed loss (3). At 4 ms resolu-
tion, speech boundaries can be ambiguous, leading to unstable
predictions as illustrated in Figure 2. The smoothed loss acts
as a regularizer, suppressing short-duration spurious prediction
spikes and encouraging gradual transitions in the output proba-
bilities. This improves stability and allows the model to remain
robust across varying SNRs while preserving the architectural
minimalism required for low-power embedded deployment.

speech, noise, and silence, 500 frames per class are randomly
selected and passed through the network, and the corresponding
LSTM hidden activations are projected onto a two-dimensional
space using t-SNE [23], as illustrated in Figure 3. The resulting
visualization exhibits distinct clusters with minimum overlap,
indicating that the model learns a well-separated latent repre-
sentation for effective speech/non-speech discrimination.

baseline models to assess the efficacy of proposed QuadV AD.

5. Conclusion
QuadV AD attains a consistent 4 ms boundary resolution
through a multi-stage training framework. It is first trained us-
ing robust alignments generated with MFA and subsequently
fine-tuned on the TIMIT corpus to enhance temporal precision.
QuadV AD demonstrates similar performance trends when eval-
uated across other languages, indicating its cross-lingual robust-
ness. The ultra-lightweight design, with a model size of 25 kB
and computational complexity of 25 MFLOPs, is optimized for
deployment on resource-constrained, always-on devices. The
system achieves an RTF of 0.0014 on a 13th Gen Intel Core i7
processor. Owing to its robustness and precise boundary de-
tection, QuadV AD is well suited for integration into conversa-
tional AI systems and smart devices, particularly for wake-word
detection applications.
