English V owel Perceptual Training under Multitalker Babble: A Comparison
of Humans and Large Language Models

1 Centre for Language and Speech Technology, Radboud University, The Netherlands
2 Donders Institute for Brain, Cognition and Behaviour, Radboud University, The Netherlands

Abstract
Perceptual training with multitalker babble might benefit sec-
ond language (L2) listeners. However, conducting perception
experiments with L2 listeners to determine the optimal num-
ber of talkers is complex and time-consuming. Neural models
are inspired by the function of the human brain and could po-
tentially serve as an alternative. A previous study examined
how babble produced by 2 and 6 talkers affected the percep-
tual learning of L2 listeners and compared human results with
Wav2Vec2.0. In this study, we extend this work by explor-
ing multilingual models (the Whisper model and a speech large
language model [LLM]: Qwen2.5-Omni-7B) and by evaluating
how closely their performance aligns with that of L2 listeners.
Our results show that the speech LLM not only improves ac-
curacy through babble training, but also exhibits trends more
similar to those of L2 listeners, and it achieves closer accuracy
to L2 listeners under speech-shaped noise conditions than the
Whisper and Wav2Vec2.0 models.
Index Terms: L2 English, perception, speech LLM, babble
training
1. Introduction
Speech perception plays an important role in second-language
(L2) pronunciation learning. Models such as the Speech Learn-
ing Model [1] and the Perceptual Assimilation Model [2] show
that learners’ ability to accurately form perceptual categories
for non-native sounds is closely related to their success in pro-
ducing them. However, many L2 learners struggle to perceive
non-native phonetic contrasts that are absent in their first lan-
guage, which limits their pronunciation development. There-
fore, improving learners’ perceptual sensitivity through targeted
training has become an important area of L2 learning research.
Perception training with computer-assisted language learn-
ing (CALL) systems can potentially support listeners in distin-
guishing, categorizing, and processing speech sounds, particu-
larly in challenging listening environments [3] or when acquir-
ing an L2 [4]. Research has demonstrated that targeted per-
ception training with computer-assisted perceptual training is
highly effective and can significantly enhance L2 learners’ abil-
ity to perceive difficult contrasts, especially when high variabil-
ity phonetic training (HVPT) [5] is applied. Corrective feed-
back during HVPT can improve listeners’ perception of L2
sounds and can especially help them generalize to new words
produced by novel talkers [6]. While perceptual training in
noisy conditions has been studied [7, 8], evidence for the spe-
cific benefits of training in noise, compared to training in quiet,
for improving perception of L2 sound remains limited and un-
clear. A recent study reported that training with a multitalker
babble (MTB) background can improve Dutch L2 learners’ per-
ception of English vowels [9]. However, these studies typically
require recruiting separate participant groups, each trained with
babble created from a different number of talkers to determine
which babble configuration is most beneficial for learners.
Given that perception studies with listeners are complex
and time-consuming, and that neural models are inspired by
the structure and function of the human brain [10, 11], inves-
tigating perceptual training with neural models could yield use-
ful insights and potentially provide alternative solutions. Such
research may allow researchers to identify effective perceptual
training conditions and directly apply them to human listeners
in CALL systems. Previous research has explored the potential
of such models for perception studies [12], for instance, by in-
vestigating Hidden Markov models (HMMs) and Kaldi-based
deep belief network acoustic models. Results showed that the
Kaldi-based neural model was able to model the difficulty of
Japanese native listeners in American English /ô/-/l/, and also
model the vowel length contrasts in Japanese, which are eas-
ier to perceive for Japanese natives than for American English
native listeners.
Moreover, research [9] has shown that comparing the Time-
delay neural network (TDNN) [13] with the Wav2Vec2.0 [14]
model under quiet and speech-shaped noise (SSN) conditions,
the Wav2Vec2.0 model showed similar improvement on the
vowel pair discrimination task as L2 listeners do after training
with MTB. However, those models were mainly trained with
a single language and may not fully reflect L2 learners’ learn-
ing patterns. Moreover, these models performed much worse
on SSN conditions than L2 listeners. Recent advances in mul-
tilingual models, such as the Whisper model [15] and speech
large language models (LLMs), have demonstrated strong ca-
pabilities in modeling complex speech conditions. Trained on
large-scale datasets, these models have shown robust perfor-
mance across diverse and challenging environments. To bet-
ter understand how multilingual neural models may mirror or
diverge from L2 learners’ perceptual learning, we address the
following research questions:
1. Can multilingual neural models (Whisper and speech LLM)
benefit from MTB training like L2 listeners?
2. Which model shows improvement in vowel pairs discrimina-
tion accuracy most similar to that of L2 listeners?
3. Does training neural models with background noise that
matches the test set yield greater improvement than training
with MTB?
Previous research [9] has compared the effects of babble
produced by two talkers in an online study using HVPT with
babble with six talkers on Dutch listeners’ perception of Amer-
ican English vowels (/E/–/æ/ and /eI/–/aI/ ) produced in mono-
syllabic words. This study also compared the TDNN and the

2. Methodology
2.1. Datasets
The datasets we used in our experiments were the same as
those in the previous study [9]: including pretest, training, and
posttest sets. The pre- and posttests had 64 trials (32 in each
of two smaller tests, made up of 8 words × 4 vowels), and
the training phase had 100 trials (25 words × 4 vowels). Two
speakers (one female, one male) produced the pretest stimuli,
and two additional speakers (one female, one male) produced
the posttest stimuli. English monosyllabic words with the tar-
get sounds (/E/–/æ/ and /eI/–/aI/) produced by American talkers
served as the stimuli.
The babble in the training conditions was created from ei-
ther two (one female and one male) or six (three females and
three males) American English speakers reading a passage from
theWonderful Wizard of Oz[17]. Their readings were randomly
taken from different parts of the passage before being mixed
to create the babble. These talkers did not produce the test or
training stimuli. Immediately before the pretest and again right
before training, 12 trials (3 words × 4 vowels) with a quiet back-
ground containing words sharing the same characteristics as the
stimuli were used in a familiarization task. These words were
unique to the familiarization task and were produced by two
talkers (one female and one male) who did not produce the test
or training stimuli.

SSN [18] is white noise whose spectrum is shaped like the
long-term spectrum of speech. The SSN in our study was de-
rived from babble produced by the same six American English
talkers (three female and three male) used for the MTB stim-
uli, recorded while they read a passage fromWhite Fang[19].
For the L2 listener experiment, we experimented with the 2-
and 6-talker babble, while for the neural models experiment,
we further compared the quiet and SSN backgrounds.
2.2. L2 listeners perception experiment
Seventy native Dutch listeners [20] were trained in an online
study to perceive the four target vowels in a two-alternative
forced choice using HVPT in a pretest-training-posttest design
in which corrective feedback was provided only during the
training phase. Half of the listeners (n = 35) were randomly
assigned to be trained with babble produced by two talkers, and
the other half, with babble produced by six talkers. Adult partic-
ipants rated their English fluency based on self-reported data as
3.76 (SD0.81) out of 5, with 1 being “helemaal niet vloeiend”
(not fluent at all) and 5 “zeer vloeiend” (very fluent). Each lis-
tener received a unique randomized order of the stimuli. During
the pre- and posttests, both groups identified the sounds in quiet
and SSN.
2.3. Neural model experiments
To better compare the performance of neural models with that
of L2 listeners, we applied two multilingual models: Whis-
per and a speech LLM (Qwen2.5-Omni-7B) in addition to the
Wav2Vec2.0 model from the previous paper [9]. The following
procedures were applied to all neural models:
• Test pre-trained models with pre- and posttest sets.
• Fine-tune models with four training conditions (2-talker and
6-talker babble, quiet and SSN).
• Test fine-tuned models with pre- and posttest sets.
We used word selection accuracy as the performance metric to
compare all experiments. In the L2 listeners’ perceptual task,
each audio was paired with two word options. To mirror this
setup, we computed the edit distance between the word recog-
nized by the Whisper model and each of the two options sepa-
rately. The option with the smaller edit distance was taken as
the model’s selected answer. For the speech LLM, we directly
provided the audio along with the two options in the prompt and
asked the model to indicate which word it heard.
2.3.1. Wav2Vec2.0 model
The pre-trained Wav2Vec2.0 model used in the previous study
was ”wav2vec2-base-960h” from Huggingface, which was
trained on the Librispeech dataset (960 hours) [21] and has
achieved competitive performance on several speech tasks [22,
23, 24]. We used the forced alignment confidence score to rep-
resent the matching degree of the speech recordings with two
options. If the confidence score of the correct word was higher
than the score of the incorrect word, we concluded that the
model chose the correct option. The alignment confidence score
was obtained through the procedures of [25].
The Wav2Vec2.0 model was fine-tuned with Connection-
ist Temporal Classification (CTC) Loss function [26], a lower
learning rate, and 2 epochs.
2.3.2. Whisper model
The pre-trained Whisper model used in our experiment was
”whisper-large-v3” from Huggingface, which was pre-trained
on 5 million hours of speech. It supports over 99 languages
and can be used for automatic speech recognition (ASR), auto-
matic pronunciation assessment, and language detection tasks,
etc [27, 28]. We fine-tuned it with a lower learning rate and
2 epochs. For the Whisper model, we computed the character-
level Levenshtein edit distance [29] between the recognized text
and each of the two word options separately. Edit distance was
defined as the minimum number of character insertions, dele-
tions, and substitutions required to transform one string into the
other. The word option with the smaller edit distance was se-
lected as the model’s response.

The speech LLM we used in this experiment was ”Qwen2.5-
Omni-7B” from Huggingface. It is a multimodal model and
accepts text, images, speech, and video as inputs. In our ex-
periment, we instructed the model to assume the role of an L2
English learner whose native language is Dutch and to act as
a participant in a vowel perception experiment. For each trial,
the model was provided with a speech stimulus along with two
response options and was asked to select the option that best
matched the speech input.
During the fine-tuning process, we employed the Low-Rank
Adaptation (LoRA) [30] method. LoRA is a parameter-efficient
fine-tuning (PEFT) approach that adapts large pretrained mod-
els by introducing small, trainable low-rank matrices while
keeping the original model parameters frozen. This allows the
model to be specialized for the target task without updating
all of its parameters, substantially reducing computational and
memory requirements.
3. Results
In result tables: ’A VI’ means the average improvement of
pretest and posttest sets, ’TA VI’ means the total average im-
provement of two vowel pairs in pretest and posttest sets, ’Avg.’
means the average accuracy of pretest and posttest sets for ASR
models. ’Pre-trained’ means the results before fine-tuning for
neural models and pretest for L2 listeners, and ’Trained’ means
the results after fine-tuning for ASR models and posttest for L2
listeners. We used the average accuracy of L2 listeners in each
babble group as the final result presented in Table 2.
3.1. L2 listeners versus neural models on babble training
For the comparison between L2 listeners and the neural model
results, it is important to note that the L2 listeners took the
pretest on the pretest set and the posttest on the posttest set to
avoid memory effects. For the neural models, both pre- and
posttests could be used for testing, so we used the average ac-
curacy of the two test sets for the comparison.
3.1.1. L2 listeners results
The results from a linear mixed-effects model [31] showed sig-
nificant effects of vowel (/E/–/æ/, /eI/–/aI/) (p<.001) and the in-
teraction between test (pretest, posttest) and background (quiet,
SSN) (p<.05). There was no significant effect of training con-
dition (2-talker babble, 6-talker babble). Post hoc analyses [32]
suggested that accuracy was significantly higher for the per-
ception of /eI/–/aI/ than /E/–/æ/ (p<.001). In both pre- and
posttests, accuracy was significantly higher when the stimuli
were presented in quiet than SSN (p<.001). However, the accu-
racy was significantly higher in the posttest than in the pretest,
but only when the stimuli were presented in SSN (p<.001), not
in quiet, with the listeners improving their accuracy by 3.44%
(pretest: 82.32% versus posttest: 85.76%). This suggested that
training was more beneficial for vowel perception with SSN
than in quiet, regardless of whether the babble had two or six
talkers during training.
3.1.2. Comparison between L2 listeners and neural models
Table 2 shows the results of MTB training on L2 listeners and
neural models. In the quiet test set, before training, discrimina-
tion accuracy for the /E/–/æ/ pair was lower than for the /eI/–/aI/
pair across all groups. For L2 listeners, the accuracies were
83.30% and 97.86%, respectively, while for the Wav2Vec2.0
model, they were 84.37% and 96.87%. The highest accura-
cies for both vowel pairs were achieved by the Whisper model,
which even outperformed the L2 listeners, with accuracies of
93.75% and 100%. In contrast, the speech LLM (Qwen2.5-
Omni-7B) showed the lowest performance, with an accuracy of
87.50% for both vowel pairs. After training with MTB sets, L2
listeners benefited more from the 6-talker training set, with an
average improvement of 2.59% across two vowel pairs. How-
ever, the difference between pretest and posttest was not sig-
nificant. For neural models, only the Wav2Vec2.0 and speech
LLM models showed improvements in the discrimination accu-
racy of two vowel pairs, and the Wav2Vec2.0 model improved
by 4.68% only under the 2-talker babble training condition; the
speech LLM improved by 4.68% and 3.12% under 2- and 6-
talker babble training conditions, respectively.
In the SSN test set, before training with MTB sets, the dis-
crimination accuracies of two vowel pairs were lower than those
in the quiet test set for all groups. However, we still observed a
similar pattern for both L2 listeners and neural models that the
discrimination accuracy of /E/–/æ/ pair was lower than that of
the /eI/–/aI/ pair. After being trained on MTB sets, both L2 lis-
teners and neural models showed improvements. In addition, all
of them benefited more from training with 6-talker babble than
from training with 2-talker babble. L2 listeners achieved accu-
racies of 80.89% for pair /E/–/æ/ and 93.21% for pair /eI/–/aI/
after being trained with 6-talker babble, and the average accu-
racies of the Wav2Vec2.0 and Whisper models were all below
70%. Only the speech LLM model’s accuracy was closer to L2
listeners: the highest average accuracy for the pair /E/–/æ/ was
78.12%, and the highest average accuracy for the pair /eI/–/aI/
was 90.62%.

4. Discussion and Conclusions
Previous research has shown that L2 listeners benefit more from
babble training in the SSN condition than in the quiet test con-
dition [9]. The Wav2Vec2.0 model exhibited a similar pattern;
however, its vowel discrimination accuracy under SSN was sub-
stantially lower than that of the L2 listeners. Moreover, because
it was trained only on English speech, it may not fully reflect
L2 learning patterns. In this paper, we further conducted MTB
training on multilingual neural models: the Whisper model and
the speech LLM. Moreover, we extended it to the quiet and SSN
training sets to study whether training and test sets from the
same background noise could benefit neural models more.
In the MTB experiments, in the quiet test set, all three neu-
ral models achieved higher discrimination accuracy than L2 lis-
teners for the /E/–/æ/ pair before training. However, for the
/eI/–/aI/ pair, the speech LLM showed the lowest average accu-
racy. We also observed that the discrimination accuracies of the
L2 listeners, Wav2Vec2.0, and Whisper models were already
relatively high, suggesting a ceiling effect and leaving limited
room for improvement. This may explain why the speech LLM
demonstrated greater gains after training with MTB. In the SSN
test set, the discrimination accuracies of both L2 listeners and
the neural models were lower than those in the quiet test set
before training. In addition, the speech LLM’s accuracy for
the two vowel pairs was closer to that of the L2 listeners than
that of other models. The accuracy of L2 listeners and neural
models improved after training on MTB sets, and showed larger
average improvement in the 6-talker babble than in the 2-talker
babble condition. Based on the above findings, we can answer
our first research question that multilingual neural models can
benefit from MTB training.
When we compare the accuracies of these three neural mod-
els with that of L2 listeners under the MTB training conditions,
we observe that the Wav2Vec2.0 and Whisper models achieved
very high accuracy on the quiet test set, closer to L2 listen-
ers than the speech LLM. However, neither of them was good
at handling the SSN condition. The average accuracy before
training was below 60%. The speech LLM showed more con-
sistency on both the quiet and the SSN test sets. Although the
accuracy on the SSN test set decreased in comparison to that
of the quiet set, it remains around 75%, which is similar to the
pattern observed in L2 listeners. Based on the above discussion,
we can answer our second research question: although all three
neural models can benefit from MTB training, only the speech
LLM demonstrates more robust performance on the SSN test
set, aligning more closely with the performance of L2 listeners.
