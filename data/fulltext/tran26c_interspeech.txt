Abstract
We present a reproducible multi-label benchmark for speech
emotion recognition (SER) built from the MSP-Podcast V2.0
corpus. Our pipeline converts crowdsourced emotion votes into
multi-hot labels with controlled ambiguity and sparsity via re-
liability filtering, deterministic vote-to-label construction, and
lightweight time-shift augmentation. On this benchmark, we
provide a compact Mamba-based fusion baseline over MFCC
and log-mel features, following prior SER fusion practice,
while using linear-time state-space backbones for efficient mod-
eling of long audio. Using a validation-only global threshold
sweep for checkpoint selection and a fixed test-time threshold
for evaluation, our fusion model attains competitive micro-F1
on two MSP-Podcast test partitions (≈0.50on both Test1 and
Test2) while remaining substantially lighter than heavier base-
lines and recent methods. We release dataset splits, construction
code, and training recipes to support future work on scalable
multi-label SER.

Podcast, fusion, Mamba, MFCC, Logmel Spectrogram
1. Introduction
Speech emotion recognition (SER) has been studied for decades
with established benchmarks and model families [1]. However,
most existing datasets and models treat SER as asingle-label
task, assuming that each utterance expresses only one domi-
nant emotion. In practice, natural speech often conveys multi-
ple emotions simultaneously (e.g., happiness with excitement,
or surprise with anger), and collapsing annotator ratings into
a single majority-vote label obscures perceptual ambiguity and
systematic disagreement in subjective affect annotation [2–5].
To better reflect the co-occurrence and ambiguity of emo-
tions in natural speech, several works have explored multi-label
SER and disagreement-aware training strategies [6–8]. How-
ever, publicly available datasets for this setting remain limited.
A Korean multi-label speech dataset with deep fusion archi-
tectures has been proposed [9], but it is not publicly released,
limiting reproducibility and broader benchmarking. Some cor-
pora such as CMU-MOSEI [10] and conversational datasets like
MEISD [11] contain multi-label affect annotations, yet they are
primarily designed for multimodal or dialogue analysis rather
than large-scale speech-only SER.
In contrast, widely used SER datasets such as MSP-Podcast
provide large-scale naturalistic speech with crowd-sourced per-
ceptual ratings [12]. Nevertheless, most downstream SER
pipelines still collapse these ratings into majority-vote labels,

and reproducible large-scale benchmark for multi-label SER on
widely used in-the-wild speech corpora is still lacking [4, 14].
On the modeling side, modern SER systems increasingly
rely on Transformer-, ViT-, or SSL-based backbones [9,15–20],

and resource-constrained deployment [21]. This motivates ef-

ability.
In this work, we introduce a reproducible pipeline that con-
verts MSP-Podcast into a multi-label SER benchmark via re-
liability filtering, deterministic vote-to-multi-hot labeling, and
long-tail combination control. We further provide an efficient
dual-branch Mamba baseline with linear-timeO(T)sequence
modeling [22, 23], where separate encoders process MFCC and
log-mel features and a lightweight fusion module integrates
complementary cues.
Our main contributions are: (i) a transparent and repro-
ducible protocol for constructing a multi-label benchmark from
MSP-Podcast; (ii) controlled dataset variants that trade off per-
ceptual ambiguity and combinatorial sparsity; and (iii) an effi-
cient Mamba-based fusion baseline demonstrating strong per-
formance under a unified evaluation protocol.
2. Multi-label Dataset Construction
2.1. Source corpus, label space, and reliability filtering
We construct a public multi-label SER benchmark from MSP-
Podcast (V2.0), using its official speaker-independent Train/De-
v/Test partitions [24]. The corpus contains naturalistic speech
segments annotated by multiple raters, each providing categor-
ical emotion votes for the perceived emotion of an utterance.
These multiple annotations reflect the inherently ambiguous na-
ture of emotional perception in speech. Fig. 1 summarizes the
overall pipeline used to construct our benchmark.
Recent work has explored converting MSP-Podcast an-
notations into multi-label supervision. For example, EMO-
SUPERB derives distributional emotion labels from annotator
votes and determines target classes by applying thresholds to
these distributions, while EMO-Debias similarly binarizes dis-
tribution labels using a fixed threshold (e.g.,1/CwhereCis the
number of emotion classes) for multi-label evaluation [13, 25].
Such approaches preserve soft annotator agreement but rely on
heuristic thresholding, which can lead to unstable label combi-
nations.

rectly from vote counts using explicit agreement constraints, en-

3. Methodology
3.1. Acoustic Features and Input Representation
Speech segments are processed after standard preprocessing.
During training we optionally apply a lightweight time-shift
augmentation (right shift with silence padding).
We extract two complementary acoustic views: (i) a 100-
dimLogMel Spectrogram, and (ii) 40-dimMel-Frequency
Cepstral Coefficients (MFCCs).
Variable-length sequences are managed using padding and
length masks. We focus on MFCC and log-mel as lightweight
and widely used representations for SER fusion and omit addi-
tional handcrafted features (e.g., VQF/eGeMAPS) to keep the
benchmark simple and reproducible [9, 26].
3.2. Dual-Branch Mamba Fusion Model
Mamba backbone.Our encoders are built on the Mamba state-
space model (SSM) [22], which enables linear-time sequence
processing (O(T)) and is therefore well-suited to long acoustic
sequences. At each time step, Mamba updates a hidden stateht
from the current inputxt and produces an outputyt:

lowed by a linear classifier producing 8 sigmoid logits for multi-
label prediction. This lightweight fusion serves as a strong val-
idation backbone for our dataset reformulation.

4. Experiments
4.1. Experimental Setup and Protocol
All experiments are conducted on our MSP-Podcast multi-
label benchmark (Sec. 2) using the official speaker-independent
Train/Dev/Test partitions, and we report results on two held-
out test sets (Test1 and Test2). For fair comparison with prior
multi-label SER work [9], all models are evaluated using a fixed
decision thresholdt= 0.45.
Because static thresholds can bias early-stage validation un-
der class imbalance, we select checkpoints via a validation-only
global sweep: for each epoch we sweep a single threshold on
Dev to maximize Dev miF1 and choose the best checkpoint ac-
cordingly. Importantly, the sweep is used only for checkpoint
selection; all Test metrics are computed with the fixedt= 0.45.
Models are trained for 100 epochs (batch size 8, seed 42)
using AdamW with gradient clipping (max-norm 1.0). To ad-
dress label imbalance we use an inverse-frequency weighted
sampler. Unless stated otherwise, the default objective isbi-
nary focal loss(Eq. 4) following prior multi-label SER prac-
tice [9]. We also test LP+CB [8] as a secondary objective, but
the main benchmark results use focal loss.
4.2. Evaluation Metrics
Ourprimary metric is micro-F1 (miF1), aggregated over all
labels. We also reportmacro-F1 (maF1), i.e., the unweighted
average F1 across labels, which is more sensitive to tail emo-
tions. To capture set-level consistency, we further reportHL
(fraction of wrong label decisions; lower is better),Jac(set
IoU),ExAcc(exact set match rate), andBiAcc(mean per-label
accuracy) (Table 2).
4.3. Main Results and Analysis
Table 2 summarizes results under the unified protocol. Un-
der the fixed-threshold evaluation, differences are most evident
in set-level metrics (HL/Jaccard/ExactAcc) rather than micro-
F1 alone, highlighting the value of standardized protocols for
multi-label SER comparisons. This aligns with our goal: estab-
lishing a standardized benchmark and evaluation protocol rather
than pursuing a large-margin model gain.
Proposed fusion.Both Fusion-Gate and Fusion-Stack
combine MFCC-Mamba and LogMel-Mamba features but dif-
fer in the fusion head. Fusion-Gate learns adaptive branch
weighting, while Fusion-Stack performs late logit fusion.
Among our models, Fusion-Gate consistently achieves the best
set-level performance, improving miF1, maF1, and Jaccard
while reducing Hamming lossrelative to unimodal branches.
Fusion-Stack remains competitive but slightly trails Fusion-
Gate on most metrics.
Comparison to baselines.Compared with the re-
implemented Korean baselines, our fusion models are
overall comparable and often stronger on macro-F1 and
set-level metrics. Fusion-Gate outperforms Fuse-KR
(MFCC+LogMel) on Test1 and remains competitive with
the stronger MFCC+LogMel+VQF system while using only
MFCC and log-mel inputs. Several prior architectures and
large pretrained models (e.g., MulT and WavLM) achieve
strong micro-F1, but our Mamba-based models provide a
favorable accuracy–efficiency trade-off with substantially
fewer parameters.

5. Discussion
Despite strong aggregate performance, multi-label SER on nat-
uralistic speech remains challenged by extreme long-tail label
distributions. Our results in Table 2 and Table 3 make this limi-
tation explicit.
Tail emotions remain difficult.Although Fusion-Gate im-
proves overall set-level consistency (higher Jaccard and lower
Hamming loss in Table 2), several rare emotions remain dif-
ficult to detect under a fixed decision threshold. As shown in
Table 3, tail classes such asFear,Disgust, andContemptobtain
very low F1 scores, particularly on Test2. This behavior reflects
the strong class imbalance typical in naturalistic emotion cor-
pora, where head emotions dominate the training distribution
and models tend to make conservative predictions for minority
labels.
The gap between micro-F1 and macro-F1 highlights this
imbalance. While micro-F1 reflects overall prediction accuracy
dominated by frequent emotions, macro-F1 is more sensitive
to minority-class performance. The relatively lower macro-F1
values therefore indicate that tail emotions remain an open chal-
lenge even when overall performance is strong.
Future directions.Future work should explore stronger
long-tail learning strategies, including imbalance-aware objec-
tives, combination-aware regularization, and data augmentation
targeting rare emotion combinations. Another direction is lever-
aging large pretrained speech encoders as teachers or feature
extractors while maintaining the unified evaluation protocol in-
troduced in this benchmark.

6. Conclusion
We addressed the challenges of label ambiguity and extreme
long-tail distributions in multi-label speech emotion recogni-
tion (SER) [1, 6]. We developed a transparent and repro-
ducible pipeline that converts the MSP-Podcast V2.0 corpus
[24] into multi-hot labels through reliability filtering and de-
terministic vote-to-label rules [2]. Our proposed dual-branch
Mamba-based fusion model demonstrated that linear-time state-
space backbones [22, 23] can achieve competitive performance
(≈0.50micro-F1) while remaining significantly lighter than
heavier Transformer-based architectures [17, 33].
Crucially, our results reveal that while architectural choices
are broadly comparable on this benchmark [9], the detection
of rare emotions remains an open challenge under fixed de-
cision thresholds [4]. These findings suggest that future re-
search should prioritize imbalance-aware objectives [8, 28] and
targeted data augmentation for rare emotion combinations over
incremental model scaling [16]. By releasing our dataset con-
struction code and training recipes, we aim to support standard-
ized and scalable multi-label SER research [13].
