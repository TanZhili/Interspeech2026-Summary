Abstract
Streaming speech enhancement must meet latency budgets
ranging from a few milliseconds (for hearing devices) to hun-
dreds of milliseconds (for telephony/offline applications). Since
latency–quality is a trade-off, an enhancer should exploit as
much lookahead as the budget allows; yet most neural mod-
els are trained for a single fixed lookahead, so multiple budgets
usually require multiple full models. We propose a latency-
controllable speech enhancement framework consisting of a
causal enhancement model augmented with Latency Control
Adapters (LCAs) that allow switching the model’s latency by
changing how much future context it can exploit. With intra-
batch multi-latency training, one LCA model supports seven la-
tency modes; it surpasses per-latency models in enhancement
quality, maintains constant inference MACs, and reduces stored
parameters by 76%.
Index Terms: speech enhancement, noise reduction, latency,
streaming, real-time, low latency
1. Introduction
Speech enhancement (SE) aims to estimate a clean speech sig-
nal from a noisy observation by suppressing undesired compo-
nents such as environmental noise and competing sounds. SE is
a core front-end technology in telecommunication, hearing as-
sistive devices, and robust speech interfaces, where it improves
perceptual quality and downstream recognition accuracy [1–5].
In many real-world deployments, SE is not judged solely
by enhancement quality. The end-to-end user experience is
strongly affected by thelatencybetween capturing a micro-
phone signal and rendering the enhanced output. For interac-
tive communication (e.g., teleconferencing or V oIP), conver-
sational quality is known to degrade as one-way delay grows
to the order of a few hundred milliseconds [6–8]. For hear-
ing assistive and acoustically transparent devices, the constraint
is much stricter: when direct sound leaks and mixes with pro-
cessed sound, even a few milliseconds of delay can cause audi-
ble coloration (comb-filtering) and reduced preference [9–14].
At the other end of the spectrum, one-way broadcast or of-
fline post-processing applications may tolerate much larger la-
tency, e.g., several hundred milliseconds or more [15,16]. Con-
sequently, the latency budget available to the SE module is
highly application- and deployment-dependent, and may vary
even within the same application due to network jitter buffers,
echo cancellation pipelines, and device-side scheduling.
In general, there is a well-known trade-off between latency
and enhancement performance [12, 17–19]. Non-causal mod-
els (or models with larger lookahead) can exploit future con-
text to disambiguate speech and noise patterns, which often
improves perceptual quality compared with strictly causal in-
ference [7, 17]. When a bounded amount of future context is
acceptable, increasing lookahead can yield better enhancement,
and the optimal operating point depends on the latency budget.
Despite this, most existing SE systems are trained and deployed
under a single, pre-determined latency. Supporting multiple la-
tency budgets in practice often requires maintaining multiple
separately trained models and switching the entire model at run-
time, which increases development, validation, and deployment
complexity.
Motivated by these observations, we propose the concept of
latency-controllable speech enhancement: a single SE system
that can operate at multiple latency budgets and leverage avail-
able lookahead to maximize quality, while sharing the majority
of parameters across budgets. The key idea is to decouple (a) a
shared backbone that performs most of the enhancement com-
putation from (b) a lightweight, latency-specific module that in-
jects the appropriate amount of future context. As a first step
toward latency-controllable SE, we investigate a mechanism for
incorporating a fixed amount of future context into a streaming
enhancement backbone, named the lookahead module (LAM).
Based on LAM, we develop Latency Control Adapter (LCA),
a lightweight adapter inserted into the SE network; by select-
ing an adapter corresponding to the available latency budget,
the system can switch its effective lookahead at inference time
without reloading the full model. Experiments show that a sin-
gle LCA-equipped model can cover multiple latency budgets
with improved performance, while avoiding the need to train
separate models for each latency.
2. Related work
2.1. Latency performance trade-offs
Several works explicitly analyze the trade-off between latency
and performance under controlled settings. Sch ¨uldtet al.stud-
ied real-time inference behavior of a convolutional time-domain
separation/denoising network and reported that a small amount
of lookahead can substantially improve denoising quality at the
cost of additional delay [17]. Satoet al.confirmed that an
additional 120 ms of lookahead substantially improved SDR
and DNSMOS values in the target speaker extraction (TSE)
task [19]. Sch ¨uldtet al.and Satoet al.employ an imple-
mentation that is similar to the convolution-lookahead LAM
explored in our study. The key difference from our approach
is that they switch the causality of the 1-d convolution layers
withinthe Conv-TasNet model, whereas LAM is a lightweight
DNNappendedto the model, designed to introduce additional
lookahead to an arbitrary existing system. More recently, Wu
and Braun conducted a comprehensive comparison of multiple
low-latency strategies under unified training and evaluation con-
ditions, and reported a performance drop at low latencies [12].

2.2. Latency-controllable models in related speech tasks
While most SE systems are designed for a fixed latency con-
figuration, the speech community has exploredcontrollablela-
tency and multi-mode operation in automatic speech recogni-
tion (ASR). Dual-mode ASR unifies streaming and full-context
recognition by sharing weights across modes and performing
joint training (optionally with in-place distillation), reducing
deployment overhead and improving streaming accuracy [20].
Adaptive non-causal architectures further relax the fixed looka-
head constraint by dynamically selecting when and how much
future context to consume, explicitly trading off latency against
accuracy during inference [21]. Accordingly, the proposed
LCA in this study could also be applied to other speech tasks
beyond SE, such as ASR.

4. Proposed method
4.1. Latency Control Adapter (LCA)
We aim to realizelatency-controllable SE, where a single model
supports multiple lookahead budgets and switches its latency
at runtime without reloading the full network. To this end,
we propose aLatency Control Adapter (LCA): a bank of rel-
atively lightweight adapters inserted into the separator, where
each adapter is an LAM configured for a specific lookahead.
Figure 2 shows the conceptual diagram. At inference time, we
select an adapter according to the available latency budget.
4.1.1. Parameter sharing across adapters
A simple implementation learns a separate parameter set for
each adapter, resulting in a linear increase in the number of
parameters with the number of latency modes. Formally, let
the number of adapters and model parameters per adapter beK
andM, respectively. Then, the total number of model parame-
ters in LCA isK·M, which raises scalability concerns when
Kis large. To address this, we consider further reducing the to-
tal number of parameters in LCA by sharing model parameters
across adapters. In this study, we evaluate a scheme where, for
thei-th 1-D Conv Block, parameters are shared across adapters
for causal blocks and, separately, shared across adapters for
non-causal blocks. In this case, the total number of model pa-
rameters of LCA becomes2·M, which has the advantage of
being independent of the number of adaptersK.
4.1.2. Intra-batch multi-latency training
We promote knowledge sharing across latency modes byintra-
batch multi-latency training. For each mini-batch, we run the
same input through allKadapters and average the losses. Using

5. Experiments
5.1. Experimental setup
5.1.1. Data
Training and evaluation were conducted on simulated noisy
mixtures generated from clean speech in LibriSpeech [31] and
noise clips from the DNS4 challenge dataset [32]. All audio
was sampled atf s = 16 kHz. Noisy mixtures were created
by adding a randomly selected noise segment at an SNR uni-
formly sampled from[0,20] dB. We generated 50,000, 3,000,
and 2,000 mixtures for training, development, and evaluation,
respectively.
5.1.2. System configuration
As the SE backbone, we used a time-domain Conv-TasNet [28],
which offers a strong accuracy–efficiency trade-off, is real-time
capable on CPU, and remains a well-established baseline with
many extensions [19,33–36]. Following the notation in [28], we
setB = 256,R = 4,X = 8,H = 512, andP = 3. We eval-
uated two encoder–decoder configurations to cover both mod-
erate and ultra-low base latency regimes: (i) astandard latency
setting with encoder kernel/stride(L len,L shift) = (320,160)
and 4096 encoder filters, yieldingτ enc = 20 ms; (ii) anultra-
low latencysetting with(L len,L shift) = (80,40)and 1024 en-
coder filters, yieldingτ enc = 5 ms. Unless otherwise stated,
results use the standard latency setting.

5.1.3. Training and evaluation
All systems were trained under the same recipe, using the neg-
ative SNR objective. For LCA, we applied intra-batch multi-
latency training, i.e., averaging losses over allKadapters in
each mini-batch. At inference time, only one adapter is selected
according to the target latency budget. As objective metrics, we
report SDR [37] and DNSMOS P.835 OVRL [38]. To charac-
terize efficiency, we report (i) multiply–accumulate operations
per second of audio (MACs, in G/s), and (ii) the total number
of learnable parameters required to support all seven latency

Figure 3 (a) and (b) compares latency–performance trade-offs
between baseline systems and LCA. We consider: (A-1) a
causal Conv-TasNet, (A-2) a non-causal Conv-TasNet, (B-1)
multi-modelconvolution lookahead with LAM, where a sepa-

All streaming systems exhibit a clear latency–performance
trade-off: larger lookahead yields higher SDR and DNSMOS.
Notably, (C-2) achieves higher SDR and DNSMOS than (B-1)
across most latency points, despite using a single shared back-
bone; the average SDR gain over the seven operating points is
0.13 dB in this setting. We attribute this improvement to intra-
batch multi-latency training, which encourages knowledge dis-
tillation across latency modes during joint optimization.
Table 1 summarizes compute and parameter costs. Adding
a LAM increases MACs compared with the causal baseline.
This runtime cost is identical for (B-1) and the LCA variant
because only one lookahead path (either a LAM or a selected
adapter) is executed at inference time. In contrast, the param-
eter cost differs substantially when supporting multiple latency
modes. To cover seven budgets, (B-1) requires storing seven
separately trained full models (104.1 M parameters total). LCA
shares the backbone across modes and stores only the adapter
bank, reducing total parameters to 16.5 M (C-1) or 24.5 M (C-
2). The shared-parameter design (C-1) keeps the adapter param-
eter overhead independent of the number of supported latency
modes, while (C-2) provides better quality at the expense of
more parameters.
Figure 3 (c) repeats the SDR analysis under the ultra-low
latency encoder configuration (τ enc = 5ms). The same qual-
itative trend holds, and LCA remains effective in this stricter
regime. When compared at the same amount of latency, the
ultra-low latency condition yields better performance than the
standard latency condition because the finer analysis window
shift in the Conv-TasNet encoder/decoder enables a more pre-
cise projection of the waveform into the feature domain at the
expense of additional MACs [19, 28, 39]. Compared with the
multi-model baseline (B-1), (C-2) yields a larger improvement
in SDR (0.53 dB on average across the seven operating points),
suggesting that joint multi-latency training is particularly bene-
ficial when the base latency is very small. It is worth noting that
under the 5 ms latency setting, system (C-2) improves SDR by
0.45 dB over the causal model (A-1). This gain is attributable
to knowledge distillation from a larger lookahead system; con-
sequently, even in scenarios where only a single low-latency
model is required, the proposed LCA can still deliver improved
enhancement performance.
Table 2 investigates factors contributing to LCA effective-
ness (using (C-2) as the reference). First, training only the
LCA on top of a pretrained backbone while freezing the rest of
the model degrades performance substantially, indicating that
adapting shared backbone parameters is important when ac-
commodating multiple latency modes. Second, removing the
causal blocks inside LCA (i.e., constructing adapters only from
the non-causal blocks required for each lookahead) also re-
duces performance, suggesting that keeping a consistent adapter
structure across modes stabilizes optimization. Finally, replac-
ing intra-batch multi-latency training with a weaker strategy
(randomly selecting a single adapter per mini-batch) leads to
lower SDR, supporting the role of joint multi-latency training
for cross-latency knowledge sharing.
6. Conclusion
This paper introduces the concept oflatency-controllable
speech enhancement, which adapts the lookahead to meet la-
tency requirements and leverages the available future context
for more effective inference. To realize this, we proposed a
Latency Control Adapter (LCA)based on alookahead module
(LAM)using dilated convolutions, and enabled runtime latency
switching by selecting a latency-specific adapter. Compared
with a baseline that prepares separate models for each latency,
the proposed LCA shares the backbone parameters across la-
tency budgets and switches only the lightweight adapter, reduc-
ing the total number of model parameters while improving en-
hancement performance at each latency.
