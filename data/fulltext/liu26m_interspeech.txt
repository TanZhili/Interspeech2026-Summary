Abstract
Multimodal emotion recognition is limited by the difficulty
of collecting and labelling large-scale emotional data, as well
as the noisy and incomplete modalities commonly observed
in emotional databases. To address these issues, we intro-
duce MultiEmoVec, an unsupervised pre-training framework
that learns general-purpose multimodal emotion representations
via cross-modal reconstructions, contrastive learning, and adap-
tive loss scaling. Masked reconstruction recovers missing infor-
mation across modalities, while denoising reconstruction im-
proves robustness to noisy inputs. Cross-modal attention with
a transformer encoder integrates features, and momentum con-
trast learns discriminative representations. After pre-training,
the encoder is frozen to extract representations for lightweight
classification on multiple databases. Results, including ablation
and cross-database evaluations, demonstrate the framework’s
effectiveness and generalisation ability.

1. Introduction
Multimodal emotion recognition (MER) integrates complemen-
tary cues from multiple modalities, improving robustness and
contextual understanding [1]. Most existing MER systems are
trained in a supervised manner [2, 3] to exploit cross-modal
cues effectively. However, these approaches rely on large-scale
labelled databases that are expensive to collect and annotate. To
address this challenge, unsupervised pre-training strategies for
MER are gaining attention, particularly those that learn multi-
modal representations without emotion annotations.
Some unsupervised multimodal systems fall into
clustering-based methods, such as multi-view clustering [4, 5].
Although these approaches were not originally developed for
emotion recognition, they have been adapted to MER. These
methods can discover sentiment clusters; however, emotional
expressions are subtle, ambiguous, and context-dependent,
leading to overlapping feature distributions and weak inter-class
separability that clustering-based methods struggle to handle,
while their reliance on shallow, hand-crafted representations
further limits their ability to capture the semantic complexity of
human emotions. Recent research has shifted toward unsuper-
vised feature representation learning, where the goal is to learn
rich, transferable emotional representations without relying
on manual annotations. For instance, using text as an anchor
to align cross-modal emotional semantics via contrastive
learning for unified multimodal emotion representation has
been explored [6]. However, this approach is fundamentally
constrained by the assumption that text is always the most
reliable emotional carrier, which may fail when textual cues are
noisy or semantically inconsistent with the actual emotional
state. More recently, graph neural networks [7] have been
applied to unsupervised emotion representation learning by
explicitly modelling cross-modal relationships through graph
structures; however, constructing reliable graph structures is
challenging and suffers from high computational costs [8].
To address these limitations, we propose MultiEmoVec, an
unsupervised pre-trained framework for multimodal representa-
tion learning in MER. Instead of directly performing unsuper-
vised emotion classification, MultiEmoVec focuses on learning
feature representations that can be transferred to downstream
tasks. Specifically, momentum contrast (MoCo) [9] is em-
ployed to construct a discriminative embedding space. Since
pure contrastive learning is susceptible to missing or damaged
input signals, and considering that emotion databases com-
monly contain noisy signals, incomplete modalities, and incon-
sistent recording conditions, we introduce a multi-task recon-
struction strategy, where masked reconstruction recovers miss-
ing modality features, while denoising reconstruction with mix-
up [10] augmentation improves robustness to noisy data and
prevents overfitting. We employ cross-modal attention with a
transformer encoder to process modality alignment and integra-
tion, explicitly aligning modalities and enabling global interac-
tions to preserve complementary cues. Unlike graph-based fea-
ture fusion methods that require carefully designed structures,
our framework uses lightweight attention-based fusion, result-
ing in fewer trainable parameters than graph-based models [7].
Finally, to stabilise multi-task training, we incorporate an adap-
tive loss scaling (ALS) mechanism that balances the contrastive
and reconstruction objectives. In summary, MultiEmoVec en-
forces semantic consistency and structural discriminability in
the learned representations, which can be transferred to down-
stream classification tasks. Our contributions are as follows:
1. We propose MultiEmoVec, an unsupervised pre-trained mul-
timodal representation model for MER. The model integrates
MoCo to learn discriminative representations without emo-
tion labels, and incorporates a dual-reconstruction objective
(masked completion and denoising) to improve robustness
against missing and noisy modalities. Furthermore, an ALS
strategy is introduced to balance contrastive and reconstruc-
tion losses for stable multi-task training.
2. To evaluate the quality and transferability of the learned mul-
timodal representations, we freeze the pre-trained encoder
and assess its performance across different databases using a
lightweight classifier. This cross-database evaluation proto-
col demonstrates that the proposed pre-trained model learns
general-purpose features that generalise effectively to diverse
databases and downstream tasks.

2. Method
2.1. Multimodal Feature Extraction
The feature extraction methods for MultiEmoVec are shown in
Figure 1 (a). Speech features are extracted by a Wav2Vec [11]-
base model, which produces a 512-dimensional embedding (fs)
for each utterance. Wav2Vec is capable of capturing both pho-
netic details and paralinguistic cues. For video modality, MA-
Net [12] integrates multi-scale attention mechanisms to capture
both local facial expressions and global visual context, provid-
ing robust representations of visual emotional cues, resulting in
1024-dimensional representationsf v. Text features are derived
from the DeBERTa [13]-large model, which refines BERT en-
coders with disentangled attention and enhanced position em-
beddings. The textual representation (ft) has 1024 dimensions.
The above feature extraction strategies are chosen from the
baseline [7]. We retain these methods to ensure that perfor-
mance differences can be attributed to our proposed framework
rather than differences in input feature quality.
2.2. Dual-Reconstruction
To enhance the robustness and completeness of multimodal rep-
resentations, we design a dual-reconstruction mechanism that
operates through two parallel pathways, which are shown in
Figure 1 (b) and (c). The first pathway, masked reconstruction,
takes the original clean features as input, randomly masks 20%
of the dimensions from a single randomly selected modality,
and attempts to reconstruct the complete original features. This
process encourages the model to develop cross-modal comple-
tion capabilities by learning to infer missing information from
the other two complete modalities. The second pathway, de-
noising reconstruction, processes features that have experienced
comprehensive augmentation, including mix-up, feature mask-
ing, and Gaussian noise injection, and aims to recover the orig-
inal clean features from these augmented inputs. Gaussian
noise improves local robustness, masked reconstruction forces
the network to recover missing features from other modalities,
while mix-up smooths decision boundaries by interpolating fea-
tures, allowing the model to learn transitional states between
samples. By jointly optimising these complementary objec-
tives, the model learns to handle both information loss and com-
prehensive feature corruption.
2.3. Feature Fusion
We adopt a 9-embedding design consisting of 3 uni-
modal (f v,f s,f t) and 6 cross-modal attention embed-
dings (f vst = [f v s;f v t;f s v;f s t;f t v;f t s]) to ensure both
modality-specific and inter-modal information are preserved.
An overview of this design is shown in Figure 1 (d). The uni-
modal embeddings retain discriminative cues from individual
modalities, while the cross-modal embeddings model asymmet-
ric dependencies. This 9-embedding representation allows a
transformer encoder to capture higher-level interactions across
embeddings effectively, generating robust representations.
2.4. Contrastive Learning
We adopt MoCo to learn discriminative representations, where
each fused feature is augmented into two views serving as
queryf f usedq and keyf f usedk. A query encoderE q(·)and a
momentum-updated key encoderE k(·)generate their represen-
tations, while a dynamic queue maintains a large set of negative
views. The contrastive objective encourages the query to align
with its positive key while separating from negatives, enabling
the model to learn discriminative and transferable representa-
tions. For each queryq=E q(ff usedq ), the corresponding pos-
itive key is given byk+ =E k(ff usedk ), while a set of negative
keys{k −}is sampled from the query. We choose MoCo be-
cause it alleviates the requirement for large batch sizes by using
a momentum encoder and memory queue, which enables effi-
cient training with a rich set of negative samples. This makes
MoCo suitable for multimodal settings, where computational
cost is high, and batch size is often limited, while ensuring sta-
ble optimisation and generalisation.
2.5. Adaptive Loss Scaling

across tasks [14]. We employ an ALS mechanism that bal-
ances the contrast and reconstruction losses. This exponential

3. Experiments
3.1. Baseline
We adopt the MGAFR framework [7] as our baseline, as it is
a state-of-the-art method for unsupervised MER that addresses
the challenges of missing modalities. MGAFR consists of
four key modules, including dynamic nearest-neighbour com-
pletion for imputing missing features, cross-modal multiplex
graph aggregation for modelling inter and intra-modal relations,
dynamic feature refinement to reduce redundancy and noise,
and cross-modal alignment via contrastive learning to obtain a
shared embedding space. By combining graph-based comple-
tion with contrastive alignment, MGAFR produces consistent
and discriminative representations, making it a strong bench-
mark for evaluating our proposed framework.
3.2. Emotional Databases
The CMU-MOSEI [15] database contains 22856 annotated
video utterances from YouTube, with sentiment scores ranging
from -3 (negative) to +3 (positive), this database is used to train
the proposed framework. CMU-MOSI [16] comprises 2199
video clips, each of which is annotated on a seven-point sen-
timent intensity scale (-3 to +3). For CMU-MOSEI and CMU-
MOSI, we adopt the 2-class (negative and non-negative) and
7-class emotion classification setting (the same setting as the
baseline). The IEMOCAP [17] includes 10 actors’ emotion ex-
pressions, annotated with 9 categorical emotions. In our setting,
we select 4 and 6 categories to construct a relatively balanced
database in a speaker-independent condition. CMU-MOSI, and
IEMOCAP are used to fine-tune the lightweight classifier.
3.3. Experimental Setup
In the experiments, the database is split into training, validation,
and test sets at 70%, 10%, and 20%, respectively. The extracted
features from all three modalities are stored in NumPy format.
Each modality is first projected into a shared 512-dimensional
embedding space using a two-layer MLP with ReLU activations
and layer normalisation. In the masked reconstruction, 20% of
one modality’s feature dimensions are randomly masked and
reconstructed. Denoising reconstruction, where feature-level
mixing with parameter 0.5 is performed on each modality em-
bedding to improve generalisation, and Gaussian noise with a
variance of 0.05 is added to enhance the model’s robustness.
We use mean squared error as the loss function for the dual re-
construction process, and the mix-up coefficientαis set to 0.5.
Cross-modal interactions are captured using 6 multi-head
attention layers (8 heads). A single-layer transformer encoder
with feedforward dimension 4 times the projection size and
GeLU activation is applied for global fusion. The output se-
quence is mean-pooled and passed through a linear layer to ob-
tain the fused representation, and projection heads further map
the representation into 1024 dimensions. The key encoder in
MoCo is updated by an exponential moving average with mo-
mentum 0.999. A queue of size 4096 is maintained for nega-
tive samples, and the InfoNCE loss is computed with temper-
ature 0.06. ALS is employed to balance the contrast and dual-
reconstruction losses, we set the momentum coefficientβto 0.9,
the scale exponentτto 0.4, the warm-up period to 10 epochs,
and the clipping bounds range from 0.5 to 2.0.
Training is performed for 80 epochs with AdamW opti-
miser (learning rate:1e-4, weight decay:1e-5). To evalu-
ate the effectiveness of the proposed framework, we freeze the
encoder obtained from cross-modal attention and fine-tune a
classifier. The classifier is optimised by Adam with a learn-
ing rate of1e-3and a weight decay of1e-5, and trained for
10 epochs. All experiments are implemented in PyTorch and
conducted on a NVIDIA RTX 4090 GPU.
4. Results and Analysis
We adopt CMU-MOSEI as the pre-training database due to
its large scale and comprehensive modalities. This database
provides diverse and naturally occurring emotional expressions
across speakers and contexts, which helps the model learn gen-
eralisable and robust representations. After pre-training, the
encoder is frozen, and its learned representations are extracted
as fixed features. These features are then used as inputs to a
downstream supervised classifier, which is fine-tuned on other
databases to evaluate the transferability of the multimodal rep-
resentations. To make a fair comparison, the final lightweight
classifier is configured identically to the baseline model.

4.2. Ablation Studies
We evaluated the performance of the MultiEmoVec under both
tri-modal and bi-modal settings on the CMU-MOSEI database,
including ACC-7, Acc-2 and BF1, as reported in Table 2. The
results indicate that the text modality contributes most signifi-
cantly to the final performance, while the video modality offers
the least. One of the possible reasons for this result is that tex-
tual transcripts contain semantic cues that are highly correlated
with emotion categories under unsupervised conditions. In con-
trast, visual information from unconstrained YouTube videos is
often affected by variations in pose, face angle, and lighting,
leading to less reliable emotional cues.
To assess the contribution of each component, we con-
ducted ablation experiments by removing these modules, with
results reported in Table 3. Compared to training only a su-
pervised classifier without pre-trained features, MultiEmoVec
improves the Acc-7 on CMU-MOSEI by 12.4%. Incorporating
MoCo-based unsupervised pre-training improves Acc-7 by over
8%, confirming the effectiveness of MoCo. Moreover, intro-
ducing reconstruction objectives further enhances performance.
Masked reconstruction improves discriminability and further
improves accuracy, while denoising reconstruction achieves the
highest BF1 (89.2%). The complete model, which combines
MoCo with masked and denoising reconstruction, generates the
best overall performance, demonstrating that joint objectives
and ALS balancing lead to robust representations.
Additionally, we analyse hyperparameter sensitivity by
varying the mask ratio from 10% to 50%. Across these settings,
the average Acc-7, Acc-2, and BF1 are 54.44%, 83.90%, and
89.06%, respectively. We also vary the mix-up coefficient (α)
within [0.1, 0.5], and the performance variation remains within
0.8% across different values ofα. As for the noise robustness,
we evaluate the variance of Gaussian noise from 0.05 to 0.5.
The results show that the impact of Gaussian noise on the final
performance is no more than 1.22%.

Considering that both CMU-MOSEI and CMU-MOSI are
collected from YouTube, their data distributions are relatively
similar, which may explain the strong cross-database perfor-
mance observed in Table 4. To more reasonably evaluate
the generalisation ability of MultiEmoVec under distributional
shifts, we further conduct experiments on IEMOCAP, which
differs substantially in recording conditions and interaction
styles. To better demonstrate the effectiveness of our unsuper-
vised pre-trained framework, we compare MultiEmoVec with
a state-of-the-art fully supervised method under a controlled
setting where the feature extraction pipeline is kept identical,
and the primary distinction lies in the learning paradigm (fully
supervised training versus unsupervised pre-training). Specif-
ically, Lian et al. [19] report weighted F1 (WF1) scores of
78.36% and 58.64% on the 4 and 6-class IEMOCAP bench-
marks, respectively. Using representations obtained via unsu-
pervised pre-training on CMU-MOSEI, MultiEmoVec achieves
74.54% and 55.70% on the same tasks. Notably, under the more
challenging 6-class setting, MultiEmoVec performs within 3%
of the fully supervised model, despite not using any emotion la-
bels during pre-training. This result highlights the strong trans-
ferability and generalisation capacity of the proposed unsuper-
vised pre-trained multimodal representation framework.
5. Conclusion
This paper presents MultiEmoVec, an unsupervised pre-trained
framework for multimodal representation learning in MER.
Motivated by the noisy and incomplete modality inputs com-
monly observed in emotional databases, we introduce a dual-
reconstruction mechanism to enhance robustness. Combined
with momentum contrast and adaptive loss scaling, the frame-
work learns transferable representations. Cross-modal attention
further promotes modality interaction. Experiments on CMU-
MOSEI, CMU-MOSI, and IEMOCAP demonstrate consistent
improvements and strong cross-database generalisation, vali-
dating MultiEmoVec as a robust pre-trained backbone for MER.
