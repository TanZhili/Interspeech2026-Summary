Abstract
As speech generation technologies advance, so do risks of
impersonation, misinformation, and spoofing. We present a
lightweight, training-free method for synthetic speech detection
and source model attribution. Our method builds on model-
specific fingerprints that are computed as the average of the
differences between audio signals and their filtered versions,
referred to as residuals. Leveraging the Mahalanobis distance
of the residual for a given audio signal to these model-specific
fingerprints allows to identify the source model as well as to
distinguish real from fake audio. A broad set of experiments
across multiple synthesis systems and languages demonstrate a
supreme performance of the proposed approach on four tasks:
open-world single-model attribution, closed-world multi-model
attribution, real vs. synthetic speech classification, and out-of-
domain detection.
Index Terms: synthetic speech classification, model attribu-

1. Introduction
With the rapid advancement of synthetic data generation tech-
nologies, distinguishing between genuine and artificial speech
signals has become increasingly challenging. Public and open-
source tools now enable even unskilled attackers to synthe-
size highly realistic voice samples that closely resemble human
speech [1]. While these technologies offer clear benefits, such
as improving availability for speech-impaired individuals [2]
and supporting multilingual communication [3], they also pose
several security risks. Spoofing attacks on automatic speaker
verification systems, impersonation of trusted speakers, and
misuse in voice-controlled interfaces are all well-documented
threats. These threats can be exploited in various harmful ways,
such as disinformation campaigns [4], bypassing biometric au-
thentication [5], forging audio evidence in legal contexts [6],
or conducting identity fraud through voice cloning [7]. The
emergence of synthetic audio as a vector for impersonation and
manipulation poses a direct challenge to the security and trust-
worthiness of modern communication systems. Current de-
fenses largely focus on binary deepfake detection, i.e., decid-
ing whether an audio signal is real or synthetic. While this
line of research is highly valuable and reflected in benchmark
challenges such as Automatic Speaker Verification Spoofing
(ASVspoof) [8] and Audio Deepfake Detection (ADD) [9], it
addresses only part of the problem. Detection confirms manip-
ulation, but it does not allow identification of the system respon-
sible for the forgery. This, however, is crucial across forensic,
legal, and security domains.
Attribution matters for two reasons. First, it provides ac-
tionable threat intelligence: linking an attack to a particular
synthesis model reveals whether adversaries relied on commer-
cial APIs, open-source implementations, or proprietary tools,
thus informing risk assessment, replication potential, and mit-
igation strategies. It also allows prosecutors to strengthen evi-
dence chains by showing not just that an audio sample is fake,
but also how it was generated. Second, it underpins account-
ability and legal claims: rights holders can identify misuse of
proprietary models. The consequences of lacking attribution
are evident in real-world incidents, e.g., in June 2025, adver-
saries used AI voice cloning to impersonate U.S. Secretary of
State Marco Rubio, contacting foreign ministers via Signal and
voicemail in an operation designed to extract sensitive informa-
tion [10]. While detection alone could flag the audio as fake,
attribution would have offered critical insights about the tool
employed, accelerating response and mitigation. Compared to
detection, model attribution (or generative source recognition)
is still relatively underexplored, particularly in the context of
audio. Our work takes a step in this direction by showing how
lightweight attribution can be both feasible and effective.
Existing speech synthesis attribution methods typically rely
on multiclass classifiers trained in closed-world settings, limit-
ing their ability to generalize to unseen models without retrain-
ing. We instead propose a lightweight, training-free approach
based onResidual Statistical Fingerprints(RSFs), derived from
standardized average residuals of the audio signal. Attribution,
classification, and out-of-domain tasks are all formulated as
distance-based decisions using the Mahalanobis distance in the
residual space. The resulting framework is simple, adaptable,
and well-suited to rapidly evolving speech generation technolo-
gies. Building on these insights, our key contributions are:
•Residual speech fingerprints for attribution:Speech syn-
thesis systems leave subtle, consistent residual artifacts, cap-
tured as model-specific RSFs.
•Unified distance-based framework:Model attribution
and out-of-domain detection (OOD) tasks are formulated as
Mahalanobis-distance decisions relative to the distribution of
RSFs for each class.
•Open-world single-model attribution efficiency:We deter-
mine whether a speech sample was generated by a given tar-
get model, rather than by an unseen synthesis system or a real
speaker, without prior knowledge of all possible synthesis mod-
els. Our method requires samples only from the target model
during training and does not rely on data from all other synthe-
sis systems.
•Extensive evaluation:The approach is validated across di-
verse architectures, languages, and noise conditions, showing
high performance and robustness to realistic audio distortions.

2. Related Work
We review prior work on synthetic speech detection and attri-
bution, with emphasis on methods applicable to practical, auto-
mated identification of generative model outputs, and revisit the
concept of model fingerprints originally proposed in the image
domain for Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs).
2.1. Synthetic Speech Detection
These systems typically consist of an acoustic feature ex-
tractor and a classifier. Common features include Linear-
Frequency Cepstral Coefficients (LFCC) [11], Mel-Frequency
Cepstral Coefficients (MFCC) [11], Constant Q Cepstral Coef-
ficients [12], and long-term variable-Q transforms [13]. These
features are paired with classifiers such as Gaussian mixture
models [14] or neural models including X-vectors [15], Light
Convolutional Neural Networks (LCNNs) [16], Residual Net-
works (ResNets) [17] and SE-ResNets [18], graph attention net-
works [19], differentiable architecture search [20], and Trans-
formers [21]. While highly effective for detection, these meth-
ods do not address model attribution.
2.2. Synthesis System Attribution
Model attribution aims to identify the specific generative syn-
thesis system responsible for producing synthetic audio. Deep
learning-based synthesis systems outperform traditional para-
metric and concatenative methods, generating high-fidelity
speech with improved prosody and speaker similarity. Among
the leading approaches are GAN-based [22], flow-based [23],
diffusion-based [24], hybrid signal processing–neural mod-
els [25], and neural codec-based models [26]. These generative
models—particularly GAN-based systems—have been studied
in various classification and attribution works. For example,
Frank and Sch ¨onherr [22] observed high-frequency artifacts in
GAN-based speech synthesis. Yan et al. [27], Li et al. [28], and
Deng et al. [29] employed neural network-based classifiers, in
some cases extended with contrastive learning, to attribute sam-
ples to known synthesis models. M ¨uller et al. [30] introduced
attacker-specific embeddings, while Stan et al. [31] demon-
strated kNN-based attribution using representations from pre-
trained self-supervised models. However, these approaches rely
on supervised learning with fixed label sets, making them inher-
ently closed-world and requiring retraining when new models
appear.
Recent work on open-world attribution and verification [32,
33] extends beyond binary detection, but largely relies on deep-
learning pipelines requiring labeled data from multiple synthe-
sis systems, with limited analysis of robustness, distribution
shifts, and generalization to real speech. Open-world evalu-
ations such as ADD 2023 [9] still assume access to multiple
known sources during training [34, 35], limiting applicability
in single-model scenarios. In contrast, our approach is training-
free, does not require access to model parameters or architec-
ture, and naturally supports open-world single-model attribu-
tion. Related but complementary approaches include proactive
audio perturbation techniques [36, 37], which aim to prevent
unauthorized synthesis before it occurs, and digital watermark-
ing methods [38], which embed ownership or provenance in-
formation directly into generated content. While effective for
prevention and model ownership verification, these approaches
address different problem settings and are outside the scope of
this work, which focuses on post-hoc, audio-only model attri-
bution for forensic and investigative purposes.
2.3. Revisiting GAN Fingerprints
Model-specific fingerprints were first introduced by Marra et
al. [39] for image GANs. They model a generated samplexas
the sum of semantic contentI(x)and a model-dependent fin-
gerprintF, such thatx=I(x) +F. By suppressing semantic
content through spatial filteringf, residualsR=x−f(x)
approximate the underlying fingerprint. Model attribution is
then performed by correlating the residuals of a test sample with
an estimated fingerprint obtained by averaging residuals across
samples. Subsequent work has extended this concept primarily
through supervised learning, often targeting specific model fam-
ilies such as GAN-based synthesizers [40, 41], or by relying on
paired real–synthetic data [42], which limits their applicability
in open-world and single-model settings.
3. Problem Settings and Methodology
We consider speech synthesis systems including text-to-speech
(TTS) [43], voice conversion (VC) [44], and neural codec (NC)-
based generators [26]. These systems produce synthetic speech
that may be used for benign or malicious purposes. The de-
fender observes only audio signals and seeks to detect, attribute,
or reject synthetic samples without access to the internal struc-
ture of the generative models.
Attacker model:We assume a non-adaptive adversary with ac-
cess to one or more speech synthesis systems, either public or
proprietary. The attacker can generate audio that may undergo
common channel distortions (e.g., noise, compression, rever-
beration). The adversary does not know the defender’s detec-
tion or attribution strategy.
Defender model:The defender aims to detect synthetic speech
or attribute it to its generating model. Their capabilities vary
across scenarios, from having access to samples from a sin-
gle synthesis model to multiple generators. Attribution is per-
formed solely from the audio samples, without access to the
internal workings of the generative models.
3.1. Problem Settings
We define the attribution problem along two axes:
• Open-world vs. closed-world: Is the synthesis model used by
the attacker assumed to be included in the construction phase
of the attribution method, or can it be a previously unseen
(unknown) model?
• Single-model vs. multi-model: Data of how many synthesis
models is available to the defender for constructing the attri-
bution technique?
These axes define four related tasks considered in this work:
(a)Open-world single-model attribution (Fig. 1a):Determine
whether a given audio sample—potentially real or gener-
ated by an unknown system—was synthesized by a specific
known model.
(b)Closed-world multi-model attribution (Fig. 1b):Identify-
ing which model generated a synthetic sample when all can-
didate models are known.
(c)Real vs. synthetic classification:Distinguishing bona fide
(genuine) speech from synthetic audio.

3.2. Methodology
Fingerprinting approaches typically assume additive generative
artifacts detectable via correlation [39], as is common in image
data. In audio, however, artifacts are temporally distributed,
spectrally correlated, and entangled with semantic content.
We introduce a model-agnosticResidual Statistical Fingerprint
(RSF) framework that derives fingerprints directly from audio
outputs without access to the generative model’s architecture or
parameters. Our approach represents audio samples using fixed-
length, time-averaged spectral representations, extracts residual
embeddings through content-preserving filtering, and performs
attribution using covariance-aware distance scoring. The pro-
posed framework addresses three core challenges specific to au-
dio fingerprinting: (I) variable-length signals, (II) suppression
of semantic speech content while preserving generative arti-
facts, and (III) robust attribution via distribution-aware distance
metrics.

4. Validation of RSF Design Choices
This section validates key RSF design choices through con-
trolled experiments on held-out data, assessing the suitability
of distance metrics, spectral filters, and time–frequency repre-
sentations rather than optimizing for benchmark-specific per-
formance.
4.1. Datasets
To evaluate attribution across diverse synthesis techniques and
deployment scenarios, we use multiple speech corpora covering
different architectures, speakers, languages, and domains.
Augmented LJSpeech (A-LJSpeech) Benchmark:We use
LJSpeech [49], a single-speaker English corpus of13,100utter-
ances, as real speech. Synthetic samples are drawn from Wave-
Fake [22] and its extension [50], which primarily include GAN-
based vocoders. To broaden coverage, we additionally gener-
ate speech using diffusion-based TTS models (FastDiff, ProD-
iff [51]) 2 and a hybrid neural source–filter (NSF) waveform
model3 trained on LJSpeech. For each real utterance, a syn-
thetic counterpart is generated, yielding paired real–synthetic
samples spanning GAN-, diffusion-, flow-, and hybrid-based
synthesis. In total, A-LJSpeech includes speech from10dis-
tinct synthesis models.
JSUT Benchmark:The JSUT corpus [52], a single-speaker
Japanese dataset, is used to evaluate cross-lingual generaliza-
tion. The basic5000 subcorpus (5,000utterances) provides real
speech, while synthetic counterparts are generated using Paral-
lel WaveGAN and Multiband MelGAN models from the Wave-
Fake dataset.
ASVspoof 2019 Logical Access (ASVspoof LA) Benchmark:
Derived from the VCTK corpus [53], ASVspoof LA [54] con-
tains recordings from107English speakers, including real
speech (bonafide) and synthetic speech from19TTS/VC sys-
tems (122k utterances). Training and development sets cover a
subset of systems, with11unseen systems in the evaluation set,
providing a realistic multi-speaker benchmark.

4.2. Distance Metric and Spectral Filter Selection
To validate our fingerprinting design, we conduct single-model
attribution experiments on two held-out ASVspoof LA sys-
tems (A16 and A19). For each system,95% of samples were
used to estimate RSFs, and5% serve as a test set. Attribu-
tion is evaluated by comparing residuals to model fingerprints
using two scoring functions—correlation and the Mahalanobis
distance—with residuals derived from multiple spectral filters
(low-/high-pass, band-stop, band-pass) applied to STFT repre-
sentations (8 ms window, 0.125 ms hop). Performance is mea-
sured using the Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteris-
tic Curve (AUROC). Results (Tab. 1) show that the Mahalanobis
distance consistently outperforms correlation, confirming that
accounting for feature covariance is critical for reliable attri-
bution. Accordingly, the Mahalanobis distance is adopted for
all subsequent experiments. Among spectral filters, the low-
pass (1 kHz) and band-pass (5–6 kHz) configurations provide
the most stable, discriminative residuals and are used through-
out.
