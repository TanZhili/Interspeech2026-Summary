Abstract
Stuttering affects approximately 70 million people worldwide,
yet automatic speech recognition systems perform poorly on
stuttered speech, and dedicated detection systems rarely inte-
grate transcription. We present DysfluentNet, a hierarchical
multi-task framework that jointly performs fine-grained stut-
tering event detection and dysfluency-aware transcription from
raw waveforms. Our model couples a frozen WavLM-Large
encoder with a lightweight dysfluency-conditioned decoder
trained under a novel stuttering-aware connectionist temporal
classification (SA-CTC) objective. We further introduce a cur-
riculum learning schedule that progressively exposes the model
to harder disfluency patterns, and an annotator-agreement-based
difficulty partitioning of SEP-28k. Evaluated on SEP-28k and
FluencyBank, DysfluentNet achieves a macro F1 of 72.4 on
stuttering event detection and a dysfluency-inclusive word er-
ror rate (DI-WER) of 18.3 on FluencyBank transcription, out-
performing the best published baselines by 6.8 and 4.1 points
respectively. Code and reproducibility scripts are released pub-
licly.

1. Introduction
Stuttering is a neurodevelopmental speech disorder charac-
terised by involuntary disruptions in the flow of speech, includ-
ing sound and word repetitions, prolongations, and blocks. An
estimated 1% of the global adult population experiences persis-
tent stuttering [1]. Despite growing deployment of voice inter-
faces, modern automatic speech recognition (ASR) systems are
trained predominantly on fluent speech corpora, causing word
error rates (WER) for people who stutter (PWS) to be substan-
tially higher than for fluent speakers [2].
Accurate automatic stuttering detection could support two
complementary downstream applications: (1) clinical monitor-
ing, where speech-language pathologists (SLPs) track dysflu-
ency counts and types over therapy sessions without manual
transcription [3], and (2) ASR accessibility, where identifica-
tion and compensation of stuttering events reduces recognition
errors for PWS [4]. Despite their complementary nature, detec-
tion and transcription have historically been addressed as sepa-
rate tasks.

Recent work has applied self-supervised learning (SSL)
representations, notably wav2vec 2.0 [5] and WavLM [6], to
stuttering detection [7, 8], and encoder-decoder architectures to
dysfluency transcription [9, 10]. Nevertheless, three gaps re-
main open: most systems treat detection and transcription as
independent pipelines, missing their mutual constraints; multi-
task formulations for stuttering are underexplored relative to
other speech pathology tasks; and standard WER penalises sys-
tems that faithfully reproduce dysfluencies by computing errors
against fluent reference transcripts.
This paper makes four contributions.(1)We propose
DysfluentNet, a hierarchical architecture sharing a frozen
WavLM-Large encoder between a multi-label dysfluency clas-
sifier and a dysfluency-conditioned CTC decoder.(2)We in-
troduceStutter-Aware CTC (SA-CTC), a modified CTC ob-
jective with stuttering token types as explicit vocabulary en-
tries and utterance-conditioned temporal alignment regulari-
sation.(3)We design acurriculum learningprotocol over
five annotator-agreement tiers of SEP-28k with explicit kappa
thresholds.(4)Code, configurations, and data splits are
released athttps://github.com/mm0718-srmist/
dysfluentnet.
2. Related Work
2.1. Stuttering Event Detection
Earlier approaches to stuttering detection relied on hand-crafted
features such as MFCCs and LPCCs with classical classi-
fiers [11]. The SEP-28k dataset [2] enabled modern deep
learning at scale. FluentNet [12] introduced an end-to-end
ResNet-BiLSTM model detecting five dysfluency types. Sheikh
et al. [13] proposed StutterNet using a time-delay neural net-
work (TDNN) backbone and showed that data augmentation
and class-balanced loss substantially improve minority-class
F1. Bayerl et al. [7] demonstrated that fine-tuning wav2vec 2.0
on stuttering corpora outperforms MFCC-based systems, and
Changawala et al. [8] showed that Whisper encoder representa-
tions, when optimised layer-selectively, further advance classi-
fication performance on KSoF and FluencyBank. Transformer-
based classification using Mel-spectrogram inputs was explored
in TranStutter [14].
2.2. Dysfluency-Aware Transcription
Heeman et al. [4] demonstrated that clinician annotations im-
prove ASR on stuttered speech. Lian et al. [9] proposed SSDM,
the first end-to-end pipeline for phonetically aligned dysfluency
transcription. SSDM 2.0 [10] extended this with a neural artic-

2.3. Multi-Task Learning for Speech Disorders
Multi-task learning (MTL) has improved detection of other
speech disorders [7]. Liu et al. [16] applied wav2vec 2.0 fea-
tures jointly across four disfluency types in a multi-task setting
for variable-length segments. Our work targets a tighter cou-
pling between detection and transcription under a shared SSL
backbone.

4. Experimental Setup
4.1. Datasets
4.1.1. SEP-28k
SEP-28k [2] contains 28,177 three-second audio clips labelled
with six dysfluency categories (BLK, PRO, SR, WR, INT,
FLU). We use the standard split of Bayerl et al. [19]: 22,541
train, 2,818 validation, and 2,818 test clips.
4.1.2. FluencyBank
FluencyBank [20] provides read-aloud speech from 32 adults
who stutter with word-level disfluency annotations. We use 315
test utterances for transcription evaluation.
4.2. Baselines
We compare against StutterNet [11], MC-SN [13], wav2vec 2.0
+ SVM [7], Whister [8], and LLM-Dys [15] for detection, and
SSDM 2.0 [10] for transcription.

classes [13]. For transcription, we report standard WER and

4.4. Implementation Details
All models are implemented in PyTorch 2.7 and trained on a
single NVIDIA GeForce RTX 5090 (32 GB GDDR7) GPU. The
WavLM-Large encoder weights are frozen; only the layer-wise
attention scalars{αl}, the detection head, and the SA-CTC de-
coder are trained. We use AdamW with a cosine learning rate
schedule: peak LR= 3×10 −4, warm-up 10% of steps, weight
decay10−2. Batch size is 48, exploiting the larger VRAM ca-
pacity of the RTX 5090. Training runs for up to 60 epochs with
early stopping (patience 10) on validation macro F1. The SA-
CTC alignment window is±15frames (≈0.3s). We setλ=
0.2andβ= 0.5. Mixed-precision training uses BF16, natively
supported on the Blackwell architecture (compute capability
sm 120), removing the need for a gradient scaler. Audio clips
are resampled to 16 kHz; training applies SpecAugment [21]
(F= 27,T= 100, 2 masks each). Code, training scripts,
evaluation utilities, and random seeds are released athttps:
//github.com/mm0718-srmist/dysfluentnet.
5. Results
5.1. Stuttering Event Detection
Table 2 reports detection results. DysfluentNet achieves 72.4%
macro F1 on SEP-28k, a 6.8-point gain over the best published
baseline (LLM-Dys). The gains are most pronounced for mi-
nority classes: block detection F1 rises from 33.1 to 44.7, sound
repetition from 51.2 to 60.8. Curriculum learning accounts for
3.3 points relative to the w/o curriculum ablation; SA-CTC con-
ditioning contributes a further 2.1 points. Using wav2vec 2.0
instead of WavLM reduces F1 by 4.6 points (the two models
also differ in pre-training objective and data scale, so this gap
reflects multiple factors).
5.2. Dysfluency-Aware Transcription
Table 3 shows transcription results. DysfluentNet achieves
DI-WER of 18.3%, improving over SSDM 2.0 by 4.1 points.
Standard WER is also marginally improved (13.8 vs. 14.3 for
SSDM 2.0), indicating that dysfluency-aware training does not
degrade transcription of fluent content. Removing dysfluency

6. Analysis and Discussion
6.1. Layer-weight visualisation
The detection head concentrates mass on layers 18–22, which
prior probing studies associate with high-level semantic and
prosodic structure in WavLM [8]. The transcription decoder
draws more evenly from layers 6–15, consistent with their es-
tablished role in encoding fine-grained phonetic detail. The di-
vergence between the two heads’ weight distributions is consis-
tent with the shared-encoder, separate-head design: each task
exploits a distinct region of the SSL hierarchy, and freezing
the encoder prevents one task from distorting representations
needed by the other.
6.2. Effect of curriculum learning
Removing curriculum learning causes a 3.3-point macro F1
drop and increases variance across seeds, consistent with insta-
bility from early gradient updates dominated by ambiguously
labelled examples. The tier schedule builds a stable decision
boundary before exposing the model to SEP-28k’s full noise
distribution.
6.3. Limitations
DysfluentNet is evaluated on English-language corpora only;
generalisation to other languages is constrained by WavLM’s
pre-training distribution and SEP-28k’s English-only labels, so
multilingual evaluation remains future work. The SA-CTC
alignment window is a fixed hyperparameter (±15frames),
which may not suit the different temporal extents of BLK
(≈200–500ms) versus INT (≈100–200ms); a learnable, class-
specific window is a natural extension. The model also uses a
frozen WavLM encoder throughout; whether partial fine-tuning
of upper layers would further improve performance is an open
question. Finally, the FluencyBank evaluation covers 32 speak-
ers, and a larger-scale study across accent, age, and severity
subgroups is warranted.
7. Conclusion
We presented DysfluentNet, a hierarchical multi-task frame-
work for joint stuttering event detection and dysfluency-aware
transcription. The key design choices—sharing a frozen
WavLM-Large encoder, conditioning the ASR decoder on
detection logits via a cross-attention gate, introducing the
SA-CTC alignment objective, and scheduling training over
annotator-agreement tiers—each contribute measurably to the
final performance. DysfluentNet sets new state-of-the-art re-
sults on SEP-28k and FluencyBank. Our work demonstrates
that tight coupling of detection and transcription is mutually
beneficial. All code and models are publicly released.
