Abstract
Speaker Diarization and Recognition (SDR) is crucial for multi-
speaker audio. Cascaded systems suffer from error accu-
mulation, while many end-to-end (E2E) models fail to pro-
vide fine-grained temporal alignment. We propose a unified
Speech-LLM framework that predicts speaker identities, text,
and millisecond-level timestamps. Our method introduces a
FIFO output sequence, integrating voiceprint IDs and tempo-
ral boundaries into autoregressive generation. Training high-
accuracy E2E SDR models requires large amounts of annotated
data, which is scarce. To address this, we use a two-stage ap-
proach: first, simulating speaker turns and timing with synthetic
data, followed by fine-tuning with a small amount of real-world
data. Experiments on AISHELL-4, AliMeeting, and OleSpeech
show that our approach outperforms E2E models in transcrip-
tion accuracy and achieves diarization comparable to cascaded
systems. Our method balances transcription precision and tem-
poral alignment in real-world corpus.
Index Terms: speech-LLM, speaker diarization, speech recog-
nition, end-to-end system, multi-task learning
1. Introduction
As Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs) [1–3] and
advanced end-to-end speech dialogue systems [4, 5] demon-
strate immense potential in processing complex interactive sce-
narios, the demand for high-quality, large-scale conversational
data has grown significantly in both industry and academia. In
real-world applications, the task of simultaneously identifying
who spoke what and when in conversational audio is known as
Speaker Diarization and Recognition (SDR) [6]. This involves
two critical sub-tasks: Speaker Diarization (SD) [7], which
aims to recognize “who spoke when,” and Automatic Speech
Recognition (ASR), which focuses on transcribing the specific
spoken content. However, acquiring precisely annotated data
that includes accurate timestamps, speaker labels, and corre-
sponding text presents a formidable cost challenge.
Traditional SDR solutions primarily rely on a cascaded
framework. This architecture typically employs an SD module
as the front-end to achieve audio segmentation through V oice
Activity Detection (V AD) and speaker embedding clustering,
followed by a back-end ASR system for transcription. Rep-
resentative open-source state-of-the-art (SOTA) tools, such as
3DSpeaker [8] and Pyannote Audio [9, 10], have shown ro-
bustness in processing real-world meeting data by integrating
neural segmentation models and speaker embedding extractors.

Furthermore, some studies have explored using LLMs to post-
process and refine the outputs of cascaded systems. Neverthe-
less, cascaded systems inherently suffer from error propagation:
temporal offsets or speaker confusion in the SD stage directly
lead to misaligned ASR results. Moreover, this segment-based
logic often fails to capture the temporal correlations between
speakers in highly dynamic conversational scenarios, such as
overlapping speech.
To overcome the limitations of cascaded architectures, end-
to-end (E2E) modeling has emerged as a frontier in SDR re-
search. These approaches attempt to perform diarization and
transcription synchronously within a single framework via se-
quence modeling. For instance, Sortformer [11] introduced
a Sort Loss mechanism based on the chronological order of
speakers’ first appearances to solve permutation ambiguity and
utilized sinusoidal speaker kernels to integrate diarization in-
formation into the ASR encoder. META-CAT [12] employed a
“meta-information concatenation” technique to inject speaker
supervision from diarization modules directly into ASR fea-
tures, achieving a unified architecture for multi-speaker and
target-speaker ASR without requiring acoustic masks or source
separation. Recently, SpeakerLM [6] further explored the po-
tential of Speech-LLMs for E2E SDR by mapping speech fea-
tures and speaker embeddings into a unified feature space, and
many existing E2E works focus on “coarse-grained” SDR tasks,
which output only the speaker order and text without providing
the millisecond-level timestamps typically offered by cascaded
models. VibeV oice-ASR [13] provides a unified framework for
the single-pass processing of audio up to 60 minutes long, en-
abled by an ultra-low frame-rate speech encoder, thereby ad-
dressing the issues of context fragmentation and pipeline com-
plexity in traditional chunking-based methods. However, its
end-to-end SDR capability relies on large-scale high-quality
annotated data, which is often difficult to obtain. TagSpeech
[14] achieves unified modeling of multi-speaker speech content,
identity, and precise timestamps by decoupling its semantic and
speaker streams and leveraging a novel interleaved time anchor
mechanism. However, the relatively limited amount of training
data used in their study results in less competitive performance
compared with other large-scale approaches.
Existing end-to-end Speech-LLM approaches still face no-
table limitations. Some methods focus on coarse-grained SDR
and are unable to provide accurate millisecond-level timestamps
while simultaneously transcribing speech content and identify-
ing speakers. Others attempt to model the full SDR task but
rely heavily on large amounts of high-quality annotated data to
achieve reliable performance. In practice, such precisely an-
notated multi-speaker datasets are scarce and difficult to ob-

To address the aforementioned challenges of the SDR task,
this paper proposes an E2E solution based on Speech-LLM de-
signed to achieve synchronous prediction of speaker identities,
textual content, and precise timestamps. Recognizing that tem-
poral perception significantly impacts speaker recognition accu-
racy, we design a novel output sequence paradigm following a
FIFO strategy to unify temporal boundaries, voiceprint IDs, and
semantic information. To enhance model robustness in complex
dialogue scenarios under data constraints, we propose a multi-
stage training strategy that utilizes massive single-utterance
ASR corpora to construct diverse pre-training tasks, thereby
strengthening the model’s ability to represent real-world con-
versations.
The primary contributions of this work are summarized as
follows:
1.Exploration of SDR with Sentence-level Timestamp via
Speech-LLM: To the best of our knowledge, this is one of the
first works to utilize a Speech-LLM to simultaneously output
speaker identifiers, transcriptions, and their corresponding
fine-grained timestamps, addressing the limitations of E2E
frameworks in temporal alignment.
2.Structured SDR Output Designed for LLM Sequence
Generation: We design a sequence format that organically
integrates speaker information and timestamp tokens. This
scheme follows the FIFO principle, effectively supporting
joint modeling in multi-speaker scenarios while remaining
compatible with the autoregressive generation paradigm of
LLMs.
3.Two-stage Multi-task Enhanced Training Strategy: Ad-
dressing the lack of high-quality dialogue data, we propose
reinforcing model capabilities through simulated dialogue
tasks. We design three distinct training tasks to enhance
SDR performance. Experimental results show that this multi-
stage strategy effectively leverages foundational ASR data
to improve generalization in overlapping speech and rapid
speaker-turn scenarios.
2. Method
2.1. Model Architecture
As illustrated in Figure 1, the proposed model adopts a
sequence-to-sequence (Seq2Seq) framework [15]. To estab-
lish robust ASR capabilities, we employ the SOTA open-source
speech model, FireRedASR-LLM-L, as the backbone network.
This model utilizes an Encoder-Adapter-LLM architecture [16],
integrates a Conformer-based audio encoder from FireRedASR-
AED, which provides acoustic representations at a temporal res-
olution of 80 ms with a Qwen2-7B-Instruct LLM foundation,
totaling approximately 8.3B parameters.
To enhance speaker discriminability beyond standard ASR
representations, we incorporate an auxiliary WavLM-Large en-
coder [17] that utilizes a linear fusion layer to aggregate multi-
layer hidden states. Following the architecture in MT-LLM 1
[18], this encoder is succeeded by an adapter consisting of a 1D
convolutional downsampling layer to align the temporal reso-
lution to 80 ms and a linear layer integrated with GELU ac-
tivation and Layer Normalization to project the features into
the LLM’s latent space. These components are initialized from
1https://github.com/cuhealthybrains/MT-LLM
a version fine-tuned by Seed-TTS-Eval 2 [19] on downstream
speaker recognition tasks to ensure rich speaker-discriminative
feature extraction.
In the input stage, features from both the Conformer
and WavLM-Large encoders are concatenated along the tem-
poral dimension. This effectively introduces rich speaker-
discriminative cues while preserving the foundational ASR ca-
pabilities of the FireRedASR backbone. The outputs of the
two encoders are denoted asE c andE w, respectively. These
outputs, along with the token sequenceE p converted from the
prompt, are concatenated to form the model’s input,X in, as
shown in the following equation:
Xin =Concat(E c, Ew, Ep)(1)
Subsequently, the LLM generates the target sequenceE T
autoregressively based onX in:
ET =LLM(X in)(2)
2.2. Output Sequence Design
To unify multi-speaker diarization and ASR within the autore-
gressive generation framework of the Large Language Model,
we propose a structured output sequence scheme. This design is
specifically crafted to handle the intricate challenges of complex
multi-speaker scenarios, enabling fine-grained speaker identifi-
cation, precise temporal alignment, and accurate text transcrip-
tion. By leveraging the autoregressive nature of the model, it
ensures that speaker turns and speech content are seamlessly
integrated, maintaining coherence and consistency across over-
lapping speech segments.
2.2.1. Timestamps and Special Token Definitions
Following the timestamp quantization approach in Whisper
[15], we divide a 30-second audio segment into 375 discrete
timestamp tokens with a temporal stride of 80 ms, ranging from
<|0.00|>to<|30.00|>. To explicitly distinguish temporal
boundaries, two pairs of qualifiers are introduced:<|st|>and
<|/st|>for encapsulating the start time, and<|et|>and
<|/et|>for the end time. Furthermore, to support conver-
sations involving up to four participants, the sequence defines
four speaker identifiers:<|spk0|>through<|spk3|>.
2.2.2. Sequence Construction and Overlapping Speech Han-
dling
Following [20], each utterance is represented by a structured
sequence comprising the speaker ID, quantized start time, text
content, and quantized end time:
<|spk0|><|st|><|0.00|><|/st|>Hello<|et|><|2.00|><|/et|>
Unlike traditional cascaded systems that rely on explicit
source separation or multi-speaker diarization modules which
often introduce acoustic artifacts and computational complex-
ity, our approach adapts overlapping speech to the LLM’s au-
toregressive paradigm. By employing a FIFO policy, we se-
rialize concurrent utterances into a chronological token stream
based on the absolute start times of the utterance. Specifically,
when an overlap occurs, the model prioritizes the completion of
the ongoing speaker’s full utterance before initiating the next.
This strategy ensures semantic coherence within the sequential
output while preserving individual temporal boundaries:

2.2.3. Training and Inference Pipeline
During training, the input to the LLM consists of a concatenated
sequence of speech embeddings, prompts, and target tokens.
The speech embeddingE S is obtained by fusing FireRedASR
encoder featuresE C with WavLM featuresE W , while the
prompt and target sequences are tokenized and mapped to em-
beddingsE P andE T through an embedding layer. The model
is trained using cross-entropy loss, which is computed solely
over the target tokens, excluding the speech and prompt embed-
dings.
During inference, only the speech embeddingE S and
prompt embeddingE P are provided, and the model generates
diarization and transcription outputs autoregressively.
2.3. Training Strategy and Task Design
The implementation of a robust end-to-end joint speaker di-
arization and ASR model heavily relies on high-quality, ac-
curately annotated multi-speaker dialogue data. However, due
to the scarcity of real-world datasets that provide precise tem-
poral annotations for multi-speaker conversations, we propose
a two-stage training paradigm. This paradigm is designed to
progressively advance from simpler scenarios to more complex
ones, allowing the model to first learn fundamental diarization
and transcription tasks before tackling the challenges presented
by overlapping speech and intricate speaker interactions. This
stepwise approach ensures that the model can adapt effectively
to increasingly sophisticated real-world dialogue scenarios.
•Stage 1: Multi-task Pre-training.Utilizing single-utterance
audio-text pairs, we construct three targeted simulation tasks
on synthetic data to strengthen the model’s perception of
speaker identity and temporal boundaries.
•Stage 2: Real-world Fine-tuning.Leveraging the founda-
tional capabilities acquired in the first stage, we use real-
world dialogue data from complex acoustic environments to

As illustrated in Figure 2, training tasks are defined as
triplets (S, P, T), representing the speech input S, the prompt
P and the target sequence T. Task decoupling and isolation are
achieved through the use of distinct prompts. We have designed
the following three enhancement tasks:
2.3.1. Task 1: Temporal Perception Enhancement
This task is designed to improve the model’s ability to precisely
localize utterance boundaries within long-form audio.
•Speech Input (S):A single-sentence utterance is embedded
at a random temporal position within 30 seconds of back-

ground silence.
•Prompt (P):“Please transcribe the audio into text and indi-
cate the start and end times.”
•Target Sequence (T):Comprises the quantized start and end
timestamps and the corresponding transcription of the speech
segment.
2.3.2. Task 2: Speaker Discrimination Enhancement
This task is designed to strengthen the model’s ability to identify
different speakers in complex dialogue scenarios while enhanc-
ing its capacity to differentiate vocal characteristics and capture
speaker turn-taking events.
•Speech Input (S):Utterances from 3 to 4 distinct speak-
ers are extracted, randomly partitioned into multiple sub-
segments, and interleaved within a 30-second silent back-
ground. The construction ensures that adjacent segments be-
long to different speakers and we allow a temporal overlap of
up to 1 second between different speakers.
•Prompt (P):“Please identify the different speakers in the au-
dio and their corresponding start and end times.”
•Target Sequence (T):Contains only speaker IDs and start
and end timestamps, arranged in FIFO order. To focus specif-
ically on speaker decoupling, no transcription is included in
this task.
2.3.3. Task 3: Dialogue Scenario Simulation
This task is designed to bridge the gap between simulated data
and real-world scenarios by simulating complex dialogue flows
and concurrent speech events.
•Speech Input (S):Speech segments from 1 to 3 speakers are
randomly selected and embedded into a silent background via
random time shifting. To mirror natural conversations, we
allow a temporal overlap of up to 1 second between different
speakers.
•Prompt (P):“Please transcribe the audio into text and label
the speakers and their start and end times.”
•Target Sequence (T):Follows the complete output sequence
format described in the previous section, performing joint
modeling of speaker IDs, timestamps, and textual content.
3. Experiments
3.1. Data Preparation and Preprocessing
The proposed two-stage training scheme leverages datasets
from diverse sources, with Stage 1 constructing simulated tasks
from foundational corpora and Stage 2 further refining the
model using real-world dialogue data. In this work, we cap the
number of speakers at four, as this covers the vast majority of
scenarios in our benchmark datasets. This design choice helps
stabilize training and reduces vocabulary size.
3.1.1. Stage 1: Corpora for Simulation Tasks
To ensure the model possesses cross-lingual modeling capabili-
ties, we select three open-source datasets in Stage 1:
•AISHELL-1[21] is a large-scale Mandarin Chinese speech
recognition dataset, consisting of 150 hours of speech from
400 speakers, with both read speech and conversational data,
providing a rich linguistic resource for training ASR systems
in Mandarin.
•AISHELL-3[22] is another Mandarin Chinese dataset, de-
signed for speaker diarization and speech recognition, con-
taining 170 hours of multi-speaker conversational speech. It
includes natural dialogues from 100 speakers, allowing for a
more realistic simulation of multi-speaker scenarios.
•LibriSpeech[23] is a widely used English speech recogni-
tion dataset, derived from audiobooks read by a variety of
speakers. It contains 1,000 hours of speech, making it one
of the largest and most diverse datasets available for English
ASR and speaker diarization tasks.
All audio is preprocessed by trimming leading and trailing
silence, enabling accurate duration estimation for constructing
timestamp supervision in the simulated tasks.
3.1.2. Stage 2: Real-world Dialogue Corpora
The second training stage uses real-world meeting and podcast
data. The datasets employed are as follows:
•AISHELL-4[24]: A Chinese meeting dataset containing
120 hours of speech data recorded with 8 microphones. It
provides rich multi-channel recordings of Mandarin conver-
sational speech.
•AliMeeting[25, 26]: A dataset with 225 hours of recordings
that feature overlapping speech and diverse speaker interac-
tions, capturing more realistic meeting scenarios with com-
plex speaker dynamics.
•OleSpeech-IV-2025-EN-AR-100[27]: A large-scale, multi-
speaker English dataset that includes high-quality transcripts
and fine-grained timestamps. It covers a wide range of pod-
casts and teleconference conversations.
These datasets provide pre-defined training and test splits,
which we adopted as per the official partition. As a result, the
training and test sets were processed independently throughout
our data preparation pipeline.
Given that these real-world corpora typically consist of
long-form audio (ranging from several minutes to tens of min-
utes) with complex sentence-level speaker indices and temporal
annotations, we adopt a data cleaning and segmentation proto-
col inspired by [11]:
•Temporal Segmentation:A sliding window is applied to
segment long audio files, ensuring that training samples have
a duration between 20 and 30 seconds.
•Speaker Re-indexing:Speaker IDs are reassigned based on
their chronological order of appearance within each segment.
Valid segments containing between 2 and 4 speakers are re-
tained.
•Boundary Constraints:To prevent boundary effect interfer-
ence from residual speech of preceding segments, no over-
lapping speech is permitted within the first and last 1 second
of a segment.
•Content Filtering:The initial utterance of the first speaker
must contain actual semantic content; segments consisting
solely of non-lexical fillers (e.g., interjections or modal par-
ticles) are excluded. If the gap between two consecutive sen-
tences from the same speaker is less than one second, they
will be merged into a single sentence.

feature extraction across datasets, following the same prepro-
cessing procedure as FireredASR-LLM. The corresponding text
transcripts are normalized by removing punctuation and special

3.2. Training Details
3.2.1. Optimizer and Learning Rate Scheduling
Model optimization is performed using the AdamW optimizer
[28]. Regarding the learning rate (LR) strategy, the initial LR is
set to1e−6, which increases to a peak value of5e−6through
a linear warm-up phase over the first 10% of the total training
steps. Subsequently, following the configuration in SpeakerLM
[6], a cosine annealing schedule is adopted to smoothly decay
the learning rate, ensuring the stability of model convergence.

The training is executed in two stages:
•Stage 1 (Pre-training):120,000 steps on 8
