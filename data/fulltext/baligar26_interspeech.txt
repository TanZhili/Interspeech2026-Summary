Abstract
Many acoustic speech metrics often reflect a combination
of respiratory, laryngeal, and articulatory contributions, thereby
preventing direct measurement of articulatory dynamics. We
have developed an articulatory weakness index (AWI) derived
from electromagnetic articulography (EMA) or acoustic data.
We learn an articulatory-state coordinateZa from healthy EMA
data, where higher values indicate restricted articulator motion.
In cohorts of individuals with Parkinson’s disease (PD) and con-
trols, AWI is elevated in PD and correlates with motor severity,
while remaining stable across in healthy controls and speak-
ers with vocal hyperfunction. AWI provides an interpretable,
disentangled articulatory weakness index from acoustic speech
recordings, complementing respiratory and laryngeal measures.
Index Terms: articulation, speech disorders, machine learning
1. Introduction
Motor speech is the observable output of interacting respiratory,
laryngeal, and articulatory (R–L–A) subsystems rather than a
single, monolithic “speech quality” channel [1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7].
In Parkinson’s disease (PD), hypokinetic dysarthria is char-
acterized by reduced respiratory drive and loudness, dimin-
ished pitch and prosodic variation, and underscaled articulatory
movements [8, 9, 10]. Most objective speech metrics used in PD
studies, however, are derived directly from the acoustic wave-
form and are therefore subsystem agnostic: perturbation mea-
sures, spectral ratios, and cepstral indices all mix contributions
from respiratory, laryngeal, and supralaryngeal vocal tract sub-
systems [11, 12, 13]. This limits our ability to ask questions
such as“how weak are the articulators, holding laryngeal func-
tion fixed?”or to build composite models in which subsystem
deficits contribute separably to communication burden.
Articulatory motion–capture methods provide a more di-
rect view of vocal tract dynamics. Electromagnetic articulogra-
phy (EMA), X–ray microbeam, ultrasound, and real–time MRI
have been used to quantify coarticulation, working articulatory
spaces, and coordination in corpora such as USC–TIMIT and
MOCHA–TIMIT datasets [14, 15, 16]. EMA studies of invidi-
vuals with PD report smaller lip and jaw excursions, reduced
tongue displacement, slower peak articulatory speeds, and al-
tered jaw–tongue coordination, with links to intelligibility and
speaking–style manipulations [17, 18, 19]. These studies sup-
port articulatory hypokinesia as a key speech component of PD,
but they rely on multiple sensor–specific measures, small co-
horts in controlled laboratory environments, and hardware that
is not typically available in the clinic.
In parallel, deep audio–to–articulatory inversion models
can now predict multi–coil EMA trajectories from acoustic
features with good geometric accuracy [20, 21, 22, 23], and
acoustic latent indices derived via principal component analysis
(PCA) have been proposed as low–dimensional “voice quality”
coordinates in pathological speech [13]. Predicted EMA is usu-
ally treated as a hidden representation inside automatic speech
recognition and text-to-speech systems, and PCA–based acous-
tic indices remain subsystem agnostic.
To our knowledge, there is no EMA–grounded, audio–
estimable,scalararticulatory index that (i) is interpretable in
terms of real articulatory kinematics, (ii) can be computed from
speech acoustics via predicted articulator trajectories, and (iii)
can correlate with motor burden in individuals with PD. Fur-
thermore, no existing framework defines subsystem-specific R-
L-A indices on a common scale or provides an EMA-grounded,
audio-estimable scalar articulatory weakness index for PD.
Without a direct articulatory weakness baseline, we evaluate
our method against clinical scales and known PD versus con-
trol contrasts.
This paper addresses that gap by developing an EMA–
grounded, audio–estimable, low-dimensionalarticulatory
weakness index(AWI) based on a single low–dimensional artic-
ulatory state time-seriesZ a(t). We analyze (i) age/sex–matched
speakers with PD and healthy controls producing comparable
connected speech to assess sensitivity, and (ii) a large cor-
pus spanning healthy voices and vocal hyperfunction (a voice
disorder not primarily affecting articulation) to assess speci-
ficity. We investigate whether AWI (a) differentiates PD from
matched controls, (b) tracks clinical motor and participation
scales, and (c) remains comparatively stable across sex, loud-
ness, and laryngeal pathology. Importantly, AWI is not pro-
posed as a diagnostic label or classifier. We treat it as a contin-
uous, articulatory–biased descriptor of speech motor state that
can be clinically interpretable and combined in future work with
analogous respiratory and laryngeal indices to form an R–L–A
factorization of disordered speech. Specific contributions:
1.EMA–grounded articulatory time-series.We define a
low–dimensional articulatory state axisZ a from multi–
sensor EMA kinematics and show that it behaves as a
data–driven articulatory hypokinesia measures (higherZ a =
smaller, slower movements).
2.Articulatory Weakness Index (A WI) and audio–based es-
timation.We introduce AWI as scalar summaries ofZ a(t)
over connected speech, and demonstrate how the same in-
dex can be estimated from audio alone via a neural audio–to–
EMA model with affine calibration to ground truth EMA.
3.Subsystem–biased characterization of PD speech.Using
real PD speech with clinical ratings and age/sex–matched
controls, plus a vocal hyperfunction cohort, we show that
AWI is elevated in PD and monotonically associated with
motor severity (Unified Parkinson’s Disease Rating Scale

2. Methods
2.1. Datasets
2.1.1. Electromagnetic articulography reference dataset
We use the USC–TIMIT dataset [14] as the healthy articulatory
reference for learning the kinematic feature space. The dataset
provides 2D/3D EMA from six coils (upper lip, lower lip, jaw,
tongue dorsum, tongue body, tongue tip) with audio recordings
for read sentences and phrases.
2.1.2. Individuals with Parkinson’s disease
The primary clinical dataset is of 21 individuals with PD col-
lected atanonymousunder IRB approval. Each individual
produced the Rainbow Passage [24] and additional connected
speech [25] under typical speech prompts [26, 27] and com-
pleted patient–reported clinical scales of motor severity (UP-
DRS Total and Part III, V oice Handicap Index, Communication
Participation Item Bank, Hoehn & Yahr scale). We compute
AWI from typical connected speech for all individuals with PD
and correlate subject–level indices with these clinical scales.
2.1.3. Age/sex–matched healthy controls
For PD–control comparisons we selected age– and
sex–matched healthy speakers from the Perceptual V oice
Qualities Database (PVQD) [28]. Connected–speech avail-
ability yielded 9 PD–control pairs, and we applied the same
audio→EMA→Z a pipeline to both PVQD and PD recordings.
2.1.4. Individuals with vocal hyperfunction
To test specificity of the AWI to articulatory kinematic changes
rather than the presence of other voice disorders (primarily af-
fecting the separate laryngeal subsystem), we use a large dataset
containing 398 speakers with and without vocal hyperfunction.
All participants produced the CAPE–V sentence set [29]; we
analyze Sentence 1 (“the blue spot is on the key again”) and
derive AWI from audio via the surrogate EMA pipeline.

2.3.2. PCA and definition ofZ a
We fit aStandardScaler+PCApipeline onF USC. The first
principal component explains the largest share of kinematic
variance and is strongly negatively correlated with global dis-
placement range and mean speed (absolute correlation≈0.8),
indicating that higher component scores correspond to smaller
and slower movements. We take this first component as a raw
articulatory state coordinate and flip its sign so that higherZ a
reflects weaker, more constrained articulatory motion. Apply-
ing the same PCA transform to windowed features from any
utterance (EMA or predicted EMA) and aligning window cen-
ters in time produces a time-seriesZ a(t), which forms the basis
for subsequent AWI computations.

3. Results
3.1. Specificity in a vocal hyperfunction cohort
We first tested whether the AWI (utterance–level mean of
Za(t)) is strongly influenced by non–articulatory factors such
as sex, pitch, or diagnosis of vocal hyperfunction (a non-
articulatory disorder).
Figure 3 shows group–averagedZ a(t)for CAPE-V Sen-
tence 1, separately for male and female speakers. For both
sexes, normal and patient trajectories nearly overlap and re-
main within each others’±1standard deviation bands, with
typical differences<0.5Z a units. Temporal patterning is sim-
ilar across groups (dips around the stressed words, rises toward
sentence offset). These results indicate that AWI is largely in-
sensitive to sex, pitch, and vocal hyperfunction and is instead
dominated by supralaryngeal, articulatory kinematics.

We next studied whether AWI differentiates connected speech
of PD speakers from connected speech by age/sex–matched
healthy controls. For each speaker with PD and their matched
healthy control, we concatenated all typical connected–speech
sentences and computed a speaker–level AWI.
Figure 4(A) shows AWI for the 9 PD–control pairs. All
gray lines slope upward: controls cluster around AWI≈

and no PD speaker has lower AWI than their control. Fig-

Healthy controls are centered well below the USC–TIMIT ref-

3.3. Correlation of A WI with motor severity
We had 21 speakers for this analysis. For each speaker with PD,
we correlated AWI from typical connected speech with UPDRS
Total and UPDRS Part III scores. In Figure 5, median AWI
increases monotonically from Low to Mid to High tertiles; for
UPDRS Part III, medians rise from≈2.1(Low) to≈2.7(Mid)
to≈2.9(High), a shift of approximately0.8units. Individual–
level correlations are moderate and in the expected direction
(Spearmanρ≈0.5, Pearsonr≈0.4).
We also examined speech–focused patient–reported out-
comes. As summarized in Table 1, higher articulatory weak-
ness (AWI) is associated with greater self–reported voice hand-
icap (VHI; positive correlations) and reduced communicative
participation (CPIB; negative correlations), at magnitudes sim-
ilar to those observed for UPDRS. In contrast, associations

tied to global motor burden and speech impact than to coarse
staging or non–motor factors.

4. Discussion and Conclusion
The proposed framework reduces multi–sensor EMA kinemat-
ics to an articulatory state coordinateZ a(t)and an utterance–
level Articulatory Weakness Index (AWI).Z a(t)is the lead-
ing principal component of windowed supralaryngeal articula-
tory kinematic features (displacement, speed, variability, jaw–
tongue coordination), oriented so higher values indicate smaller,
slower, more constrained movements. AWI, thus, quantifies ar-
ticulatory hypokinesia rather than generic speech severity, with
transparent kinematic coupling ensuring mechanistic interpre-
tation.
Across three datasets, AWI demonstrates articulatory speci-
ficity and clinical sensitivity. In the vocal hyperfunction cohort,
Za(t)is invariant to sex and voice disorder, confirming AWI is
not driven by pitch or laryngeal pathology. In the PD cohort
with healthy controls, AWI is systematically higher in PD for
every pair, with frame–levelZ a distributions strongly shifted
toward constrained states. Within PD, AWI correlates with UP-
DRS Total and Part III and moderately with VHI and CPIB, but
not with age, disease duration, cognition, or non–motor scales.
Audio→EMA inversion enables AWI computation from
audio–only corpora but has limitations: the surrogate model
is trained on healthy EMA, PD kinematics may be out–of–
distribution since speakers with PD and their controls come
from different corpora. This evaluation covers a small sam-
ple size, English language, read speech, and a single inversion
architecture [23]. Future work could test alternative models,
spontaneous speech, and other neurological disorders affecting
speech articulatory kinematics (amyotrophic lateral sclerosis,
ataxic dysarthria, etc.)[30, 31].
AWI represents the articulatory component of a
respiratory–laryngeal–articulatory (R–L–A) factorization
of speech motor control. Analogous laryngeal and respiratory
indices could create a low–dimensional multi–subsystem space
enabling precise therapy targeting, severity stratification, and
subsystem–specific tracking. We introduced a physiologically
groundedZ a(t)and AWI computable from EMA or audio
via inversion. In comparing speech of speakers with PD
with matched controls, AWI is stable across sex and vocal
hyperfunction, yet separates PD from healthy speech and tracks
motor and speech impact, providing a compact, interpretable
building block for subsystem–specific models of disordered
speech.
