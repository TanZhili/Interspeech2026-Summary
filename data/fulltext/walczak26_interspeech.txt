Abstract
Sibilant fricatives are shaped by multiple linguistic and develop-
mental factors, yet word-position effects in perceptually norma-
tive Polish /s/ remain underdescribed. This pilot study examines
whether word-initial, word-medial, and word-final positions are
associated with acoustic differences in /s/ produced by Polish-
speaking preschool children aged 5–8 years. The dataset com-
prised 816 /s/ tokens from 102 children. Acoustic measures in-
cluded spectral centroid (CoG), skewness, kurtosis, and spectral
flux. Word position significantly affected the spectral proper-
ties of /s/: the strongest pairwise differences was present within
word-initial and word-medial tokens (CoG, spectral flux, kur-
tosis, skewness), whereas medial–final contrasts were generally
smaller and often non-significant, except for skewness.
Index Terms: Polish, child speech, sibilant fricatives, spectral
measures, fricative acoustics, word position
1. Introduction
Sibilant fricatives are characterized by high-frequency turbu-
lent noise, and their acoustic properties are often analyzed using
spectral measures that quantify the distribution of frication en-
ergy [1, 2, 3]. These measures are widely used in phonetic anal-
ysis because they capture variation that may not be detected by
auditory perception alone. One plausible source of such vari-
ation is word position. Initial, medial, and final word posi-
tions differ in prosodic organization, boundary-related timing,
and coarticulatory demands [4, 5, 6]. Yet word-position effects
in perceptually normative Polish /s/ remain underexplored, par-
ticularly in children’s speech [7, 8]. Children’s sibilant spec-
tra change over development and may show substantial contex-
tual variability, which makes language-specific baselines neces-
sary [9, 10]. Moreover, child productions may exhibit a covert,
subphonemic structure that is not reliably captured by auditory
judgment [11, 12]. Such subphonemic distinctions are com-
monly described as covert contrast [13, 14].
Mentioned research gap motivates a targeted examination
of /s/ across boundary-adjacent and within-word contexts. This
study addresses two research questions:
• (RQ1) Do the acoustic properties of perceptually normative
/s/ differ across initial, medial, and final word positions in
Polish-speaking children?
• (RQ2) Does any word-position effect remain after accounting
for speaker- and lexical-item-level variability?
The expected position effect is motivated by three position-
related mechanisms. Word-initial strengthening may improve
articulatory target achievement. Electropalatographic evidence

shows that onset consonants involve greater tongue–palate con-
tact and longer duration word-initially than word-medially [4].
Word-final lengthening, by contrast, reflects boundary-related
timing adjustments that facilitate utterance segmentation [6];
because this timing effect can alter the duration and internal
organization of the fricative interval, it may also affect spectral
flux and the distribution of spectral energy [5]. Finally, word-
medial position may expose /s/ to stronger coarticulatory influ-
ence from neighboring segments. Finally, polysyllabic short-
ening provides an additional timing-related source of variabil-
ity: as more syllables are added to the right of a base syllable,
the duration of that syllable tends to decrease [15]. These tim-
ing and strengthening effects may change coarticulatory overlap
and constriction configuration, with consequences for the reso-
nant properties of the vocal tract and the spectral distribution of
fricative noise [7, 16, 14, 17].
To examine these effects, we analyzed spectral centroid
(CoG), skewness, kurtosis, and spectral flux. These measures
capture complementary aspects of /s/ frication, including spec-
tral location, spectral shape, and temporal variation. The in-
clusion of spectral flux is motivated by evidence that sibilant
spectra may vary over the time course of a fricative, consistent
with continuous articulatory movement [18].
Based on earlier research on boundary-related strengthen-
ing and timing effects [4, 5, 6, 15], we anticipated that word
position would influence frication acoustics even in perceptu-
ally normative /s/. We further predicted the strongest contrast
between word-initial and word-medial /s/, because this compar-
ison contrasts a position associated with stronger articulatory
target achievement with a position more exposed to within-word
coarticulation.
2. Methodology
2.1. Speech corpus
The lexical corpus consisted of eight Polish words drawn from
basic child vocabulary (e.g., food, animals, and occupations).
The lexical items are listed in Table 1.
The study involved 102 Polish-speaking preschoolers (aged
5-8) with normative /s/ productions, selected from the PA VSig
database [19]. Recordings were obtained using Panasonic WM-
61 electret microphones positioned at a fixed distance of ap-
proximately 15 cm from the speaker’s mouth. Speech signal
was sampled at 44.1 kHz with 16-bit resolution.
The database included normativity judgments of Polish /s/
made independently by two speech-language pathology experts
for eight words in initial, medial, and final word positions. Nor-
mative Polish /s/ was defined as a voiceless, non-palatal, non-
nasal fricative, produced with either the apex or lamina of the
tongue. This yielded approximately 816 tokens (102 partici-

2.2. Signal preprocessing and feature extraction
Sibilants were first manually segmented from the whole record-
ings. The onset of /s/ was defined as the first frame showing
visible high-frequency aperiodic frication energy, and the offset
was defined as the disappearance of this frication energy before
the following segment or word boundary.
The signals were divided into 20 ms frames with a 10 ms
overlap, processed with the Hamming window. For each frame,
the short-time Fourier transform (STFT) was computed, yield-
ing a spectral resolution of 50 Hz. Based on the STFT rep-
resentation, five acoustic features were analyzed: spectral cen-
troid (CoG), kurtosis, skewness, and spectral flux. CoG, and the
higher-order spectral moments describe the location and shape
of frication energy, and spectral flux captures temporal spectral
change. Signal processing was performed in Matlab 2023b.
The first spectral moment (M1), i.e., the spectral center
of gravity (CoG), was used as a primary descriptor of the fre-
quency location of frication-noise energy. CoG is widely used
in acoustic analyses of fricatives because it depends on constric-
tion configuration and noise characteristics. For /s/, CoG values
are typically high (often in the kHz range) and can help differ-
entiate /s/ from other fricatives [20, 9].
Spectral kurtosis (M4) indexes the peakedness (energy con-
centration) of the frication-noise spectrum. In sibilants, it may
be sensitive to tongue position and to the concentration of en-
ergy in the dominant spectral region [14]. However, as with
other spectral moments, it should be interpreted together with
complementary measures rather than as a direct one-to-one ar-
ticulatory index [14, 21].
Spectral skewness (M3) reflects asymmetry in the frication-
noise energy distribution relative to the centroid (CoG/M1). It
therefore provides information about spectral shape beyond the
location of energy. In the sibilant-related literature, M3 has been
used to distinguish fricatives, including the /s/- /S/ contrast [14].
More generally, spectral moments, including M3, are part of the
traditional acoustic toolkit to investigate the place and quality of
the fricatives [16].
Spectral flux indexes frame-to-frame spectral change and
thus captures temporal variability in frication, complementing
static spectral moments [22]. It was included to characterize
not only the static spectral shape of Polish /s/ (as captured by
CoG, skewness, and kurtosis), but also the temporal evolution
of frication noise [22].
Together, these features provide a compact and complemen-
tary description of /s/ acoustics and were used to test the effect
of word position (initial, medial, final) on perceptually norma-
tive Polish /s/. The features were averaged per token before
statistical modeling.
2.3. Statistical analysis
Each acoustic feature was modeled using a linear mixed-effects
model (LME) with the following structure:Feature∼
word position + (stress + preceding phone
+ following phone | speaker) + (1 | parent
word). Word position was included as a fixed effect with the
initial level specified as the intercept (reference). The random-
effects structure comprised by-speaker random intercepts and
slopes for stress, preceding phone, and following phone, and a
random intercept for each word. Age of the speaker was treated
as a sample descriptor rather than as a covariate in the present
analysis. This decision kept the model focused on the primary
factor of interest, word position. However, it also means that
age-related developmental effects within the 5–8-year range
cannot be evaluated here and should be addressed in future
work using a larger and more balanced lexical corpus.
Differences between word-position levels (initial, medial,
final) were evaluated using estimated marginal means (EMMs)
derived from the fitted LME models. EMMs were obtained
withemmeanspackage [23]. Bonferroni-adjusted pairwise
contrasts were used. Effect sizes for contrasts between word-
position levels were expressed asdand interpreted using Co-
hen’s guidelines, considering between 0.2 and 0.5 as small ef-
fect; 0.5 to 0.8 as medium, and above 0.8 as large effect [24].
All of the statistical analyses were performed in
R 4.4.2 [25]. Linear mixed-effects (LME) models were fitted
using thelme4[26] package.

means (EMMs), which describe the direction of positional dif-
ferences, followed by pairwise contrasts, which identify which
word-position comparisons drive the observed effects. Figures
1—4 show the EMMs by word position, with error bars indicat-
ing confidence intervals and asterisks marking significant pair-
wise contrasts.

4. Discussion
The results partially support our hypothesis that word posi-
tion affects the acoustic realization of normative Polish /s/
in children. The clearest and most consistent differences
were observed between word-initial and word-medial tokens,
whereas medial–final contrasts were weaker and generally non-
significant, except for skewness. Thus, word position appears
to influence /s/ acoustics, but the effect is not uniform across
acoustic parameters or across all positional contrasts. The
prominence of the initial–medial contrast is consistent with the
boundary literature, which links prosodic edges to local tim-
ing effects that distinguish boundary-adjacent from within-word
positions [17, 27]. This places word-final /s/ in an intermediate
position, possibly reflecting a mix of final-lengthening effects
and within-word coarticulation. Higher CoG and spectral flux
in the medial position may reflect differences in spectral orga-
nization between within-word and boundary-adjacent contexts.
However, because frication duration, articulatory data, and a
matched adult Polish control group were not included, both the
articulatory and developmental interpretations should remain
cautious. These findings may also be interpreted in the con-
text of speech motor development. Specifically, the positional
differences may reflect subphonemic variability that is not fully
captured by auditory normativity judgments, coherent with the
broader literature on covert contrast in child speech [13, 14].
This is consistent with evidence of a protracted developmental
course for sibilants and continued maturation of speech motor
timing well into later childhood, even when speech is perceptu-
ally acceptable [28, 29]. Position-controlled acoustic baselines
may therefore be useful even when covert contrast is not directly
assessed.
A key limitation of the present study is the small and un-
balanced lexical corpus. Although the token count is appropri-
ate for a pilot acoustic study involving children, the eight-word
design limits the extent to which positional effects can be sepa-
rated from lexical, segmental, and vowel-transition effects. The
inclusion of parent word as a random effect reduces, but does
not eliminate, this limitation. In addition, age was not modeled
as a covariate, and no matched adult Polish control group was
included. For this reason, future work should use a larger and
position-balanced lexical set, model age-related change explic-
itly, and compare child productions with adult Polish /s/ pro-
duced under matched recording and lexical conditions.
5. Conclusions
Mixed-effects models revealed reliable word-position effects
for several spectral properties of normative Polish /s/ in chil-
dren. The clearest pattern was an initial–medial contrast for
CoG, spectral flux, kurtosis, and skewness. Initial–final differ-
ences were also observed for these spectral measures, whereas
medial–final differences were limited and reached significance
only for skewness.
