Decoding Order Matters in Autoregressive Speech Synthesis
Minghui Zhao, Anton Ragni
School of Computer Science, University of Sheffield, United Kingdom
mzhao39@sheffield.ac.uk, a.ragni@sheffield.ac.uk
Abstract
Autoregressive speech synthesis has traditionally followed a
left-to-right order, yet generation order is a modelling choice.
This paper investigates decoding order through a masked diffu-
sion framework that enables arbitrary decoding orders at infer-
ence time. To isolate the effect of decoding order from induc-
tive biases introduced by learned discrete encoders, we oper-
ate on scalar quantised Mel-spectrograms. Our results demon-
strate that the left-to-right strategy is suboptimal for capturing
complex acoustic dependencies. The reverse, right-to-left, or-
der consistently outperforms the traditional approach, while the
adaptive confidence-based strategytop1achieves the highest
Mean Opinion Score (MOS) among all model outputs. Analy-
sis reveals that the most effective orders maintain local clusters
of consecutive frames to balance long-range temporal depen-
dencies with local coherence. These results establish decoding
order as a critical parameter for improving synthesis quality.
Index Terms: speech synthesis, masked diffusion models,
order-agnostic decoding
1. Introduction
Autoregressive (AR) generation has long been central to speech
synthesis, from earlier systems such as Wavenet [1] and
Tacotron 2 [2] to recent approaches that represent speech as dis-
crete tokens such as [3, 4, 5]. In these systems, speech is gen-
erated sequentially, always following a left-to-right order that
mirrors the natural progression of speech.
From a modelling perspective, left-to-right generation is not
necessarily optimal. Speech dependencies extend beyond a sim-
ple causal chain: pauses and emphasis often depend on global
context, while coarticulation reflects interactions between both
past and future phones. Even when the model is conditioned on
the full text or phone sequence, the decoding order can influ-
ence how effectively these acoustic dependencies are captured.
Exploring alternatives to left-to-right generation is therefore im-
portant not only for assessing the widely adopted and unchal-
lenged convention, but also for improving synthesis quality.

The masked diffusion model (MDM) [6, 7, 8] is a recently

ously revealed tokens, allowing direct assessment of how or-
der affects generation quality. To ensure that such analysis is
not confounded by representation learning, we obtain tokens by
directly discretising Mel-spectrograms via scalar quantisation.
Using this framework, we investigate how different decoding
orders affect the quality of AR speech synthesis.
2. Related work
2.1. Generative modelling framework
Many recent works explore combining diffusion with autore-
gression for sequence generation. Block Diffusion [9] and
speech models such as DiffAR [10] and DiTAR [11] group
consecutive frames into blocks and maintain a left-to-right AR
generation across blocks, while predicting content within each
block in parallel. We instead adopt the order-agnostic frame-

2.2. Speech tokenisation
Speech tokens are typically derived through learned quantisa-
tion [3, 4] or clustering-based methods [15]. Because these dis-
cretisation mappings are trained under specific reconstruction
or self-supervised objectives, they may introduce latent induc-
tive biases to the resulting token inventory. As a result, analyses
based on a particular learned tokeniser may not generalise to
alternative or future tokenisation schemes. To isolate the effect
of decoding order from the influence of a learned encoder, we
apply scalar quantisation directly to Mel-spectrograms. This
signal-derived, deterministic, parameter-free approach ensures
that the tokenisation remains a transparent reflection of the
acoustic signal. Scalar quantisation has previously been used
for discrete generative modelling in the image domain, notably
by FSQ-VQ [16]. As demonstrated in Sec. 4.2, these quantised
Mel-spectrograms remain compatible with off-the-shelf HiFi-
GAN [17] vocoders without the need for specialised retraining.
3. Method
Autoregressive sequence decoding can be characterised by two
design choices, the orderσin which frames are generated and
the update size|B i|, i.e., how many elements are decoded at
each step. While many models impose a fixed schedule (e.g.,
left-to-right, one by one), the framework used here performs
iterative masked prediction (Fig. 1), producing a predictive dis-
tribution for all positions at each step. A subset of positions
is then selected according to a decoding schedule and updated,
and the process repeats until all tokens are generated. To study
decoding order, we restrict the model to update one position at
a time (|Bi|= 1for every step) without replacement, making it
directly comparable to conventional AR models, as in [7].
The following sections describe inference and training. Let
µdenote a prior derived from text, with temporal lengthT.
Conditioned onµ, the model generates an output sequencey
of lengthT. In models such as Grad-TTS [18] and Matcha-
TTS [19], all frames are updated simultaneously, whereas our
model updates one frame at a time forTsteps. Since MDMs op-
erate on discrete data, Sec. 3.3 details the quantisation method,
while the motivation for this choice is discussed earlier in
Sec. 2.2. Although decoding could be applied to individual
time–frequency bins, we perform generation at the frame level.
Sec. 3.4 presents the decoding strategies.

3.4. Decoding strategies
We examine fixed orders, left-to-right (l2r), and right-to-left
(r2l) as well as adaptive top-Kand duration-guided strategies.
These adaptive strategies prioritise high-confidence frames, in-
herently resulting in a non-linear generation path. To further
explore this property, we evaluate stochasticity by interpolating
betweenl2rand fully random (uro) orders. This allows us
to observe how the model reacts to non-linearity, whether the
specific decoding order is confidence driven or not.

3.4.2. Duration-guided decoding
While inspecting the adaptively determined orders (see
Sec. 4.4), we observed that the model often decodes contigu-
ous positions, selecting adjacent frames consecutively. This
suggests a preference for local continuity. To examine whether
chunk-wise decoding improves generation quality, we first seg-
ment each utterance. A simple approach is to use the duration
predictor, which estimates how many frames each frame spans.
To select which segment to decode, we compute the average
frame-level confidencesk(t)within each segment (Eq. (5)), and
select the segment with the highest score; however, since the
confidence is based on predicted probabilities, we note these
probabilities may not be well calibrated [20]. The frames within
the chosen segment are then updated in a random order, al-
though different strategies could also be considered. This de-
coding scheme is denoted asdur.
3.4.3. Stochastic path interpolation
To evaluate the impact of non-linearity, we control the de-
gree of randomness by perturbing the left-to-right orderσ l2r
with swaps between two randomly chosen positions. Theo-
retically, approximatelyTlogTswaps produce a near-uniform
permutation distribution [8]. We scale this quantity by a factor
β∈(0,1)to obtain a coarse measure of randomness, yielding
orders close tol2rasβ→0and close touroasβ→1.

4.2. Effect of Mel-spectrogram quantisation
Following Sec. 3.3, we conduct preliminary experiments us-
ing scalar quantisation of Mel-spectrograms. On the valida-
tion set, Mel features are discretised intoQlevels and vocoded
with HiFi-GAN. As expected, lowerQincreases MCD and
logF 0 (see Fig. 2). AtQ= 10, both remain low (2.40
and0.19), indicating limited degradation relative to continuous
Mel-spectrograms. WER is already relatively low atQ= 5
(7.48), decreases with largerQ, and stabilises at5.22from
Q= 100onward. These results show that Mel-spectrograms
are sufficiently redundant to support simple scalar quantisa-

standard vocoders such as HiFi-GAN can reconstruct high-

5. Conclusion and discussion
This study shows that the conventional left-to-right decoding
order in AR speech synthesis is suboptimal for capturing com-
plex acoustic dependencies. Using an order-agnostic MDM, we
find that strategies such as right-to-left and adaptive confidence-
based decoding improve both objective and subjective metrics,
withtop1achieving the highest perceived naturalness. Our
results indicate that decoding order is a critical yet overlooked
modelling factor. Performance improves when decoding bal-
ances global context with local coherence, particularly through
contiguous frame generation with a locally dominant right-
to-left progression. Scalar-quantised Mel-spectrograms ensure
that these improvements stem from decoding order rather than
encoder-specific token representations. Experiments also show
substantial redundancy in Mel-spectrograms, as high-quality
speech can be reconstructed from heavily quantised inputs. Fu-
ture work will examine whether these findings generalise to
multi-speaker systems or more diverse acoustic conditions.
