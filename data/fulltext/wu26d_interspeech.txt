Abstract
This study uses real-time magnetic resonance imaging (rtMRI)
to quantify articulatory correlates of the Mandarin alveo-
lar–retroflex contrast. Air–tissue boundaries were extracted us-
ing grid-based vocal tract tracking, yielding four measures: nor-
malized constriction location, constriction length, front cavity,
and back cavity. Results showed robust retroflex effects on
constriction location and front cavity, indicating consistent pos-
terior displacement and systematic anterior cavity expansion.
Constriction length effects were less consistent, and back cav-
ity weakly discriminated consonant class. rtMRI frames fur-
ther revealed frequent tongue-tip-down postures for retroflex
tokens. Overall, constriction location and anterior cavity ex-
pansion emerged as stable correlates of the contrast, suggesting
articulatory targets that may inform pronunciation instruction.
Index Terms: Mandarin Chinese, alveolar–retroflex contrast,
real-time MRI
1. Introduction
Standard Mandarin includes a set of coronal consonants tradi-
tionally classified as “retroflex” in Chinese phonology: zh /tù/ ,
ch /tù h / , sh /ù/ , and r /ü/ . These segments are widely re-
ported to be difficult for L2 learners to acquire [1]. Traditional
descriptions attribute Mandarin “retroflex” sibilants to tongue-
tip curling; however, instrumental evidence frequently indicates
non-curling realizations. X-ray and palatographic studies re-
port postalveolar constrictions without clear tongue-tip curl-
ing [2, 3], while ultrasound and MRI data further document
systematic tongue-body bunching and substantial interspeaker
variation [4, 5]. Because constriction location and cavity con-
figuration jointly determine anterior vocal tract geometry, they
provide an articulatory basis for acoustic contrast, for exam-
ple through cavity-resonance mechanisms such as F3 lowering
associated with increased anterior cavity length or volume [6].
Cross-linguistic work has shown that retroflex consonants are
articulatorily heterogeneous, with languages such as Hindi and
Polish illustrating that retroflex or retroflex-like categories can-
not be defined solely by backward curling of the tongue tip [7].
This motivates an imaging-based approach that can quantify
both constriction location and vocal-tract cavity configuration.
To address these discrepancies between traditional classifi-
cations and instrumental findings, the present study uses rtMRI
to quantify the articulatory basis of the alveolar–retroflex con-
trast across multiple vowel contexts. We examine four coronal
pairs: ts –tù , ts h –tù h , s –ù , and l –ü . Although /l/ differs in man-
ner from /ü/ , it is included as an alveolar reference category
to enable a place-focused comparison of oral constriction loca-
tion and cavity configuration with /ü/ within a unified rtMRI
measurement framework. By identifying the most robust ar-
ticulatory dimensions distinguishing the alveolar–retroflex con-
trast, we aim to clarify the phonetic implementation of Man-
darin “retroflexion” and to provide an evidence-based, learner-
oriented articulatory account to inform pronunciation instruc-
tion and support L2 learners in developing more accurate pro-
duction targets.
2. Method
2.1. Materials
The target stimuli comprised 96 monosyllabic items formed by
combining 8 Mandarin consonants ( /ts, ts h , s, l, tù, tù h , ù, ü/ )
with 12 following vocalic contexts ( /i [¦, §], a, aI, an, AU, AN,
7, eI, @n, oU, u, ueI/ ). All items were produced with Tone 4,
the high-falling tone. Each stimulus item was produced once
by each speaker. If a production error was detected, the ex-
perimenter immediately notified the speaker and elicited a rep-
etition, yielding one acceptable production per item for each
speaker.
In total, 78 real words and 18 phonotactically legal nonce
words were presented in separate sessions in randomized order.
All stimuli were embedded in the carrier phrase “Zh `e ge
hˇen n´an d´u.” (“This is difficult to read.”).
2.2. Participants
Four native speakers of Mandarin (2 male, 2 female; mean age
= 26.75 years, SD = 3 .5) participated in the study. Three
were from Beijing and one from Tianjin, and all had resided
in Northern China until at least 18 years of age. All reported
Standard Mandarin Chinese as their primary language of daily
use and no history of speech or hearing disorders.
2.3. Imaging Methodology
rtMRI data of the midsagittal plane during speech production
were acquired using a 3T MRI scanner (MAGNETOM Prisma,
Siemens) at the ATR-Promotions Brain Activity Imaging Cen-
ter (Kyoto, Japan). Participants were positioned supine in
the scanner and stabilized with a head restraint to minimize
head motion. Speech sounds were recorded using an MRI-
compatible microphone positioned inside the scanner bore near
the participant’s mouth.
Imaging parameters were as follows: matrix size = 256
pixels × 256 pixels; in-plane spatial resolution = 1 mm × 1
mm; slice thickness = 10 mm; and temporal resolution = 13.78
frames per second.

2.4. Data Analysis
For each target consonant, the frame corresponding to its
steady-state portion was manually selected, defined as the frame
immediately preceding the onset of articulatory movement for
the following vowel.
Imaging data were analyzed using a custom MATLAB tool-
box implementing grid-based air–tissue boundary segmentation
for vocal tract tracking [8, 9]. Following the procedure de-
scribed in [8], the airway midline was manually traced along
the open vocal tract, and three anatomical landmarks were iden-
tified: (i) the lowest point of the upper lip; (ii) the posterior
boundary of the hard palate; and (iii) the superior margin of the
larynx. Gridlines orthogonal to the midline were then placed
at 2-mm intervals (Figure 1, left). At each gridline, upper and
lower air–tissue intersections were automatically detected based
on intensity discontinuities to generate boundary contours (Fig-
ure 1, right). All contours were visually inspected and manually
corrected where necessary.
The vocal tract was defined from anterior gridline index s
to posterior gridline index e. The distance between the upper
and lower boundaries at grid index i was denoted as di, and
the sequence of di values constituted the midsagittal vocal-tract
aperture function for each selected consonant frame. Distances
< 0.5 mm were operationally defined as constrictions, with
first and last indices cs and ce, respectively. The anterior cav-
ity was defined from s to cs, and the posterior cavity from ce to
e. Cavity measures were defined as two-dimensional width-sum
(pseudo-area) indices computed from these midsagittal aperture
values:

Articulatory differences in the alveolar–retroflex contrast were
quantified along four dimensions:
1. Constriction location: Computed in two steps. First, the ge-
ometric center of the constriction interval was defined as the
midpoint between cs and ce. Second, this midpoint was nor-
malized relative to the oral region and expressed on a 0–1
scale, where 0 corresponds to the anterior oral boundary and
1 to the posterior oral boundary.
2. Constriction length: The number of grid positions spanned
by the constriction interval (cs to ce).
3. Front cavity: Sfront, the pseudo-area of the anterior cavity (s
to cs).
4. Back cavity: Sback, the pseudo-area of the posterior cavity
(ce to e).
For each articulatory measure (constriction location, constric-
tion length, front cavity, and back cavity), a linear mixed-effects
model was fitted by restricted maximum likelihood using the
nlme package in R (v4.5.2) [10, 11]. Fixed effects included
consonant class (alveolar vs. retroflex), consonant pair ( ts –tù ,
ts h –tù h , s –ù , l –ü ), and their interaction. V owel context was in-
cluded as a covariate. To control for interspeaker anatomical
differences, two vocal tract length (VTL) terms were included:
(i) each speaker’s mean VTL and (ii) each token’s deviation
from the speaker-specific mean VTL. Speaker was included as a
random intercept. For multiple planned comparisons, p-values
were adjusted using the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery
rate procedure [12].

3. Results
Figure 2 shows a representative example of the alveolar–
retroflex contrast (/tsa/ vs. /tùa/ ) in the /a/ context, produced by
a male speaker from Beijing in the present study. The same ar-
ticulatory pattern was observed across speakers. As illustrated
in the right panel, the retroflex consonant is produced with a
tongue-tip-down posture, rather than the tongue-tip-up gesture
traditionally associated with retroflexion [13].
Figure 3 plots the mean vocal-tract aperture functions ( ±1
standard error, SE), defined as the air–tissue boundary distance
at each normalized grid position along the midsagittal vocal
tract (0 = anterior; 1 = posterior), for each consonant pair. The
functions were averaged across speakers and across all vowel
contexts for each consonant. Across all four alveolar–retroflex
pairs, retroflex consonants show systematically more posterior
constriction locations than their alveolar counterparts. In ad-
dition, several pairs exhibit greater front-cavity opening for
retroflex tokens.
To quantify these patterns, four articulatory metrics were
extracted for each token—constriction location, constriction
length, front cavity (Sfront), and back cavity (Sback)—and sum-
marized by consonant class within each pair (Figure 4). Two de-
scriptive trends emerge across pairs. First, retroflex consonants
show a robust posterior shift in constriction location relative to
alveolars. Second, retroflex consonants exhibit a consistent in-
crease in front cavity, indicating systematic anterior cavity ex-
pansion. In contrast, constriction length varies by pair, and back
cavity provides comparatively weak separation between classes.

accompanied by anterior cavity expansion. In contrast, constric-
tion length and back cavity measures show less stable behavior
across consonant pairs.
4. Discussion
The present rtMRI analysis indicates that the Mandarin
alveolar–retroflex contrast is most consistently implemented
through (i) posterior displacement of the primary oral constric-
tion and (ii) systematic expansion of the anterior cavity. Rep-
resentative frames further suggest that retroflex tokens often
involve a tongue-tip-down posture rather than the stereotypi-
cal tongue-tip curl, consistent with prior instrumental reports of
non-curling realizations and variable tongue configurations for
Mandarin retroflexes [2–5, 14].
These articulatory differences have plausible acoustic con-
sequences. A posteriorly displaced constriction and enlarged
anterior cavity may alter the filtering of sibilant noise and re-
lated spectral properties. Previous accounts associate increased
anterior cavity length or volume with lower F3, with lip con-
striction or protrusion potentially contributing by enlarging the
anterior cavity [4]. By contrast, back cavity measures provided
weak class separation in the present data, suggesting that poste-
rior cavity differences are either less stable for this contrast or
less accurately captured by midsagittal pseudo-area indices.
This study is limited by the small speaker sample and re-
liance on manually selected steady-state frames. Future re-
search should include larger and more diverse speaker popu-
lations, examine time-varying articulatory trajectories, and test
acoustic–articulatory relationships directly using synchronized
acoustic recordings within a unified modeling framework.
