Abstract
Clinical risk interviews depend on who said what and on
meaning-critical cues such as negation, modality/uncertainty,
and time. We present DIALOG-DeID, a configurable
pipeline for long-form clinical dialogue that integrates
ASR, diarization, automatic role mapping (clinician vs.
interviewee), and transcript de-identification within a unified
segment representation. We propose an evaluation recipe
beyond WER by combining (i) speaker-attributed WER
(sWER) to capture misattribution, and (ii) Qualifier/Temporal
Preservation (QTP-F1), an auditable lexical cue metric
targeting negation, modality/uncertainty, and temporal
anchors. On PSYCHS-Bench, the best aggregate WER was
13.3±1.4 while role-attributed sWER remained 33.1 ±6.7;
semantic audit found cue drops in 20–24% of snippets and
hard negation flips in 2–4%, motivating meaning-aware
evaluation for privacy-constrained clinical ASR. Code:
https://github.com/GuiCamargoX/dialog-deid.

1. Introduction
With the growing integration of Natural Language Processing
(NLP) in clinical care and research [1], accurate processing of
dyadic health conversations such as intake visits, structured
assessments, telehealth encounters, and psychotherapy
are crucial for documentation and downstream modeling
tasks [2]. Mental health interviews are particularly sensitive to
transcription quality [3], since subtle differences in wording,
speaker role, and temporal qualifiers directly influence clinician
ratings.
Recent speech processing pipelines have demonstrated
substantial progress in role-aware transcription [4], telehealth
automatic speech recognition (ASR) evaluations [5], and
prediction from ASR-derived signals and uncertainty [6, 7].
Despite these advances, current evaluation practices remain
dominated by word error rate (WER) and diarization error
rate (DER) [8], which do not quantify mismatches in
clinical meaning. Infrequent errors in negation, temporality,
certainty/hedging, or attribution can reverse clinical
interpretation or misinterpret patient intent, even when
overall WER appears low—a vulnerability amplified in clinical
interviews with rapid turn taking, where short expressions (e.g.,
“not”, “maybe”, “last week”) often carry critical meaning.

Mental health data also introduces stringent privacy
constraints: clinical audio contains biometric identifiers
and transcripts can include extensive personally identifiable
information (PII), necessitating transcript de-identification
(DeID) and careful governance. Prior efforts include
DeID toolkits (e.g., Presidio) [9], audio-to-text redaction
pipelines [10], dataset-level PII handling during collection [11],
and acoustic anonymization [12]. Despite strong standalone
tools, there is limited standardization in how diarization,
role attribution, DeID, and meaning-critical evaluation are
integrated and audited in Institutional Review Board (IRB)-
governed clinical dialogue workflows.
To address these evaluation and privacy challenges, we
ground our analysis in semi-structured psychiatric interviews
using the Positive SYmptoms and Diagnostic Criteria for
the CAARMS Harmonized with the SIPS (PSYCHS)
questionnaire [13, 14] for clinical high risk for psychosis
assessment. This setting provides a stringent test case because
symptom ratings depend directly on precise wording and
correct speaker attribution. Prior work often relied on manual
or manually corrected transcripts [15, 16, 17], implicitly
assuming high fidelity to meaning and speaker role. When
this step is automated, even rare ASR errors in negation,
temporality, certainty/hedging, or attribution can change
symptom interpretation and scoring.
This work makes three main contributions by (i) an
evaluation recipe that complements WER/DER with speaker-
attributed WER (sWER; permutation-invariant stream
alignment) and an auditable cue metric, Qualifier/Temporal
Preservation F1 (QTP-F1), used to triage clinically
consequential distortions; (ii) DIALOG-DeID, a modular
pipeline combining ASR, diarization, LLM role mapping, and
automated PII/PHI de-identification to produce role-labeled,
de-identified transcripts with auditable attribution and privacy
outputs; and (iii) evaluation on PSYCHS with cross-domain
validation on AMI, showing similar WER can mask large
differences in attribution-correct fidelity and cue retention,
including speed–stability trade-offs under time-compression.
2. DIALOG-DeID
The DIALOG-DeID pipeline (Figure 1) consists of i) ASR with
diarization; ii) text PII redaction; iii) downstream scoring.

2.2. ASR, diarization, and speaker/role mapping
We support multiple ASR backends behind a shared interface:
WhisperX, Amazon Transcribe, Azure Speech-to-Text, and
Google Speech-to-Text (long-form) . WhisperX 1 provides
word-level timestamps via V AD and forced alignment [18].
WhisperX can be paired with pyannote.audio diarization2 [19];
for cloud providers, we ingest available timing outputs into the
same segment representation.
When diarization is not provided by the backend, we
run pyannote.audio [19] and align turns to ASR tokens
by timestamp overlap. For multi-speaker validation, we
map diarized speaker labels to reference speaker IDs using
maximum time-overlap matching (Hungarian assignment) per
session; this mapping is used only for attribution metrics.
For clinical interviews, we additionally map speakers
to coarse roles (clinician vs. interviewee) using a prompt-
based LLM classifier applied to speaker-attributed segments.
We aggregate each diarized speaker’s text within a session
and apply a fixed prompt template (constraining outputs
to the two-role label set) following standard prompt-based
learning/template practice [20, 21].
To test temporal robustness and efficiency, we run each
ASR modelm on the original audio at 1.0× playback and treat
the output as a model-specific reference. We then create pitch-
preserving time-compressed versions (e.g.,1.5×), run the same
model without re-tuning, and measure 1 ×-relative word error
rate,

2.3. PII detection and redaction
Text DeID uses interchangeable backends under a HIPAA-like
label space [22]: (i) Microsoft Presidio NER [23]; (ii) LLM
span extraction; and (iii) an optional cloud PII API backend.
For the LLM backend, we use a prompt template adapted
from prior LLM de-identification work [24] that specifies
the label schema and requires JSON output of span offsets
and labels (Figure 2). For evaluation, we apply the same
deterministic post-processing policy to both gold labels and
predictions to retain only “identifying information” spans under
our schema and drop non-identifying mentions; retained spans
are replaced with placeholders (e.g., [NAME], [DATE]) while
preserving turn boundaries and tags, and we report span-level
precision/recall and micro-F1 after this filter.
2.4. PSYCHS positive symptom severity scoring
Using de-identified, role-labelled transcripts, we estimate a
continuous PSYCHS-aligned composite positive-symptom
severity score with an interpretable feature set. For each
session, we extract four features: (1) normalized word count,
and densities of (2) negation cues, (3) hedging/modality cues,
and (4) distress cues, computed as the percentage of tokens
matching fixed lexicons (e.g., no/not/never, maybe/possibly/I
think, anxious/scared/worried). We fit a ridge regressor on
these features and evaluate with leave-one-session-out (LOSO)
cross-validation to produce one out-of-fold prediction per
session and avoid leakage.

3. Experimental setup
Two datasets were used in this study:
PSYCHS-Bench: We use semi structured psychiatric
interviews from AMP SCZ 3, sampling 25 English language
sessions with 10 minute excerpts. Gold transcripts are produced
by the service TranscribeMe using human transcribers. Data are
access controlled through the NIMH Data Archive.
AMI Meeting Corpus 4: a public, multi-party meeting
benchmark with fragmented turns and overlapping speech, used
as an overlap-rich attribution stress test because PSYCHS is
access controlled. We use the Hugging Face AMI (Kaldi)
release, which provides transcripts, timestamps, and reference
speaker id under close-talking ( ihm) and far-field ( sdm)
conditions. For a fixed 5:00–15:00 window, we reconstruct a
timeline-preserving mixture waveform by placing each segment
at its true offset and mixing overlaps. To control compute and
API cost, we evaluate this window on 16 test meetings forihm;
for sdm, we run the same protocol on an anchor subset of 5
meetings and report paired SDM–IHM comparisons.
3.1. System variants and ablations
We evaluate ASR-only and ASR + diarization . ASR-only
reports standard transcription quality on the pooled transcript
(WER), while ASR + diarization aligns diarization turns to
ASR segments by timestamp overlap to enable speaker/role-
conditioned evaluation (sWER, QTP-F1, and DER). Within
each configuration, we swap ASR backends while holding
other modules fixed. Unless otherwise stated, cross-backend
comparisons fix diarization to pyannote and text DeID to
Presidio.
3.2. Metric correlations and semantic audit
We descriptively assess whether standard ASR accuracy
captures cue-sensitive quality by correlating WER, sWER,
and QTP-F1 on PSYCHS-Bench across all session ×system
pairs (Spearman ρ, Pearson r), treating these correlations as
exploratory because sessions repeat across systems. We also
run an extended audit on up to 50 cue-bearing snippets from
25 sessions (negation, temporal, modality): per session, we
select up to one snippet with an automatically detected cue
drop (“risky”) and one without (control), where a cue drop is
defined as a reference cue token missing from the time-aligned
ASR window. We record cue drops and hard meaning flips
(dropping not/never/don’t/didn’t).

4.3. Privacy and clinical utility (feasibility)
For DeID, we report span-level F1 when PHI labels are
available; raw audio is handled under IRB controls. Clinical
utility is evaluated on de-identified, role-labeled transcripts by
regressing clinician-rated PSYCHS composite scores on an
11-session subset using LOSO, reporting RMSE, CCC, and
Spearman’sρ.

We audit semantic fragility on Amazon and WhisperX
(Table 4) using 50 cue-bearing, time-aligned snippets (±1.5s);
cue drops occur in 20.0% (Amazon) and 24.0% (WhisperX),
and hard negation flips ( not/never/don’t/didn’t dropped) in
2.0% vs. 4.0%. Controls are no-drop by definition (0/25 drops).
On AMI (5:00–15:00 windows), speaker-attributed sWER
and QTP-F1spk show expected degradation under SDM relative
to IHM (Table 1). For text de-identification under a narrowed
“identifying-only” policy, Presidio and AWS-PII achieve high
precision and strong F1, while the LLM-based backend is
recall-limited for dates/locations (Table 5). Finally, pitch-
preserving time compression highlights speed–stability trade-
offs (Table 2); WhisperX is fastest (RTF 0.06) but drifts

a lexical-cue ridge regressor on de-identified, role-labeled
transcripts achieves feasibility-level agreement under LOSO
(RMSE=3.84, CCC=0.44, Spearman’s ρ=0.53).

6. Discussion and conclusion
Aggregate transcription metrics can hide clinically important
failures in long-form health conversations, where both who
said what and meaning-critical cues matter. On PSYCHS-
Bench, role-attributed sWER is much higher than WER across
backends, the attribution penalty (sWER−WER) varies widely,
and cue preservation is poorly predicted by standard accuracy—
indicating complementary error modes. Diarization remains a
key bottleneck: “getting the words right” is insufficient when
interpretation depends on attribution and qualifiers like negation
and modality.
A modest-cost extended audit makes rare-but-important
behaviors visible: cue drops were common (20–24%) and
hard meaning flips rare but non-zero (2–4%). On AMI, we
see condition-sensitive degradation (SDM vs. IHM), and time-
compression exposes runtime–drift trade-offs as an operational
feasibility check.
Under an “identifying-only” labeling policy, Presidio
remains competitive—slightly best on micro F1 via higher
recall while maintaining high precision—making it a practical
default, with targeted pattern augmentation when recall is
critical. De-identified, role-labeled transcripts still support
feasibility-level PSYCHS-aligned scoring, suggesting privacy-
preserving processing can retain clinically relevant signal when
attribution-aware fidelity and cue monitoring are treated as first-
class. Limitations include the small clinical evaluation, lack
of acoustic anonymization, no isolated DER or role-mapper
decomposition, and the intentionally lexical nature of QTP-F1.
Future work should add speaker-level audio privacy, richer
semantic fidelity checks, and robustness under overlap and
far-field conditions.
