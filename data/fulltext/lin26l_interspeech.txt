Abstract
Recent speech-to-speech (S2S) models generate intelligible
speech but often lack natural expressiveness. A key bottleneck
is the absence of an objective, automatic metric of expressive-
ness. Existing approaches rely on subjective ratings or low-level
acoustic features, which are either costly and unscalable or fail
to capture perceptual nuance. We introduce DeEAR, a frame-
work that maps human perception of expressiveness to objec-
tive scores. Grounded in phonetics and psychology, DeEAR
evaluates speech along three dimensions: Emotion, Prosody,
and Spontaneity. With fewer than 500 annotated samples, it
achieves strong agreement with expert ratings (SRCC=0.85).
DeEAR further enables scalable benchmarking and evaluation-
driven data curation. Using DeEAR, we curate 14K expres-
sive utterances to build a bilingualExpressiveSpeechdataset
and fine-tune an S2S model, leading to clear improvements in
perceived expressiveness in human evaluations.
Index Terms: Speech expressiveness, objective metric, human
preference alignment, speech-to-speech models, data curation
1. Introduction
Recent speech-to-speech (S2S) models generate intelligible
speech but often sound robotic. They still lack the expressive-
ness vital for engaging applications such as role-playing and AI
companions [1]. While challenges like Blizzard [2] and V oice-
MOS [3] successfully evaluate general speech quality, a scal-
able objective metric for expressiveness remains elusive.
Subjective human evaluation (e.g., Mean Opinion Score
(MOS)[4], typically used to measure general speech quality and
naturalness) remains the gold standard. However, capturing ex-
pressiveness requires evaluating complex attributes such as in-
tonation and emotion [5], making subjective evaluation costly
and unscalable [6]. Conversely, existing objective methods
fail, e.g., low-level acoustic features miss perceptual subtleties,
while emotion recognition is too narrow [7]. Thus, an objective
and automated metric is urgently needed.
To address this gap, we introduceDeEAR, a framework
that converts subjective human preference into four objective
DeEAR scores on a 0–100 scale: an overall expressiveness
score and three sub-scores. We operationalize phonological [8]
and psychological [9] theories into three quantifiable dimen-
sions:Emotion,Prosody, andSpontaneity. A unified model
aligns these with human perception. Trained on fewer than 500
annotated samples, the fusion module achieves a Spearman cor-
relation (SRCC) [10] of 0.85.

2. DeEAR
This section introduces the methodology ofDeEAR. Directly
modeling an abstract concept such asexpressivenessis unreli-
able due to its subjectivity and data scarcity. To address this, we
design a four-stage framework: (1) decompose expressiveness
into concrete, solvable tasks; (2) design specialized methods for
each task to ensure accuracy; (3) align outputs with human pref-
erence using a small amount of data; and (4) enhance efficiency
and scalability. The overall pipeline is illustrated in Figure 1.
2.1. Decomposing Expressiveness for Alignment
Building on the Circumplex Model of Affect [9, 13], we iso-
late Emotion Intensity as the acoustic correlate of physiologi-
cal arousal. We distinguish this from Prosody, which we de-
fine through the lens of Autosegmental-Metrical (AM) Phonol-
ogy [8] as the structural signaling of intent rather than mere
melodic fluctuation. Finally, we incorporate Spontaneity as a
distinct dimension of Speech Perception Quality [14], reflect-
ing the cognitive load and authenticity markers identified in re-
cent speech production studies [15]. This tripartite decompo-
sition reflects the modern shift toward disentangled represen-
tation learning [16], enabling a modular fusion approach that
aligns better with human preference than monolithic models.
2.2. Proxy Modeling for Sub-Dimensions
Given the varying characteristics and resource maturity across
dimensions, we adopt a task-adaptive strategy, employing spe-
cialized proxies tailored to each dimension’s constraints.
2.2.1. Emotion Intensity Scoring
We define emotion intensity as Arousal, a primary driver of
perceived expressiveness. While arousal correlates with basic
acoustic features like pitch and energy, these low-level met-
rics fail to capture complex paralinguistic nuances like tim-
bre. To address this, we employ a representation learning ap-
proach, fine-tuning a pre-trained wav2vec2 model1. Unlike sim-
ple acoustic baselines, this model extracts rich, non-linear rep-
resentations from raw audio. We further fine-tuned it on 12,000
Chinese samples from CNSCED [17] and 2,000 English sam-
ples from IEMOCAP [18] to ensure bilingual robustness.
2.2.2. Prosodic Richness Scoring
We quantify prosodic richness through four dimensions: Pitch,
Tempo, Loudness, and Pausing. Our preliminary experiments
with rule-based heuristics, such as F0 variance, proved ineffec-
tive at capturing the communicative intent of variations.
To address this, we employ Gemini-2.5-Pro as an auto-
mated expert evaluator. We designed a Chain-of-Thought (CoT)
prompting strategy that forces the model to reason step-by-
step: it must first document observable acoustic phenomena,
such asmonotone pitchorrhythmic acceleration, before as-
signing scores based on fixed weights. Crucially, the prompt
enforces a Semantic Neutrality principle, ensuring scores reflect
howspeech is delivered rather thanwhatis said. This approach
significantly aligns with human perception (SRCC= 0.73),
offering a scalable alternative to costly manual annotation.

2.2.3. Spontaneity Scoring
We define spontaneity as an unscripted conversational style.
Our scoring assumes that perceived spontaneity (sounding
unscripted) requires perceived naturalness (sounding human).
Technically flawless but rigidly read synthetic voices trigger a
speech uncanny valley effect [19, 20], severely reducing per-
ceived spontaneity [21]. We refer to this phenomenon as per-
ceptual incongruence, namely a mismatch between high acous-
tic quality and a non-human speech style.
We employ a two-stage, knowledge-guided supervised
strategy.

designed a heuristic function combining a categorical base level
of spontaneity,L base∈{1,3,5,7,9}(manually assigned at the
dataset level, whereL max = 9), and an acoustic quality metric,
Mavg (the mean of four DNSMOS outputs [22]). To explicitly
model perceptual incongruence, the score is calculated condi-
tionally:

2.4. Unified Student Model Distillation
To enable scalable deployment, we distill the ensemble of our
three specialized scorers (the Teacher System) into a single stu-
dent model, DeEAR-Base.
We first generated pseudo-labels forS emo,S pros, andS spon
by running the Teacher System on 20,000 unlabeled utter-
ances. DeEAR-Base was then trained to predict these scores
via multi-task regression. To ensure robust performance on
both Chinese and English speech, we selected the cross-
lingualwav2vec2-large-xlsr-53[23] backbone. Fi-
nally, DeEAR-Base outputs the three sub-scores, which are ag-
gregated by the XGBoost fusion module (Section 2.3) to yield
the final expressiveness score,Sexpr. This pipeline combines the
efficiency of a single neural network with the interpretability of
our modular design.
3. High-Expressive Bilingual Dataset
Existing speech datasets are dominated by neutral read speech,
lacking the variability needed for engaging interactions. To ad-
dress this, we developedExpressiveSpeech, a bilingual dataset
comprising 51 hours of high-quality, expressive utterances cu-
rated from diverse real-world scenarios.
The dataset contains∼14K samples with a balanced
Chinese-English ratio, aggregated from five emotional dialogue
corpora: Expresso [24], NCSSD [1], M 3ED [25], MultiDia-
log [26], and IEMOCAP [18]. Through a rigorous filtering
pipeline, we selected only samples meeting strict acoustic and
expressive standards. As shown in Table 3, the curated subset
achieves a mean expressiveness score of 80.2, significantly out-
performing the uncurated baselines.

4. Experiments
4.1. Validity: Alignment with Human Perception
To validate DeEAR, we conducted four distinct subjective eval-
uations targeting Emotion, Prosody, Spontaneity, and Overall
Expressiveness. For each dimension, we curated a dedicated
100-utterance test set from held-out subsets of our source cor-
pora (Table 3) and S2S-Arena [30], encompassing real-world
conversations, professional recordings, and synthetic speech.
We recruited 10 speech processing experts to rate the target
dimension of each sample on a 5-point Mean Opinion Score
(MOS) scale. Standardized protocols and anchor examples (see
Supplementary Material) ensured strong reliability, achieving
an average Krippendorff’s alpha ofα= 0.75. For the overall
expressiveness task, we also evaluated against general quality
metrics (DNSMOS [22] and UTMOS [31]) as baselines.
As shown in Table 4, the general quality baselines (DNS-
MOS, UTMOS) show a negative correlation with human ratings
of expressiveness. This is because metrics designed for acoustic
cleanliness tend to penalize the dynamic variations in expres-
sive speech. In stark contrast, DeEAR accurately captures these
perceptual nuances. It achieves a PCC of0.91and an SRCC of
0.85for overall expressiveness, while also maintaining strong,
consistent positive alignment across all three sub-dimensions.

4.2. Application 1: Automated Benchmarking of SOTA
Models
To demonstrate DeEAR’s practical utility, we applied it to
benchmark seven leading state-of-the-art (SOTA) S2S mod-
els: Doubao [11], Grok-4 V oice [32], GPT-4o Audio [33],
Sesame [34], Step Audio 2 [35], Qwen2.5-Omni [12], and
Gemini-2.5 Pro [36]. Our results show that DeEAR enables reli-
able automated evaluation, achieving a near-perfect rank corre-
lation (SRCC=0.93) with human judgments, thereby overcom-
ing the cost and subjectivity inherent in manual listening tests.
We compared DeEAR’s automated rankings against hu-
man judgments collected from 10 experts in speech processing.
Raters evaluated the overall expressiveness of each model’s out-
put on a standard 5-point MOS scale using 20 diverse conversa-
tional prompts. To facilitate direct comparison within a 0-100
range, the human MOS ratings were linearly normalized2.
The results in Table 5 quantitatively substantiate our
claim. DeEAR not only aligns closely with human rankings
(SRCC=0.93, 95% CI:[0.71,0.95]) but also exhibits strong
discriminative power, creating an overall score gap of nearly
70 points (Wilcoxonp <0.01) between the top and bottom-
performing systems. This confirms that DeEAR can reli-
ably serve as an objective surrogate for manual evaluations in
system-level model comparisons.

Having validated DeEAR, we now demonstrate its utility in
evaluation-driven data curation. Since human perception is the

4.3.1. Experimental Design
Our foundation model, S2S-Base, adopts the architecture of
MinMo [37] and Qwen2.5-Omni [12], integrating a 7B LLM
with a 1.5B Audio Language Model (ALM) via the S3tokenizer.
The ALM was pre-trained for 230K hours and post-trained for
4K hours. To create S2S-FT, we fine-tuned S2S-Base on the 51-
hour ExpressiveSpeech dataset for one epoch at a1e-5learning
rate.
Models were evaluated on a balanced 100-utterance test set,
split intoin-domain(held-out from source corpora) andout-of-
domain(unseen texts from Emilia) scenarios. For subjective
evaluation, 10 native speakers conducted a blind A/B test. They
were explicitly asked:“Which audio is more expressive?”Lis-
teners could select a preferred audio or declare a tie.
4.3.2. Results and Analysis
Subjective Results: In blind A/B tests, listeners overwhelm-
ingly favored our S2S-FT model (78.5%) over the baseline
(10.0%), with11.5%ties. This strong preference is statistically
significant (p <0.001), proving that DeEAR-curated train-
ing data directly translates to audible improvements in human-
perceived expressiveness.
Objective Results: DeEAR scores (Table 6) corroborate
these human findings. Our model significantly outperforms the
baseline across all metrics (p <0.001). The largest gains are in
emotion (S emo) and spontaneity (S spon), exactly aligning with
our curation criteria. Furthermore, the minimal score drop on
out-of-domain data demonstrates robust generalization.
The strong agreement between human preference and
DeEAR scores proves that a reliable metric can systematically
drive the development of expressive conversational AI.

5. Conclusion
In this paper, we introduced DeEAR, a human-aligned and
data-efficient metric for multi-dimensional speech expressive-
ness. By capturing Emotion, Prosody, and Spontaneity, DeEAR
achieves strong correlation with human perception and scales
beyond costly subjective evaluation. Leveraging this metric,
we curated ExpressiveSpeech, a large-scale bilingual dataset of
highly expressive speech, and fine-tuned a baseline S2S model
to substantially improve expressiveness. Our findings establish
a paradigm of evaluation-driven data curation, underscoring that
reliable metrics are crucial for advancing expressive speech syn-
thesis. Future directions include extending DeEAR to reinforce-
ment learning for end-to-end expressiveness optimization.
