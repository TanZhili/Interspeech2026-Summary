Abstract
Self-supervised learning (SSL) has become popular in speech
processing because it generalizes well across downstream tasks.
However, many SSL methods focus on capturing long-term
contextual and speaker information, often making their repre-
sentations invariant to background acoustics, which is an is-
sue for speech quality assessment as it depends heavily on
non-speech and fine-grained acoustic cues. These models also
tend to be parameter heavy, limiting their use on resource con-
strained devices. In this work, we introduce an encoder that in-
corporates acoustic detail by combining local spectral–temporal
modeling, a frame wise spectral relationship aggregator, and
explicit noise and reverberation information alongside speech
content. Using this pre-training framework for speech quality
assessment across multiple datasets, we show that the encoder
effectively extracts fine-grained acoustic features and achieves
performance comparable to much larger models.
Index Terms: speech quality, self-supervised learning, rever-
beration, background noise
1. Introduction
Self-supervised learning (SSL) has achieved similar or better
performance than supervised-only models across many down-
stream tasks, with approaches such as Wav2vec 2.0 [1], Hu-
BERT [2], Dasheng [3] and WavLM [4] widely used in practice.
These SSL approaches capture long-term temporal information
through contrastive learning or masked prediction. Numerous
speech- and speaker-focused downstream tasks have benefited
from SSL, including phoneme recognition [1], sensitive word
detection [5], speaker diarization [4], and speech enhancement
[6]. PASE [7], a multi-task learning based SSL model, was used
for automatic speech recognition and speaker identification. In
[8], large SSL models are fine-tuned for mean opinion score
(MOS) prediction using a smaller human-labeled dataset com-
pared to the larger datasets typically used for supervised mod-
els. Although SSL embeddings improve performance for many
speech-related downstream tasks, their performance on percep-
tual tasks [9, 10, 11] or auditory scene understanding [3] is not
always significantly better than that of non-SSL approaches.
Additionally, for MOS-based prediction, SSL models still strug-
gle to generalize across distributions and domains [12]. This
may occur in part because SSL models are often pre-trained
on clean speech and are therefore less robust to noisy and re-
verberant environmental conditions, even though these factors
strongly affect perceptual quality [13]. It is worth noting that
XANE [14] extracts neural embeddings related to background
acoustic properties, while PASE+ considers noisy-reverberant

input signals [15]. These approaches, however, are not evalu-
ated for MOS prediction. In our previous work [16], inspired by
[7, 14], a baseline encoder was pre-trained by introducing learn-
ing objectives, known as workers, to embed latent representa-
tions with speech and noise information. More specifically, they
introduced objectives optimized for the prominent spectral en-
ergy band (e.g., low, mid, high), the signal-to-noise ratio (SNR),
and the category of noise present in noisy speech signals. A re-
gressive head was then fine-tuned to estimate MOS from the
learned embeddings. This approach improved MOS prediction
across multiple datasets compared to baseline models that em-
bed only speech information. Moreover, MOS is linked to hu-
man perception and is evaluated based on multiple factors such
as spectral quality, temporal characteristics, noise, artifacts, and
overall quality. It is crucial to understand the behavior of dif-
ferent types of noise, the range of speech and noise intensity
levels, and the variation in noise sources with respect to tempo-
ral and spectral characteristics, as these factors affect perceptual
quality [17]. Reverberation also influences speech quality and
intelligibility, and the direct-to-reverberation ratio (DRR) is an
important measure for characterizing acoustic room configura-
tions [18].
In Speech COntrastive REgression for Quality (SCOREQ)
[12], it was suggested that supervised contrastive learning is
better than a L2 loss for regression-based speech quality as-
sessment. They show that the contrastive loss improved do-
main mismatch for both out-of-domain (ODM) and out-of-
distribution (ODS) test sets. They also note that speech sig-
nals contain multiple entangled factors that are irrelevant to
quality, such as speaker identity and spoken content, making
it difficult to learn a low-dimensional space. It is important to
identify which parts of the speech signal lead to lower scores;
therefore, an assessment model must extract low-level features
from speech. Recent work in video quality assessment [19, 20]
has focused on extracting fine-grained representations to cap-
ture the complexity of visual content and temporal dynamics.
Video-Panda [21], a parameter-efficient architecture for fine-
grained feature extraction, captures both frame-level and video-
level relationships. This encoder-free, parameter-efficient ap-
proach demonstrates performance that is comparable or supe-
rior to standard benchmarks and is effective on fine-grained
video question-answering tasks. Since audio and video process-
ing share similar representation-learning principles, audio qual-
ity assessment may also benefit from leveraging fine-grained
feature understanding.
Inspired by [21], we propose a Fine-grained Acoustically-
aware encoder as a pre-training framework for Speech Qual-
ity Assessment (FASQA). Our framework consists of a Local
Spectral-Temporal Encoder for modeling spectral–temporal de-
pendencies and a Frame-wise Spectral Relationship Aggregator

2. Proposed Model
An overview of our pre-training framework is shown in Fig. 1.
The encoder is optimized with multiple learning objectives to
embed noise and reverberation information into latent repre-
sentations from clean, noisy, and noisy-reverberant inputs. We
develop a FASQA encoder that is trained using seven mul-
tilayer perceptron (MLP)-based workers: four for regression
tasks (waveform, log power spectrum (LPS), mel-frequency
cepstral coefficients (MFCC), and prosody), three for binary
classification (local info max (LIM), global info max (GIM),
and sequence predicting coding (SPC)).
2.1. Proposed Encoder: FASQA
In Video-Panda [21], a Local Spatio-Temporal Encoding
(LSTE) module captures fine-grained spatio-temporal patterns
by enabling each token to aggregate context from neighbor-
ing frames and spatial regions. A Frame-wise Spatial Rela-
tionship Aggregator (FSRA) module then captures frame-wise
spatial relationships, while a Global Spatio-Temporal Relation-
ship Aggregator (GSTRA) captures broader video context. The
main advantage of Video-Panda is its parameter efficiency and
strong performance on fine-grained video question-answering

2.2. Proposed acoustic workers
Our classification workers consist of the noise-only workers
from [16], which include: noise type, SNR, and spectral energy
distribution classification. (1) Noise type, based on AudioSet
[23] categories (e.g., human, animal, music, vehicle, natural,
background, surface contact). Reverberant signals are labeled
as “background,” and an additional class is used for clean sig-
nals. (2) SNR level classification for -5, 0, 5, 10, and 15 dB,
plus a class for clean or reverberant signals. (3) Spectral energy
region (low, mid, or high) with the highest concentration of en-
ergy. We extend [16] by introducing two additional workers:
(4) Direct-to-reverberation ratio (DRR) classification, and (5)
Frequency bandwidth classification (narrowband or wideband)
for the distortion. Noise type and SNR level classifications are
the same as [16]. Spectral energy region helps the network
learn where noise exists across frequency regions, while the
narrowband/wideband label helps the model learn how broadly
the noise spans the spectrum - both factors that can influence
perceived speech quality. Each of the five acoustic workers is
implemented using an MLP with a single hidden layer of 256
units and a Parametric ReLU (PReLU) activation function. The
output layer uses cross-entropy loss.
2.2.1. Spectral Energy Distribution
This worker learns noise distributions that affect perceived
speech quality. To incorporate this, we compute the distortion’s
spectrum via the Fourier transform and divide it into three equal
regions: low, mid, and high frequencies, following [16]. The re-
gion with the highest energy defines the ground-truth class, with
an additional class for clean signals. For noisy inputs, only the
noise signal is used to determine the class. For reverberant sig-
nals, we use early and late components; for noisy-reverberant
signals, all components are considered. An MLP then classifies
whether the input is clean or dominated by low, mid, or high-
frequency noise energy.
2.2.2. Direct to reverberation ratio (DRR)
We simulate reverberant speech by convolving clean signals
with room impulse responses (RIRs), denoted ash=h d +
he +hl, whereh d,h e, andh l represent the direct, early, and late
components, respectively. The direct component spans 1 ms af-
ter the first peak; the early component lasts 50 ms [24]; and the
remainder is considered late reverberation. DRR quantifies the
energy ratio between direct and reverberant sound in dB [25].
For each room, we simulate three reverberation times (T60),
randomizing source and speaker positions while maintaining a
2 m separation. DRR is computed for each RIR and catego-
rized into three balanced classes: less than−6.25dB,−6.25
to−0.15dB, and−0.15to15dB, corresponding to strongly,
moderately, and weakly reverberant environments. The ratio-
nale behind these DRR class thresholds is that different rever-
beration times (T60 values of 0.3 s, 0.6 s, and 0.9 s) help charac-
terize how strongly reverberation affects a signal. Additionally,
we label anechoic signals (no reverberation) separately. The
DRR worker, implemented as an MLP, is trained to classify the
input signal’s DRR category, following the approach used for
the other workers.
2.2.3. Narrowband/Wideband
This worker classifies the noise spectral distribution based on
being wideband or narrowband. Wideband noise is defined by
a 16 kHz sampling rate (max freq. of 8 kHz). To create narrow-
band versions, we apply a low-pass filter with a 4 kHz cutoff,
followed by zero-padding to retain the 16 kHz rate. We use an
equal number of wide- and narrowband samples, along with an
additional class for clean or reverberant signals (i.e., no additive
noise). An MLP classifies inputs into one of these 3 categories.

3. Dataset
The encoder is trained on the LibriSpeech dataset [27]. We
collect 11,175 noise samples from FSDKaggle2018 [28], the
Hu Corpus [29], and NoiseX-92 [30]. Noisy speech sig-
nals are generated at SNR levels of -5, 0, 5, 10, and 15 dB.
We simulate 2,694 RIRs using the ten room configurations
from [24] with T60 values of 0.3 s (958 RIRs), 0.6 s (868
RIRs), and 0.9 s (868 RIRs). RIRs from the first five
rooms are used for reverberant signals and the remaining five
for noisy-reverberant signals. In total, the training set con-
tains 23,868 signals with 2,652 additional signals for vali-
dation. Noisy and reverberant signals are evenly distributed
across SNR and room conditions. Of all training signals,
75% are noisy, reverberant, or noisy-reverberant, and 25%
are clean. All audio is sampled at 16 kHz. For quality
prediction, we use the NISQA TRAIN SIM (6,000 samples),
NISQA V ALSIM (2,500 samples), and NISQA TEST FOR
(240 samples) datasets [31]. Additionally, we include the IUB
dataset [32], which contains 6,000 samples each from the CO-
SINE corpus [33]. Original MOS scores ranging from 0 to 100
are rescaled to a 1–5 range using min-max normalization to
align with the NISQA format. We also evaluate on TMHINT-
QI [34], which contains Taiwanese Mandarin speech along with
enhanced speech outputs.

4.2. Comparison with other SSL approaches
We use mean squared error (MSE), linear correlation coefficient
(LCC), and Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient (SRCC) to
evaluate MOS prediction performance. Table 1 presents results
across all datasets. For all SSL models, we use pre-trained ver-
sions, except for the PASE baseline [7], PASE w/ Noise Work-
ers [16], PASE w/ Acoustic Workers and FASQA w/ Acous-
tic workers (Small) which are trained from scratch using a 7
hr dataset. We keep all pre-training encoders frozen in Ta-
ble 1 and train the MOS prediction head with the respective
MOS-labeled training dataset. The encoder is used as a fea-
ture extractor and the features from the pre-training encoder do
not change since our motive is to make the model architecture
computationally efficient. For this experiment, FASQA (Large)
was trained on a 78 hr dataset. On the NISQA and COSINE
dataset, PASE w/ Acoustic Workers outperforms PASE base-
line and PASE w/Noise workers in both MSE and correlation
metrics. PASE w/ Acoustic Workers also shows stronger corre-
lation than the baseline PASE, indicating that training solely on
clean data limits generalization to challenging acoustic condi-
tions. Compared to PASE w/ Noise Workers, the acoustic ver-
sion performs better, highlighting the value of the added Nar-
rowband/Wideband and DRR workers. Our encoder FASQA
w/ Acoustic (Small) outperforms PASE w/ Acoustic, Wav2vec
2.0 [1] and HuBERT [2] on NISQA and TMHINT datasets,
which implies that the encoder having fine-grained information
is helpful for speech quality assessment. Dasheng outperforms
all model architectures including FASQA (Small) because it is
trained with speech, music, and environmental sounds for gen-
eralization across audio datasets, though FASQA w/ Acous-
tic (Large) achieves competitive performance as Dasheng [3]
with large dataset. Overall, our proposed encoder is performing
well compared to large SSL models that have been trained with
multiple datasets. To assess the new workers’ contribution to
MOS prediction, we perform an ablation study by removing two
workers from FASQA w/ Acoustic workers (Small). Removing
the new workers increases MSE and reduces correlation.
5. Conclusion
We propose an encoder which incorporates fine-grained infor-
mation and adds noise and reverberation information alongside
speech content. Our proposed encoder embeddings can make
clearer clusters compared to PASE baseline and also show com-
petitive performance with SSL model architectures. In addition,
our encoder, which learns fine-grained information, is helpful
for speech quality assessment. We experimented with vari-
ous encoders and real-world acoustic environments using our
proposed acoustic workers, which improved both speech qual-
ity prediction and model robustness. Incorporating background
acoustic information enhanced performance for signals contain-
ing speech alone or mixed with noise. Results show that the
proposed encoder with acoustic workers significantly improves
prediction across multiple datasets. Despite its smaller archi-
tecture (15M parameters), our model achieves competitive per-
formance compared to larger SSL models, thanks to its noise-
variant and fine-grained feature representation design. In fu-
ture work, we plan to explore evaluations on out-of-distribution
(ODS) and out-of-domain (ODM) test sets.
