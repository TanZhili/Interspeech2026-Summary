Improving Streaming Speaker Diarization for LLM Based Multi-talker Speech
Understanding
Ju Lin, Ruizhi Li, Ruizhe Huang, Jing Pan, Xuan Zhang, Zili Huang,
Jing Zheng, Ming Sun, Florian Metze
Meta, USA
julincs@meta.com
Abstract
Previous work has shown that source separation with post-
processing can help large language models (LLMs) handle
multi-talker speech by serving as an external speaker diariza-
tion module. However, simple threshold-based approaches lack
robustness under diverse acoustic conditions. We propose a
two-stage framework comprising a source separation model fol-
lowed by a statistical classifier (e.g., Gradient Boosting Deci-
sion Trees) for speaker tagging. To improve efficiency, we ex-
plore a Conformer-based architecture that significantly reduces
parameter count. Additionally, to accommodate varying mi-
crophone geometries across smart glasses devices, we develop
a multi-geometry model enabling a single source separation
model to generalize across devices. Experiments on real-world
multi-talker recordings demonstrate improvements in both ac-
curacy and efficiency for speech recognition and translation
tasks.
Index Terms: speech recognition, speech translation, speaker
diarization, smart glasses
1. Introduction
Modern wearable devices, such as smart glasses, are commonly
equipped with microphone arrays capable of capturing spa-
tial audio signals. Recent research has leveraged these multi-
channel audio inputs to enable real-time captioning and speech
translation, thereby enriching human communication experi-
ences through robust and efficient speech-understanding sys-
tems [1, 2, 3, 4, 5]. Prior work [1] has demonstrated that multi-
microphone arrays can effectively distinguish among the de-
vice wearer, direct conversational partners, and ambient speak-
ers, achieving superior noise robustness over traditional single-
channel beamforming methods.
Multimodal large language models (LLMs) have recently
demonstrated strong speech-understanding capabilities, yet
their audio input is predominantly monaural. To address this
limitation, recent work has explored integrating spatial audio
understanding into LLMs, typically by fine-tuning pretrained
models on spatial audio representations with textual supervi-
sion. For instance, Tang et al. [6] adapted LLMs to multi-
channel spatial audio, achieving state-of-the-art performance on
sound source localization, far-field speech recognition, and text-
prompted speech extraction in overlapping speech scenarios.
Similarly, BAT [7] introduced a binaural audio encoder cou-
pled with an LLM to address spatial audio question answering,
enabling localization, distance estimation, and speaker identifi-
cation.
To further address multi-talker scenarios, several works
have incorporated Serialized Output Training (SOT) [8] into the
LLM fine-tuning paradigm. Xie et al. [9] proposed Directional-
SpeechLlama, which employs serialized directional output
training for joint speech recognition and source localization.
Shi et al. [10] introduced Serialized Output Prompting (SOP),
an adaptive prompting method that guides the model to separate
overlapping speakers based on textual cues describing the mix-
ing conditions. Meng et al. [11] presented an LLM-based SOT
framework that leverages pretrained speech encoders fine-tuned
on single-channel multi-talker data.
However, spatial audio LLMs fine-tuned on multichannel
inputs can be sensitive to microphone array geometry, as a ded-
icated encoder must be trained for each device configuration,
effectively locking the model to that specific setup. To address
this limitation, Dementyev et al. [12] proposed PhaseCoder, a
geometry-agnostic spatial audio encoder that produces Spatial
Audio Tokens for LLM integration, enabling complex spatial
reasoning and targeted transcription from arbitrary microphone
arrays. An alternative approach [13] decouples spatial process-
ing from language modeling by employing an external front-
end module (e.g., speaker diarization) to handle multi-talker
understanding; in this framework, the LLM remains device-
agnostic, and only the front-end component requires device-
specific adaptation.
In this work, we propose a two-stage streaming speaker di-
arization framework tailored for smart glasses, where a wearer
(near-field source) engages in conversation with a distant part-
ner (far-field source). The distinct spatial and acoustic charac-
teristics between these sources—arising from differences in dis-
tance, direction, and signal-to-noise ratio—can be effectively
exploited through multichannel processing. Prior work [13]
employed a simple chunk-wise Root Mean Square (RMS)-ratio
comparison for speaker tagging; however, this approach can be
sensitive to varying noise levels and acoustic conditions. To im-
prove robustness, we propose a two-stage pipeline. The first
stage employs a source separation model to disentangle wearer
and partner speech from the multichannel input. The second
stage utilizes a lightweight classifier—such as Support Vec-
tor Machines (SVM) [14] or Gradient Boosting Decision Trees
(GBDT) [15]—operating on acoustic features including funda-
mental frequency (F0) and RMS to predict speaker tags: self,
other, or non-speech. These tags subsequently inform down-
stream LLM-based tasks. We further explore several extensions
to enhance system quality and efficiency. First, we investi-
gate multi-task learning, enabling the source separation model
to jointly perform separation and speaker classification. Sec-
ond, we evaluate a lightweight Conformer-based architecture as
an alternative to prior LSTM-based approaches. Finally, since
beamforming transforms geometry-dependent array signals into
direction-indexed representations (e.g., 13 fixed directions), we
explore training a unified multi-geometry model capable of gen-
eralizing across diverse microphone array configurations.

2. Proposed Approach
As shown in Figure 1, a 5-channel microphone array embed-
ded in smart glasses feeds N spatially-steered beamformers with
geometry-agnostic encoding. A source separation module de-
composes the beamformed signals into three streams: a refer-
ence channel, self (wearer), and other (conversation partner).
From the separated streams, F0 and RMS energy features are
extracted and classified by a GBDT into three categories: Self,
Other, and Non-speech. The reference channel audio, together
with the predicted speaker tags, is forwarded to a frozen LLM
for speaker-attributed ASR and translation. Specifically, the
predicted tag is used to select a task-specific prompt—such
as transcribe in [target language] or translate to [target lan-
guage]—enabling the model to generate speaker-aware outputs
for the designated task. Accurate speaker tagging is critical for
this application: if the wearer’s speech is mis-attributed to the
partner (or vice versa), the system will transcribe or translate
into the wrong language, producing erroneous outputs. The fol-
lowing subsections describe each component in detail.
2.1. Improved Source Separation Model
Following [13], our source separation (SS) model adopts an
encoder-decoder architecture with several key improvements.
The model is trained to isolate wearer speech (originating from
the mouth direction) and partner speech (within ±60° frontal
arc) while suppressing background noise and bystander audio
(rear hemisphere). This directional partitioning enables the
model to output two distinct audio streams corresponding to the
wearer and partner, respectively. To further improve speaker
discrimination, we incorporate a multi-task learning objective
by adding a classification head as an auxiliary task. The total
loss combines a reconstruction term and a classification term.
The reconstruction loss comprises an L1 loss in the time do-
main, a weighted Short-Time Fourier Transform (STFT) loss
(WSTFT) [16], and a Log SI-SDR loss, ensuring both spectral
fidelity and signal quality. Regarding architectural efficiency,
we observe that the recurrent layers in prior designs [13] dom-
inate parameter count, while the convolutional encoder and de-
coder remain lightweight. To reduce model size while main-
taining performance, we replace Gated Recurrent Unit (GRU)
layers with Conformer blocks [17]. The Conformer architec-
ture combines the strengths of convolutional neural networks
and Transformers: convolution modules capture local acoustic
patterns, while self-attention modules model long-range tempo-
ral dependencies. This hybrid design yields a more parameter-
efficient model while preserving separation quality compared to
recurrent baselines.
2.2. Second-stage Classifier
Real-world speech understanding tasks operate across diverse
acoustic environments—restaurants, streets, and other noisy
settings—where background noise can render simple threshold-
based approaches ineffective. To address this challenge, we
employ a two-stage post-processing pipeline, as illustrated in
Figure 1. Once the source separation model produces self and
other audio streams, a second-stage classifier processes acoustic
features (F0 and RMS) extracted from both separated channels
and the reference channel. We evaluate two classifiers—SVM
and GBDT—each predicting three speaker tags: self, other, and
non-speech, which subsequently inform the LLM’s downstream
transcription and translation tasks. The rationale for this decou-
pled design stems from data availability: while insufficient la-
beled multichannel data exists to train a fully integrated end-to-
end model, we have adequate annotations to train a lightweight
post-processing classifier.

2.3. Multi-device model
We employ Non-Linearly Constrained Minimum Variance (NL-
CMV) beamforming [5] to process raw multichannel audio
through a set ofNfixed beamformers:N−1horizontally-
steered directions around the smart glasses, plus one directed to-
ward the wearer’s mouth. Each beamformer uses predetermined
coefficients tailored to the specific microphone array geometry.
This beamforming stage transforms device-specific multichan-
nel signals into a geometry-agnostic directional representation,
enabling joint training across devices with heterogeneous array
configurations. Following [5], we design device-specific beam-
former coefficients for each hardware platform while sharing all
downstream model parameters.
3. Experimental Results
3.1. Data
Due to the scarcity of real-world multichannel recordings, all
source separation training data is simulated based on a 5-
microphone array geometry similar to Project Aria glasses [18].
Room impulse responses (RIRs) from real environments are
used to simulate 12 azimuthal directions at 30° intervals, with
five frontal directions (−60 ◦,−30 ◦,0 ◦,30 ◦,60 ◦) designated
for the conversation partner to reflect typical conversational sce-
narios. Training samples are constructed by spatially position-
ing single-channel audio from LibriSpeech [19] to represent the
wearer and partner, with non-overlapping partner and bystander
speech and noise from arbitrary directions; further simulation
details are provided in [1]. For the second-stage classifier, we
use 1,000 in-house multichannel conversational recordings with
human-annotated speaker timestamps. Evaluation is conducted
on three language pairs: Spanish–English, French–English, and
Italian–English, each comprising 200–350 real multichannel
conversational recordings captured under diverse noise condi-
tions. There is no overlap between the training and evaluation
sets.
3.2. Setup
The source separation model architecture is adapted from [3,
13]. We extract 257-dimensional complex STFT features for
each beamformer direction or raw microphone channel, and
concatenate features across all directions/channels as input. The
model is trained for 60 epochs using the Adam optimizer with
a tri-stage learning rate scheduler: a peak learning rate of
4×10 −4, 10k warmup iterations, and forced annealing after
10 epochs.
The baseline GRU-based model contains approximately
20M parameters. For multi-task learning, we add a classi-
fication head comprising two linear layers (1280→256→3)
with ReLU activation [20] and dropout (p= 0.3), predict-
ing three speaker classes: self, other, and non-speech. For the
Conformer-based variant, we replace the two GRU layers with
Conformer encoder layers, each configured with an input di-
mension of 128, 4 attention heads, a feed-forward dimension of
256, and a convolution kernel size of 15. We employ rotary po-
sitional embeddings, macaron-style half-step residual connec-
tions, pre-layer normalization, and a dropout rate of 0.1 across
all sub-modules. This substitution reduces the total parameter
count to 5.5M. During inference, the model operates in chunk-
wise streaming mode with a chunk size of 600ms. We adopt
Gemma-3n 4B1 as our base LLM—an open-source, instruction-
tuned multimodal model that natively accepts audio and text
inputs while generating text outputs, making it well-suited for
speech understanding tasks.
The GBDT classifier is trained using scikit-learn’s Gradient
Boosting Classifier with 100 boosting iterations, a learning rate
of 0.1, and a maximum tree depth of 3. The model is trained
on an 80/20 train-test split with stratified sampling to preserve
class distribution. The SVM classifier uses an RBF kernel with
regularizationC= 1.0,γ=scale, balanced class weights, and
z-score normalized features.
The multi-device configuration extends the single-device
architecture to support two different smart glass devices
(Device-A and Device-B) by incorporating device-specific
beamformer weights for their respective microphone array ge-
ometries. During training, we apply layer normalization across
frequency and channel dimensions to standardize spectral fea-
tures, along with audio volume perturbation for data augmen-
tation. We employ a multi-domain training strategy that com-
bines data from both device types with a 0.25 mixing ratio for
the secondary device. Additionally, in the downstream GBDT-
based post-processing stage, we incorporate the array ID as an
additional feature to enable device-aware decision making.

4. Conclusions
In this work, we presented a two-stage streaming speaker di-
arization framework for LLM-based multi-talker speech under-
standing on smart glasses. The first stage employs a source
separation model to isolate wearer and partner speech, while
the second stage leverages a lightweight classifier (SVM or
GBDT) operating on acoustic features to predict speaker tags.
We demonstrated the effectiveness of this approach on both
speech recognition and translation tasks across three language
pairs, achieving substantial reductions in both Word Error Rate
and Speaker Attribution Error Rate compared to single-stage
baselines. Furthermore, we explored multi-task learning to
jointly optimize separation and classification, and introduced a
parameter-efficient Conformer-based architecture that reduces
model size from 20M to 5.5M parameters while maintaining
performance. Finally, by leveraging geometry-agnostic beam-
forming, we developed a unified multi-device source separation
model capable of generalizing across diverse microphone array
configurations—an important step toward device-agnostic de-
ployment in real-world wearable applications.
