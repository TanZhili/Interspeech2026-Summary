Abstract
Current CAPT systems prioritize identification and correction
of all learner deviations from native speech, thereby overlook-
ing the main goal of language acquisition: effective commu-
nication. In response, we propose an intelligibility-centered
approach, which targets only selected segmental aspects of
speech influencing communicative effectiveness. We employ
a regression model based on phoneme substitution errors. Us-
ing AdaBoost feature importance, we propose an intelligibility-
based ranking of segmental errors, shedding light on the rela-
tionship between the mispronunciation of individual phonemes
and overall speech intelligibility. This approach, validated
on human ratings of speech samples from V oxPopuli, spee-
chocean762, and an internal dataset, not only predicts intelligi-
bility levels but also directs ESL learners toward the acquisition
of the most critical phonemes. By foregrounding intelligibility
over nativeness, it supports more learner-centered and inclusive
CAPT solutions.
Index Terms: automatic pronunciation assessment, CAPT, in-
telligibility, segmental errors, AdaBoost regression
1. Introduction
As English as a Second Language (ESL) learners frequently
signal the need to improve their second language (L2) speech,
computer-assisted pronunciation training (CAPT) can be a pos-
sible solution by providing personalized and instant feedback
on the learner’s pronunciation proficiency. Current automatic
pronunciation assessment solutions used in education often rely
on evaluating all segmental features of speech, i.e. individual
vowels and consonants of a target language [1, 2], occasionally
including suprasegmental features to a limited extent [3, 4, 5, 6].
This approach, while easily quantifiable, may not be the most
suitable for assessing pronunciation, as it assumes that the ul-
timate goal of pronunciation learning is to sound like a native
speaker. Moreover, it also provides limited and non-actionable
feedback in pronunciation instruction [7]. At the same time, ex-
tensive research [8, 9, 10] has shown that achieving native-like
pronunciation may not be necessary or realistic, suggesting the
need to shift the focus to more practical goals relying on speech
intelligibility, i.e. how well the speaker’s intended message can
be correctly identified and understood by the listener. This ap-
proach paves the way to creating an intelligibility-based rank-
ing of errors, allowing ESL learners to focus on those aspects
of pronunciation that affect their communicative effectiveness,
rather than the proximity to the native model. Consequently,
it promotes more equitable participation in spoken interaction
within multilingual contexts.

fecting speech intelligibility relate to suprasegmental features

speech intelligibility and allows to create a practical ranking of
errors for automatic pronunciation assessment.
2. Method
Our proposed method, outlined in Figure 1, consists of three
stages: (1) the selection of L2 speech datasets suitable for in-
vestigating speech intelligibility, (2) the preparation of data to
align with the objectives of our study, which includes recording
selection to ensure diverse representation across speaker char-
acteristics, recording rating to establish a ground truth for the
model, and feature extraction for training the model, and (3)
model training and evaluation. The following sections delve
into each of the stages in more detail.

2.1. Datasets
We used three datasets, each characterized by varying speaker
profiles including first language (L1) background, English pro-
ficiency, gender, and age. Two of these datasets were publicly
available speech corpora, while the third was an internal cor-
pus collected from Pearson’s product users. All recordings ad-
hered to a standardized format, featuring a wave extension and
a 16kHz, 16-bit, mono-channel configuration.
V oxPopuli[14] offers nearly 30 hours of transcribed speech
collected from 2009-2020 European Parliament sessions. It
features 364 L2 English speakers representing 15 different L1
backgrounds, with predominant contributions from Germanic,
Slavic, and Romance languages.
speechoncean762[15] features 5,000 English sentences
read by 250 L2 speakers, including adults and children, with
L1 Chinese. The sentences relate to daily life situations and
include transcriptions.
Theinternal dataset, collected between 2020 and 2022,
comprises recordings from Pearson’s product users who pro-
vided informed consent for research use. This dataset consists
of 2200 transcribed speech samples from L2 English speakers
with diverse L1 backgrounds, including Romance, Slavic, Indo-
Aryan, and Turkic languages. The recordings were sourced
from exam preparation activities, incorporating such pronunci-
ation tasks as read-aloud and text repetition.
2.2. Data preparation
The data were prepared through recording selection and rating
as described in the following subsections to ensure that the cor-
pora met our research requirements.
2.2.1. Recording selection
The selection process comprised two stages: participant profile
filtering and recording duration selection. In the first stage, we
excluded child recordings fromspeechocean762, due to uncer-
tainty regarding the effectiveness of automatic phoneme recog-
nition services on child speech. Additionally, we selected par-
ticipant profiles based on their L1 background, gender, and age
to ensure a diverse representation across these variables (see
Table 1). The diversity in English proficiency among the par-
ticipants was not explicitly indicated in the metadata; how-
ever, it was achieved through the inclusion of datasets fea-
turing ESL learners at different stages of English acquisition
(speechocean762and theinternal dataset), alongside a dataset
comprising proficient English speakers from professional con-
texts (V oxPopuli). Next, we selected a subset of recordings to
achieve normal distribution of duration between 5 and 15 sec-
onds for each corpus, offering a thorough evaluation of intelli-
gibility without burdening raters with excessively long record-
ings. The final dataset comprised 600 transcribed recordings
(mean length = 7.81s, SD = 2.39), with 275 from theinter-
nal dataset, 175 fromV oxPopuli, and 50 fromspeechocean762.
This distribution was guided by the alignment of each dataset
with our research objectives, with the internal dataset prioritized
due to its compatibility with potential implementation scenarios
in Pearson’s products and its diverse range of speaker character-
istics.

expect biased perception of intelligibility when the speaker’s
L1 matches the listener’s L1 or when the listener is frequently
exposed to the speaker’s L1 accent. This layer of subjectivity
could result in differing evaluations of the same data, thereby
reducing the agreement metric but not necessarily invalidating
the reliability of the dataset. These evaluations constituted the
ground truth for the model’s output.
2.2.3. Features extraction
Model training utilized automatically collected features, cate-
gorized into two types:
• The mispronounced phoneme ratiomispronounced a, indi-
cating the frequency of mispronounced phonemes relative to
the total expected occurrences of each phoneme within the
speech sample.
• The phoneme pair substitution ratiosubstitution a b, reflect-
ing the frequency of substituting phonemeawith phoneme
b, or vice versa, relative to the total expected occurrences of
those phonemes within the speech sample.

2.3. Model training
Several regression models were evaluated: Support Vector Re-
gression (SVR), Gaussian Process Regression (GPR), K Neigh-
bors Regression (KNR), Gradient Boosting Regression (GBR),
Random Forest Regression (RFR), and Adaptive Boosting with
Decision Tree Regression (AdaBoost DTR).
Several hyperparameters were manually selected for train-
ing the regression models. The SVR model used a Radial Basis
Function (RBF) kernel. GPR was conducted using the rational
quadratic kernel with the L-BFGS-B optimizer. KNR utilized
the KDTree algorithm with 5 nearest neighbors. For GBR, we
incorporated a squared error loss, a learning rate of 0.1, a total
of 100 estimators, and utilized the mean squared error with im-
provement score by Friedman to measure the quality of a split.
For AdaBoost DTR, a minimum sample split of 5 and 50 es-
timators was selected. Factors such as minimalization of error
and avoidance of overfitting were key in determining these op-
timized hyperparameters for training the models.
A comparison of the regression models was conducted to
determine the most efficient model for predicting intelligibility
ratings based on phoneme mispronunciation features.

While the utilization of a larger dataset could improve the
results, it is noteworthy that the segmental-based features em-
ployed for training represent just one among many variables
influencing L2 speech intelligibility. As stated previously, ex-
isting literature [11, 12, 13] suggests that suprasegmental fea-
tures exert a more significant impact on intelligibility. Conse-
quently, our results align with the hypothesis suggesting that
models trained on segmental features can predict the level of
intelligibility to a limited extent.
3.1. Feature importance
The applicability of the segmental error ranking in evaluating
L2 intelligibility was assessed via the feature importance of
the AdaBoost model. AdaBoost’s feature importance is derived
from its base classifier, in this case Decision Tree. It is calcu-
lated as a weighted average of feature importance (Gini impor-
tance) provided by each estimator in the ensemble model. The

3.1.1. V owels
Our results highlight the substantial effect of phonemes like /E/,
/@/, /I/, and /i/ on intelligibility, with corresponding importance
scores of 0.072, 0.066, 0.064 and 0.038, respectively. These re-
sults are in line with former experimental and theoretical works
on speech intelligibility. First, the highest result for /E/ is con-
nected to the /E–ae/ contrast, which is problematic for many L2
speakers [17, 18]. Next, /I/ and /i/ have a high functional load
and occur in many minimal pairs, making them crucial in suc-
cessful communication [19]. Finally, the high result for /@/ is in
agreement with the fact that it is the most frequent sound in En-
glish and plays a significant role in lexical stress and language
rhythm, as it is only found in unstressed syllables. Indeed,
vowel reduction is a characteristic feature of stressed-timed lan-
guages, and the ability to master this particular phoneme allows
ESL learners to achieve more fluent and natural pronunciation
[20].
3.1.2. Consonants
Fricatives /z/, /s/, /D/, and /v/ have a significant influence on
L2 speech intelligibility due to their functional roles in En-
glish. /z/ significantly impacts intelligibility with a score of
0.092, possibly due to its role in plurals and verb tenses – often
mispronounced as /s/, which is also one of the most important
phonemes for the model, can lead to confusion in semantic in-
terpretation. /D/, with an importance score of 0.025, is a highly
marked sound, rarely present across the world’s languages [21],
and is considered difficult for L2 speakers of English, frequently
substituted with perceptually similar phonemes found in their
L1 phonetic system. Next, the reason why plosives /k/ (impor-
tance of 0.049) and /t/ (importance of 0.027) impact L2 speech
intelligibility can be twofold. When appearing word-initially,
these sounds become aspirated. This feature is a crucial cue
for English voiceless stops [22]. When appearing word-finally,
they can be variably unreleased, causing potential problems for
ESL learners [23, 24]. Finally, the high importance of /r/ and /l/
likely reflects the presence of Japanese and Korean ESL learn-
ers, who are known to experience persistent difficulty distin-
guishing and producing these sounds [25, 26].
4. Pedagogical implications
Currently available CAPT solutions focus on mastering all
learner deviations from the perfect L1 speaker model [1, 2, 3, 4].
This approach may not be the most efficient for ESL learners, as
it appears to neglect the ultimate aim of language acquisition:
effective communication [8, 9, 10]. Our approach attempts to
bridge this gap between current CAPT tools and ESL learners’
goals by targeting specific aspects of L2 speech that directly
affect communicative effectiveness, rather than addressing all
deviations between L1 and L2 speech.
This approach offers several pedagogical benefits. First,
it lightens the learning burden by concentrating solely on sig-
nificant pronunciation mistakes, thereby reducing the effort re-
quired for language advancement and providing immediate, tan-
gible results. It also enhances motivation, as ESL learners can
perceive tangible improvement without being subjected to un-
realistic comparisons to L1 speakers, reinforcing the legitimacy
of diverse accents and speaking styles in successful communi-
cation. Additionally, it enables the delivery of more targeted,
actionable feedback without overwhelming learners.
Overall, this approach holds significant promise for enhanc-
ing L2 learning quality and fostering positive attitudes toward
CAPT solutions. It acknowledges that effective communication
does not demand perfection, thus conveying an important mes-
sage to ESL learners.
5. Limitations
Several limitations could have influenced the results of this
study. The primary one revolves around the use of automatic
phoneme recognition services. As these services utilize ma-
chine learning models, their accuracy may have varied across
samples due to factors such as speaker characteristics, speech
clarity, and recording quality. This, in turn, could have propa-
gated errors throughout subsequent stages of the analysis.
Furthermore, the study design lacked representation from
speakers with lower levels of English proficiency. The absence
of such participants likely contributed to the dominance of rat-
ings above the average 3.83, which we mitigated by assigning
weights to the training samples based on the inverse frequency
ratio of rating scores. Nevertheless, including speakers from di-
verse proficiency levels could offer a more holistic view of the
model’s efficacy across different language proficiencies.
Additionally, the dataset used in the study was relatively
small and sparse, thus potentially affecting the robustness and
reliability of findings. Further studies with larger datasets could
provide a more comprehensive understanding of the investi-
gated phenomenon.
Lastly, we did not consider the positional context of
phonemes within words, which could be crucial in evaluating
speech intelligibility. This aspect presents an opportunity for
future exploration.
6. Conclusion
In this paper, we propose a regression model that uses phoneme
substitution errors to predict intelligibility within a speech sam-
ple. By leveraging feature importance derived from the Ad-
aBoost model, we construct an intelligibility-based ranking of
segmental errors, offering insight into the relative impact of
specific phonemes on L2 speech intelligibility. This approach
not only enables the prediction of intelligibility levels but also
supports ESL learners by directing attention to mispronuncia-
tions that most negatively affect communicative clarity, thereby
enhancing overall effectiveness in English. While acknowl-
edging certain limitations, we consider this study an impor-
tant step toward future research and development in CAPT sys-
tems. By foregrounding intelligibility as the primary criterion,
this work also aligns pronunciation assessment with a commu-
nicative perspective in which successful interaction, rather than
native-like conformity, defines effective speech and supports in-
clusion across multilingual contexts.
