Abstract
We present Mixture of Phonetic Experts based Low-Rank
Adaptation (MoPE-LoRA), a parameter-efficient adaptation
framework for accented English speech recognition. Recent
mixture-of-experts approaches typically allocate experts per ac-
cent, implicitly treating accent identity as the primary axis of
variation. In contrast, we organize adaptation along phonetic
categories, motivated by the observation that accent differences
are often expressed as systematic distortions of phoneme real-
ization. MoPE-LoRA introduces a fixed set of six low-rank ex-
perts aligned with manner-of-articulation classes and performs
frame-level routing through a hybrid mechanism that combines
phoneme supervision with learned acoustic gating. Because
the expert set is shared across accents, the model does not re-
quire accent-specific modules and naturally extends to previ-
ously unseen accents. Experiments on L2-ARCTIC show that
MoPE-LoRA consistently improves over single LoRA and full
fine-tuning. In zero-shot evaluation where one accent is held
out during training, the proposed method yields a 12.3% rela-
tive improvement over single LoRA. These results indicate that
phonetic-category-based decomposition offers a scalable and
transferable alternative to accent-level adaptation.
Index Terms: ASR, Accent Adaptation, Conformer, LoRA
1. Introduction
While modern ASR systems achieve high accuracy on native
English speech [1], their performance often degrades on non-
native or accented English, particularly in low-resource sce-
narios [2, 3]. Accented speech typically involves systematic
shifts in pronunciation and phoneme realization, which models
trained primarily on native speech do not fully capture. Improv-
ing robustness to such variation remains an important challenge.
A range of adaptation strategies have been explored. Do-
main adversarial training [4, 5] aims to learn accent-invariant
representations, while multi-task learning [6] introduces auxil-
iary objectives such as accent classification or phoneme predic-
tion. While these methods can partially mitigate performance
drops, they often require carefully balanced training objectives
and additional annotated data, which may not be feasible in low-
resource accented speech scenarios.
More recently, parameter-efficient fine-tuning (PEFT)
methods, particularly low-rank adaptation (LoRA) [7, 8], have
become attractive for adapting large pre-trained ASR models
with limited additional parameters. However, standard LoRA
applies a single adaptation mechanism across all phonetic con-
texts, without explicitly modeling how accent variation mani-

fests at the phoneme level [9].
Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) approaches provide another di-
rection by routing inputs through specialized sub-modules [10,
11, 12, 13]. In multi-accent settings, experts are often assigned
per accent or language. While effective when accent identity
is known, this formulation treats accent as the primary axis of
variation and may require the number of experts to grow with
the number of supported accents. In practice, however, accent
differences are largely expressed through systematic distortions
of phonetic realizations, such as vowel shifts or consonant sub-
stitutions, which may recur across multiple accents. Modeling
adaptation strictly at the accent level may therefore overlook the
shared phonetic structure underlying accent variability.
Motivated by this observation, we propose MoPE-LoRA,
a phonetic routing-based adaptation framework for Conformer
ASR models. Inspired by Switch Transformer [14] and ST-MoE
[15], our method introduces phoneme-aware expert routing, al-
lowing the model to dynamically select experts based on pho-
netic content rather than accent or language identity. By in-
tegrating LoRA in selective layers with phonetic routing, our
method adapts to accented speech using a fixed set of six low-
rank experts aligned with manner-of-articulation classes [16].
To our knowledge, this is the first use of phoneme-category-
grounded LoRA experts with frame-level hybrid routing for
non-native multi-accent ASR.
We evaluate our approach on the L2-ARCTIC dataset [3], a
widely used benchmark for non-native English speech recogni-
tion. Our method improves WER over single LoRA adaptation,
demonstrating that phoneme-aware MoE with phonetic routing
provides an accurate, interpretable, and parameter-efficient so-
lution for accented ASR. To further assess generalization, we
conducted unseen-accent experiments by holding out one ac-
cent from the L2-ARCTIC dataset for testing while training on
the remaining accents. Across all evaluated accents, our ap-

2. Proposed Method
To handle phonetic variations in accented speech, we use
MoPE-LoRA, which decomposes adaptation into linguistically
motivated experts. Each expert targets a phonetic category (e.g.,
vowels, fricatives) via low-rank adapters, providing inductive
bias for phonetic correction. Unlike conventional MoE, MoPE-
LoRA leverages phonetic priors for focused adaptation (Fig-
ure 1).
2.1. Mixture of Phonetic Experts (MoPE) Architecture
We group English consonants into five articulatory classes
following standard manner-of-articulation distinctions [16],
namely Stops/Plosives, Fricatives, Affricates, Nasals, and Liq-
uids/Glides, and add V owels as a sixth category, yielding six
phonetic expert groups in total. Table 1 maps phonemes to the
six linguistic expert categories used for routing and evaluation.

group corresponds to a dedicated LoRA expert, allowing the
model to learn specialized weight adjustments for different ar-
ticulatory classes.

motivated by the fact that attention projections jointly govern
how acoustic context is aggregated across frames, a process di-
rectly affected by phonetic distortion in accented speech. By
adapting all three projections, the experts can modify both what
the model attends to (via Q and K) and the content that is prop-
agated (via V).

3. Experimental Setup
3.1. Datasets
We use the L2-ARCTIC dataset [3], containing approximately
24 hours of non-native English speech from 24 speakers (4 per
accent) across Mandarin, Korean, Spanish, Hindi, Arabic, and
Vietnamese L1 backgrounds. Following the evaluation protocol
of [10], we enforce both speaker- and sentence-disjoint parti-
tions across 4 splits, which is stricter than speaker-only disjoint-
ness as it prevents the model from memorizing sentence-level
patterns during training. In each split, one speaker per accent
(6 speakers) is held out for testing on sentences excluded from
training, while the remaining 18 speakers are used for train-
ing and development. Speaker assignments rotate so that every
speaker is evaluated exactly once, and results are averaged. Ta-
ble 2 reports per-accent statistics. For zero-shot evaluation, one
accent is entirely excluded from training and used only for test-
ing.

3.2. Training Configuration
We use the NeMo Conformer CTC Small model (∼13M pa-
rameters) as the base encoder, with audio resampled to 16 kHz.
Models are trained for 50 epochs with batch size 16. MoPE-
LoRA adapters withE=6experts are applied to Q, K, V pro-
jections in layers 6–16, each with rankr=8andα=16. The
routing network uses top-2 expert selection with hybrid routing
(blendβis a learnable parameter, initialized toσ(0.5)). The
learning rate is1×10 −3 with cosine annealing to5×10 −6.
Training is conducted on an NVIDIA RTX 5090.
Phoneme Model. To provide frame-level phoneme super-
vision for expert routing, we train a separate phoneme-level
CTC model on LibriSpeech [21], using a Conformer CTC Small
architecture (16 layers, dimension 176). Grapheme-to-phoneme
conversion usesg2p-en. The model is trained for 25 epochs
with Adam (β1=0.9,β 2=0.999, weight decay1×10 −5), learn-
ing rate1×10 −3 with cosine annealing and 10% warmup. Dur-
ing MoPE-LoRA training, this model remains frozen.

4.2. Layer Selection Strategy
Not all encoder layers contribute equally to phonetic adaptation.
Prior studies suggest that early layers capture low-level acoustic
features, middle layers encode most phonetic information, and
upper layers focus on contextualized representations [24].
Figure 2:Mutual information between layer representation and
phoneme label.
To verify this, we computed the mutual information (MI)
between each Conformer layer and phoneme labels on TIMIT.
Continuous embeddings were discretized via K-Means, and MI
was calculated between cluster assignments and ground-truth
phonemes (Figure 2). The MI shows an inverted-U trend: mid-
dle layers contain the most phonetic information, while early
and upper layers contain less. t-SNE visualizations (Figure 3)
confirm this pattern.
Based on these results, we focus phonetic adaptation on
layer ranges that include the middle, phoneme-rich layers, while
varying the inclusion of early and upper layers.

5. Conclusion
We introduce MoPE-LoRA, a Mixture of Phonetic Experts
framework adapting Conformer ASR to accented English
via phoneme-category experts and hybrid frame-level routing,
with no accent-specific modules required. On L2-ARCTIC,
it achieves 10.43% WER and 44.3% relative improvement
over the unadapted baseline in zero-shot transfer, confirm-
ing phoneme-level decomposition as a principled, parameter-
efficient solution for accent-agnostic ASR.
