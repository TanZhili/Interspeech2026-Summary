Abstract
Code-switching (CS) speech, characterized by language alter-
nation within a single utterance, is prevalent in multilingual
communities and poses challenges to automatic speech recog-
nition (ASR) due to rapid language transitions. We propose
LLM-HB, a language-aware large language model-guided hot-
word biasing framework for code-switching ASR (CS-ASR).
The framework incorporates a mixture-of-experts (MoE) adap-
tor and an auxiliary language prediction head to better capture
and differentiate multilingual representations, while hotwords
are injected through LLM-based prompting. Experiments on
the ASRU2019 code-switching challenge test set demonstrate
that LLM-HB achieves a 20.30% relative reduction in mixed
error rate (MER) over the baseline, reaching 5.85% MER under
a 15-distractor hotword setting. To the best of our knowledge,
this is the first work to integrate LLM-guided biasing into code-
switching ASR. We further release the extracted ASRU2019
hotword list to support future research.
Index Terms: code-switching, automatic speech recognition,
hotword biasing, MoE adaptor
1. Introduction
Code-switching (CS) refers to the alternation between multi-
ple languages within a single utterance or discourse. Com-
pared with monolingual settings, automatic speech recognition
(ASR) faces additional challenges in CS scenarios due to fre-
quent inter- and intra-sentence language transitions [1, 2, 3].
Although conventional ASR systems can be applied to code-
switching speech similarly to monolingual data, early studies
typically perform language identification prior to recognition or
jointly model language identification and transcription [4, 5].
More recent CS-ASR approaches mitigate cross-language con-
fusion by incorporating language-aware components into the
ASR architecture.
Prior work has explored various strategies to address cross-
language interference in CS-ASR. A representative method em-
ploys a Transformer-based bi-encoder architecture that decou-
ples English and Mandarin modeling using independently pre-
trained encoders [6, 7]. Beyond explicit structural separa-
tion, language-aware frameworks dynamically specialize rep-
resentations for different languages [8, 9]. Mixture-of-Experts
(MoE) adaptors enable dynamic expert specialization to en-
hance language-dependent representation learning and distin-
guish language boundaries [10, 11, 12]. Other approaches in-
corporate frame- or token-level language posteriors into acous-
tic representations prior to decoding [13, 14], while cross-
modality learning aligns speech and generated code-switching
text embeddings in a shared latent space to better model switch-
ing dynamics [15].
In contextual biasing, extensive efforts have focused on im-
proving recognition of named entities and rare words. Prior
to large language models (LLMs), contextual biasing methods
typically integrated hotword embeddings with acoustic repre-
sentations during decoding. Such integration can be performed
within the speech encoder [16, 17], after the encoder [18], or
before the ASR decoder [19]. With the introduction of LLMs,
hotwords or their embeddings are incorporated as prompts to-
gether with acoustic representations. One line of work gener-
ates an initial transcription and refines it by retrieving semanti-
cally similar hotwords [20]. Another adopts contrastive learn-
ing to align acoustic and hotword embedding spaces, followed
by nearest-neighbor retrieval (e.g., FAISS [21]) for LLM con-
ditioning [22, 23].
Despite these advances, most CS-ASR approaches focus on
mitigating cross-language interference within acoustic and lin-
guistic modeling without explicitly considering contextual bi-
asing. Conversely, existing contextual biasing techniques are
primarily developed for monolingual ASR systems. To date,
limited efforts have attempted to integrate contextual biasing
into CS-ASR, and these approaches do not fully exploit the ca-
pability of LLMs for adaptive multilingual biasing [24].
To address these limitations, we proposeLLM-HB:
a language-awareLargeLanguageModel-guidedHotword
Biasing method for CS-ASR. Our approach integrates MoE
adaptors after the speech encoder to enable language-
specialized acoustic representation learning. An auxiliary lan-
guage prediction head is attached to the LLM representations
and jointly optimized with the ASR objective to provide ex-
plicit language supervision, enhancing language discrimination.
Hotwords are incorporated through LLM prompting, enabling
adaptive contextual biasing under multilingual settings. By
jointly modeling language separation and LLM-guided bias-
ing, the proposed framework reduces cross-language interfer-
ence while improving recognition of rare and named entities in
code-switching speech.
We evaluate LLM-HB on the ASRU2019 code-switching
challenge test set [25] under a 15-distractor hotword setting 1,
achieving a 20.30% relative reduction in mixed error rate
(MER) over the baseline and reaching 5.85% MER. In addi-
tion, LLM-HB achieves 4.94% character error rate (CER) and
13.21% word error rate (WER), with CER and WER reduced
by 1.98% and 49.29% relative to the baseline, respectively.

2.1. Overall Architecture
Figure 1(a) presents the overall architecture of LLM-HB. Given
an input speech spectrogramX∈R M×T , whereMdenotes
the number of Mel-frequency bins andTthe number of time
frames. Acoustic featuresSare extracted using a pre-trained
Whisper encoder [26] and fine-tuned during training. The en-
coded features are passed through an MoE adaptor to produce
language-specialized speech embeddingsE S∈R T×D , where
Dis aligned with the LLM representation dimension.
The hotword listH=h 0,h 1,...,h N , containingNhot-
words and a special<no-bias>token, is tokenized using a
frozen LLM tokenizer. LetN ′ denote the total number of to-
kens. The tokenized sequence is encoded by the LLM text en-
coder to obtain hotword embeddingsE H ∈R N ′×D. During
training, the ground-truth transcription is processed similarly to
produce target text embeddingsE T∈R L×D, whereLdenotes
the number of transcription tokens.
The speech embeddingsE S and hotword embeddingsE H
are fed into a LoRA-adapted [27] LLM. Special marker tokens
H⃝, T⃝, and E⃝denote the beginning of hotword embeddings,
transcription embeddings, and the end-of-sequence token, re-
spectively. During training, the LLM input sequence is con-
structed as[E S, H⃝,E H, T⃝,E T, E⃝].
The LLM outputs hidden statese t, which are projected to
the vocabulary space for token prediction. An auxiliary lan-
guage prediction head is attached toe t to provide explicit lan-
guage supervision. The modules enclosed by the red dashed
boxes in Figure 1(a), including the auxiliary language head and
the ground-truth text encoding branch, are used only during
training and removed during inference.

3. Experiment
3.1. Dataset and Experimental Setup
Experiments were conducted on three benchmark datasets:
the 1000-hour Mandarin corpus AISHELL-2 [28], the 960-
hour English corpus LibriSpeech [29], and the 200-hour code-
switching dataset ASRU2019-CS. Depending on the training
configuration, models were trained either on the full multi-
dataset setup (AISHELL-2, LibriSpeech, and ASRU2019-CS)
or solely on ASRU2019-CS, while evaluation was consistently
performed on ASRU2019-CS. To construct the evaluation hot-
word lists, all English words in the test set were selected, and
Chinese named entities were extracted using HanLP 2 [30] .
These words were used to form hotword lists containing 15 dis-
tractors per utterance for evaluation.
We report the mixed error rate (MER), which combines
word error rate (WER) for English and character error rate
(CER) for Mandarin, together with bias-oriented metrics, in-
cluding B-MER, B-WER, and B-CER [31].
For the proposed LLM-HB framework, hyperparameters
were set as follows. We employed Whisper-medium 3 as the
speech encoder for its strong multilingual capability in code-
switching scenarios. As the LLM backbone, we adopted
Qwen3-4B4 , which provides a favorable trade-off between effi-
ciency and representational capacity. The Whisper encoder pro-
duces 1024-dimensional acoustic embeddings, which are pro-
jected to 2560 dimensions via a dense MoE adaptor with two
experts and top-2 gating to match the LLM input space.
For hotword biasing, the predefined hotword list is tok-
enized and encoded by the frozen LLM text encoder to obtain
hotword embeddings. During training, the ground-truth tran-
scription is processed in the same way to ensure consistency
in the shared embedding space. The auxiliary language head
takes the 2560-dimensional LLM outputs and applies an FFN to
project them into three dimensions corresponding to Mandarin,
English, and other tokens.
During training, we optimized the model using AdamW
with a triangular learning rate schedule. One cycle spanned
47,040 steps, and the model was trained for two cycles (94,080
steps). The peak learning rate was1.5×10 −4. In each mini-
batch, each utterance had a 50% probability of being selected
for biasing, from which 1–4 tokens were randomly sampled
to form the hotword list. Training was conducted on approx-
imately 2,200 hours of speech using 8 MLU590 GPUs. The
loss weights in Equation 6 were set toλ 1 = 0.6andλ 2 = 0.3.

When trained only on the 200-hour code-switching dataset,
the proposed modules exhibit distinct behaviors. The MoE
adaptor alone leads to a slight performance degradation (MER
increases from 9.59% to 9.65%), suggesting that the additional
parameters introduced by the adaptor may require larger-scale
data for effective optimization. In contrast, both the auxil-

improvements, achieving MERs of 9.30% and 7.48%, respec-

plete LLM-HB further improves MER to 7.36%, demonstrat-
ing that the proposed framework remains effective even under
limited training data. This suggests that, although the MoE
adaptor alone may be difficult to optimize effectively under lim-
ited data, its interaction with language supervision and biasing
signals still provides complementary representational benefits
when trained jointly. Notably, the hotword prompt delivers the
most significant individual gain, reducing MER to 7.48% and
substantially lowering B-MER from 26.91% to 16.00%, con-
firming its strong biasing capability in low-resource conditions.
Under larger-scale multi-dataset training (2,200 hours), all
modules become consistently beneficial. The complete LLM-
HB model, integrating all three components, achieves the best
MER of 5.85%, corresponding to a 20.30% relative reduction
over the baseline (7.34%). Notably, the MoE adaptor now con-
tributes positively, indicating that sufficient data enables expert
specialization to emerge more reliably.
The improvements are particularly pronounced on hotword-
related metrics, with B-MER decreasing from 22.11% to
8.99%, B-CER from 19.26% to 3.01%, and B-WER from
22.90% to 10.93%. Importantly, the auxiliary language head
also plays a structural role in shaping MoE behavior. Although
the routing mechanism is not explicitly supervised, the language
prediction loss is applied to the shared LLM representations ob-
tained after expert aggregation. This supervision encourages
the fused embeddings to retain language-discriminative cues,
which in turn drives the gating network to allocate tokens to ex-
perts in a more language-consistent manner through backprop-
agation. Furthermore, since the injected hotwords span both
Mandarin and English, the biasing objective interacts with the
language-aware supervision, reinforcing expert specialization
toward language-aligned patterns. As a result, expert differenti-
ation emerges implicitly, enabling more effective utilization of
bilingual hotword information.
These results indicate that the three modules operate com-
plementarily: the MoE adaptor provides capacity for specializa-
tion, the auxiliary language head guides language-aware repre-
sentation learning and indirectly influences expert routing, and
the hotword prompt introduces targeted biasing signals.
Comparing the two training setups, all methods benefit sub-
stantially from larger-scale training. The relative improvements
remain consistent across variants, with the complete LLM-HB
achieving a MER of 5.85% under the full multi-dataset setting.
Under the code-switching-only setting, the complete LLM-HB
achieves 7.36% MER, indicating that the framework remains ef-
fective even with 200 hours of training data, while larger-scale
training further unlocks the benefits of expert specialization.

3.3. Ablation Studies and Analysis
To investigate the impact of MoE adaptor design, we conduct
ablation experiments under the full 2,200-hour multi-dataset
training setup. Table 2 compares four configurations with dif-
ferent numbers of experts and top-kselection strategies.
The results indicate that the expert configuration influences
performance. To ensure a fair comparison, we adjust the hidden
dimensions of each variant to maintain comparable parameter
counts. The single-expert configuration (1 choose 1) achieves a
MER of 7.23%, which is inferior to the proposed multi-expert
setting. Among the MoE variants, the proposed 2 choose 2 con-
figuration achieves the best overall performance (7.19% MER),
slightly outperforming both 4 choose 2 (7.47% MER) and 2
choose 1 (7.21% MER), while the fully activated 4 choose 4
setting yields 7.33% MER. These findings suggest that dense
top-2 activation is marginally more beneficial than sparse top-1
selection, while increasing the number of experts from 2 to 4
does not yield additional gains.
We hypothesize that this behavior is related to the bilingual
nature of the code-switching task. Since only Mandarin and
English are involved, two experts may be sufficient to capture
their distinct acoustic-phonetic characteristics. From a capac-
ity perspective, the MoE adaptor provides scalable modeling
flexibility that enables expert specialization without proportion-
ally increasing computation; however, in this bilingual setting,
additional experts may introduce unnecessary complexity and
optimization difficulty without clear representational benefits.

4. Conclusion
In this work, we presented LLM-HB, a framework designed to
enhance code-switching ASR through LLM-guided hotword bi-
asing. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first attempt to
incorporate LLM-guided biasing into code-switching ASR. The
core components include a MoE adaptor that dynamically in-
tegrates speech and text representations, an auxiliary language
head that provides language identity supervision, and a hotword
prompt that introduces targeted biasing signals. Experimental
results on ASRU2019-CS demonstrate that the complete model
achieves substantial improvements over the backbone, reducing
MER from 7.34% to 5.85%. Ablation studies further show that
dense top-2 expert activation is more effective than sparse alter-
natives in bilingual settings, and that the bias loss weight regu-
lates the trade-off between character-level and word-level accu-
racy. These findings confirm the effectiveness of LLM-guided
biasing for code-switching ASR and provide insights for future
multilingual speech research. In future work, we plan to extend
the approach to address near-homophones and to scale to larger
hotword vocabularies.
