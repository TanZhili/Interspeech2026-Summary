Disentangling Depression from Cognitive Decline in Elderly Speech
Using Concurrent Clinical Assessments

Abstract
Detecting depression from speech in elderly patients with mild
cognitive impairment (MCI) is complicated by overlapping
acoustic effects of cognitive decline. Without disentangling
these, classifiers risk learning cognitive rather than depression-
specific patterns. We present a Korean elderly speech corpus
of 89 MCI speakers collected over three years with concurrent
depression (SGDS) and cognitive (MMSE) assessments. Both
linear mixed-effects models and partial correlations identify the
same pattern: of seven feature groups, only formant features are
associated with depression after controlling for cognitive func-
tion, while widely used F0 features carry no signal. The F1+F2
subset achieves UAR 0.760 with just 12 features, outperform-
ing both the full eGeMAPS baseline and self-supervised rep-
resentations. These findings show that concurrent clinical as-
sessments can identify interpretable, depression-specific acous-
tic markers in populations where cognitive and affective symp-
toms co-occur.
Index Terms: depression detection, elderly speech, mild cog-
nitive impairment, concurrent clinical assessments
1. Introduction
Depression is highly prevalent among elderly individuals with
mild cognitive impairment (MCI), affecting approximately 32%
[1] compared to 7% in the general elderly population [2]. Accu-
rate detection is clinically critical because untreated depression
accelerates cognitive decline. Speech-based screening offers a
promising non-invasive approach [3, 4], achieving strong per-
formance on healthy adults using corpora such as DAIC-WOZ
[5] with handcrafted features [6] and self-supervised represen-
tations [7]. Recent studies have further explored longitudinal
monitoring [8, 9], foundation models [10, 11], domain adapta-
tion [12], multimodal fusion [13], and ordinal regression [14].
However, a fundamental challenge arises in MCI popula-
tions: both depression and cognitive decline produce overlap-
ping acoustic changes such as reduced pitch range, decreased
vocal energy, and altered voice quality [15, 16], so classifiers
may learn cognitive patterns rather than depression-specific
ones. A recent systematic review confirms that insufficient
control of cognitive confounders remains a key limitation in
speech-based depression detection for older adults [17].
In general adult populations, F0 features such as pitch range
and variability are among the most widely studied acoustic
markers of depression [3], while formant and vowel space fea-
tures have also been identified as robust indicators: reduced

unclear which of these changes observed in MCI populations
reflect depression and which reflect cognitive decline.
Resolving this ambiguity requires concurrent depression
and cognitive scores at each assessment, enabling identification
of which features are specifically associated with depression af-
ter accounting for cognitive function. Young et al. [22] pro-
posed feature weighting to mitigate depression–dementia con-
founding in elderly speech, but without concurrent cognitive
scores to identify which specific features are depression-specific
vs. cognition-related. No prior study has used dual clinical as-
sessments to systematically separate depression-specific from
cognition-related acoustic features in MCI elderly speech.
In this paper, we make the following contributions:
1. We introduce the first Korean elderly MCI speech corpus: 89
individuals recorded annually over three years (209 obser-
vations), with concurrent depression (SGDS) and cognitive
(MMSE) scores at every recording. Within-subject depres-
sion status changes across years enable longitudinal analy-
sis of depression-specific acoustic effects in cognitively im-
paired populations.
2. Two independent methods—longitudinal LMMs and cross-
sectional partial correlations—both identify formant as
the only depression-specific feature group among seven
eGeMAPS groups after controlling for cognitive function,
while widely used F0 features carry no signal. This conver-
gence across methods provides evidence robust to method-
ological choice.
3. The statistically identified formant group (18 features)
achieves UAR 0.747, and the F1+F2 subset further improves
to 0.760 with just 12 interpretable features, outperform-
ing both the full 88-feature eGeMAPS baseline and self-
supervised representations.

Importantly, 18 of 89 subjects changed depression status
across years, with 11 transitions from non-depressed to de-

subject variation enables the LMM analysis in Section 3, where
fixed effects of SGDS and MMSE are estimated from within-
subject change over time. Cognitive function was measured by
the Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) [25], which re-

Each recording session comprises four speech tasks—
passage reading, sustained vowel phonation, sentence repeti-
tion, and a structured depressive symptom interview—along
with additional clinical measures including anxiety, quality of
life, and perceived stress. This study analyzes the passage read-
ing task using depression and cognitive scores; the remaining
tasks and clinical measures are available for future research.
2.2. Speech task
Participants read aloud the “Autumn” passage [26], a standard-
ized Korean reading passage used in clinical speech assessment,
in a quiet room at a university hospital. The passage contains
approximately 210 syllables and takes 30–60 seconds to read
aloud. Recordings were captured at 48 kHz, 16-bit with an 8-
channel array and downmixed to mono for analysis. This read-
ing task was chosen for two reasons. First, it controls linguistic
content across participants, ensuring that acoustic differences
reflect vocal rather than linguistic variation, which is critical
when using phonetically sensitive features such as eGeMAPS.
Second, reading reduces cognitive load compared to sponta-
neous speech, avoiding the risk that high cognitive demands
during free speech confound acoustic measurements with task
difficulty effects in MCI populations [16].
2.3. Feature extraction
Examiner instructions at the beginning of each recording were
identified using WhisperX [27] and removed prior to feature ex-
traction. We extract the extended Geneva Minimalistic Acous-
tic Parameter Set (eGeMAPS) [28], yielding 88 interpretable
acoustic features spanning seven groups: fundamental fre-
quency (F0, 10 features), energy/loudness (12), spectral balance
(17), MFCC (16), voice quality (10), formant (18), and tempo-
ral (5). Additionally, we compute character error rate (CER)

3. Methods
In populations with concurrent depression and cognitive de-
cline, standard classification risks conflating the two condi-
tions in the learned representation. Our approach therefore pro-
ceeds in two stages. First, we use statistical analysis to iden-
tify which acoustic feature groups are specifically associated
with depression after controlling for cognitive function (Sec-
tions 3.1–3.2). Second, we evaluate whether these statistically
identified groups also yield better classification performance
(Section 3.3).
3.1. Group-level feature representation
Testing 88 individual features introduces a substantial multiple
comparison burden and yields results that are difficult to inter-
pret clinically. We therefore analyze at the group level, where
each group corresponds to an acoustically coherent family of
features. Based on the parameter categories in [28], we orga-
nize the 88 features into seven functionally motivated groups
(Table 2). These groups are defined before any classification
experiment, ensuring that group definitions are independent of
classification outcomes. This reduces the number of statisti-
cal tests from 88 to 7 while producing interpretable conclusions
such as “formant features are associated with depression” rather
than lists of individual feature names.
For each group, we standardize its constituent features to
zero mean and unit variance, then extract the first principal com-
ponent (PC1) via PCA. PC1 captures the dominant pattern of
covariation within each group and serves as the dependent vari-
able in the statistical analyses described in Section 3.2.
3.2. Disentangling depression from cognitive effects
To ensure that the identified associations are not artifacts of a
single statistical framework, we apply two independent methods
to each group’s PC1: one capturing within-subject change over
time, and one capturing between-subject differences at each
time point.
Linear mixed-effects model (LMM). We fit the following

style), so the fixed effects of SGDS and MMSE capture only
within-subject associations between clinical scores and acous-
tic change. We use a random intercept rather than random slope
model because many subjects have only two observations, in-
sufficient for slope estimation. All features are z-scored across
all observations before model fitting.
By including both SGDS and MMSE as fixed effects, the
βSGDS coefficient estimates each group’s association with de-
pression severity after accounting for cognitive function. This
isolates depression-related acoustic variation from changes that
may reflect cognitive decline, a critical distinction for MCI pop-
ulations where the two conditions frequently co-occur.
Partial correlation. As the between-subject counter-
part, we compute partial Spearman correlations between each
group’s PC1 and SGDS, controlling for MMSE, age, and gen-
der:

Convergence between these two methodologically indepen-
dent approaches (one longitudinal, one cross-sectional) pro-
vides stronger evidence that the identified association is robust
and not an artifact of either statistical framework.
3.3. Classification
For binary depression classification (SGDS≥ 8), we use a sup-
port vector machine (SVM) with RBF kernel and C = 0.1, a
standard classifier for small-sample paralinguistic tasks. Each
feature group is evaluated using all its constituent features (not
PC1), so that the statistical analysis identifies which group to
use while the classifier exploits the full feature space within that
group. Class weights are set inversely proportional to class fre-
quencies to address the label imbalance (41 depressed vs. 168
non-depressed). Features are standardized within each cross-
validation fold: the scaler is fit on training data only and applied
to both training and test sets to prevent information leakage.
Evaluation uses leave-one-subject-out (LOSO) cross-
validation on all 209 observations (89 subjects), where all ob-
servations from the same subject are held out together. We re-
port unweighted average recall (UAR), sensitivity, specificity,
accuracy, and F1 score.
We compare two handcrafted baselines: (1) full eGeMAPS
(88 features), representing the standard feature set, and (2) CER
+ WER from Whisper large-v3 (2 features), representing speech
intelligibility measures. As self-supervised baselines, we eval-
uate four models—WavLM-Base [31], wav2vec2-Base [32],
HuBERT-Base [33], and WavLM-Large—using mean-pooled
embeddings with both SVM and a linear classification head.
Linear head results are averaged over 5 random seeds for sta-
bility.

4. Results
4.1. Disentangling analysis
Table 3 presents the group-level results from both the LMM and
partial correlation. Of the seven feature groups, formant is the
only one that reaches significance in both methods: βSGDS =
−0.095,p = 0.011 in the LMM, andr =−0.196,p = 0.004
in the partial correlation. The negative coefficients indicate that
greater depression severity is associated with a systematic shift
in the formant pattern. Energy and spectral also reach LMM
significance withp = 0.023 andp = 0.028, but neither is con-
firmed by the partial correlation, suggesting these groups may
capture within-subject changes that do not generalize across in-
dividuals. Because both methods control for MMSE, these as-
sociations reflect depression severity independent of cognitive
function. The remaining four groups, F0, MFCC, voice quality,
and temporal, show no significant association with depression
in either method.
The formant partial correlation survives Bonferroni correc-
tion for the seven group tests ( αadj = 0.05/7 = 0 .007), and
the convergence of two independent statistical frameworks on
the same group strengthens the evidence that this association is
robust.
F0 is one of the most widely used features in speech-based
depression detection, yet it shows no association with depres-
sion in this MCI population, with p = 0.894 in the LMM and
p = 0.478 in the partial correlation.
4.2. Classification
Table 4 presents the classification results. The formant group
achieves the highest UAR of 0.747 with 18 features, outper-
forming both the 88-feature eGeMAPS baseline at 0.655 and
the best SSL model, HuBERT with a linear head, at 0.729.
F0 yields a UAR of 0.512, essentially at chance, confirm-
ing that these features carry no useful signal for depression in
this population. The remaining groups fall between these ex-
tremes (UAR 0.585–0.641). The three groups that were LMM-
significant in Table 3 are also the three best classifiers, while
all four non-significant groups score below 0.600. This corre-
spondence suggests that the statistical analysis in Section 4.1
successfully identifies the groups carrying genuine depression
signal.
By contrast, selecting the top 18 individual features by par-
tial correlation or LMM, matching the dimensionality of the
formant group, yields UAR of 0.655 and 0.637 respectively, no
better than the full baseline. Individual features drawn from het-
erogeneous groups lack the coherent signal that a functionally
related group provides, confirming the advantage of group-level

4.3. Formant subgroup analysis
We next examine which formants drive the formant group’s per-
formance. Each eGeMAPS formant contributes six features:
mean and coefficient of variation for frequency, bandwidth, and
amplitude. Table 5 reports all single- and pairwise combina-
tions.
F2 alone matches the full formant group at UAR 0.747
with only 6 features, while F1 at 0.618 and F3 at 0.641 per-
form considerably worse. Adding F1 to F2 yields the highest
UAR of 0.760, whereas adding F3 to any F2-containing sub-
set degrades performance. This indicates that F2 carries the
primary depression signal and F1 provides complementary in-
formation, while F3 introduces noise. F1 and F2 are the pri-
mary acoustic correlates of tongue position and vowel quality,
suggesting that depression in this population is associated with
articulatory changes. F3, which primarily reflects lip round-
ing, does not contribute to this signal. Permutation tests con-
firm that the F1+F2 advantage is significant over both the full
eGeMAPS baseline (p = 0.037) and individual feature selec-

5. Discussion
5.1. Error analysis and interpretation
Three sources of variation beyond depression affect acoustic
features in this population: gender, aging, and cognition.
Error analysis of the F1+F2 model reveals a gender asym-
metry: 43 of 48 false positives are female, yielding male speci-
ficity of 0.911 versus female specificity of 0.616. Gender is a
major source of formant variance, and in a gender-pooled model
this variation can be confounded with depression-related for-
mant changes, disproportionately affecting females who consti-
tute 67% of our sample. Vlasenko et al. [19] similarly found that
gender-dependent formant features outperform gender-pooled
ones, and gender-specific normalization could reduce this bias.
The absence of F0 signal may reflect age-related F0 vari-
ation overwhelming clinical signal in elderly populations [16],
implying that depression models trained on younger adults may
fail for elderly MCI populations. A sensitivity analysis con-
firms that controlling for age and gender alone is insufficient:
temporal features lose significance only when MMSE is added
(p: 0.004→ 0.066), while formant features remain significant
across all control levels (p = 0.004). Concurrent cognitive as-
sessment is therefore essential for feature selection in MCI pop-
ulations.
5.2. Limitations
Several limitations should be noted. The sample size of 89 sub-
jects and 209 observations is modest. The formant partial cor-
relation survives Bonferroni correction ( p = 0 .004), but the
LMM p-value does not, and replication on larger cohorts is
needed. The gender imbalance of 67% female and the absence
of gender-specific modeling limit specificity for female partic-
ipants. We evaluate only a reading task; spontaneous speech,
which better reflects naturalistic communication patterns, could
yield complementary markers. Finally, cross-linguistic valida-
tion is needed, as our corpus is Korean only.
6. Conclusion
In this paper, we showed that concurrent clinical assessments
enable disentangling depression from cognitive decline in el-
derly MCI speech, revealing formant as the only depression-
specific feature group. We achieve UAR 0.760 with just 12
F1+F2 features, outperforming both the full eGeMAPS base-
line and self-supervised representations. Future work includes
gender-specific modeling, spontaneous speech analysis, and
longitudinal tracking of acoustic change leveraging the within-
subject variation in our corpus.
