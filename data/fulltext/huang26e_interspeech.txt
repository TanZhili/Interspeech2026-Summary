Framework with Trajectory and Ambient Speech

Abstract
Stress detection is widely studied for mental health monitoring,
with most approaches relying on physiological signals such as
HRV . These methods require wearable devices, limiting scal-
ability in practical deployment. Audio signals, particularly
speech, provide a non-intrusive alternative, yet models relying
solely on sound lack robustness across contexts. Stress per-
ception is inherently context-dependent and influenced by spa-
tial factors beyond speech alone, especially for hospital work-
ers in high-pressure clinical environments. This study pro-
poses a context-aware framework extracting distinct features
from speech and mobility trajectories. By employing a late fu-
sion mechanism to combine vocal and movement embeddings,
the proposed approach improves robustness, outperforming uni-
modal audio baselines on a hospital worker dataset. Experimen-
tal results show the framework improves the F1 score by 9.32%
and the Matthews correlation coefficient (MCC) by 0.038 over
the audio-only baseline.

1. Introduction
Occupational stress is highly prevalent among healthcare work-
ers and adversely affects mental well-being, job performance,
and patient safety [1, 2]. Continuous stress monitoring is there-
fore essential for early intervention and workplace health man-
agement [3]. Existing emotion and stress recognition stud-
ies have predominantly relied on physiological signals such as
heart rate variability (HRV) [4, 5, 6], which provide informative
biomarkers of autonomic nervous system activity. However,
HRV-based monitoring typically requires dedicated wearable
devices and may impose practical and intrusive constraints in
real-world clinical environments [7]. In contrast, speech signals
offer a low-cost and accessible alternative that can be passively
collected in daily settings. Prior research has demonstrated that
speech carries rich paralinguistic cues related to human psycho-
logical states, suggesting its strong potential for scalable stress
detection in healthcare contexts [8, 9]. Moreover, speech can be
continuously captured during routine clinical communication
without requiring additional user intervention, making it suit-
able for unobtrusive and long-term monitoring in busy health-
care environments [10, 11].
However, daily stress is inherently context-dependent and
cannot be fully explained by acoustic cues alone [12]. The psy-
chophysiological impact of environmental stimuli is shaped not
only by their spectral characteristics but also by the situational
and behavioral context in which they occur [13]. In particu-
lar, human mobility patterns reflect individuals’ daily activi-
ties, workload distribution, and exposure to different environ-
ments, all of which are closely associated with stress responses.
In healthcare settings, workers frequently transition across het-
erogeneous clinical areas that differ in task intensity, patient
acuity, and environmental demands. For example, stress lev-
els among nurses have been shown to differ significantly across
specialties, with higher stress reported in emergency and high-
intensity units compared to other wards [14]. Location-specific
variations suggest that mobility trajectories may provide com-
plementary contextual information beyond what can be cap-
tured from speech signals alone, motivating the incorporation
of trajectory-based context for stress recognition.
In addition to its behavioral relevance, mobility information
is practical to acquire in real-world settings. Advances in perva-
sive sensing technologies enable passive and continuous track-
ing of human movement through commonly available infras-
tructure such as Bluetooth beacons, WiFi signal measurements,
and global positioning system (GPS) data [15]. These ubiqui-
tous sensing capabilities have been widely adopted in mobile
and healthcare research, enabling fine-grained observation of
individuals’ daily behavioral patterns [16]. Particularly in hos-
pital environments, indoor positioning based on WiFi and Blue-
tooth infrastructure has become increasingly feasible due to the
widespread deployment of wireless networks [17]. This sens-
ing approach further enables longitudinal monitoring of staff
movement without disrupting routine clinical workflows. These
sensing modalities allow mobility trajectories to be collected
longitudinally with minimal user burden and without requiring
intrusive wearable devices. Compared with physiological mon-
itoring, trajectory-based context can therefore be obtained in a
more scalable and cost-effective manner. Such characteristics
make features extracted from mobility data a promising com-
plementary modality for robust real-world stress recognition.
To address these challenges, this work proposes a context-
aware stress recognition framework that integrates speech fea-
tures with human mobility trajectory information through a late-
fusion strategy. By incorporating spatial context inferred from
mobility patterns, the proposed approach models how stress re-
sponses may vary across clinical locations and environmental
conditions, enabling the system to capture behavioral cues that
are not fully observable from acoustic signals alone. The frame-
work is evaluated on the TILES dataset [18], which contains
real-world multimodal data collected from hospital workers.
Experimental results demonstrate that integrating mobility con-
text consistently improves performance compared with models
relying solely on acoustic features, highlighting the comple-
mentary value of mobility information for robust stress recog-
nition in healthcare settings. Further analysis across different
mobility frequency conditions indicates that the proposed fu-
sion framework provides more noticeable benefits when mobil-
ity dynamics are more distinctive.

2. Methodology
This work proposes Trajectory Speech Embedding (TSE), a
multimodal stress detection framework that integrates acoustic
information and mobility trajectory dynamics at the day level.
Figure 1 illustrates the proposed TSE framework. The model
consists of two modality-specific encoders: an acoustic encoder
that transforms the daily acoustic feature vector into a com-
pact embedding capturing distributional sound characteristics,
and a trajectory encoder that models daily spatial transitions to
generate a representation of mobility dynamics and contextual
movement patterns. The embeddings produced by the two en-
coders are concatenated to form a joint multimodal represen-
tation, which is subsequently fed into a multilayer perceptron
(MLP) classifier for binary stress prediction. The entire frame-
work is trained end-to-end, enabling complementary contextual
information from mobility patterns to enhance acoustic-based
stress inference.
2.1. Dataset and Feature Preprocessing
The TILES-2018 dataset contains physiological measurements
and survey responses collected over ten weeks from 212 hospi-
tal workers in real-world settings. Participants wore wearable
devices to monitor daily activities and physiological signals,
while self-reported questionnaires were used to assess physi-
cal and mental health. In this study, foreground speech is used
as the input to the audio model, and the corresponding loca-
tion labels are used for the trajectory model. Self-reported
stress ratings (5-point scale) are binarized using the global mean
(µ= 1.8) as the threshold, resulting in 31.9% of samples la-
beled as stress. These binary labels are used for model training.
2.1.1. Audio
For acoustic representation, we utilize the low-level descriptors
(LLDs) in the dataset, which were originally extracted using
openSMILE toolkit [19]. Feature set consists of two groups: 8
prosodic descriptors and 16 spectral descriptors. Prosodic fea-
tures include fundamental frequency (F0), RMS energy, inten-
sity, loudness, voice probability, and zero-crossing rate (ZCR),
which characterize voice production and energy dynamics. The
spectral features comprise average energy in predefined fre-
quency bands, spectral roll-off statistics, spectral centroid, spec-
tral entropy, spectral flux, and spectral shape descriptors, cap-
turing distributional properties of the acoustic signal.
2.1.2. Trajectory
Mobility trajectory data were collected in a hospital environ-
ment using an indoor sensing infrastructure consisting of 243
fixed devices deployed across 21 nursing units and five room
types, including nurse station, patient room, lounge, medication
room, and laboratory. The trajectory data cover the same 166
participants as the audio modality. In total, the dataset contains
2,878 days with available audio recordings and an additional
2,426 days that contain only trajectory information. These
trajectory-only samples are utilized for trajectory representation
learning and pre-training. To obtain accurate indoor locations,
we adopt the received signal strength indicator (RSSI) values
from the sensing devices to determine the most likely room-
level position at each timestamp. The corresponding room co-
ordinates are also retained to provide spatial information. The
resulting location sequence is then converted into a minute-level
trajectory for subsequent modeling.
2.2. Audio Representation
Since stress annotations are defined at the day level, segment-
level acoustic features are aggregated into daily representations.
For each acoustic descriptor,15statistical functionals are com-
puted across all segments within the same day, including mea-
sures of central tendency (maximum, minimum, mean, me-
dian), dispersion (standard deviation, percentile-based ranges),
distributional shape (skewness and kurtosis), and extreme tem-
poral positions (argmin and argmax). Percentile-based statis-
tics further include the 1st, 25th, 75th, and 99th percentiles, to-
gether with the corresponding inter-percentile ranges (99th–1st
and 75th–25th). This transformation converts variable length
daily segment sequences into fixed dimensional representations
while preserving distributional characteristics and capturing ex-
treme behavioral patterns. With24acoustic descriptors and15
functionals per descriptor, each day is ultimately represented by
a 360 dimensional acoustic feature vector. Such daily-level sta-
tistical abstraction improves robustness against short-term fluc-
tuations and enhances privacy preservation by eliminating fine-
grained temporal information.

2.3. Mobility Trajectory Modeling

based formulation of the Hierarchical Wi-Fi Trajectory Embed-
ding (HWTE) framework [20]. For each day, location records
are sampled at one-minute intervals after the first detected sig-
nal, yielding a minute-level sequence of length 1440. Time pe-
riods before the first detected location and after the last detected
location are labeled as [OUT], indicating that the participant is
outside the monitored area. Additionally, continuous gaps of
20 minutes without location observations are also marked as
[OUT]. After preprocessing, each day is represented as a com-
plete minute-level sequenceL={l 0,l 1,...,l 1439}, wherel t
denotes either a valid location token or the special token [OUT]
at minutet.

The acoustic embeddingz a and trajectory embeddingz t are

acoustic and trajectory embeddings are concatenated to form a
joint representationz f . The fused representation is then fed into
a multilayer perceptron classifier to produce the final stress pre-
diction. To enhance modality-specific representation learning,

tion. The trajectory encoder also employs a two-layer Trans-

the remaining parameters use10 −4. The batch size is 32, and
training runs for up to 500 epochs with early stopping. The tra-
jectory loss weight is set toλ= 0.5. Experiments follow a
subject-independent split (80% train, 10% validation, 10% test)

3.2. Trajectory Encoder Pre-training
To enable the trajectory encoder to capture intrinsic spatial re-
lationships between locations, we perform self-supervised pre-
training using trajectory-only data that are not involved in the
main framework. These trajectories are processed using the
same session construction pipeline described earlier and then
fed into the trajectory Transformer encoder. A next-location
prediction objective is adopted, where at each session step the
contextualized hidden stateg i is used to predict the subsequent
location through a linear classification head followed by soft-
max normalization. The model is optimized using the cross-
entropy loss between the predicted distribution and the ground-
truth next location, encouraging the encoder to learn transition
patterns and spatial dependencies among clinical areas. The
pre-trained model achieves a Top-1 accuracy of38.85%and a
Top-3 accuracy of60.40%on the next-location prediction task.
During downstream stress classification, the trajectory encoder
is initialized with the pre-trained weights and further fine-tuned
jointly within the proposed framework.
3.3. Stress Detection Results
Table 1 summarizes the performance comparison of different
models on stress recognition task. The Transformer baseline
achieves limited performance, with an MCC of 0.074, indicat-
ing that acoustic features alone provide insufficient robustness
for stress prediction. The MSLoss-based approach reaches an
MCC of 0.051, suggesting that although individual-aware mod-
eling helps mitigate inter-subject variability, acoustic-only rep-
resentations remain limited in capturing stress-related patterns.
Incorporating trajectory information leads to notable improve-
ments. The TSE model increases the MCC to 0.142, demon-
strating the added value of mobility context for stress recogni-
tion. Notably,Coordinate TSEfurther improves performance,
achieving the best results with an MCC of 0.147 and a BACC
of 58.91%, benefiting from the use of ground-truth location co-
ordinates. Compared with the Bi-LSTM acoustic baseline, the
proposed framework improves the MCC by 0.038, validating
the effectiveness of integrating mobility-aware representations.

4. Conclusion and Future Work
In this paper, we proposed a multimodal framework for stress
recognition among healthcare workers by jointly modeling
acoustic signals and mobility trajectories. The proposed ap-
proach incorporates trajectory-aware representation learning
and next location prediction pre-training to enhance contex-
tual understanding. Experimental results on the TILES dataset
demonstrate that the proposed framework consistently improves
stress recognition performance compared with unimodal and
conventional baselines, highlighting the effectiveness of inte-
grating behavioral mobility context with acoustic features. For
future work, we plan to explore more fine-grained contextual
modeling and longer temporal dependencies to further improve
stress prediction. In addition, extending the framework to addi-
tional sensing modalities and real-world deployment scenarios
is a promising direction.
