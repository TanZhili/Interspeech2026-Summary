Abstract
Automated speaking assessment is crucial for computer-
assisted language learning applications. Research has mainly
focused on intermediate proficiency levels in languages with
many L2 learners, predominantly English, where learner cor-
pora are available. For low-resource languages lacking large
annotated corpora, models based on self-supervised learning
can help overcome this problem. In this work, we investi-
gate whether multilingual self-supervised speech models can
effectively discriminate C1-level proficiency in Basque, a low-
resource language, and show that mHuBERT-147 achieves a
statistically significant improvement over wav2vec 2.0 xlsr. To
illustrate reproducibility, we replicate our experiments in the
ICNALE corpus of L2 English, providing a complementary per-
spective in different linguistic and proficiency contexts.
Index Terms: automated speaking assessment, automatic oral
proficiency assessment, automatic speech evaluation, spoken
language assessment, computer-assisted language learning
1. Introduction
Automated speaking assessment (ASA) is a valuable tool in
computer-assisted language learning (CALL), which could also
be helpful for educators in assessing oral proficiency of second
language (L2) learners. A recent meta-analysis [1] found a sig-
nificant correlation between automatic and human speech eval-
uation, highlighting the importance of ASA systems in CALL.
The vast majority of ASA research has focused on the evalu-
ation of various linguistic levels of the most spoken languages
such as English or Chinese [2], while research in low-resource
languages remains limited.
Many ASA graders use automatic speech recognition
(ASR) to obtain transcriptions of L2 learner speech (ASR-based
approach) to evaluate vocabulary learning [3] or grammar [4].
Other ASR-based approaches typically extract features from
transcriptions to predict proficiency scores or to classify pro-
ficiency level [5, 6, 7, 8, 9], while recent approaches employ
Large Language Models [10, 11, 12, 13, 14]. Although auto-
matic graders using manually transcribed L2 speech have been
shown to outperform graders using ASR transcriptions , their
combination might show improvements [5], highlighting the
importance of manually transcribed corpora. Nevertheless, the
high cost of manual transcriptions constitutes a key barrier to
scaling ASA systems for under-represented languages. This
has motivated a shift towards purely speech-based approaches
[5, 6, 7, 15, 16].

Self-supervised learning (SSL) has emerged as a powerful
approach to obtain speech representations from unlabeled audio
without the need for extensive manually annotated data. Models
such as wav2vec 2.0 [17] or HuBERT [18] offer a promising
venue for purely speech-based ASA systems, as they are able to
learn contextualized speech features for downstream tasks with
a limited amount of labeled audio.
Previous speech-based approaches to L2 assessment have
primarily focused on segmental and suprasegmental aspects of
pronunciation rather than holistic proficiency classification. In
these studies, comparisons between wav2vec 2.0 and HuBERT
have shown mixed results depending on the linguistic feature
being modeled. For instance, HuBERT has been reported to
outperform wav2vec 2.0 in L2 English [19]. Conversely, in [20]
it was found that wav2vec 2.0 performs better in learning the
speech representation of Mandarin tone, while HuBERT per-
forms better in English stress and pitch accent. Recent studies,
however, have targeted holistic proficiency classification, com-
paring ASR-based graders using BERT [21] and purely speech-
based graders, and found that purely speech-based graders out-
perform their ASR-based counterparts in proficiency level clas-
sification metrics [5, 6, 7, 15].
Although purely speech-based approaches overcome the
obstacle of curating human-annotated datasets, the availability
of standardized large learner corpora is limited. Only a handful
of those limited resources are suitable for ASA of holistic scores
evaluating the overall proficiency level according to the Com-
mon European Framework of Reference (CEFR), namely the
International Corpus Network of Asian Learners of English (IC-
NALE) [22], the CLES corpus of spontaneous L2 English [23],
and the Speak & Improve Corpus 2025 [24]. However, there
is an over-representation of intermediate levels in most corpora.
Therefore, this under-representation of high proficiency levels
challenges the generalizability of ASA systems to those lev-
els. Although the modeling strategies followed in [7] handle
the unbalanced class distribution, the improvement in classifica-
tion accuracy is more prominent in the lower and intermediate
levels. On the one hand, systems trained in corpora with rep-
resentation of various proficiency levels (e.g., ICNALE, Speak
& Improve Corpus 2025) that are able to predict holistic profi-
ciency are useful in cases such as placement tests. On the other
hand, systems that specialize in specific adjacent levels of pro-
ficiency (e.g. using CLES) are more useful in cases where a
specific level is being evaluated, such as in CEFR level exams.
The novelty of this study lies in three aspects. The primary
focus of our study is the classification of highly proficient learn-
ers with a CEFR level of C1 or higher. Specifically, we inves-
tigate whether SSL models are able to represent speech char-

2. Data
This study uses two datasets with complementary character-
istics. The Basque corpus consists of 36 hours of monologs
from official C1 certification exams. Due to institutional re-
strictions, these recordings cannot be publicly distributed. To
facilitate reproducibility and comparison with a publicly avail-
able benchmark, we additionally conduct parallel experiments
on ICNALE, an English L2 learner corpus. Although the two
datasets differ in language and proficiency levels, this contrast
enables us to evaluate the robustness of self-supervised speech
representations under distinct linguistic and assessment condi-
tions. The following subsections describe each dataset in detail,
including their composition and proficiency distributions.
2.1. C1-HABE
The C1-HABE database consists of 309 audio files oral exams
(approximately 107 hours) for CEFR C1 level of Basque. Those
were the official exams carried out by HABE, an autonomous
institution of the Basque Government’s Department of Culture
and Language Policy, between 2014 and 2022 (excluding 2020).
Each file contains the recording of an oral exam of approxi-
mately 20 minutes, which includes two examinees (except for
one recording that includes only one examinee), and are con-
ducted in the presence of one or two examiners. Each exam
has three different parts: a) an introduction, where the exami-
nees present themselves, b) a monolog, where each examinee
presents a text that was provided to them, with occasional ques-
tions by the examiners, and c) a dialog between the examinees.
In each exam in our corpus, both examinees received the same
qualification (“Pass”/”Fail”). Examinees in C1-HABE could
participate in oral exams only after having received a pass grade
on the written essay of the C1 level exam. Thus, we can be
confident that the level of all students who received a ”Fail”
qualification was not lower than B2. An examinee receives the
“Pass” qualification for an overall score equal to or greater than
15/30. However, this database contains only examinees who
received a mark over 20/30. The distribution of the exams’
qualifications in this database is unbalanced (140 “Pass”, 169
“Fail”). The L1(s) of all but 11 examinees was either Basque,
Spanish, or both. The exam recordings were manually anno-
tated for speaker turns, as well as for the part of the exam.
In this study, we focus on student monologs, which provide a
controlled speech segment suitable for automated assessment.
Since our goal was binary classification of exams based on pure
speech data, we balanced the gender distribution in the training
and development splits to prevent model bias toward a particular
gender. As the gender distribution in the database was skewed
towards women, the test split was not gender-balanced. We also
ensured that each split included exams from all recording years,

2.2. ICNALE
The ICNALE corpus [22] contains speech recordings and text
essays of students from Asia (China, Hong Kong, Indonesia,
Japan, Pakistan, the Philippines, Singapore/Malaysia, South
Korea, Taiwan, and Thailand) and native English speakers. The
student data contain the proficiency level obtained, which is
aligned with the following CEFR levels: A2, B1.1, B1.2, B2+
(i.e., B2 or higher). For the purpose of this study, we focused
on the students’ monologs of the speech recordings, to be con-
sistent with the type of task of the C1-HABE database. Each
monolog has a duration of approximately 60 seconds.
Although intuitively the obvious choice would be to use the
data of the levels lower than B2 to resemble the ”Fail” data of
C1-HABE, and the B2+ level and the native speaker data to
resemble the ”Pass” data, we decided to include only the data
belonging to the B1 and B2+ levels, corresponding to ”Fail”
and ”Pass”, respectively. This choice was based on two reasons
considering the differences between the two datasets. First, in
ICNALE, the students had different L1s and the native speakers
had a different L1 from the students, while in C1-HABE, most
of the students of both qualifications were fluent in Spanish.
Second, in ICNALE, students who received the B2+ qualifica-
tion could actually have a B2, C1 or C2 level of proficiency, pos-
sibly overlapping with the proficiency levels of native speakers,
while in C1-HABE, the contamination between the two classes
is less, with ”Pass” potentially including C1 or C2 level.
Finally, we divided the monologs into three splits for our
experiments, dividing each monolog into 20-second segments
to match the segment length used with the C1-HABE data.
For monologs shorter than 60 seconds, overlapping segments
were allowed. Nevertheless, only nine monologs had segments
that showed an overlap greater than one second. For monologs
with a duration greater than 60 seconds, three 20-second seg-
ments were created, discarding the extra parts of the recordings.
For the splits, we used the same distribution of the number of
monologs as the one used in [5]. Table 2 shows the number of
monologs and segments per split for each class.

3. Model architectures
In this section, we briefly introduce the SSL frameworks used
in this study: wav2vec 2.0 and mHuBERT-147. Both frame-
works share a common encoder architecture, as described in the
following.
3.1. wav2vec 2.0
Wav2vec 2.0 is a widely used SSL framework that is able
to learn speech representation [17], by relying on contrastive
learning of masked latent representations. To obtain speech
representations, raw speech audio signals are first fed into a
convolutional neural network (CNN) encoder. Subsequently, a
transformer-based encoder extracts the contextual frame-level
speech representations.
In our experiments, the hidden states were aggregated into
a fixed-dimensional segment-level representation output after
using a mean-pooling layer. The resulting representations
were passed to a linear binary classification head using the
Wav2Vec2ForSequenceClassification class, trained with cross-
entropy loss. The pre-trained models were fine-tuned keeping
the CNN encoder frozen. Speech input audio was resampled
to 16 kHz. We applied a 10% dropout within the Transformer
layers. The model was optimized using the AdamW optimizer
(epsilon: 1e-8, weight decay: 0.01) with a linear learning-rate
scheduler, and a warm-up ratio of 0.1. Training was performed
with a batch size of 4, and gradient accumulation over 4 steps.
Early stopping with a 3-epoch patience was employed based on
the performance on the dev split.
3.2. mHuBERT-147
The multilingual extension of the HuBERT SSL framework
[18], mHuBERT-147 [25], is designed to learn language-
agnostic speech representations, trained on approximately 90k
hours of open-license data from 147 languages belonging to 19
language families, including Basque. Both HuBERT and mHu-
BERT learn speech representations by predicting the correct
cluster-based targets of masked latent representations, obtained
by the same encoder architecture described above (see 3.1).
For our experiments, we used the exact same configuration
and hyperparameters as those we used with wav2vec 2.0, except
that the HubertForSequenceClassification class was used for the
binary classification head.
4. Experiments and results
All experiments described in this section follow the same pro-
cedure: each model is trained with four different learning rates
(lr: 1e-5, 2e-5, 2.5e-5, 5e-5), with the same seed. The lr used
in the experiments was chosen on the basis of a single seed per-
formance on the dev set by choosing the lr showing the lowest
equal error rate (EER). The best performance was achieved for
a peak lr of 1e-5 for all models except for the wav2vec 2.0 xlsr
model (see 4.2) in the experiments with ICNALE (lr: 5e-5). For
the learning rate chosen, we trained each model using five seeds
(101-105) to ensure the reproducibility and stability of our mod-
els. Since each monolog was divided into multiple 20-second
segments but a single label was required per student, segment-
level predictions had to be aggregated.
Moreover, given the use of multiple random seeds, we also
combined predictions across seeds using three strategies:
• a model soup (MS) approach [27], where we loaded the mod-
els of all seeds and computed the layer-wise average weights
• seed majority vote (SMV), where for each seed model we
calculated the prediction label per student monolog by major-
ity voting, and then calculated the majority vote per student
monolog across seeds
• probability averaging majority vote (PMV), where for each
seed model we averaged segment-level probabilities to ob-
tain a monolog-level probability, then we calculated the pre-
diction label based on that probability, and finally majority
vote per student monolog across seeds
For the inference of each model-seed combination on the
test set, we used the decision threshold based on the EER on the
dev set.
In order to statistically explore the performance difference
between model architectures and between strategies, we fitted
mixed-effects logistic regression in R [28] using the lme4 pack-
age [29]. The data frame consisted of one line per student, per
architecture-strategy combination, including random intercept
for each student id. We calculated the variableCorrect(1/0 for
correct/incorrect predictions), which served as the dependent
variable. The variablesArchitectureandStrategy, and their in-
teraction were used as predictors, using sum coding. We calcu-
lated marginal means using the packageemmeans[30] to assess
the effect of our predictors with more than two levels on the
dependent variable, and Bonferroni-correctedp-values, to ac-
count for multiple comparisons. In the following experiments,
only statistically significant effects are discussed.
4.1. C1-HABE
We initialized our experiments from two pre-trained models:
An mHuBERT-147 model1 that was previously fine-tuned for
ASR in Basque but had the last connectionist temporal classifi-
cation (CTC) layer removed and a wav2vec 2.0 xlsr [31] model2
which was also fine-tuned for ASR in Basque. It should be
noted that the embedding dimensions of these models differ,
with 768 for the former and 1024 for the latter. Nevertheless,
mHuBERT-147 has been found to outperform larger wav2vec
2.0 models [25].
Table 3 shows the results of each model and strategy in
the test set, in terms of accuracy and F1. As we can observe,
mHuBERT-147 shows the best performance in both metrics
compared to wav2vec 2.0 xlsr. Statistical analysis showed that
the predictions of mHuBERT-147 were significantly more ac-
curate than those of wav2vec 2.0 xlsr (β= 0.30,95% CI: 0.02,
0.59,p= .036).

5. Discussion
In this paper, we compared wav2vec 2.0 and mHuBERT-147
models, fine-tuned to predict C1 proficiency level of Basque, a
low resource language isolate, in a propietary corpus, and B2
proficiency level of English in ICNALE. Our results show that
mHuBERT-147, shows a modest but significant improvement
in classification accuracy of C1 proficiency level in Basque
compared to wav2vec 2.0 xlsr, despite mHuBERT-147 having
a lower dimensionality. In contrast, wav2vec 2.0 base per-
forms significantly better than mHuBERT-147 in English B2-
level classification accuracy. We speculate that this discrepancy
can be due to various factors, including the native language of
the learners, the target language, the level of proficiency being
assessed, and the composition of each corpus.
The native language of the learners could play a crucial
role in the performance of the classifiers. Recalling the results
of [20], wav2vec 2.0 models are better at capturing supraseg-
mental features in Mandarin (i.e., tone), while the HuBERT ar-
chitecture shows superior performance in English accent and

stress. This is highly relevant for the interpretation of our re-
sults, as many of the languages spoken in the regions present in
the ICNALE corpus are tonal languages (e.g., Mandarin, Can-
tonese, Thai), thus capturing the presence of such features could
affect the classification accuracy of the learners’ proficiency
level. Conversely, the L1s in C1-HABE (i.e., Basque and Span-
ish) are phonetically more similar to each other.
English is overrepresented in speech technology applica-
tions and many models such as wav2vec 2.0 base [17] are pre-
trained using only English data. Conversely, models as in the
case of wav2vec 2.0 xlrs [31] and mHuBERT-147 [25] are pre-
trained in more than 50 languages. This makes these models
more suitable for multilingual applications or applications not
exclusively targeting English, specifically in the case of Basque,
which is a non-Indo-European language isolate lacking mono-
lingual speakers and it is a low resource language regarding
speech technologies.
Finally, factors that we should take into account include the
proficiency level being predicted and the composition of each
corpus. In the case of the C1-HABE database, the positive class
(”Pass”) contained speakers whose level in Basque was C1 (or
higher), while the negative class (”Fail”) had achieved a level of
at least B2, since examinees had already passed the written ex-
ams of C1. However, in the case of ICNALE, the positive class
contained examinees whose level was B2 or higher, while in the
negative class the level was B1. Thus, the positive class in the
case of C1-HABE could contain levels C1 or C2, while in the
case of ICNALE, levels B2, C1 or C2. Additionally, the level
differences could also be a contributing factor, as examinees of
both classes in the C1-HABE database would have likely mas-
tered the phonetical and phonological aspects of Basque which
are much closer to their L1, while this might not always be the
case in the lower levels B1 and B2 in ICNALE. Furthermore,
in the case of C1-HABE, the test split contains more than four
times the amount of data than ICNALE, making each incorrect
prediction on the English dataset much more influential than in
the case of Basque.
A secondary finding of our study addresses the effect of
multi-seed ensemble strategies. Although for the classification
models for the C1 level of proficiency in Basque we did not
find any significant difference based on ensemble strategy, we
found a statistically significant effect for B2+ level of English
proficiency models. Importantly, SMV was significantly better
than the MS approach, which in turn was significantly better
than PMV , also reflected in superior performance in F1.
