Abstract
Wearable devices such as smart glasses increasingly demand
selective listening. Accordingly, recent works often explore
region-based spatial target speech extraction (TSE). However,
conventional fixed-boundary selection is sensitive to small spa-
tial shifts near boundaries, and methods relying only on distance
or azimuth become ambiguous under spatial overlap (e.g., shar-
ing the same distance or azimuth). To address these issues, we
propose SPOT-TSE, a multi-channel point-guided TSE frame-
work where the target is specified by a continuous spatial point
query in distance and azimuth. We stabilize point-based con-
ditioning via spatial query encoding and proximity-weighted
soft targets during training. To further handle overlap cases,
we adopt hard-negative spatial sampling. Experiments on sim-
ulated smart glasses datasets show improved spatial selectivity
and lower word error rate, while reducing computational cost
compared to a recurrent baseline.

1. Introduction
The ability to isolate a specific speaker in a noisy, multi-speaker
environment is a fundamental requirement for wearable audio
devices such as smart glasses and hearables [1, 2]. In such
scenarios, users often seek selective listening, where speech
of interest is preserved while interfering speakers and back-
ground noise are suppressed. Target speech extraction has been
widely studied to achieve this by utilizing various auxiliary
cues [3]. While effective for personalized extraction, identity-
conditioned TSE systems typically require enrollment utter-
ances or pre-defined speaker embeddings [4, 5]. Such cues are
often impractical when the target is specified only by spatial lo-
cation in wearable scenarios, and they may also raise privacy
concerns by exposing personal voice attributes [6].
To enable TSE without enrollment, multi-channel spatial
selection systems have leveraged directional cues through neu-
ral spatial filtering, beamforming, and azimuth-conditioned sep-
aration [7–13]. These approaches exploit inter-channel infor-
mation to emphasize signals from a desired direction, but re-
lying on azimuth alone can degrade performance when mul-
tiple sources share similar or identical azimuths despite being
located at different distances. Motivated by proximity as an-
other strong criterion for selective listening, recent work has
also explored distance-based TSE [14–16]. In particular, [15]
conditioned extraction on an exact queried distance, making it
one of the few prior approaches that targets a specific distance
rather than a distance interval. However, conditioning on only
one spatial dimension (azimuth or distance) remains vulnerable

instead of isolating only the source at the queried distance itself.
Moreover, strict hard-label supervision induced by rigid region

To address these limitations, we propose spatial point-
guided target speech extraction (SPOT-TSE), a multi-channel
TSE framework that conditions extraction on a continuous spa-
tial point query in distance and azimuth. SPOT-TSE combines
spatial query encoding (SQE) with ambiguity-aware training
for precise point-guided conditioning under spatial overlap and
in near-boundary cases, while adopting a Mamba-based time-
frequency grid network (TF-GridNet) backbone to reduce com-
putational cost [21, 22]. Figure 1 shows the SPOT-TSE frame-
work. Our main contributions are as follows:
• We propose SPOT-TSE, a point-guided spatial TSE frame-

spatial point query in distance and azimuth.
• We introduce SQE to represent continuous spatial point
queries defined by distance and azimuth for precise point-

2.2. Model Architecture
Given the multi-channel time-domain mixture, we first trans-
form it into the short-time Fourier transform (STFT) domain
and construct the input by concatenating the real–imaginary
spectra with inter-channel spatial cues. Specifically, we com-
pute inter-channel level difference (ILD), inter-channel phase
difference (IPD), and coherent-to-diffuse ratio (CDR) features
relative to a reference microphone using the remaining chan-
nels, and concatenate them with the complex STFT features be-
fore the front-end convolutional encoder. ILD and IPD are ef-
fective for azimuth discrimination, but can be less informative
when sources share similar azimuths. CDR complements them
as a diffuseness-related cue; under diffuse-field assumptions,
it is correlated with the direct-to-reverberant ratio (DRR) [23],
providing additional near/far information.
To reduce computational cost for wearable deployment, we
replace the bidirectional long short-term memory (BiLSTM)
blocks and causal long short-term memory (LSTM) blocks in
the TF-GridNet backbone used in SoundBubble [1] with Bi-
Mamba and Mamba blocks [22], respectively. BiMamba mod-
els global spectral dependencies along the frequency axis, while
causal Mamba handles streaming temporal modeling. This de-
sign leverages the linear scaling of state-space models while
preserving the original streaming formulation.

3. Experiments
3.1. Dataset and Simulation Setup
Following the multi-channel simulation pipeline in SoundBub-
ble [1], we generated 5-second acoustic mixtures with a sam-
pling rate of 24 kHz using pyroomacoustics [26]. Clean
speech was sourced from the VCTK Corpus [27] and the Lib-
riSpeech family [28], with background noise from WHAM!
[29]. We simulated a 7-channel smart glasses array based on
the Project Aria topology [30].
To systematically evaluate spatial generalization, we con-
structed three datasets with increasing geometric complexity:
1) D1 Fixed room size and fixed microphone position: The
room geometry was fixed to 7×8×3 m. The 7-channel array
center was fixed at (3.5, 4.0, 1.1) m with a fixed yaw of 90◦.
Reverberation was controlled with a fixed reverberation time
(RT60) target of 0.2 s (equivalently fixed absorption in simula-
tion).
2) D2 Fixed room size and random microphone posi-
tions: The room remained 7×8×3 m with the same RT60 tar-
get ( 0.2 s), while array placement was randomized per mix-
ture: x ∈ [1, 6], y ∈ [1, 7], z ∈ [1.2, 1.8] m and yaw
∈ [−180◦, 180◦].
3) D3 Random room sizes and random microphone po-
sitions: Both room and array were randomized. Room di-
mensions were sampled as L ∈ [5, 8], W ∈ [4, 8], and
H∈ [2, 4] m. The array placement followed the same margin
and height constraints as D2, with random yaw. Reverberation
was diversified by sampling absorption in [0.1, 0.9], yielding a
broad range of effective RT60 values.
Across D1–D3, the speaker policy was shared: three can-
didate speakers were sampled in the frontal hemisphere (±95◦,
0.5–3.5 m), and 1–2 rear interferers were added with probabil-
ity 0.35 outside±100◦. Minimum inter-speaker spacing was
0.5 m. Thus, the number of sourcesK ranged from 3 to 5 across
mixtures. To implement hard-negative spatial sampling, we
used azimuth and distance margins ofτθ = 5◦ andτd = 0.5 m,
respectively, and generation followed a deterministic curricu-
lum: 30% close in azimuth but far in distance, 30% close in
distance but far in azimuth, and 40% default random place-
ment. For the main separation evaluation, we used the full split
with train/validation/test = 50,000/5,000/5,000 mixtures. For
ablation studies, we used a reduced subset (train/validation/test
= 10,000/2,000/2,000) under the same simulation protocol to
reduce computation time. For objective evaluation, we re-
port SDR [31], perceptual evaluation of speech quality (PESQ)
[32], and short-time objective intelligibility (STOI) [33]. For
automatic speech recognition (ASR)-oriented evaluation, we
report WER, computed by transcribing signals with Whisper
small.en [34] and scoring them against the clean target tran-
script with standard text normalization.
3.2. Training Setup
Front-end and backbone. We used a streaming STFT front-
end module with a 288-point Hann window and a 192-point
hop, corresponding to a 12 ms analysis window with an 8 ms
current chunk and 4 ms lookahead at 24 kHz. The resulting 7-
channel complex STFT was concatenated with the aforemen-
tioned spatial cues via early fusion. For all experiments, we
used the first channel (microphone 0) as the reference micro-
phone for both spatial-cue computation and definition of the tar-
get imagesk,ref. The separator was a 6-layer TF-GridNet [21]
(B = 6 , feature channels = 32 ), using the same streaming
setup as the front-end. Intra-frame processing used BiMamba
blocks, and the query was injected via FiLM from the second
block onward. For SQE, we set (dmin,d max) = (0.5 m, 3.5 m)
and (θmin,θ max) = (−95◦, 95◦), L = 32 Fourier frequency
bands, and set Dq = 128 for the spatial query embedding di-
mension.
Optimization. Training used 4.5 s random crops with batch
size 4. For proximity-weighted soft targets, we set σd = 0.5 m
and σθ = 5.0◦ with βd = βθ = 2 . Following SoundBub-
ble [1], we optimized the signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) recon-
struction loss,L =−SNR(strgt, ˆs), where ˆs denotes the es-
timated target signal, for 200 epochs using Adam [35] (initial
learning rate 1.2× 10−3), with gradient clipping at 1.0 and au-
tomatic mixed precision (AMP).
4. Results
4.1. Overall Separation Performance
Table 1 reports separation performance, perceptual quality, and
ASR metrics across D1–D3, which increase in geometric and
acoustic complexity. The unprocessed mixtures remain chal-
lenging across all three settings, whereas SPOT-TSE yields
large gains in every case. On D1, D2, and D3, it improves SDR
from -4.94, -4.79, and -4.64 dB to 14.95, 13.94, and 11.05 dB,
respectively. Over the same datasets, it reduces WER from 1.05,
1.03, and 1.05 to 0.12, 0.14, and 0.22, indicating that the sepa-
rated outputs remain effective for downstream speech recogni-
tion. Performance decreases gradually from D1 to D3, but the
gains remain substantial even in the most challenging setting.
The smaller gap between D1 and D2 suggests that randomiz-
ing microphone placement alone is less challenging than jointly
randomizing room acoustics and array placement, as in D3. The
consistent gains in PESQ and STOI further indicate that contin-
uous spatial point conditioning improves perceptual quality and
intelligibility while suppressing interfering sources.

4.2. Spatial Selectivity and Query Robustness
We further evaluated robustness on two ambiguity-focused sub-
sets of reduced D3: azimuth-close mixtures ( |∆θ|≤ 5◦) and
distance-close mixtures (|∆d|≤ 0.5 m). For each subset, we
averaged SDR over nearby offsets along the other spatial di-
mension, rather than at a single exact offset, to summarize local
spatial selectivity around the queried point. Table 2 also reports
the overall SDR on the full reduced D3 subset.
Removing hard-negative (HN) sampling degraded the
azimuth-close case (4.15 vs. 2.93 dB) and lowered the overall
SDR (8.31 vs. 8.17 dB), while the distance-close case remained
comparable (7.95 vs. 8.46 dB). This suggested that HN mainly
helped when the model had to avoid over-relying on azimuth
cues and instead use both distance and azimuth information un-
der confusable conditions. Removing proximity-weighted soft
targets degraded both ambiguity-focused cases (3.33 and 7.16
dB) and lowered the overall SDR to 7.31 dB, supporting its role
in reducing boundary sensitivity around the queried point.
Removing spatial cues (ILD, IPD, and CDR) lowered the
azimuth-close and overall SDR to 2.38 and 7.14 dB, respec-
tively, while the distance-close case remained relatively close
to the full model at 7.55 dB. This indicated that explicit
inter-channel spatial cues were especially helpful for azimuth-
confusable cases. Removing either query axis also degraded
performance: removing distance consistently lowered SDR,
while removing azimuth pushed SDR below 0 dB across set-
tings. This confirmed that both query dimensions were neces-
sary for reliable point-guided extraction. We compared SQE
against a simpler query-conditioning variant that directly pro-
jected the normalized distance and azimuth scalars with a linear
layer. Replacing SQE with this scalar-only input caused large
drops in both settings ( −2.51 and−0.97 dB) and in overall
SDR (−0.61 dB), confirming that richer query encoding was
important for precise point-guided separation.

4.3. Computational Complexity
For wearable and always-on scenarios, computational com-
plexity is strictly constrained. To evaluate this, we compared
the proposed BiMamba-based SPOT-TSE against a conven-
tional LSTM-based TF-GridNet baseline from SoundBubble
[1]. For a controlled comparison, both models were trained
and evaluated on the same reduced D3 subset. As shown in
Table 3, replacing recurrent BiLSTM/LSTM layers with Bi-
Mamba/Mamba blocks reduced MAC/s by 74% (from 40.19
to 10.54 G/s) while maintaining comparable SDR (8.31 vs.
8.29 dB), although the parameter count increased (1.46M vs.
0.52M). These results suggest lower arithmetic complexity with
similar separation performance in this setting.
5. Conclusion
We presented SPOT-TSE, a multi-channel point-guided TSE
framework that conditions extraction on a continuous spatial
point query rather than a fixed spatial region. By combining
SQE, proximity-weighted soft targets, and hard-negative spa-
tial sampling, the proposed method improved robustness un-
der spatial overlap and other confusable scenarios. Exper-
iments demonstrate improvements in separation quality and
downstream ASR performance across increasingly challeng-
ing settings, while reducing computational cost relative to an
LSTM baseline. Future work will explore stronger distance-
aware modeling and validation on real-device recordings and
real-world datasets.
