Abstract
Digital speech measures are promising for remote monitoring
of amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS). However, single speech
features can be sensitive to measurement noise. In this work,
composite index scores, with feature weights estimated from a
natural history dataset, were evaluated for reliability, measure-
ment error, and their ability to capture disease progression on
the VRG50635 ALS clinical trial data. We conducted a sys-
tematic psychometric evaluation to quantify measurement error
reduction in composite scores relative to their constituent fea-
tures. Linear mixed effects models were applied to assess longi-
tudinal change relative to the ALSFRS-R. The results show that
index scores have excellent ICC (>0.9) and reduce the MDC
compared to individual features, while maintaining sensitivity
to track longitudinal changes. Our findings show that compos-
ite index scores can be applied across datasets and provide great
potential as robust endpoints in clinical trials.
Index Terms: speech biomarkers, composite index, remote
monitoring, clinical trials, amyotrophic lateral sclerosis
1. Introduction
Amyotrophic Lateral Sclerosis (ALS) is a progressive motor
neuron disease causing muscular atrophy and loss of volun-
tary motor control in persons with ALS (pALS). Despite ma-
jor investments in clinical research, only 6 out of 38 placebo-
controlled ALS clinical trials with over 100 participants (1996-
2022) showed statistically significant evidence of efficacy [1].
Reasons for this include poor trial design that introduces or ex-
acerbates variability (e.g., measurement noise, placebo effects,
rater bias), and the lack of sensitive, reliable and objective end-
points to detect disease progression (including toward speech
and bulbar impairment) and potential treatment effect [2].
Digital health technologies (DHTs) emerged in recent years
as promising exploratory endpoints in clinical trials [3]. Prior
work showed the feasibility, scalability, and clinical utility
of digital speech measures for monitoring ALS progression
[4, 5, 6, 7, 8]. An open challenge is that measures – both digital
speech or standard clinical scales – can be sensitive to differ-
ent sources of noise, for example to varying recording condi-
tions, placebo/Hawthorne effects or participant fatigue. Conse-
quently, the standard error of measurement (SEM) and the min-
imal detectable change (MDC) [9] can be too large to reliably
detect change, which is critical for clinical trial endpoints.
Composite scores have several advantages over individ-
ual measures. Aggregating multiple indicators reduces random
measurement error predictably, as described by the Spearman–
Brown formula [10, 11], and it reduces the multiplicity problem,
which is desirable for primary reporting in clinical trials [12].
Index scores can have better construct coverage than individual
measures. The current gold standard endpoint for ALS trials,
the ALS Functional Rating Scale - revised (ALSFRS-R) [13],
is a composite score. However, it is based on subjective judge-
ment and it lacks sensitivity to change in the early stages of bul-
bar disease onset [14, 15, 16]. Previous work on speech-based
index scores in ALS demonstrated feasibility and good perfor-
mance of weighted linear feature combinations to distinguish
pALS with bulbar onset vs. non-bulbar onset [17].
In this work, we leveraged a natural history dataset to derive
feature weights for composite scores using different machine
learning methods. We then applied these weights to data from a
Phase 1b clinical ALS trial and conducted a systematic psycho-
metric assessment of the resulting index scores. To the best of
our knowledge, this is the first study that evaluates composite
speech scores in such a cross-context setting in order to assess
their generalizability. The contributions of this work include:
1. Investigating different methods of combining digital speech
measures into interpretable composite scores for ALS that
optimize for either (i) bulbar symptom involvement or
(ii) perceived listener effort [18, 19].
2. Performance analysis of speech measures and composite in-
dexes thereof as measured by efficacy of modeling longitudi-
nal ALS progression and correlations with established stan-
dard measures – ALSFRS-R, percent predicted slow vital ca-
pacity (PP/SVC) and neurofilament light (NfL).
3. Systematic psychometric evaluation of composite scores
spanning intraclass correlation coefficients (ICC), SEM,
MDC at 95% confidence (MDC95), test-retest reliability, and
Bland–Altman limits of agreement [20, 21, 22].
4. Head-to-head comparison of composite indexes against their
component speech measures, empirically quantifying mea-
surement error reduction attributable to aggregation.
5. Demonstration of generalizability: models trained on natural
history data validated on an independent clinical trial cohort.

2.3. Speech Tasks and Feature Extraction
Speech assessments were captured in both studies using the
Modality.AI assessment platform [24, 25, 26], a web-based ser-
vice for remote clinical assessments. A virtual guide walks par-
ticipants through a structured set of tasks and elicits different
types of speech samples that have been shown to capture differ-
ent aspects of ALS progression [8, 27, 28, 29, 30]. The follow-

and the Montreal Forced Aligner (v2.0.0.a22) [32]. Linguis-
tic features were computed using the Python package spaCy
(v3.7.2), based on automatic transcriptions obtained with AWS

3. Methods
3.1. Composite Index Score Construction
For constructing composite index scores we followed the ap-
proach described in [17]. Four methods for a weighted linear
combination of features were applied: a baseline with equal
weights and three approaches based on finding optimal weights
for a binary classification task – namely, logistic regression, lin-
ear discriminant analysis (LDA), and a stepwise linear model to
optimize Youden’sJstatistic. We usedscikit-learn[33] for lo-
gistic regression and LDA, and the R packageSLModels[34]
for the stepwise model. For the classification task we explored
two clinically relevant constructs: (i) bulbar impairment as in-
dicated by the ALSFRS-R bulbar subscore being below 12 or
equal to 12, and (ii) perceived listener effort (LE) as rated on

3.2. Modeling Longitudinal Progression
To evaluate sensitivity to longitudinal change due to disease
progression, we fitted linear mixed-effects models (LME) [36]
with random intercepts and slopes by participant, fitted with the
nlmeR package [37]. Fixed effects were estimated for the inter-
cept and slope. LMEs allow us to estimate group level trajecto-
ries and to derive best linear unbiased predictors (BLUP) [38]
of random effects to represent the rate of change for each indi-
vidual. To investigate differences between bulbar affected and
unaffected participants, we applied a two-group LME with fixed
effects for cohort, time, and their interaction, and random inter-
cepts and slopes for each participant.
To assess whether speech features and index scores cap-
ture clinically meaningful change, we correlated their rate of
change to the rate of change in clinical variables, including
the ALSFRS-R, PP/SVC, and NfL. Specifically, Spearman’sρ
was calculated based on the BLUPs for all variables. For the
ALSFRS-R, we focus on the bulbar subscore (first three ques-
tions, ranges from 0 to 12), the speech subscore (first question,
ranges from 0 to 4), and the total score (ranges from 0 to 48).
3.3. Psychometric Evaluation Framework
All analyses follow Classical Test Theory, in which an ob-
served score comprises a true score plus random measurement
error [9]. Test-retest and Bland–Altman analyses used consecu-
tive sample pairs within a 14-day window for each participant.

3.3.1. Internal Consistency and Test-Retest Reliability
Cronbach’sα[39] and McDonald’sω[40] were computed on
the complete-case matrix of all 10 features to assess internal
consistency of the feature set. The coefficient of variation (de-
fined as standard deviation divided by the absolute mean) was
computed for each feature. Test-retest reliability was quanti-
fied using Pearson’srand a two-way mixed-effects, absolute
agreement, single-measures intraclass correlation coefficient,
ICC(2,1) [20, 41]. Bothrand ICC(2,1) were computed on con-
secutive within-window visit pairs (≤14 days) to minimize the
contribution of true disease progression.

4.2. Sensitivity to Disease Progression
Figure 2 shows two-group LMEs fitted on the bulbar affected
and unaffected cohorts (according to King’s ALS staging) for
one individual features, RPSD, and for the Youden’s J based
index score optimized on perceived LE. Both depicted models
show significantly different rates of change for the two cohorts
(p <0.01) and a consistently steeper slope for the bulbar af-
fected group. LMEs fitted on the entire dataset (not shown here)
had a slope significantly different from zero (atp <0.01) for
all index scores and for seven out of 10 individual features.
Figure 3 shows Spearman correlations between the LME
based slopes (BLUPs) for speech features and index scores and
the BLUPs for the clinical variables ALSFRS-R, PP/SVC, and
NfL. Among the individual features we observed strong sig-
nificant correlations between timing related features (RPSD,
RPCTA, RPPPT, SITPPT, DDKSC) and the ALSFRS-R scores
and PP/SVC. All index score variants exhibit strong correla-
tions (|ρ|>0.6) with these measures as well, and moderate
significant correlations with NfL. Overall, speaking duration of
the reading passage (RPSD) has the largest absolute correla-
tions with the ALSFRS-R scores, followed closely by the index
scores, which are all in a narrow range to each other. These
findings suggest that all composite scores, regardless of their
optimization criterion and underlying method, capture clinically
meaningful change in ALS disease progression.
4.3. Internal Consistency of Component Features
Internal consistency of the 10 component features was eval-
uated on the complete dataset (n=716). Cronbach’sα=
0.773(SE= 0.013)and McDonald’sω= 0.844indicate ac-
ceptable to good internal consistency, with the gap between the
two coefficients reflecting tau-inequivalence among features —
that is, unequal factor loadings — for whichωis the more ap-
propriate estimate [40]. Split-half reliability (mean Spearman–
Brown correctedr= 0.777,SD= 0.063across 200 random
splits) was consistent with both estimates, confirming that inter-
nal consistency is not sensitive to item grouping.
4.4. Reliability and Minimal Detectable Change
Table 3 compares reliability metrics across individual features
and composite index scores. All four composite index scores
achieved ICC>0.9(range0.91–0.95), meeting the ”excellent”
benchmark of Koo & Mae [41], compared to a median ICC of
0.82across individual features (range0.66–0.95). Critically,
theMDC 95 of all composite scores fell below the between-
subject SD (61–84% of SD), whereas 7 of 10 individual features
hadMDC 95 exceeding their own SD, meaning their noise floor
is wider than the observable spread across patients.
5. Discussion
The composite speech scores evaluated in this work consistently
showed excellent test-retest reliability (ICC>0.90) and cap-
tured ALS disease progression, as evidenced by significant lon-
gitudinal LME slopes and strong correlations with ALSFRS-
R scores and pulmonary function (PP/SVC). Near-zero Bland–
Altman bias confirms that reliability improvement is not ac-
companied by systematic measurement drift. MDC 95 fell be-
low the between-subject standard deviation for all composites
(58–84 %SD) but exceeded it for the majority of individual fea-
tures, demonstrating that feature aggregation reduces measure-
ment error to a level where within-person change is detectable
above noise. The multi-domain composition of the index scores
— spanning timing and voice quality features across different
assessment tasks — provides broader construct coverage than
any single feature. Furthermore, we demonstrated cross-context
generalizability by transferring composite weights trained on a
natural history cohort to an independent, multi-lingual clinical
trial while maintaining excellent reliability.

Several limitations warrant consideration. Reading passage
speaking duration (RPSD) was the most dominant feature, ex-
hibiting the highest individual ICC (0.951) and strong clinical
correlations, raising the question whether a reduced feature set
could achieve comparable performance. Noise robustness was
assessed indirectly through test-retest reliability; a formal per-
turbation analysis via controlled Gaussian noise injection would
more directly quantify robustness. Imputation of missing fea-
ture values could smooth trajectories and thereby affect reliabil-
ity estimates. In our data, the proportion of missing samples was
low (between 0.0% to 5.2% across features), making it unlikely
that imputation materially inflated reliability estimates. Finally,
all composites were optimized using binary classification crite-
ria. Weighting strategies based on continuous target constructs
such as the ALSFRS-R bulbar subscore may yield index scores
with greater sensitivity to graded disease progression.
The present results support the readiness of composite
speech index scores as valuable endpoints in ALS trials: all
composites satisfy the ICC>0.90threshold for clinical mea-
surement [41], and the reduced SEM relative to individual fea-
tures translates directly to increased statistical power [42, 12].
Prospective validation in interventional trials and extension to
other neurodegenerative diseases are important next steps to-
ward regulatory acceptance of digital speech biomarkers.
