Abstract
Neural audio codecs have gained recent popularity for their use
in generative modeling as they offer high-fidelity audio recon-
struction at low bitrates. While human listening studies remain
the gold standard for assessing perceptual quality, they are time-
consuming and impractical. In this work, we examine the relia-
bility of existing objective quality metrics in assessing the per-
formance of recent neural audio codecs. To this end, we conduct
a MUSHRA listening test on high-fidelity speech signals and
analyze the correlation between subjective scores and widely
used objective metrics. Our results show that, while some met-
rics align well with human perception, others struggle to capture
relevant distortions. Our findings provide practical guidance for
selecting appropriate evaluation metrics when using neural au-
dio codecs for speech.

1. Introduction
Quality assessment of audio signals is crucial for many use-
cases, such as broadcast and streaming, telephony, or generative
applications (e.g., text-to-speech models). These applications
ideally work with high compression during the generation or
transmission of audio content while maintaining high percep-
tual quality. Neural Audio Codecs (NACs) present a promis-
ing solution to this compression-quality trade-off, achieving re-
markable compression efficiency while maintaining high per-
ceptual audio quality [1].
Operating below 8 kbps, and in the case of speech as low
as 1 kbps [2–4], NACs leverage deep learning architectures to
synthesize high-fidelity audio from compressed representations,
significantly outperforming traditional audio codecs in recon-
struction fidelity at similar bitrates [3, 5–7]. However, evaluat-
ing the perceptual quality of reconstructed speech signals from
these novel neural codecs remains an open question, especially
for stereophonic content, where spatial fidelity plays a crucial
role in the overall listening experience.
While subjective listening tests such as MUSHRA (MUl-
tiple Stimuli with Hidden Reference and Anchor) [8] and the
related Basic Audio Quality (BAQ) scale, remain the gold stan-
dard for evaluating perceptual audio quality, they are costly,
time-consuming, and impractical for large-scale evaluations or
iterative development. Although listening tests in controlled
environments are the most reliable method for assessing BAQ,
there is a clear need for reliable, objective evaluation metrics.
Traditional objective quality metrics such as PESQ [9] and
PEAQ [10] are widely used for speech and general audio cod-
ing, but their effectiveness in capturing distortions introduced
by recent NACs remains underexplored.
Previous work conducted a comprehensive evaluation of
objective perceptual audio quality measures across different ap-
plication domains, specifically focusing on traditional (non-
neural) audio coding and source separation [11]. The study
revealed that most objective measures exhibit domain depen-
dence, performing well only in their intended application do-
main. Therefore, the question remains: How do reconstructed
speech signals from recent NACs correlate with widely used
objective quality metrics? To this end, we build on previous
work [11, 12] and perform a MUSHRA listening test to find the
correlation between four current state-of-the-art NACs [3,4,6,7]
capable of encoding both speech and general audio in a low-
bitrate domain. Additionally, we test against two state-of-the-
art vocoders that were trained to reconstruct audio from the la-
tent space of the EnCodec model [13, 14]. While this list of
models is by no means exhaustive, the selected models span the
sub-10 kbps range and have been widely used in practice for
audio, speech, and music generation tasks, making them ideal
candidates for our study. For our experiments, we use clean
speech samples and speech samples containing background au-
dio from ODAQ [12].
Our contributions can be summarized as follows:
• We review widely used objective quality metrics for audio
and speech by conducting a MUSHRA listening study on
high-fidelity speech and audio samples encoded and decoded
by current state-of-the-art neural audio codecs.
• We analyze the correlations between the objective quality
metrics and the MUSHRA results, giving recommendations
on which metrics best capture human perception. This guide-
line helps future evaluations of novel neural audio codecs for
speech and audio.
• We publicly release the collected MUSHRA ratings to facili-
tate future research on objective metric development for neu-
ral audio codecs.
2. Methodology
To evaluate whether widely used objective metrics correlate
well with subjective human judgment on high-fidelity speech
signals, we run the following experiment. We use the 11 clean
speech samples and the 11 combined (speech and background)
samples from ODAQ [12], which are either encoded at 44.1 kHz
or 48 kHz. We resample all signals to 48 kHz. As all of the
tested NACs do not natively support stereo, we encode and de-
code each channel separately. We encode each sample with six
different models (cf. Section 2.1) and conduct a MUSHRA hu-
man listening study (cf. Section 2.3). The resulting scores are
then used to calculate the correlation with the objective metrics
(cf. Section 2.2).

2.1. Models
We evaluate six recent neural audio codecs and vocoder archi-
tectures for speech and general audio. For EnCodec [6] we use
the 24 kHz, 6 kbps model. Multi-Band Diffusion (MBD) [14]
is a vocoder that decodes EnCodec latents with band-wise dif-
fusion. V ocos [13] is another vocoder operating in the Fourier
domain that reconstructs EnCodec latents. DAC [7] is able to
reconstruct audio at 44.1 kHz, achieving 7.74 kbps and outper-
forming EnCodec on objective metrics and listening tests across
speech, music, and environmental sounds. SNAC [3] extends
DAC, achieving between 1-3 kbps bitrate. For our study, we
use the 44.1 kHz, 2.6 kbps model. Mimi [4], built on EnCodec,
produces acoustic and semantic tokens, compressing 24 kHz
speech to 4.4 kbps. The selected models by no means cover
the entire spectrum of available NACs, especially since there
has been a large body of recent work on neural speech codecs.
However, compared to neural speech codecs, the selected mod-
els are widely used for all types of audio (general audio, speech,
and music) and also span the sub-10 kbps range, making them
ideal candidates to evaluate both speech-only and combined au-
dio.
2.2. Objective Metrics
We evaluate audio quality with objective measures spanning
distortion, perceptual quality, intelligibility, and no-reference
mean opinion score (MOS) estimation. Distortion metrics in-
clude SNR [15], scale-invariant SI-SNR and C-SI-SNR [16,17],
as well as SDR, SI-SDR, and SA-SDR [15, 18, 19]. Percep-
tual metrics comprise PESQ [9] and PEAQ (we report the Ba-
sic ODG combining 11 MOVs) [10, 11, 20], in addition to the
2f-Model that aggregates ADB and AvgModDiff1 [11, 21]; we
exclude POLQA due to lack of access and its limited gains over
PESQ [11,22]. Speech intelligibility is measured by STOI [23],
and spectrogram similarity by ViSQOL (Audio at 48 kHz and
Speech at 16 kHz) [24, 25]. Non-intrusive MOS predictors in-
clude DNSMOS [26] and NISQA [27], while NORESQA and
NORESQA-MOS estimate relative and absolute MOS with-
out exact references [28, 29]. Furthermore, we report W ARP-
Q [30] and SCOREQ [31], two recently proposed metrics. Of
these, DNSMOS, NISQA, NORESQA, NORESQA-MOS, and
SCOREQ (no ref) are non-intrusive metrics that do not require
a reference signal, all remaining metrics are intrusive.
2.3. Subjective Listening Test
We conducted a double-blind MUSHRA [8] listening test to
evaluate the perceptual quality of neural audio codecs. We
recruited participants from a crowd-sourcing MUSHRA tool. 1
Stimuli were drawn from ODAQ [12], resampled to 48 kHz,
and loudness normalized to -28 dB to avoid clipping. Partici-
pants were required to take short breaks between trials to avoid
fatigue. Each trial consisted of nine stimuli: The signals recon-
structed from the models as well as a hidden reference and two
anchors (downsampled reference to 3.5 kHz and 7 kHz, respec-
tively). Listeners rated each stimulus on a 0–100 scale relative
to the reference. Stimuli orders were randomized and playback
was unrestricted. In accordance with the MUSHRA protocol,
we removed the raters who scored the reference below 90 at
least twice (out of 11 trials, this results in a failure rate of 18%
and therefore above the 15% recommended by the protocol).
After filtering, we were left with 13 participants for the speech-
only test and 17 participants for the combined audio test.

3. Results
3.1. Subjective Results
The results from the MUSHRA listening study (cf. Fig. 1) re-
veal several findings across speech-only audio and combined
audio (speech with background audio). DAC [7] consistently
outperform other NACs, but given its higher bitrate, this is not
entirely surprising. Interestingly, for the speech-only test, expe-
rienced participants were sometimes unable to distinguish the
reference signal from DAC, which underlines the excellent per-
ceptual quality of this model. Additionally, it is worth noting
that the results on combined audio showed generally lower per-
formance compared to speech-only conditions, likely due to the
compression artifacts being more noticeable in the broader fre-
quency spectrum of mixed content, whereas speech-only audio
occupies a more limited bandwidth.
Furthermore, we observe a substantial difference in perfor-
mance between V ocos [13] and EnCodec [6] when comparing
speech-only and combined audio results. While V ocos signifi-
cantly outperformed EnCodec in the speech-only setting, it per-
formed comparably to EnCodec in the combined audio setting.
This discrepancy can likely be attributed to V ocos being trained
predominantly on clean speech data, making it less robust to
mixed-content scenarios. Additionally, MBD [14] performed
significantly worse than expected. Notably, the ODAQ speech-
only samples contain speech panned to the phantom center, so
both channels carry an identical signal and independent chan-
nel coding introduces no spatial degradation. For combined
audio, however, the background sounds contain genuine stereo
content, and encoding channels independently can disrupt inter-
channel amplitude and phase relationships, potentially causing
spatial artifacts. This may partly explain the lower MUSHRA
scores for combined audio and MBD’s particularly poor perfor-
mance in that condition.

Overall, PESQ and SCOREQ stand out as the models with the
highest correlations and can thus be regarded as a good practi-
cal choice. Additionally, none of the evaluated metrics account
for spatial quality, which may contribute to the weaker correla-
tions observed on combined audio, where spatial artifacts could
influence subjective ratings without being captured by the met-
rics.
In general, intrusive metrics that compare the reconstructed
signal against a reference tend to correlate more strongly
with subjective ratings than non-intrusive ones. Among the
reference-free metrics,SCOREQ (no ref)achieves the best per-
formance on speech-only audio, but degrades considerably for
combined audio. In fact, all reference-free metrics perform
poorly on the combined dataset. This is expected, as these met-
rics were designed to assess speech clarity and are therefore
sensitive to the presence of background sounds, which they tend
to penalize regardless of whether they were part of the origi-
nal signal. This makes them unsuitable for evaluating NACs
on audio that intentionally contains non-speech content. Addi-
tionally, none of the evaluated metrics account for spatial qual-
ity, which may contribute to the weaker correlations observed
on combined audio, where spatial artifacts could influence sub-
jective ratings without being captured by the metrics. Overall,
PESQ and SCOREQ stand out as the most reliable predictors of
perceptual quality across both conditions, making them a prac-
tical choice for evaluating neural audio codecs on speech data.
Statistical significance tests (p-values<<0.05) confirmed the
reliability of these observed differences and provide a strong
validation of our findings.
4. Conclusion & Insights
In this work, we evaluated commonly used objective quality
metrics on neural audio codecs by conducting a MUSHRA lis-
tening test and analyzing correlations with subjective scores.
Our results show that PESQ and SCOREQ consistently align
best with human perception across both speech-only and com-
bined audio, making them reliable choices for practical eval-
uation. Notably, these two metrics are complementary in na-
ture: PESQ relies on a hand-crafted psychoacoustic model with
auditory transforms and masking, while SCOREQ is a data-
driven approach based on contrastive regression over learned
neural embeddings. Using both thus provides assessment from
two fundamentally different perspectives. Interestingly, simpler
distortion measures such as SNR and SDR performed compa-
rably to or even outperformed several metrics specifically de-
signed to predict perceptual quality. Based on our findings,
we offer the following practical recommendations. For evalu-
ating NACs on clean speech, SCOREQ provides the strongest
alignment with subjective ratings, with PESQ as a robust alter-
native. For mixed-content audio, PESQ is preferable as most
other metrics degrade substantially. Reference-free metrics
such as DNSMOS and NISQA showed limited correlation for
combined audio, as they are designed to assess speech clarity
and are confounded by the presence of intentional background
sounds. However, for clean speech content, reference-free met-
rics remain a viable option, with SCOREQ (no ref) offering the
strongest alignment with subjective ratings in that condition. To
support reproducibility and future research, we publicly release
the subjective ratings collected in this study.
